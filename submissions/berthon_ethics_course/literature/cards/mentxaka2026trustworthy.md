## Summary
A conceptual framework, not a study, that sorts AI's effects on democracy into seven risks (AIRD) and seven
conditional contributions (AIPD) and maps each risk onto the seven requirements of the EU High-Level Expert Group's
*Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI* (2019), with governance responses and responsible actors. The authors say it
"has not yet been empirically validated" (p. 1) and that the mappings "do not establish the causal effectiveness of the
proposed measures" (p. 2).

## Key facts it relies on
- The contributions are conditional: "These opportunities, however, should not be understood as direct or automatic
  outcomes" (p. 4); AIPD4 "Informed Citizenry" claims AI "greatly improves access to information" (p. 6).
- Transparency (Rq4) is the requirement mapped to most risks, six of seven (p. 14), though the authors grant that
  "explaining a model's output does not establish how an intervention would affect democratic outcomes" (p. 4).
- "Trust is not only about system reliability or transparency in isolation; it depends on power relations, perceived
  legitimacy, and the capacity for democratic oversight" (p. 15).
- Table I (image, p. 13) lists only institutions as "Responsible actors" (regulators, platforms, providers, auditors,
  courts, public institutions, certification bodies, civil society).

## Critical notes
- **For this arm's fifth failure (returns promised and not delivered):** the AIPD catalogue is a list of promised
  goods, but the authors hedge it themselves ("conditional", p. 4), so it is not an instance of overselling. It is a
  useful foil: the promise stated with its conditions, against Kamperman and Graff's (2026) overselling argument.
- **Principles approach:** it builds on the HLEG principles and so inherits Mittelstadt's (2019, card
  `mittelstadt2019principles`) worry that principles without enforcement give "false assurances". Its remedy for
  eroded trust is "certifying compliance with trustworthy AI requirements" (p. 12).
- Scope is democracy and public institutions; it says nothing about literacy's history.
