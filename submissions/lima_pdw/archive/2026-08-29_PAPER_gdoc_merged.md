# **Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation**

**Roger B. Hunt III** · Bentley University · rhunt@bentley.edu

*Dissertation Paper 2. Full paper for the Organization Studies / Organization Theory Paper Development Workshop, Universidad de Piura UDEP, Lima, 5–7 October 2026.*

# **Abstract**

Algorithmacy defines the interpretive sensibility an individual exercises when coordinating with another person through an opaque, adaptive algorithmic intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. While organizational scholarship has formalized the governance dynamics of hierarchies, markets, networks, and classical co-optation, the emerging form of coordinative co-optation remains theoretically incomplete, leaving the structural standing and individual capacity of the enrolled actor unmodeled. Rather than exploring legal or institutional standing/sovereignty, this article supplies the behavioral micro-foundation required to coordinate within such triadic architectures. A systematic review of seven established competence frameworks reveals a persistent structural omission: models centering the algorithmic system bracket the human counterpart, whereas models centering interpersonal interaction treat the technological artifact as a passive conduit or an external tool. To resolve this theoretical vacancy, we formalize algorithmacy through three constitutive operations derived deductively from the informational and relational deficits of the triad: *interpreting*, *specifying intent*, and *keeping track*. Finally, we present an empirical research strategy that pairs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol with a three-wave longitudinal survey panel deployed within a specialized software-engineering field setting.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication sensibility · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# **Orientation and Theoretical Motivation**

This article formalizes an emergent, widely shared organizational phenomenon within the conceptual apparatus of organization theory: the interpretive sensibility individuals exercise when navigating work mediated by automated systems. While contemporary discourse often treats artificial intelligence as an unprecedented disruption, modern platform architectures function as the latest instantiation of a broader organizational reality—the institutional intermediary through which individuals coordinate everyday activity. Professionals across diverse fields have long developed informal, experiential interpretive practices to navigate bureaucratic and computational gatekeepers. This capacity resembles an experienced pilot operating an automated flight-control system: the primary task is neither manual flight from origin to destination nor passive reliance on automation, but the cognitive readiness to monitor automated drift, interpret subtle telemetry shifts, and intervene decisively when the system encounters boundary conditions.

Operating alongside and through an automated system constitutes a distinct behavioral mode from unmediated manual execution. It requires an actor to remain attuned to an ambient computational environment, actively discerning when to allow automated routines to proceed and when to assert strategic agency. This capacity represents a foundational communicative sensibility. Paralleling the established developmental lineages of literacy, oracy, and numeracy, this article introduces *algorithmacy*: the individual capacity to interpret, communicate through, and track the shifting parameters of an opaque, adaptive intermediary.

# **Introduction**

Organization theory lacks an account of how individuals coordinate with one another when an automated platform mediates their relationship. Classical scholarship identifies three canonical coordination forms: hierarchies governed by managerial authority, markets governed by price mechanisms, and networks governed by ongoing social relationships. Modern digital platforms introduce a distinct structural configuration by using algorithms to enroll, evaluate, and match participants. While recent research identifies the individual capacities workers deploy directly against an automated system (Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), it leaves the human counterpart outside the analytical frame. Organization theory has not yet named the sensibility an individual must exercise to coordinate with another person reached exclusively through an opaque, binding algorithm. This article develops that structural and behavioral account.

First, we briefly summarize the findings from Paper 1, which identifies the structural properties of coordinative co-optation alongside classical archetypes (hierarchy, market, network, and classical co-optation), demonstrating that platform organizing leaves the formal standing and individual capacity of the enrolled actor theoretically unmodeled. Second, we evaluate seven candidate constructs across organizational theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction, demonstrating that none simultaneously accounts for an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary. To resolve this vacancy, we formalize algorithmacy to a satisficing threshold in Simon’s (1997) behavioral sense, defining the individual sensibility required to navigate this triadic structure without making unearned claims about broader institutional justice. Third, we present an empirical research strategy based on an insider action-research design, piloted across developmental cohorts and formally fielded in a specialized software-engineering course.

Extant theoretical frameworks fail to capture algorithmic coordination because they cannot simultaneously model an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary. Rather than sharing a single flaw, candidate constructs diverge across distinct analytical omissions. Some frameworks focus exclusively on technical mechanics; Long and Magerko (2020) treat the algorithm as an object of formal knowledge, leaving no communicative counterpart in view. Others personify the system into an autonomous interlocutor; Guzman and Lewis (2020) position the machine itself as the communicative partner, entirely displacing the second human actor. Even frameworks that incorporate two human actors fail to capture the intermediary's binding authority. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) model the computational artifact merely as a delegate operating on a sender's behalf, while Zhou et al. (2025) measure how workers navigate platform algorithms while reducing the human evaluator to an external score.

The underlying boundary across this literature is precise: no construct combines an active human counterpart with an opaque, consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. Where a counterpart appears, the intermediary functions as a passive transmission channel, a personal delegate, or infrastructure to bypass. Where the intermediary operates as an opaque, binding evaluator, the counterpart disappears or shrinks to a passive rating source. While online dispute resolution scholarship recognized an analogous structural configuration through Katsh and Rifkin’s (2001) "fourth party" concept, it theorized that position from the perspective of system architecture rather than participant capacity.

This coordination dynamic appears clearly in the empirical setting examined in this study. A student submits a software pull request to an automated evaluation gate. While her peers' qualitative reviews and upvotes remain publicly visible, the algorithmic weighting that converts those inputs into an authoritative pass-fail verdict remains concealed from everyone: the submitter, the peer reviewers, and the instructor, who holds no vote. The focal actor must coordinate with human peers she can observe through an evaluative rule she cannot see.

We model algorithmacy on the foundational lineage of literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Just as literacy enables participation in communication mediated by text, numeracy equips individuals to navigate environments structured by quantitative data, and oracy governs interaction through spoken discourse, algorithmacy enables sustained participation in coordination structured by an algorithm. We define algorithmacy as the sensibility required to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, specify intent through its constrained channels, and track its shifting parameters over time. This sensibility allows an actor to coordinate effectively when an automated system evaluates both transacting parties and executes binding decisions neither party controls.

In this formulation, opacity means the participant cannot observe the governing rule directly. Adaptivity means the underlying evaluation rule shifts dynamically over time. Intermediary designates the algorithmic system mediating between the focal actor and her human counterpart. While literacy, numeracy, and oracy represent sensibilities for navigating a symbolic medium, algorithmacy operates as an interpretive sensibility for an organizational coordination form, fulfilling the micro-foundational requirement of platform organizing.

We examine this sensibility through an in-editor harness interview protocol, wherein participants complete a technical task, encounter an incompletely explained outcome, and articulate both how they interpreted the decision and how they structured their subsequent actions. Drawing on preliminary calibration interviews, we refine this protocol within a specialized software engineering course at Hult International Business School, establishing the empirical foundation for formal longitudinal validation.

# **The Coordination-Form Gap**

Mature organization theory defines a coordination form by specifying both its primary operational mechanism and the formal standing of the participants it governs. Established scholarship evaluates coordination forms across six core dimensions: operational mechanics, required integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, primary instruments, failure modes, and empirical identification criteria. Four classical forms clearly pair a governing mechanism with a defined participant standing:

  - **Hierarchy:** An employee operates within an authority-governed zone of acceptance, where managerial direction secures alignment through formal standard operating procedures (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978).
  - **Market:** An autonomous transacting party evaluates a posted price term backed by an unrestricted right of refusal, relying on the price mechanism as an aggregated public statistic (Hayek, 1945).
  - **Network:** A partner invests in an elective, revocable relationship grounded in informal social reciprocity and mutual reputational capital (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990).
  - **Classical Co-optation:** An external challenger accepts a conferred administrative seat, transforming outside opposition into structured institutional participation (Selznick, 1949).

Across a managerial zone, a posted market price, an elective relationship, or an institutional seat, established theory explicitly articulates how the structural form operates alongside the formal status accorded to the individual.

The emerging governance model of coordinative co-optation breaks this classical pairing pattern because it does not address *participant* *standing*. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) define the structural departure of platform organizing by observing that while actors in hierarchies command, in markets contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted. This fifth coordination form operates through automated enrollment: digital platforms deploy algorithmic architectures to match unchosen parties, evaluate their ongoing interactions, and unilaterally terminate accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Within this architecture, the enrolled participant functions neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price terms, nor a collaborative partner in an elective network.

This structural omission exposes a fundamental micro-foundational gap within organizational theory. While macro-structural scholarship examines how platform firms leverage informational asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016) and non-portable reputational ratings (Curchod et al., 2020) to enforce administrative control, it leaves unmodeled the behavioral mechanisms that enable enrolled individuals to achieve day-to-day coordination. Regulatory debates and legal classifications focus on the standing a participant ought to hold, yet organization theory lacks an account of the individual capacity required to coordinate through an opaque, adaptive intermediary regardless of formal institutional standing. Isolating this micro-foundational capacity requires evaluating candidate constructs across adjacent literatures to determine why existing frameworks fail to model triadic algorithmic coordination.

# **Extant Constructs**

Evaluating candidate constructs for algorithmic coordination requires analyzing the precise structural role each framework assigns to the computational intermediary. Within this inquiry, a construct designates a formalized conceptualization of individual capacity, whether operationalized as a functional competence, an infrastructural literacy, or an interpretive sensibility. While adjacent organizational frameworks already incorporate a human counterpart, they assume relational conditions that algorithmic management systematically eliminates. Relational coordination theory, for instance, conceptualizes shared communication and relational ties among interdependent organizational members (Gittell, 2002). However, that model presupposes unmediated interactants who directly observe, contact, and negotiate with one another. Because an algorithmic intermediary severs direct mutual visibility, assessing candidate constructs requires determining whether a framework treats technology as a passive conduit, an autonomous interlocutor, or an opaque third party. The theoretical traditions governing extant scholarship establish the exact boundary conditions of what each individual-level construct can explain.

Seven theoretical constructs from organizational theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction approach the individual capacity required for algorithmic coordination. We evaluate each framework against a singular structural question: where does the model theoretically locate the human counterpart situated on the far side of the interaction, such as the client commissioning labor or the peer evaluator whose rating alters administrative standing? To qualify for this comparative evaluation, a construct must model individual capacity relative to an algorithmic system while addressing, even partially, the presence of an interdependent human actor. The four analytical boundary conditions detailed later in this section formalize the explicit criteria applied across all seven construct hearings.

## **Computer-Mediated Communication Competence**

Spitzberg’s (2006) model of computer-mediated communication competence imports the classical architecture of unmediated interpersonal interaction directly into digital settings by treating technology as a passive conduit. Conceptualizing computer-mediated communication as "any human symbolic text-based interaction conducted or facilitated through digitally-based technologies" (p. 630), his framework organizes individual capacity across five foundational dimensions: motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and relational outcomes. In this model, motivation captures "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in a given CMC context" (p. 640), while knowledge encompasses "the cognitive comprehension of content and procedural processes involved in conducting appropriate and effective interaction" (p. 641). Behavioral execution rests on four core skill clusters: attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness (p. 638), all conditioned by five discrete facets of context (p. 644). Underpinning this entire formulation is the theoretical premise that digital mediation recalibrates the speed and bandwidth of message exchange while leaving the fundamental structure of interpersonal competence undisturbed. The technical infrastructure operates merely as an electronic transmission channel connecting two communicators, leaving the human actors stationed at either terminal as the sole coordinators and evaluators of the interaction.

Spitzberg’s model locates communicative competence in relational co-construction rather than isolated cognitive faculties. Rooted in the relational communication tradition, his framework establishes that interacting partners jointly produce and evaluate communicative competence. As Spitzberg notes, "competent interactants can facilitate the competence of cointeractants," whereas "an incompetent interactant can diminish a normally competent cointeractant's performance" (p. 650). In this formulation, communicative acts achieve legitimacy only when evaluated against receiver expectations conditioned by the digital medium. Far from treating the human counterpart as an ancillary variable, the model positions that counterpart as its essential evaluative instrument. Five relational outcome criteria govern the framework: appropriateness, effectiveness (encompassing task achievement and efficiency), coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development (p. 648). These criteria presuppose an unmediated relational architecture where both interactants observe and perceive one another. Coorientation and relationship development operate as irreducibly bilateral dynamics; appropriateness, defined as the "perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context," requires a conscious human perceiver; and satisfaction, operationalized as "positive affect associated with fulfillment of positively valenced expectancies," demands an interacting subject capable of affective resonance.

Spitzberg’s relational architecture establishes its own theoretical boundary by presupposing a direct connection between a counterpart’s appraisal and its communicative consequence. Consider an actor who demonstrates complete mastery across every dimension of Spitzberg’s model, including baseline motivation, media knowledge, attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness. When placed within an arrangement where an opaque algorithm evaluates both parties and issues a binding determination, this actor reaches an immediate structural impasse. This breakdown does not stem from a personal deficit in individual competence, nor does it require the human counterpart to disappear. Instead, the algorithmic intermediary severs the functional link between the counterpart's evaluation and its tangible outcome. The counterpart may still observe the exchange and register a judgment, whether privately or in public view, but that appraisal reaches the focal actor only as an isolated input to an opaque aggregation that neither party controls. Consequently, interactants cannot engage in the iterative, mutual calibration required to administer Spitzberg’s criteria. The algorithmic arrangement does not merely depress a communicator’s competence score; it structurally disconnects the relational evaluative instrument from the authoritative verdict that governs both parties.

Algorithmacy directly inherits Spitzberg’s normative evaluative criteria while rejecting the relational topology his model presupposes. Although our three constitutive operations define what an actor cognitively and behaviorally executes, they do not independently establish what constitutes communicative success. Appropriateness and effectiveness remain the most robust theoretical standards available for evaluating communicative efficacy. However, algorithmacy cannot adopt the transmission architecture that underpins those criteria: a directly accessible human receiver stationed at the terminal node of a digital channel whose unmediated appraisal serves as the operational measure of competence.

## **Human–Machine Communication**

Human–machine communication replaces the classical transmission conduit by elevating the computational artifact into an autonomous communicative partner, thereby displacing the second human actor from the analytical frame. Guzman and Lewis (2020) formalize this paradigm shift by arguing that communicative artificial intelligence systems operate as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). Articulating a programmatic research agenda across conversational agents, social robotics, and automated text generation, they direct the subfield toward "questions of communication as they relate to technologies designed to fulfill the communicator role" (p. 74). In this formulation, meaning-making unfolds "within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine" (p. 74). This departure from computer-mediated communication is structurally definitive. Where the default communication model Guzman and Lewis describe has people exchanging information through a technology that mediates human interaction (p. 73), human–machine communication reconfigures the technical apparatus as an active interlocutor across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions. Guzman and Lewis acknowledge that communicative technologies can blur mediator and communicator functions simultaneously, a structural distinction that reinforces our core premise that an algorithmic intermediary operates neither as a passive conduit nor as a conventional conversational partner. Inaugurating the subfield's dedicated journal, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) establish this constitutive analytical boundary, defining human–machine communication as the study of meaning-making between humans and machines wherein the technical artifact occupies the position of a communicative subject.

The internal critique mounted by structurational theorists within human–machine communication highlights the limits of isolating person–machine encounters, yet it still fails to incorporate an interdependent human counterpart. Gibbs, Kirkwood, Fang, and Wilkenfeld (2021) argue that scholarship fundamentally mischaracterizes human–machine interaction by treating it as an isolated dyad rather than an organizational structuration. In their formulation, computational systems embody institutional rules and resources that human actors draw upon, reproduce, and selectively subvert, thereby distributing agency across the human user, the technical artifact, and the governing institutional architecture. Targeting the field’s methodological individualism, they draw on organizational cases such as algorithmic management to demonstrate that analyzing isolated person–machine interactions obscures the broader structural forces governing technological deployment.

This structurational turn leaves the theoretical vacancy unresolved because it introduces institutional context rather than an interdependent human actor. Structuration theory yields a triad linking the human actor, the computational artifact, and institutional structures; our coordination framework links the focal worker, the algorithmic intermediary, and an unseen human counterpart. Neither formulation accords an analytical position to the specific human actor on the opposite pole of the coordination process, whose reception defines the ultimate purpose of the exchange yet remains structurally inaccessible. This distinction establishes an explicit theoretical boundary. An individual may achieve comprehensive mastery within human–machine communication by treating the computational system as an interlocutor, decoding its symbolic cues, and sustaining relational rapport, yet still lack the interpretive sensibility required to coordinate interdependent labor through an automated system that simultaneously evaluates and binds both parties. The capacity theorized by human–machine communication remains, by structural definition, an orientation directed toward the machine.

Locating algorithmacy within the individual does not repeat the methodological individualism that structurational theorists critique. While Gibbs et al. (2021) caution against reducing structural dynamics to isolated individual attributes, algorithmacy represents an individual micro-foundation rooted in social antecedents (Felin, Foss, & Ployhart, 2015). The construct reconciles individual action with institutional structure through two foundational properties. First, algorithmacy develops through collective socialization rather than solitary trial and error; empirical research shows that workers acquire platform fluency through informal peer networks, community sensemaking, and shared lore (Sutherland et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2025). Second, while socially cultivated, algorithmacy remains individually held and enacted during live coordination. This individual variation explains why formally equivalent participants, placed in identical algorithmic environments, achieve starkly disparate coordination outcomes. Developing an individual-level construct with social origins satisfies the micro-foundational requirement of organization theory without collapsing into atomistic reductionism.

Human–machine communication provides the analytical demarcation that allows algorithmacy to define its structural boundaries. Guzman and Lewis (2020) establish the clearest theoretical distinction between the passive transmission conduits of computer-mediated communication and the active communicative partners of human–machine communication. We adopt this operational distinction while departing from its ontological premise, reconceptualizing the computational apparatus not as an autonomous conversational subject, but as an opaque, binding intermediary operating within an asymmetric triad. By defining their field around machine-directed meaning-making, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) establish the analytical baseline from which this paper diverges, shifting the focal inquiry from how individuals communicate with computational systems to how they coordinate interdependent work through them.

## **AI-Mediated Communication**

AI-mediated communication reintroduces the human counterpart to mediated interaction, yet it confines the computational system to the role of a partisan delegate serving a single communicator. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) define it as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 90). This conceptual model explicitly preserves both human interactants across empirical phenomena such as predictive text engines, automated smart replies, and synthetic media. The authors characterize this dynamic across five constitutive parameters: magnitude, reflecting the extent of computational alteration; media type, designating the medium of exchange; optimization goal, defining the objective guiding message generation; autonomy, capturing the degree of operation without sender supervision; and role orientation, identifying which communicator the system represents.

A purely structural node count fails to differentiate AI-mediated communication from algorithmacy because Hancock et al. (2020) already model a triad comprising two human interactants and a computational agent. Within their architecture, the receiver occupies a central rather than marginal analytical position: her cognitive inferences constitute the primary dependent variable of the empirical research agenda, while accompanying ethical inquiries address what communicative transparency she is owed. Hancock and colleagues candidly document the vulnerability inherent in the receiver's position. They observe that explicit disclosure of automated assistance remains rare, that recipients typically presume a human counterpart authored the communication, and that the entire arrangement hinges on the unverified assumption that "the message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency" (p. 90).

The decisive difference separating the two frameworks is categorical rather than parametric: AI-mediated communication governs symbolic messages, whereas algorithmacy governs binding institutional verdicts. Hancock and colleagues model an agent that modifies, augments, or generates text. Every dimension of their framework—magnitude, media type, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation—characterizes symbolic message alteration, even when optimization aligns with platform priorities rather than individual sender goals. In contrast, the algorithmic intermediary in our setting renders authoritative decisions by matching unchosen parties, ranking performance, and terminating accounts. No combination of autonomy or message magnitude transforms an assistant that edits text into an autonomous institutional authority that binds both interactants to determinations neither party commissioned. Hancock and colleagues' definitional requirement that the agent operate "on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90) underscores this boundary. In our coordination setting, the algorithmic third party operates on behalf of no one present at the interaction. While their model exposes the counterpart strictly to a modified message, our framework exposes both interactants simultaneously to an authoritative, binding determination.

We adopt the optimization goal parameter from Hancock et al. (2020) as the definitive analytical axis separating the two frameworks. Whereas their computational agent optimizes for the interpersonal or communicative objectives of a designated sender, the algorithmic intermediary in our model optimizes for institutional coordination metrics external to both interactants. We also build upon Hancock and colleagues' assessment of receiver vulnerability, wherein a receiver's comprehension and acceptance of algorithmic mediation are structurally presumed rather than actively secured. This dynamic provides the conceptual foundation for our formulation of an unseen counterpart who remains structurally barred from occupying an evaluative position altogether.

## **AI Literacy**

AI literacy frameworks treat algorithmic architecture as an object of formal technical comprehension, shifting analytical focus away from interpersonal coordination toward individual system mastery. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace." They structure this domain around five foundational conceptual inquiries: what artificial intelligence is, what it can execute, how it operates, how it should be deployed, and how humans perceive its agency. From these core questions, the authors derive seventeen discrete competencies including system identification, machine learning representations, algorithmic decision-making, model training workflows, sensor integration, ethical evaluation, and programmability.

Only two of the seventeen competencies reference human actors, and both confine humans to positions outside live interaction. Competency 10 (Human Role in AI) requires learners to "recognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems" (p. 6), while Competency 5 positions human cognition strictly as a comparative baseline against which to evaluate automated performance. Within this framework, human beings enter solely as upstream software engineers or comparative benchmarks rather than as interdependent counterparts in ongoing coordination. Neither competency accords another individual an operational role within the workflow the learner executes.

  

Long and Magerko’s (2020) conceptualization of collaboration reinforces this structural boundary by directing every communicative and collaborative action exclusively toward the computational artifact. The framework explicitly defines its objective as communicating and collaborating "with AI" and targets cognitive misconceptions that "limit people's ability to effectively use, collaborate with, and act as critical consumers of AI" (p. 2). Interpersonal collaboration appears only within auxiliary instructional recommendations, where Design Consideration 11 (Social Interaction) advises educators to "consider designing AI learning experiences that foster social interaction and collaboration" (p. 7). While human peers populate the physical classroom, they remain entirely absent from the theoretical competence model. Achieving ceiling performance across all seventeen competencies fails to equip a participant to establish communicative legibility or convey intent to an unseen human counterpart through an opaque, binding system, because all five foundational inquiries interrogate the technical properties of the computational apparatus rather than interpersonal coordination mediated by it.

This structural omission propagated directly into subsequent psychometric operationalizations, institutionalizing a dyadic model across empirical research. Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) synthesized Long and Magerko's seventeen competencies into four overarching dimensions: knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics. Subsequent measurement scales inherited this fourfold taxonomy, operationalizing individual competence strictly as an actor's unilateral transaction with a computational artifact. The omission of the interdependent human counterpart thus became codified into the standard psychometric tradition seeded by the 2020 framework.

Algorithmacy diverges from AI literacy by deriving its constitutive operations deductively from structural coordination constraints rather than compiling competencies inductively. Long and Magerko assemble their catalog using scoping criteria grounded in definitional alignment, bibliographic prevalence, and pedagogical utility. This inductive approach lacks an underlying architectural principle to explain why nine competencies govern technical mechanics while only one addresses normative ethics. In contrast, the constitutive operations of algorithmacy derive deductively from the specific informational deficits that an opaque, adaptive, and binding coordination architecture imposes on its participants. We adopt Long and Magerko’s foundational literacy genealogy, which anchors the construct within the evolutionary continuum of human symbolic capacity by tracing the historical trajectory of literacy from alphabetic text through digital, computational, and data literacies.

## **Algorithmic Competency**

Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) provide the first psychometrically validated scale measuring individual capability within algorithmic management, establishing the empirical benchmark for construct development in this domain. Their multi-stage protocol originated in 99 semi-structured qualitative interviews with Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers to generate 14 candidate items, which were subsequently refined through expert content validation. Across four successive empirical samples spanning an exploratory factor analysis (N=275), a confirmatory factor analysis (N=213), a construct validation study (N=230), and a three-wave longitudinal panel (N=225), the authors validated a second-order, four-factor structural model embedded within a nomological network of organizational antecedents and performance outcomes. The final 12-item instrument retains an aggregate internal reliability of .85 across four subdimensions, formally defining algorithmic competency as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2).

Zhou et al.’s operationalization extends beyond declarative technical knowledge by incorporating behavioral navigation and affective disposition directly into the construct. Three of the four validated dimensions capture capacities distinct from cognitive comprehension: Understanding encompasses knowledge of algorithmic management alongside the ability to navigate dynamic operational rules; Embracing captures affective willingness to trust the efficiency and fairness of automated allocations; Leveraging denotes the strategic capacity to exploit algorithmic affordances for personal benefit; and Remediating reflects a worker's ability to correct, circumvent, or supplement algorithmic failures. Zhou and colleagues establish rigorous discriminant validity for this multidimensional structure. General digital competence correlates moderately with the aggregate scale (*r* = .37, *p* \< .01) but exhibits a non-significant correlation with Embracing (*r* = .04, *p* \> .05), confirming that conventional digital literacy measures fail to capture affective orientations toward automated control.

The theoretical boundary of Zhou et al.’s instrument lies in the structural position assigned to the human counterpart, who appears strictly as an external evaluative outcome rather than an active coordination partner. While the counterpart surfaces across several scale items, none positions her as an interlocutor within the competency itself. Item 8 frames the customer as a distant punitive consequence ("I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback"), directing the worker's behavioral adaptation toward the platform rules rather than the relational counterpart. Item 11 reduces triadic coordination to an abstract attitudinal belief regarding matching logistics ("I think the platform AM is highly efficient, such as in customers–workers matching"), yet this item resides within the dimension exhibiting the weakest inter-factor correlation and measures no interactive capacity toward the matched individual. While customer-oriented service behavior enters the authors' structural equation model through Peccei and Rosenthal’s (1997) scale, this modeling choice treats the human counterpart as an external downstream outcome rather than a constitutive element of algorithmic coordination.

The sole contestation mechanism in Zhou et al.’s scale routes worker grievances exclusively through the technical infrastructure, bypassing the human counterpart entirely. Item 6 within the Remediating subdimension operationalizes whether a worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve vulnerabilities in AM" (p. 8). The qualitative fieldwork underpinning this item illustrates an asymmetrical dispute process: when confronted with an unjustified customer evaluation, a courier compiles objective documentation (such as audio recordings or timestamped delivery logs) and submits an administrative appeal directly to the platform's automated apparatus. While the customer generates the initial negative rating, the grievance procedure does not engage that customer. By resolving disputes through an automated administrative channel, the mechanism treats the interpersonal relationship as irrelevant to the determination.

Ceiling performance on Zhou et al.’s twelve-item scale fails to guarantee coordination efficacy because the construct's underlying architecture remains strictly dyadic. A worker can demonstrate complete mastery across automated dispatch dynamics, express faith in algorithmic matching, exploit platform rules to secure preferred orders, and remediate systemic errors through technical appeals, yet still possess no capacity to reconstruct what an unseen counterpart intended or project communicative legibility toward that individual. The psychometric instrument cannot detect this coordination breakdown because the model bounds the construct prior to introducing the counterpart, treating the human other strictly as an exogenous dependent variable. Appending additional survey items cannot resolve this theoretical omission because the foundational definition links only the focal worker and the algorithmic apparatus. In addition, because the authors bind empirical indicators directly to commercial gig features such as app-based appeals and order routing, the scale cannot transfer to peer-mediated evaluative environments where aggregate scoring rules and decision thresholds remain structurally concealed.

Algorithmacy inherits Zhou and colleagues' scale development protocol while building upon their empirical findings regarding construct acquisition. In their structural equation model, the primary organizational antecedents predicting algorithmic competency are social mechanisms, specifically informal peer support systems moderated by collectivist cultural orientations. This empirical evidence provides direct support for our theoretical proposition regarding construct socialization. Rather than mastering opaque algorithmic systems through isolated trial and error, individuals cultivate this interpretive sensibility through shared community practices, informal lore, and collective peer sensemaking.

## **Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation**

Rahman’s (2021) framework of the "invisible cage" conceptualizes how workers experience and navigate algorithmic evaluation systems that replace codified bureaucratic rules with structural indeterminacy. Investigating an online freelancing platform's transition from a transparent five-star rating system to an opaque, percentile-based Job Success Score, she contrasts the fixed, legible procedures of Max Weber’s classical iron cage with the shifting opacity of algorithmic management. Five structural properties define this invisible cage: hidden and fluctuating evaluation criteria, assessments executed at unpredictable intervals, unidentifiable score calculation magnitudes, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority divided between the corporate platform and the individual client (p. 976). Rather than presenting an inductive inventory of platform features, these five properties establish an analytical proof of systemic closure. As Rahman demonstrates, "even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque" (p. 976).

Rahman analyzes algorithmic work through a labor-process framework of managerial control rather than an organizational theory of coordination capacity. Within this regime of systemic opacity, workers navigate the invisible cage through two distinct behavioral adaptations: experimental reactivity, wherein individuals execute trial-and-error adjustments to reverse-engineer scoring criteria and recover fallen metrics, and constrained reactivity, wherein workers restrict their platform activity to insulate established reputational capital against volatility. Strategic selection between these modes depends on an individual's financial dependence on the platform and prior exposure to negative evaluation shocks. These empirical dynamics impose necessary discipline on our framework, demonstrating that individual behavioral adaptations often deepen structural subordination while entrenching enduring evaluative uncertainty over multi-year horizons.

Rahman’s empirical fieldwork documents the human counterpart, yet his formal theoretical construct excludes her relational agency. To investigate the labor process, Rahman conducted qualitative interviews with 18 clients alongside 80 platform freelancers, recruiting clients through worker referrals because the interface systematically foreclosed direct contact (p. 956). As Rahman notes, registered freelancers could access a client’s full profile only after submitting a job application, and even then the platform withheld direct contact information (p. 954). The methodological scope of these client interviews remained strictly bounded, serving primarily to examine client evaluation practices and triangulate freelancer accounts (p. 954). Rahman’s empirical records systematically document the counterpart’s structural blindness to her own evaluative impact, noting whether individual clients understood how their private ratings altered a freelancer’s composite Job Success Score. Yet because the overarching theoretical framework models algorithmic systems through the lens of labor discipline and managerial control, this triadic coordination dynamic remains outside the construct. Platform protocols institutionalized this mutual severance from both sides, assuring clients that their evaluative feedback would remain anonymous and never appear directly to the freelancer (p. 960).

While Rahman’s fifth structural property acknowledges an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority, it reduces the human counterpart to an interpretive ambiguity in metric calculation rather than an active partner in coordination. Within the invisible cage, a worker confronted with an unexpected score fluctuation cannot discern whether the shift stems from client dissatisfaction or an unannounced recalibration of the platform's algorithmic baseline. In Rahman's formulation, this uncertainty remains an epistemic problem concerning the behavior of the metric itself. In contrast, algorithmacy addresses the communicative and interpretive challenge of deciphering the latent expectations of the human counterpart through an opaque intermediary. The sole behavioral adaptation in which Rahman’s freelancers direct action toward the counterpart reinforces this theoretical boundary. Under constrained reactivity, workers attempt to bypass algorithmic opacity by migrating platform-originated relationships to external, unmediated communication channels. This off-platform disintermediation directly violates contractual terms of service, demonstrating that within a framework of labor reactivity, reaching the human counterpart requires exiting the algorithmic apparatus entirely and risking administrative termination to restore direct bilateral interaction.

Algorithmacy directly incorporates three foundational insights from Rahman’s framework. First, shifting and opaque evaluative criteria operate as an enduring environmental condition of algorithmic coordination, establishing the structural necessity of our third operation, keeping track. Second, Rahman's five constitutive properties systematically decompose the informational deficits the algorithmic architecture imposes on participants, highlighting an interpretive challenge at the boundary of the system: resolving whether to attribute evaluative shifts to machine logic or human discretion. Third, Rahman’s empirical demonstration that structural platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks drive worker behavioral variance introduces a formidable rival hypothesis to individual-level constructs, establishing a baseline control that any empirical test of algorithmacy must account for. Rahman's study ultimately illustrates the persistent pull of the dyadic paradigm: despite incorporating 18 human clients directly into the qualitative fieldwork, the resultant theoretical construct remained strictly dyadic, binding only the focal worker and the algorithmic apparatus while leaving the interdependent counterpart untheorized.

## **Gig Literacies**

Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) approach the structural configuration of algorithmic coordination by incorporating both market sides into their empirical research design, yet their theoretical construct remains bounded by a dyadic struggle over platform autonomy. Their formulation builds directly upon Jarrahi and Sutherland’s (2019) conceptualization of algorithmic competencies, which documented the sensemaking, circumventing, and manipulating tactics gig workers deploy against automated systems. That earlier framework established an adversarial dyad centered on worker-versus-platform appropriation, assigning no operational role to the human counterpart whom the algorithm matches to the worker. Sutherland et al. (2020) extend this lineage under the broader umbrella of gig literacies, examining the "critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing" (p. 457) that allow independent contractors to "adapt their skills and work strategies in order to leverage platforms creatively and productively, and as a component of their 'personal holding environment'" (p. 457). They formalize this capacity as platform literacy, defining it as "the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470).

To examine these practices empirically, the authors paired in-depth qualitative interviews across 20 freelancers and 19 clients with a dual-sided digital walkthrough in which researchers engaged the platform simultaneously as buyers and sellers, with one investigator registering as a client and another as a freelance contractor (pp. 461–463). Within this research design, client accounts served primarily an evaluative verification function, providing "some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers" (p. 463).

Sutherland et al. categorize these worker practices into a fivefold typology of platform literacies that spans administrative navigation, operational efficiency, and interpersonal engagement (pp. 463–469): building reputation through Job Success Scores, platform badges, private feedback channels, and strategic refunds; self-presentation through profile optimization, targeted bidding proposals, and chat interactions; maintaining productivity through personal task routines and third-party software integrations; mitigating transaction risk by leveraging escrow protections, documenting communication trails, vetting prospective clients, and evaluating counterpart trustworthiness; and building relationships through direct interpersonal communication. Within this final dimension, independent contractors deliberately cultivate client rapport "to build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" aimed at securing repeat contract engagements (p. 467, participant P10).

Although this relational dimension approaches the interpersonal domain of coordination, the authors' empirical evidence demonstrates that relationship-building functions primarily as a mechanism for escaping algorithmic governance rather than mastering coordination within it. When participant P39 confronted an algorithmically depressed rating that could not be rehabilitated within the platform interface, the worker systematically migrated established client relationships off-platform to preserve economic viability (p. 469). In Sutherland et al.’s account, interpersonal rapport serves as an instrument of structural disintermediation, extracting the collaboration from the computational system and restoring an unmediated bilateral channel where algorithmic surveillance and scoring no longer apply. This exit strategy marks the definitive theoretical boundary of platform literacy. Whereas gig literacies rely on off-platform evasion to bypass systemic constraints, algorithmacy conceptualizes the interpretive capacity required to sustain interdependent coordination and convey communicative intent directly through an opaque, binding intermediary.

Sutherland et al. delineate this structural boundary by contrasting freelance marketplaces with deterministic dispatch platforms, establishing an analytical distinction that clarifies where their construct terminates. Specifically, they contrast online talent marketplaces such as Upwork, where participants retain the discretionary capacity to evaluate, select, and converse directly with prospective clients, with automated dispatch systems such as Uber, wherein algorithmic architectures assign labor deterministically (p. 470). Relational cultivation serves as an empirical foundation for sustained commercial engagements on Upwork precisely because that platform architecture preserves bilateral agency that deterministic dispatch systems systematically eliminate. Consequently, their framework conceptualizes competencies enacted adjacent to a digital intermediary rather than communicative and coordinating action executed through an opaque, binding triadic architecture. Because their model presupposes an institutional environment where market actors retain the structural capacity to circumvent the apparatus and establish unmediated bilateral contact, incorporating relational indicators into such an instrument captures literacies exercised alongside the platform rather than coordination accomplished through its constraints.

Algorithmacy inherits two foundational insights from Sutherland and colleagues' account. First, platform enrollment without institutional standing shifts systemic coordination burdens entirely onto individual participant capacity. Second, workers acquire fluency with opaque evaluative metrics through situated social learning: independent contractors master platform demands through informal peer sensemaking and punitive experiential feedback rather than formalized technical documentation.

## **What These Boundaries Share**

A cross-construct synthesis reveals a uniform structural limitation: no extant framework simultaneously theorizes an active human counterpart and an opaque, consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. The theoretical vacancy across these seven literatures does not stem from an outright omission of the second human actor, as AI-mediated communication, algorithmic competency, and gig literacies each incorporate a counterpart within their empirical scope. Rather, the limitation lies in how the intermediary and the counterpart are structurally coupled. Where a human counterpart is present, the computational system is reduced to a passive transmission conduit, a partisan delegate, or an administrative obstacle to circumvent, never operating as an autonomous authority that renders binding determinations. Conversely, where the algorithmic intermediary is modeled as opaque and binding, the counterpart is demoted to an exogenous evaluative score or an outcome variable that the system measures, rather than an interdependent actor with whom the focal participant coordinates.

Recent systematic reviews across communication, information systems, and organizational scholarship substantiate this structural boundary. In an analysis of 50 empirical investigations across four research databases, Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) document pervasive conceptual fragmentation within algorithmic literacy research. Similarly, in a comprehensive review of 169 peer-reviewed studies, Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub (2024) identify an entrenched absence of unified theoretical frameworks governing user interactions with algorithms. Evaluating empirical research against Nutbeam’s (2000) tripartite literacy hierarchy, Iyamu et al. (2026) observe that communicative literacy remains largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and psychometric instruments. Within Nutbeam’s model, communicative literacy—operationalized as a clinician explaining algorithmic outputs to a patient whom the system does not simultaneously govern or bind—represents the literature's closest empirical approximation of a second human actor. Yet expanding survey indicators across diverse empirical domains has consistently failed to unite that second human actor with an autonomous, binding intermediary. When confronted with an inherently triadic coordination problem, extant scholarship continues to populate the relational space with system-facing operationalizations.

Visibility labor scholarship approaches this structural intersection from cultural and media studies, yet it stops short of modeling interdependent coordination. Abidin (2016) theorizes the strategic self-presentation that followers enact to achieve visibility before a specific social media influencer, examining an identifiable human counterpart while explicitly bracketing computational algorithms as analog affective labor. Cotter (2019) subsequently integrates both elements, analyzing how Instagram creators pursue human audiences through an opaque, evaluative system by playing what she terms "the visibility game" against inferred algorithmic rules.

Despite incorporating both human audiences and algorithmic curation, visibility labor diverges from algorithmacy across three constitutive dimensions. First, it theorizes communal practices and occupational subcultures rather than an individual capacity grounded in deductively derived operations. Second, its target audience constitutes an aggregate market pursued for attention and engagement rather than an interdependent counterpart collaborating on jointly executed work. Third, the platform algorithm evaluates and binds the creator alone, curating feeds for consumers rather than rendering a singular, mutually binding determination that governs both parties. Organizational and communication theory currently lacks a construct that formalizes the individual sensibility required to navigate both poles of this triadic coordination environment.

Adjacent disciplines have identified triadic structural configurations, yet these frameworks analyze the arrangement from system-design, normative, or labor-control perspectives rather than modeling participant-side coordination capacity. In online dispute resolution, Katsh and Rifkin (2001) conceptualize computational infrastructure as an active "fourth party" that exerts autonomous, systemic influence over human disputants, establishing a setting in which two active human counterparts interact through an authoritative intermediary. However, Katsh and Rifkin theorize this architecture from the perspective of system design, while Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021) evaluate its opaque, protection-withholding variants through a normative legal framework. Neither framework articulates the participant-level capacity required to navigate this arrangement. They supply institutional design benchmarks and governance standards rather than a dimensional model of what an individual actor must cognitively and behaviorally execute when coordinating through an opaque, binding third party.

Organizational and labor-process theorists similarly formalize triadic platform structures while leaving individual coordination sensibilities untheorized. Investigating platform-mediated evaluation, Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) demonstrate how automated feedback regimes dismantle traditional bilateral market exchanges to produce an asymmetrical triad of platform operators, buyers, and sellers, sustained by a structural visibility gap that permits buyers to evaluate workers invisibly. Cameron (2024) formalizes this architecture as the "algorithmic labor triangle," detailing how gig workers navigate platform constraints through continuous, bounded choices that ultimately generate labor consent and system stability. Healy and Pekarek (2025) integrate this triangular framework into labor process theory to explain worker vulnerability and institutional control. While these structural perspectives rigorously explain what the triad is, how platforms enforce managerial discipline, and how systemic equilibrium emerges, they do not account for individual-level variance in coordination capacity. They explain the institutional outcomes the arrangement produces, but they omit the dimensional capacity that separates an actor who coordinates effectively within the triad from one who does not.

Demarcating this theoretical boundary requires satisfying four analytical conditions that distinguish triadic coordination from adjacent organizational models. First, the construct must operationalize an individual actor's behavioral and interpretive capacity rather than an institutional affordance or communal subculture. Second, the human counterpart must function as an active, interdependent collaborator within the focal coordination episode, excluding frameworks that position humans solely as upstream software engineers, informal peer advisors, or passive evaluative recipients. Third, the computational intermediary must independently evaluate and execute binding determinations upon both interactants, ruling out passive transmission conduits, partisan automated delegates, and macro-level design standards that lack participant-side operationalizations. Fourth, the critique must substantiate a genuine structural vacancy within organizational and communication theory rather than a semantic dispute over taxonomic labels. Evaluated against these criteria, the comparative analysis across all seven established frameworks, reinforced by recent systematic meta-analyses, yields a definitive conclusion: no extant competence model accords the interdependent human counterpart a constitutive analytical position within an opaque, binding algorithmic architecture.

Emerging empirical fieldwork already documents this latent capacity operating within naturalistic organizational settings. Manky (2025) demonstrates that ride-hailing drivers in Lima (N=40) systematically interpret opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness and ensure physical safety. This practice reveals an explicitly counterpart-directed interpretive sensibility, mobilized for bodily security, that currently lacks formal conceptualization. Organizational scholarship therefore requires a communicative construct formulated specifically for the triadic structural position that empirical research has uncovered yet left theoretically unequipped.

# **Algorithmacy: A Construct for Coordinative Co-optation**

Construct clarity within organizational theory requires establishing a precise definition, delineating explicit scope conditions, demarcating semantic relationships with neighboring constructs, and demonstrating theoretical coherence (Suddaby, 2010). Satisfying these criteria demands a systematic specification of algorithmacy’s boundary conditions, dimensional structure, ontological status, and analytical utility as an individual-level micro-foundation for organizational coordination.

Developing algorithmacy as a micro-foundational construct requires decoupling an actor’s behavioral capacity from her formal institutional standing. Whether an enrolled participant possesses the statutory protections of an employee, the unilateral exit rights of a market buyer, or the procedural hearing rights of a co-opted stakeholder remains an institutional question of governance and legal design. Algorithmacy operates independently of how this institutional status is resolved. It formalizes the individual capacity an enrolled actor must mobilize to coordinate interdependent action within whatever standing an arrangement assigns. Delineating what an individual can execute cognitively and behaviorally does not prescribe what protections she is normatively owed, preserving a strict theoretical boundary between micro-level capacity and macro-level governance.

This conceptual independence holds because the constitutive operations of algorithmacy remain invariant across candidate resolutions of institutional standing. The construct derives directly from opacity, adaptivity, and bindingness, which constitute structural properties of the coordination architecture rather than formal attributes of participant status. Institutional modifications that leave these three properties intact do not alter the behavioral operations required of the participant. A procedural hearing right that leaves underlying algorithmic rules undisclosed, or an administrative appeal mechanism that cannot overturn binding determinations, alters formal standing without alleviating the informational deficits that demand algorithmacy. Conversely, governance reforms that eliminate opacity, adaptivity, or bindingness do not produce a standing-endowed participant who no longer requires the capacity; rather, they dissolve the structural conditions that define coordinative co-optation, moving the interaction outside the construct's scope conditions. Decoupling individual capacity from institutional standing establishes a rigorous analytical boundary that preserves construct validity across diverse governance regimes.

## **Scope and Boundary Conditions**

Algorithmacy applies specifically to organizational arrangements where a computational intermediary operates as opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding upon interacting parties. The construct ceases to operate, or operates only trivially, in environments governed by static administrative rules or in open market regimes where transacting parties negotiate contractual terms directly. A posted market price accompanied by an unrestricted right of refusal defines the classical market archetype (Hayek, 1945), wherein price signals coordinate decentralized actors without intermediary evaluation. Conversely, a codified administrative procedure administered by a designated hierarchical superior defines the formal bureaucracy (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978), where procedural rules remain legible and authority resides in identifiable human offices.

Organizational arrangements fall along an analytical continuum based on how strictly these three structural properties govern the coordination episode. Online freelance labor platforms occupy a hybrid boundary because participants retain the discretionary capacity to negotiate terms or migrate collaborative relationships off-platform (Sutherland et al., 2020), mitigating systemic opacity through direct bilateral communication. In contrast, on-demand dispatch networks, algorithmic piecework queues, and peer-evaluation systems that structurally conceal scoring criteria and decision rules reside at the core of the construct's domain. These scope conditions delineate structural configurations of coordination rather than specific industrial sectors, providing empirical researchers with clear diagnostic criteria to identify both the presence and absence of algorithmacy across diverse organizational settings.

## **Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation**

Establishing construct and discriminant validity requires embedding algorithmacy within a formal nomological network of neighboring constructs. We formalize these semantic relationships as testable empirical predictions across four established measurement traditions.

First, regarding Algorithmic Competency (Zhou et al., 2025), we hypothesize moderate positive correlations between congruent operational dimensions: Zhou and colleagues' *Understanding* should correlate moderately with *interpreting*, while *Leveraging* should correlate moderately with *specifying intent*. In contrast, *Embracing* exhibits no theoretical alignment with algorithmacy, as an affective orientation toward platform efficiency represents a normative disposition rather than an operational coordination capacity.

Second, Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation (Rahman, 2021) operates along an orthogonal theoretical axis. Whereas reactivity categorizes degrees of behavioral adaptation versus defensive withdrawal under labor discipline, algorithmacy captures directional communicative orientation and temporal tracking across an asymmetric triad. Evidence of discriminant validity requires that latent correlations between these constructs remain low to moderate; empirical convergence strong enough to collapse these dimensional boundaries would indicate a failure of theoretical differentiation.

Third, Computer-Mediated Communication Competence (Spitzberg, 2006) provides the foundational evaluative criteria of appropriateness and effectiveness that algorithmacy adapts to environments lacking co-present evaluators. We predict that *interpreting* and *specifying intent* will correlate moderately and positively with Spitzberg’s competence measures. A correlation approaching zero would indicate that the constructs measure unrelated behavioral domains rather than shared communicative standards enacted under distinct structural constraints.

Fourth, Gig Literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020) presents a bifurcated pattern of hypothesized association. Algorithmacy should exhibit weak or negative correlations with Sutherland et al.’s *relationship-building* subdimension, as that practice functions as a strategy for off-platform disintermediation rather than mediated coordination. Conversely, algorithmacy should correlate positively with the remaining four subdimensions (reputation management, self-presentation, productivity maintenance, and transaction risk mitigation), which reflect active navigation within platform infrastructure rather than evasion of it.

## **Constitutive Operations: The Tripartite Model**

The operational architecture of algorithmacy comprises three core operations derived deductively from the structural constraints of the mediated triad rather than aggregated inductively from ad hoc practices:

  - **Interpreting:** Reconstructing, from ambiguous and fragmented system feedback, the underlying actions, constraints, and intentions of both the algorithmic intermediary and the unseen human counterpart.
  - **Specifying intent:** Structuring and encoding communicative action so that intended meaning survives the narrow, standardized signaling channels permitted by the intermediary, enabling the counterpart to decode it intact.
  - **Keeping track:** Detecting temporal shifts, continuous drift, and unannounced recalibrations in the algorithmic rules that govern and bind both interactants over time.

A dyadic competence model restricted to the person-system relationship requires only an interpretive dimension directed toward machine feedback, which prior frameworks of algorithmic sensemaking already capture. In contrast, triadic coordination requires coupling machine-facing decoding with counterpart-facing encoding and temporal tracking across shifting rules.

This formulation subsumes a candidate fourth operation derived from Rahman's (2021) fifth dimension of opacity: determining whether the platform infrastructure or the human client drove an evaluative score change. We integrate this attribution capacity directly within *interpreting*, as evaluating whether an outcome stems from technical rules or human discretion represents a core interpretive task in parsing system signals. This structural boundary remains empirically falsifiable. If psychometric testing or field data reveals that actors successfully decode system signals and counterpart intent yet display a distinct, uncoupled factor for resolving the locus of evaluative authority, this attribution capacity would emerge as an independent fourth dimension within the measurement model.

The broader literacy tradition provides the construct's nomenclature while establishing the functional boundaries of the underlying metaphor. Long and Magerko (2020) trace this lineage across textual, digital, computational, and data literacies, yet their operationalizations remain confined to person-system dyads. The literacy analogy supplies historical grounding rather than operational architecture. Printed texts, datasets, and static software interfaces do not evaluate or execute binding determinations upon their users, meaning that the constitutive mechanics of reading or computing cannot transfer directly to an active, evaluating third party. The three operations derive instead from the specific informational deficits imposed by the algorithmic triad. The literacy lineage marks the point of departure: *keeping track* possesses no equivalent in traditional literacy frameworks because conventional media remain temporally stable.

## **Why Each Operation Requires the Full Triad**

Each constitutive operation derives its theoretical validity from requiring the computational intermediary and the human counterpart simultaneously rather than the technical system in isolation. *Interpreting* satisfies this criterion directly: because platform opacity precludes separate communication channels, an actor must reconstruct the intermediary's operating rules and the counterpart's latent intentions from the same ambiguous, composite feedback.

*Specifying intent* satisfies this triadic requirement through dual-audience encoding constraints. In conventional computer-mediated communication, encoding involves compressing meaning to fit the bandwidth of a transmission channel. Within an evaluative triadic architecture, the transmission channel operates simultaneously as an authoritative evaluative instrument. A single communicative act, such as drafting the rationale for a technical contribution or submitting an administrative proposal, must satisfy the computational intermediary's parsing rules and the human counterpart's interpretive expectations concurrently, without the benefit of unmediated bilateral backchannels.

*Keeping track* requires the triadic configuration through an evidentiary mechanism rather than a purely structural definition. In fully transparent or inspectable environments where participants can examine underlying source code or explicit administrative rubrics, algorithmic updates generate direct, observable system signals, causing the tracking function to converge with routine technical monitoring. Under conditions of systemic opacity, unannounced rule changes and algorithmic drift produce no explicit platform notifications. Instead, underlying rule shifts register only through altered counterpart behavior, unexpected evaluative outcomes, or systematic variations in downstream peer ratings. An actor who monitors only direct platform telemetry fails to detect this category of unannounced systemic recalibration, as the primary empirical evidence of algorithmic change surfaces through shifts in the relational counterpart's actions and evaluative rewards.

The three operations function as a recursively linked process rather than an inventory of discrete, static traits. As illustrated in Figure 1, a critical coordination incident typically begins with *interpreting* an ambiguous outcome or unexpected system determination. Finally, *keeping track* evaluates whether the underlying algorithmic rules or evaluative thresholds have shifted over the course of the interaction, generating updated empirical priors that feed back into *interpreting* for subsequent coordination episodes.

This process framing also resolves a core measurement tension regarding the construct's psychometric architecture. The three operations are neither interchangeable reflective indicators of a single underlying latent trait (a participant can readily exhibit high interpretive skill while struggling with dual-audience encoding) nor merely formative components that aggregate into a static index, because the outputs of *keeping track* continuously update the priors governing *interpreting* and *specifying intent*. Instead, the operations function as sequential stages in a recursive coordination cycle. This duality explains why the qualitative research arm traces complete critical incidents through the entire operational cycle rather than evaluating each dimension in isolation, even though the quantitative survey panel treats the three dimensions as psychometrically separable for discriminant validity testing. The methodological dualism is complementary rather than contradictory: the survey validates that the operations are distinct from neighboring constructs, whereas the qualitative critical incident technique demonstrates that they operate as the recursive feedback loop depicted in Figure 1.

## **Categorical Delineation: Competency versus Skill and Capability**

Algorithmacy constitutes an individual competency rather than an isolable skill or a firm-level capability, occupying a distinct ontological category within organizational theory.

  - **Distinction from Skill:** A skill represents a procedural routine that can be codified, standardized, and transmitted through explicit didactic instruction. In contrast, the governing rules of opaque algorithmic systems remain proprietary, unobservable, and dynamic, precluding the codification required for rule-based skill acquisition.
  - **Distinction from Capability:** A capability resides in organizational routines and administrative assets that a firm owns and deploys (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). In platform environments, the platform firm exclusively owns and modifies the matching algorithms, rating systems, and governance parameters; the individual participant holds no proprietary ownership or control over the infrastructure.
  - **Phenomenographic Foundation:** Sandberg (2000), examining engine optimizers at the Volvo Car Corporation, demonstrated that competence reflects an actor's qualitative conception of work rather than an abstract inventory of independent attributes. Sandberg identified three distinct interpretive frameworks: optimizing separate technical components, balancing interacting qualities, or managing the entire process from the customer's holistic perspective. In that final conception, the customer represents an interpretive orientation toward the task rather than an unseen counterpart reached through an opaque, binding intermediary. Sandberg's phenomenographic findings establish that competence resides in the worker's qualitative interpretive framing of work—an insight that algorithmacy extends to mediated triadic coordination.

Conversely, Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) argue that interpersonal communication competence is not an isolated individual attribute, but rather a relational quality jointly enacted and evaluated by interacting parties. Spitzberg (2006) carries this relational commitment into mediated settings, where interactants mutually negotiate and co-construct communicative competence. Algorithmic coordination disrupts this relational model, though not by eliminating the counterpart's judgment, as she continues to render appraisals either visibly or invisibly. She never occupies a position from which mutual communicative competence can be negotiated, because the computational aggregation that converts her appraisal into a binding consequence is withheld from both parties. Where the structural conditions for relational co-construction are absent, competence necessarily reverts from a jointly enacted judgment to an individually held capacity—reflecting the interpretive conception of work described by Sandberg (2000)—with the counterpart's appraisal surviving solely as an opaque input to the intermediary's verdict. Algorithmacy identifies the interpretive and expressive capacities an individual must supply under precisely that structural reversion.

## **Foundational Properties: Experiential Acquisition and Uneven Distribution**

Algorithmacy exhibits two foundational properties that govern its emergence and empirical manifestation:

  - **Acquisition through Participation:** Because platform operators structurally withhold underlying decision rules, formal didactic training cannot substitute for experiential immersion. Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp, and Raupach (2023) developed the SNAIL scale to evaluate formal AI-literacy coursework, measuring declarative knowledge acquired through explicit instructional curricula. Algorithmacy predicts that classroom instruction alone cannot cultivate this capacity; fluency with opaque, adaptive systems requires iterative, direct participation where actors confront ambiguous feedback and navigate actual evaluative stakes.
  - **Uneven Distribution:** Algorithmacy is distributed heterogeneously across individuals whom the platform treats as formally equivalent. If the capacity were uniformly distributed, it could not account for the persistent performance and coordination variance observed among participants who operate within identical algorithmic architectures and face equivalent institutional constraints.

## **Theoretical Implications**

Formalizing algorithmacy reorients organizational research on algorithmic management from mechanisms of control to the mechanics of execution. Where labor process theorists conceptualize algorithmic workplaces as contested terrain centered on algorithmic direction, evaluation, and discipline (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020), algorithmacy isolates the behavioral competencies required to coordinate through those administrative constraints. This distinction clarifies policy and design interventions: regulatory mandates requiring algorithmic transparency modify the opacity of the information environment, whereas cultivating algorithmacy enhances an actor's capacity to interpret and act within that environment regardless of its transparency.

This micro-foundational framework supplies the operational engine that macro-structural models of platform organizing presuppose. While organizational theorists have detailed how platform architectures exploit coordination gaps (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024) and generate structural consent through algorithmic enrollment (Cameron, 2024; Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, & Neysen, 2020), these accounts focus on the institutional forces that secure compliance rather than the communicative capacities that sustain daily coordination. Specifying the recursive operations of interpreting, specifying intent, and keeping track explains how participants achieve working alignments within asymmetric triads, providing the individual-level behavioral foundation that macro-structural platform theory has left unmodeled.

The construct deliberately operates at a bounded behavioral threshold, adopting an aspiration level in Simon's (1997) sense rather than attempting an exhaustive sociological account of the platform worker. Algorithmacy models the baseline capacity an actor must deploy to coordinate under opacity, adaptivity, and binding evaluation; it intentionally stops short of theorizing formal institutional standing, legal entitlements, normative legitimacy, or collective labor resistance. Isolating this communicative competency from broader questions of legal classification establishes an empirical baseline for investigating how individual capability interacts with institutional governance across diverse platform architectures.

Scholarship in online dispute resolution provides a clear benchmark for evaluating these structural deficits by formalizing the normative obligations owed by institutional intermediaries. Extending Katsh and Rifkin’s (2001) foundational concept of the computational "fourth party," Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021) argue that an authoritative dispute system must guarantee participants procedural transparency, institutional accountability, meaningful contestability, and informed consent. Algorithmic coordination regimes operate by systematically inverting these standards: proprietary decision rules remain unobservable, automated determinations lack administrative accountability, contestation channels are structurally foreclosed, and default enrollment displaces voluntary consent. Curchod et al. (2020) document the empirical consequences of this institutional design, demonstrating how marketplace sellers are evaluated across an asymmetric visibility gap by buyers they cannot observe or directly engage. The three operations of algorithmacy constitute the individual-level capacities participants must mobilize precisely because these institutional protections are withheld.

## **A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves**

A participant's algorithmacy represents a behavioral capacity to operate within an arrangement rather than an endorsement of its procedural or distributive fairness. Because capacity and fairness vary independently, higher competency does not inherently signal greater welfare or institutional legitimacy. This decoupling yields an explicit aggregation mechanism that links micro-level behavior to macro-structural stability. Cameron (2024) demonstrates how on-demand workers navigate continuous, confined choices in ways that manufacture consent and stabilize algorithmic labor regimes, yet her model leaves unspecified the individual capacity that differentiates successful navigation from failure. Formalizing algorithmacy translates this insight into a workforce-level distributional mechanism: an enrolled population's collective competency, rather than isolated individual compliance, determines whether operational frictions trigger visible system breakdown or are quietly absorbed by participants. This distributional dynamic fulfills the aggregation requirement expected of micro-foundational organizational theory (Felin, Foss, & Ployhart, 2015), explaining how individual coordination capacities aggregate to sustain the overall organizational form.

This aggregation mechanism yields testable, falsifiable predictions at the collective level. Cohorts exposed to identical task architectures, matching gates, and algorithmic evaluation rubrics but differing in mean levels of algorithmacy should exhibit systematic differences in observable friction, such as voluntary attrition, formal contestation volume, and coordination failures. Observing identical friction rates across cohorts with divergent mean algorithmacy would falsify this macro-structural aggregation claim, even if individual competency continues to predict actor-level performance outcomes.

Three further theoretical propositions follow from this tension, clarifying why algorithmacy should not be interpreted as an unalloyed individual or organizational benefit:

**Proposition 1.** *Higher algorithmacy improves an individual participant's coordination outcomes while leaving the arrangement's procedural justice unchanged; performance gains concentrate in private navigation rather than in institutional disclosures won, formal appeals granted, or rules rendered legible to peers.*

This dynamic synthesizes the micro- and macro-level tensions identified in prior platform research: Rahman (2021) documents how individual reactivity can inadvertently reinforce managerial subordination, while Sutherland et al. (2020) demonstrate how informal literacies shift systemic coordination burdens directly onto labor. Proposition 1 extends these insights to a formalized competency, demonstrating that individual mastery compensates for structural deficits without altering the underlying opacity of the regime.

**Proposition 2.** *Because algorithmacy is distributed unevenly across participants whom the platform treats as formally equivalent, that variance introduces a secondary axis of inequality among actors whom the organizational form designates as interchangeable.*

This proposition transposes the logic of second-level digital divides (Hargittai, 2002) from general digital literacy to triadic algorithmic coordination. Within formally undifferentiated labor pools, latent differences in algorithmacy generate persistent earnings, safety, and evaluation disparities that platforms attribute to individual merit rather than structural informational asymmetries.

**Proposition 3.** *Increasing an intermediary's structural transparency reduces the level of algorithmacy required to coordinate through it, establishing the construct's scope conditions as a malleable institutional lever rather than a fixed analytical perimeter.*

Regulatory mandates that enforce algorithmic transparency or require explainable decision logic lower the cognitive and communicative burden imposed on participants. Consequently, institutional disclosure policies provide an alternative governance pathway that mitigates coordination frictions by redesigning the information environment rather than requiring individuals to develop compensatory competencies.

These propositions, alongside the workforce-level stabilization mechanism, underscore why algorithmacy represents an empirical capacity to be measured rather than a normative virtue to be maximized. The construct formalizes how participants survive and coordinate within opaque, binding architectures, providing a diagnostic foundation for evaluating both individual performance and the institutional persistence of algorithmic management.

## **Dissertation Coherence**

The three papers of this dissertation construct a single cumulative argument rather than three independent investigations of a shared domain. Paper 1 establishes the structural foundation by identifying an unformalized governance arrangement: organizational theory has not theorized coordinative co-optation alongside classical archetypes of hierarchy, market, network, or co-optation, leaving unmodeled the behavioral mechanism through which an enrolled actor coordinates within its boundaries. Paper 2 supplies that micro-foundation by developing algorithmacy as the individual-level capacity required to navigate an opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding intermediary. Paper 3 tests this theoretical architecture empirically, examining the construct's operational distribution across formally equivalent participants, its effects on coordination outcomes and collective friction, and its discriminant validity against neighboring measurement traditions. Each paper fulfills a distinct analytical function that the remaining two presuppose.

# **Empirical Strategy**

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), the empirical program differentiates the core action research project from the formal research project conducted upon it. The core initiative comprises a longitudinal intervention spanning multiple iterations across distinct institutional configurations, wherein cyclical evaluations directly informed ongoing pedagogical, gate-mechanism, and instrument redesigns. The empirical study presented in Paper 3 constitutes insider research focused on this program's mature operational configuration (Brannick & Coghlan, 2007). In this phase, the investigator executes no further administrative interventions within the focal cohorts, and data collection protocols remain strictly anonymized to decouple pedagogical facilitation from participants' reported accounts.

The research design leverages this empirical context as a strategic research site (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Merton, 1987) to investigate the micro-foundations of triadic coordination. Data collection and analysis follow an abductive analytical protocol (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) designed to reconcile observed participant behaviors with theoretical categories. The following subsections establish the structural alignment between the empirical site and the scope conditions of coordinative co-optation, delineate the focal research questions alongside their corresponding measurement instruments, and state the current operational status of the empirical program.

## **Why Hult Instantiates Coordinative Co-optation, Not a Hybrid Form**

A potential critique might interpret the focal research site as an ordinary hybrid of pedagogical hierarchy, peer review, and automated grading—essentially traditional bureaucratic authority operating behind digital interfaces. Structural properties of the coordination gate refute this interpretation. The computational gate renders binding determinations on each participant submission by aggregating peer appraisals, automated test suites, and peer upvotes into an evaluative verdict governed by proprietary, concealed weighting parameters. This outcome is neither a grade assigned through instructor discretion nor a transparent tally of raw votes. The gate's determination binds both interactants simultaneously: the submitter’s progression depends on an aggregated score she cannot inspect, while peer evaluators contribute assessments to an opaque algorithm whose ultimate weighting logic remains hidden from them. Consequently, neither party occupies the role of a hierarchical superior administering a legible administrative rule (Weber, 1978). The course instructor exercises no direct vote or manual override within the gate mechanism. Because the underlying codebase against which submissions are evaluated evolves continuously as participants merge contributions, the evaluative benchmark shifts dynamically over time, directly satisfying the scope condition of environmental adaptivity.

To maintain methodological transparency, two specific operational parameters of the gate mechanism warrant explicit identification as ongoing design specifications in the empirical protocol. First, the formal research design is documenting the exact pairing architecture: whether the platform algorithm deterministically allocates peer reviewers to specific submissions or allows participants to self-select into evaluation queues. Second, the protocol is cataloging the precise institutional constraints governing participant exit options and dispute escalation pathways when an evaluative determination is contested. Specifying these institutional mechanics ensures that the empirical site precisely maps the structural parameters of coordinative co-optation during subsequent psychometric and field validation.

## **Research Questions and Instruments**

The empirical investigation is structured around four primary research questions, each mapped directly to a constitutive operation and the specific boundary condition it tests:

  - **RQ1 (Interpreting):** How do participants decode, reconstruct, and make sense of unexplained or contradictory evaluative outcomes generated by the opaque gate mechanism?
  - **RQ2 (Specifying Intent):** How do participants conceptualize and communicate with their peer counterparts—specifically, whether they address the peer as an active, interdependent party within the coordination episode or direct communicative action exclusively toward machine legibility?
  - **RQ3 (Keeping Track):** How do participants identify, monitor, and adapt to unannounced temporal shifts and continuous drift in the evaluative benchmarks enforced by the gate?
  - **RQ4 (Evaluative Attribution):** How do participants attribute perceived evaluative errors or discrepancies across the mediated triad (to algorithmic weighting, to peer reviewer judgment, or to their interaction), and does resolving the locus of evaluative authority function as an independent, fourth constitutive operation rather than a facet of *interpreting*?

The formulation of RQ4 explicitly decouples error attribution from *keeping track*, resolving a key conceptual boundary in the measurement model. Diagnosing longitudinal drift in system rules constitutes a distinct behavioral phenomenon from attributing causal responsibility for an isolated evaluative breakdown: an actor may successfully detect that an evaluative standard has shifted over time without diagnosing the specific locus of failure for a single outcome, and conversely, may assign fault for an isolated error without recognizing broader systemic drift.

To operationalize these inquiries, the semi-structured critical incident interview protocol comprises four corresponding diagnostic modules. Each module isolates participant sensemaking, dual-audience encoding, longitudinal tracking, and causal attribution through detailed reconstructions of specific critical coordination incidents encountered within the platform environment.

## **Instruments and Status**

The multi-method empirical design couples an AI-administered critical incident interview protocol embedded directly within the development environment with a three-wave longitudinal survey panel administered at weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the semester. Tracking the developmental trajectory of algorithmacy over time requires linking individual responses across all three panel waves without compromising participant confidentiality. To reconcile within-person longitudinal linkage with researcher-blind data collection, each participant generates a self-derived alphanumeric identifier at intake. Participants re-enter this unique token across successive survey waves, enabling multi-wave panel tracking while maintaining complete anonymity from the investigative team. The participant consent documentation formalizes this specification and aligns it with institutional review board standards prior to field deployment.

Preliminary piloting of the in-editor interview protocol provides early operational insights into the micro-mechanisms of triadic coordination. In an initial diagnostic run, a participant navigating the intent-specification module resolved the communicative constraint by routing their explanation through an external language model to optimize parsing legibility, entirely bypassing direct engagement with the human peer counterpart. This emergent adaptation exemplifies how participants dynamically mobilize secondary computational tools to satisfy the dual demands of machine parsability and peer comprehension, underscoring the necessity of capturing multi-agent intermediary routing within the formal critical incident coding rubric. Formal data collection across the complete cohort will systematically evaluate these behavioral trajectories to test the construct's psychometric structure and process dynamics.

# **Conclusion and Theoretical Boundaries**

This article formulates the theoretical architecture, construct boundaries, and constitutive operations of algorithmacy rather than its definitive empirical validation. Because the multi-wave field investigation remains prospective, this paper contributes an analytical model, formal construct definitions, and a measurement apparatus refined through diagnostic piloting. Whether the three constitutive operations load onto a coherent higher-order factor, maintain empirical independence, or cleanly separate from general software literacy and traditional communicative competence remain open empirical questions that subsequent psychometric testing must resolve. This transition introduces a fundamental measurement consideration: following Spitzberg and Cupach (1984), competence operates across both molar and molecular levels of analysis that cannot be collapsed into a single metric. Psychometric operationalization must therefore demonstrate that standardized survey scales preserve the holistic interpretive conception of work (Sandberg, 2000) rather than atomizing it into isolated, decontextualized behavioral traits.

Neighboring frameworks provide competing explanations that psychometric validation must systematically evaluate. Empirical testing must determine whether existing measures of algorithmic sensemaking (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025) fully capture the variance observed in triadic environments, or whether Sutherland et al.’s (2020) relational gig literacy sufficiently accounts for worker coordination. While current relational literacy frameworks theorize peer engagement that operates alongside algorithmic systems, algorithmacy models coordination executed directly through an opaque, evaluative intermediary. Emerging scholarship continues to examine adjacent domains, including algorithmic skills (Hong, Cheng, & Liu, 2026), platform literacies (Scolari, Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés, 2026), human-AI collaboration dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), and metacognitive algorithmic competence (Ayasrah, Al-Rousan, Almulla, & Almulla, 2026). Yet these models remain confined to person-system dyads, treating human counterparts as external peers, offline collaborators, or passive recipients of automated outputs. This article advances a claim regarding a structural position within an asymmetric, evaluative triad rather than incremental taxonomic refinement.

Boundary cases across adjacent disciplines highlight the precise scope conditions of this framework:

  - **Clinical Medicine:** Recent models of AI-mediated medical education incorporate the patient alongside diagnostic algorithms, yet ultimate clinical and legal authority resides with the human physician rather than an autonomous intermediary.
  - **Public Child Welfare:** Models of AI competence in social work address client trust and algorithmic risk scoring (Yang & Liechty, 2026), but frame the algorithmic system as an advisory decision-support tool rather than a binding administrative authority.
  - **Algorithmic Matchmaking:** Interpersonal frameworks in digital dating examine relationship-formation competencies while omitting algorithmic curation (Dredge & Anderson, 2021), whereas computational accounts analyze algorithmic awareness while omitting relational counterpart dynamics (Hu & Zhan, 2024).

These comparisons bound the construct within the coordination of interdependent work, excluding advisory decision aids, dyadic software tools, and unmediated peer collaboration.

Algorithmacy isolates the individual-level capacity required to coordinate through opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding systems, establishing the micro-foundational engine that macro-structural models of platform governance presuppose. By formalizing the recursive operations of interpreting, specifying intent, and keeping track, this framework provides an analytical foundation for examining how participants navigate algorithmic regimes and how individual competence aggregates to stabilize or disrupt emerging forms of organizational control.

# **Appendix A: Comparative Framework of Coordination Forms**

This analytical foundation draws from the first essay of this dissertation program, which evaluated five archetypal coordination forms against a standardized analytical architecture to assess the theoretical completeness of extant organizational scholarship. Extending the comparative traditions established by Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980), each coordination form is evaluated across six core questions: governing mechanism and participant standing, requisite integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, operational instruments, failure modes, and empirical markers. A coordination mechanism is defined as the primary administrative operation securing alignment paired with the formal standing of the actor reached by that operation. The integrative deliverables map directly to the three core conditions synthesized by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009): predictability, common understanding, and accountability.

This comparative matrix translates classical foundational theory into a contemporary coordination vocabulary. For example, Hayek (1945) demonstrates that the price mechanism bears the primary coordinative burden because it functions as an aggregated public statistic that no participant needs to reconstruct privately. In modern coordination terms, this public statistic simultaneously delivers predictability and common understanding, while locating market accountability in contractual enforcement and reputational sanction. The four established governance traditions demonstrate theoretical completeness. As Stark and Pais (2020) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) observe, digital platforms do not coordinate through administrative command, price discovery, or relational reciprocity; instead, they enroll participants through automated matching algorithms that pair unchosen parties, monitor performance metrics, and enforce unilateral account sanctions. The participant operates neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price signals, nor a partner in a mutual network alliance. The actor is structurally enrolled, yet organizational theory has not articulated the micro-foundational individual competency required to coordinate through this opaque, adaptive architecture.

# **Appendix B: Action Research Design, Instrument Architecture, and Empirical Protocol**

**Research Questions and Analytical Mapping**

The empirical program investigates how individuals acquire, develop, and mobilize algorithmacy within opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding coordination regimes. The inquiry addresses four core research questions:

  - **RQ1 (Interpreting):** How do participants decode, reconstruct, and make sense of unexplained evaluative outcomes generated by an automated gate mechanism?
  - **RQ2 (Specifying Intent):** How do participants conceptualize and communicate with their human counterparts, specifically whether they treat the peer as an active, interdependent party or direct communicative action exclusively toward machine legibility?
  - **RQ3 (Keeping Track):** How do participants identify, monitor, and adapt to unannounced temporal shifts in what the algorithmic intermediary evaluates and rewards?
  - **RQ4 (Evaluative Attribution):** How do participants attribute perceived evaluative errors across the system, peer reviewers, or their interaction, testing whether locating the locus of authority constitutes an independent, fourth constitutive operation?

These four inquiries map directly onto the construct’s theoretical architecture. Decoding unexplained outcomes operationalizes *interpreting*; conceptualizing an unseen counterpart captures the dual-audience encoding demanded by *specifying intent*; detecting unannounced rule shifts captures *keeping track*; and attributing perceived errors isolates *evaluative attribution* as a candidate fourth dimension rather than a component of longitudinal tracking. Following Pratt (2009), qualitative rigor depends on methodological alignment with the underlying theoretical puzzle rather than adherence to generic templates. The empirical design employs insider action research (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014), wherein the primary investigator’s dual role as course designer and researcher is treated as an epistemic asset that provides deep institutional access and context-sensitive instrument design.

**The Action Research Program and Institutional Trajectory**

Insider action research proceeds through iterative spirals consisting of four continuous phases: constructing, planning action, taking action, and evaluating action, with each cycle's evaluation informing subsequent construction (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). The empirical program spans five distinct institutional configurations across two years:

1.  **Secondary School Cohorts (July–August 2025):** The initiative launched in Trinidad and Tobago across two secondary educational institutions.
2.  **Community Youth Cohorts (Autumn–Winter 2025/2026):** Operating as GauntleTT, the program expanded across the constituency of La Brea in partnership with the Member of Parliament, the Ministry of Digital Transformation, local non-governmental organizations, and the community policing division administering the Hearts and Minds youth intervention initiative.
3.  **Public Technical Sprint Cohorts (April–May 2026):** An open-enrollment public iteration operated in Boston, Massachusetts, testing web-based development pipelines.
4.  **Preliminary Business School Cohorts (July–August 2026):** The curriculum transitioned to Hult International Business School, piloting credit-bearing assessment workflows.
5.  **Formal Academic Course (Spring 2027):** A formal, sixteen-week, credit-bearing engineering course at Hult serves as the primary data-collection site for Paper 3.

Within this mature setting, participants engineer production-grade software and submit deliverables as peer-reviewed pull requests. Across each institutional transition, cyclical evaluations drove substantive structural modifications: site variations altered peer-evaluation networks, gate refinements systematically varied information visibility, and demographic shifts isolated behavioral variance from confounding human-capital baselines.

**The Trinidad Field Setting as a Strategic Research Site**

The early Caribbean cohorts functioned as a strategic research site in Merton’s (1987) sense, providing an empirical setting where the focal phenomenon manifested with unusual analytical tractability. As Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) argue, single-case site selections are justified when an empirical setting offers revelatory access to an unexamined process. Conventional platform-work research typically examines labor forces characterized by extensive platform tenure and pre-existing human capital differences (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025), where participant sensemaking is heavily confounded by prior domain experience.

In contrast, platform labor across the Anglophone Caribbean remains concentrated in traditional logistics, ride-hailing, and hospitality (ILO, 2025), with empirical scholarship on algorithmic coordination in the region remaining sparse (Chigbu, 2026). Studying non-traditional research contexts prevents the misapplication of mature institutional assumptions and allows emergent behavioral phenomena to surface in unobstructed form (Bamberger & Pratt, 2010; Bothello, Nason, & Schnyder, 2019). Because participants in the Trinidad cohorts encountered artificial intelligence coordination interfaces simultaneously without prior platform conditioning, observed divergences in how individuals decoded identical gate outcomes could not be attributed to historical tenure or pre-sorted skill tiers.

Across these cohort iterations, automated evaluation gates withheld information across three distinct operational layers:

  - **Cursor Boston Iteration:** Large language models screened initial submissions prior to peer voting, displaying only an aggregated final score to the participant.
  - **Hult International Business School Iteration:** Qualitative peer reviews and upvotes remained publicly inspectable while the underlying aggregate mathematical weighting remained concealed.
  - **Trinidad Iteration:** Automated operational logs were not retained, necessitating the deployment of retrospective historical protocols to reconstruct gate mechanics.

This multi-site architectural variation demonstrates that opacity constitutes an intrinsic property of algorithmic coordination rather than an idiosyncratic feature of a single software user interface.

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), the core action research cycle (the ongoing pedagogical initiative) remains ontologically distinct from the thesis research cycle (the formal academic study presented in Paper 3). The preliminary cohorts provided the empirical ground for construct crystallization, instrument calibration, and protocol refinement; they constitute the developmental foundation rather than the formal empirical corpus.

**Research Site Architecture and Researcher Positionality**

The formal empirical investigation takes place within a sixteen-week credit-bearing course at Hult International Business School in spring 2027. Students submit technical code to an automated gate that returns determinations without explicit procedural rationales. The gate mechanism embodies the theoretical scope conditions of coordinative co-optation: the automated system functions as the opaque intermediary, its unexplained verdict embodies operational opacity, and the underlying codebase against which work is evaluated evolves dynamically as peer contributions merge into production.

This setting represents a conservative "hard case" test for opacity. Because student participants construct and inspect the codebase itself, informational opacity does not stem from hidden source code, but rather emerges from the concealed aggregation weights and shifting decision thresholds governing peer evaluations. The formal research protocol catalogs the precise peer-assignment mechanics (algorithmic allocation versus participant queue selection) and institutional escalation rights to ensure clear structural parameterization.

Role duality is managed through deliberate structural safeguards rather than methodological disavowal (Riordan, 1995; Anteby, 2013). The investigator’s instructional role is restricted to macro-level course design, whereas the automated gate operates autonomously without instructor voting or manual grading overrides. To eliminate power asymmetries and instructional demand characteristics (Ferguson, Yonge, & Myrick, 2004; Mercer, 2007), participant intake for the qualitative interview protocol is strictly anonymized at the client level, preventing the instructor from identifying participating students. Furthermore, interviews occur exclusively after evaluative project cycles have closed. The research design secures methodological trustworthiness through an auditable research trail (Pratt, Kaplan, & Whittington, 2020; Tracy, 2010).

**The In-Editor Interview Protocol and Abductive Coding Framework**

The qualitative arm employs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol administered directly within the participant’s integrated development environment. An embedded conversational agent guides respondents through four structured diagnostic modules:

1.  **Decoding Gate Criteria:** Reconstructing the latent rules and peer evaluations behind an unexplained outcome.
2.  **Transmitting Communicative Intent:** Encoding code comments and pull-request documentation to satisfy both computational test suites and human peer reviewers.
3.  **Identifying Unannounced Change:** Detecting shifts in evaluative benchmarks over time without formal platform announcements.
4.  **Attributing Evaluative Errors:** Allocating causal responsibility for perceived evaluative failures across the intermediary, peer reviewers, or their interaction.

Each module applies the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954), prompting participants to detail a complete behavioral episode comprising the baseline objective, the system determination, the tactical adaptation, and the ultimate resolution. If a respondent offers a high-level generalization, the agent prompts for a specific behavioral incident; if an initial response is negative, the agent executes an automated reframing prompt from an alternative angle before concluding the module. The protocol omits formal theoretical terminology to elicit native cognitive categories. The agent executes real-time, client-side redaction of personal names, institutions, and geographic identifiers before the participant reviews, edits, and validates the final transcript for transmission.

Data analysis follows an abductive logic of inquiry (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) operationalized through three iterative analytical moves:

  - **Revisiting:** Returning repeatedly to inscribed transcripts across multiple analytical passes to examine how emergent categories reframe earlier observations.
  - **Defamiliarization:** Treating automated transcription as a technique of analytical estrangement, subjecting self-evident participant explanations to critical theoretical scrutiny.
  - **Alternative Casing:** Evaluating raw transcripts against competing theoretical frameworks (e.g., individual skill acquisition, relational gig literacies, dyadic sensemaking) to ensure the empirical data demand the triadic construct.

The constitutive operations of algorithmacy function as sensitizing concepts (Blumer, 1954; Bowen, 2006). Initial coding adheres strictly to first-order, participant-centric terminology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013) before second-order theoretical categories are applied. A disconfirming negative case is explicitly defined: a participant who consistently resolves gate breakdowns through individual technical coding mastery alone, without conceptualizing or communicating with the human peer counterpart, constitutes evidence falsifying the necessity of the triadic construct.

**Longitudinal Survey Panel Architecture**

The quantitative arm comprises a three-wave longitudinal survey panel administered at weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the semester. To reconcile within-person longitudinal tracking with researcher blindness, participants generate a self-derived confidential token at baseline (derived from invariant personal elements) and re-enter this token across successive survey waves. This protocol enables multi-wave linkage while preserving complete anonymity from the instructional and research team (Yurek, Vasey, & Havens, 2008).

Given a cohort size of approximately fifty students, the panel is not powered to perform exploratory or confirmatory factor analyses. Instead, the survey serves two defined analytical purposes: (1) tracking within-person developmental trajectories across the semester, and (2) administering Zhou et al.’s (2025) scale at wave 2 as a descriptive convergence check with *interpreting* and *specifying intent*. Formal large-sample discriminant validity testing is reserved for subsequent field studies. Ethical oversight was established under Bentley University Institutional Review Board exempt determination Protocol 260511078.

**Preliminary Instrument Piloting Results**

Diagnostic testing of the interview protocol across initial pre-qualitative runs identified three key instrument refinements:

1.  **Elicitation of Concrete Critical Incidents:** The protocol successfully elicited a complete critical incident detailing an adverse gate determination, the participant's retrospective sensemaking, and the perceived absence of contestability channels.
2.  **Identification of Secondary Intermediary Routing:** A participant satisfied the intent-specification module by routing explanations through an external language model to optimize automated parsability rather than engaging the peer counterpart directly. This finding led to the integration of explicit protocol probes distinguishing direct peer communication from multi-agent intermediary routing.
3.  **Automated Incident Forcing:** The protocol was refined to replace generalized summaries with mandatory incident anchors and automated reframing prompts in the tracking and error-attribution modules.

# **Appendix C: Programmatic Research Agenda**

Four sequential empirical studies translate algorithmacy from a conceptual framework into an empirically validated construct, establishing explicit methodological thresholds and falsification criteria across distinct analytical levels.

**Study 1: Multi-Method Field Validation and Dimensional Boundary Resolution**

The initial study executes the multi-method investigation within the primary field setting, coupling the in-editor critical incident interview protocol with the three-wave longitudinal survey panel. Qualitative analysis proceeds not to a traditional grounded-theory saturation metric but to an abductive sufficiency criterion—the threshold at which incremental incidents generate no empirical casings that the derived operations cannot accommodate.

This corpus empirically resolves the candidate fourth constitutive operation (*evaluative attribution*). Rahman’s (2021) "who-influences" dimension will be formalized as an autonomous fourth operation if participant accounts systematically reveal actors who successfully decode observable feedback yet remain structurally incapable of discerning whether the platform intermediary or peer evaluators determined the evaluative outcome. If participants instead resolve causal responsibility as an integral facet of baseline sensemaking, the tripartite architecture stands as specified.

**Study 2: Large-Sample Psychometric Testing and Discriminant Validation**

The second study establishes large-sample psychometric validation by pooling survey administrations across successive cohorts until reaching a sample size of 200 to 250 respondents, matching the methodological baseline established by Zhou et al. (2025). This scale powers a formal confirmatory factor analysis and evaluates pre-registered latent correlation hypotheses between algorithmacy dimensions and established algorithmic competency subscales:

  - *Interpreting* paired with *Understanding*
  - *Specifying Intent* paired with *Leveraging*
  - An unassigned null baseline paired with *Embracing*

Latent inter-construct correlations within the .30 to .60 range will demonstrate convergent yet distinct validity. Discriminant validity is evaluated using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT; Henseler, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2015). An HTMT value exceeding the conservative .85 threshold between any proposed operation and a neighboring subscale falsifies the claim of construct distinctiveness on that dimension.

**Study 3: Distributional Inequality and System Stabilization**

The third study tests the macro-structural aggregation mechanisms formalized in Propositions 1 and 2 across a population of formally equivalent participants. By measuring baseline algorithmacy alongside technical human capital, task complexity metrics, and platform tenure, this study examines whether individual variance in algorithmacy accounts for performance differentials among structurally undifferentiated actors.

This design also tracks low-scoring cohorts longitudinally to evaluate how the organizational arrangement absorbs coordination friction. By measuring rates of voluntary attrition, formal dispute volume, and the psychological internalization of systemic coordination failures, the study quantifies the operational and human costs an organization incurs when this competency is scarce.

**Study 4: Cross-Sector Generalizability and Transparency Boundary Conditions**

The fourth study evaluates external generalizability by deploying the measurement instruments across naturalistic, non-builder platform environments where the investigative team holds no pedagogical or administrative role. Target empirical settings include on-demand transportation fleets, distributed micro-task queues, and digital freelance marketplaces where algorithmic matching operates without worker agency over system design.

Observing the operational separation of the three constitutive dimensions within these commercial environments confirms that algorithmacy represents a generalized organizational coordination capacity rather than a setting-specific artifact of software engineering environments. In addition, comparative field sites exhibiting natural variation in algorithmic disclosure provide a direct empirical test of Proposition 3, evaluating whether institutional transparency mandates systematically lower the baseline algorithmacy required to sustain coordination.
