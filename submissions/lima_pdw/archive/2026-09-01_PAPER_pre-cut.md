# **Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation**

**Roger B. Hunt III** · Bentley University · rhunt@bentley.edu

*Dissertation Paper 2. Full paper for the Organization Studies / Organization Theory Paper Development Workshop, Universidad de Piura UDEP, Lima, 5–7 October 2026.*

## **Abstract**

Algorithmacy names the interpretive sensibility an individual exercises when coordinating with another person through an opaque, adaptive algorithmic intermediary that evaluates and binds them both. Organizational scholarship has formalized the governance dynamics of hierarchies, markets, networks, and classical co-optation; coordinative co-optation — enrollment by an algorithm that matches unchosen parties, scores their exchange, and can revoke their accounts — remains theoretically incomplete, leaving the enrolled actor's standing and capacity unmodeled. Rather than adjudicating legal or institutional standing, this article supplies the behavioral micro-foundation coordination inside that arrangement requires. A structured comparative evaluation of eight candidate constructs reveals a persistent omission: models centering the algorithmic system bracket the human counterpart, while models centering interpersonal interaction treat the technological artifact as a transmission channel or an external tool. No construct models individual capacity where the intermediary evaluates and binds both parties engaged in interdependent joint work. I formalize algorithmacy through three constitutive operations, each answering one deficit the triad imposes: *interpreting* the intermediary's rules and the unseen counterpart's intentions from the same composite feedback; *specifying intent* so that meaning survives constrained channels and reaches the counterpart intact; and *keeping track* of the rule's unannounced shifts. An empirical strategy pairs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol with a three-wave longitudinal survey panel in a software-engineering field setting.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication sensibility · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

## **Introduction**

A student submits her code for review. Her classmates read it, comment on it, and vote, all in public where she can watch. An algorithm none of them can inspect then weighs those inputs by a concealed formula and returns a verdict: pass or fail. Her instructor holds no vote and cannot override the outcome, and the weights themselves are retuned as the term runs. To get through, she must coordinate with people she can see through a rule she cannot — reading the verdict for what her peers meant, writing an explanation that a test suite will parse and a person will still understand, and noticing when the hidden rule has moved.

Arrangements of this shape now carry a growing share of everyday work, and organization theory lacks an account of how individuals coordinate with one another inside them. Classical scholarship identifies three canonical coordination forms: hierarchies governed by managerial authority, markets governed by price mechanisms, and networks governed by ongoing social relationships. Digital platforms introduce a distinct configuration by using algorithms to enroll, evaluate, and match participants. Recent research identifies the capacities workers deploy directly against an automated system (Zhou et al., 2025), and leaves the human counterpart outside the analytical frame.

Fragments of the sensibility this student exercises are already documented elsewhere. Bucher (2017) shows that ordinary users build working theories of an opaque feed algorithm — an *algorithmic imaginary* — and act on what they believe it wants. DeVito et al. (2018) find those folk theories assembled from many sources and put to work in the service of self-presentation. Litt (2012) names the *imagined audience*, the mental picture of unseen others against which a person calibrates a message she cannot watch land. Manky (2025) watches ride-hailing drivers in Lima read opaque platform metrics to judge whether a passenger is safe to accept. In each case an opaque, adaptive system stands between a person and human others; in none of them does that system return a single determination binding both sides of a jointly executed task. The defensible claim is therefore narrower than the one the phenomenon invites: no construct models the individual capacity to coordinate interdependent joint work with a human counterpart through an intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. That position stands empty.

I model algorithmacy on the developmental lineage of literacy, numeracy, and oracy — the last of these named by Wilkinson (1965), who argued that speaking and listening deserved the standing schooling already granted reading and counting. Literacy enables participation where text mediates communication, numeracy equips individuals for environments structured by quantitative data, and oracy governs interaction through spoken discourse. Algorithmacy enables sustained participation in coordination structured by an algorithm: the sensibility required to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary *and* the counterpart reached through it, to specify intent through the channels that intermediary permits, and to track its rule as that rule shifts. Three terms in the definition carry the structure. Opacity means the participant cannot observe the governing rule directly; adaptivity means the rule itself moves over time; intermediary designates the algorithmic system standing between the focal actor and her human counterpart. Where literacy, numeracy, and oracy are sensibilities for navigating a symbolic medium, algorithmacy is an interpretive sensibility for an organizational coordination form.

The term is not algocracy, and the resemblance is a trap worth closing at first use. Aneesh (2009) uses *algocracy* for a mode of organization — rule by code, set beside bureaucracy and the market as a third way of coordinating dispersed labor — whereas algorithmacy names the individual capacity a person exercises inside such an arrangement (see also Danaher, 2016). One is a property of the system; the other, of the person acting within it.

Paper 1 names that arrangement *coordinative co-optation*, building the compound term on the mechanism Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) themselves identify — "on platforms they are co-opted" — and a reader's intuition for co-optation will misfire without a gloss. In Selznick's (1949) classical sense, an organization absorbs an external challenger by conferring a seat on him: opposition becomes participation, and the seat carries a formal standing. The platform confers nothing of the kind. It does not hire you, buy from you, or befriend you — it enrolls you, on terms it sets, scores, and can revoke.

Extant frameworks cannot hold an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary in the same frame, and they fail in different ways. Long and Magerko (2020) treat the algorithm as an object of formal knowledge, leaving no communicative counterpart in view; Guzman and Lewis (2020) seat the machine itself in the interlocutor position, leaving the far-side human untheorized; Hancock et al. (2020) model the artifact as a delegate acting on one designated party's behalf; Zhou et al. (2025) reduce the human evaluator to an external score. Two of those four are one field at two moments: human–machine communication has carried the machine further toward the interlocutor's seat than any other research program, and the evaluation below hears it first and at field depth. Where a counterpart appears, the intermediary functions as a transmission channel, a personal delegate, or infrastructure to bypass; where the intermediary evaluates and binds, the counterpart shrinks to a passive rating source. Online dispute resolution scholarship recognized the configuration through Katsh and Rifkin's (2001) "fourth party," and theorized it from the perspective of system design rather than participant capacity.

Naming this capacity matters because its distribution is consequential and currently invisible. Algorithmacy is held unevenly by participants the platform treats as formally equivalent, so it sorts identical workers into winners and losers while the arrangement attributes the outcome to merit — the claim Proposition 2 formalizes below. Lima's drivers supply the sharper version: reading a black-box metric is how a driver decides whether a stranger is safe to pick up (Manky, 2025). An inequality with no name cannot be measured, taught, or compensated for.

The article proceeds in three moves. I first summarize Paper 1, which sets coordinative co-optation beside the classical archetypes and shows that platform organizing leaves the enrolled actor's standing and capacity unmodeled (Appendix A). I then evaluate eight candidate constructs from organization theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction, none of which accounts at once for an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary. Algorithmacy is a bounded behavioral construct: it defines the sensibility the triad demands of a participant and stops there, leaving institutional justice to the governance literatures equipped to judge it (Suddaby, 2010). Third, I present an empirical strategy built on an insider action-research design and fielded in a specialized software-engineering course (Appendix B). It runs on an in-editor harness protocol: participants complete a technical task, encounter an incompletely explained outcome, and articulate how they read the decision and what they did next.

## **The Coordination-Form Gap**

Mature organization theory defines a coordination form by specifying both its primary operational mechanism and the formal standing of the participants it governs. Established scholarship evaluates coordination forms across six core dimensions: operational mechanics, required integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, primary instruments, failure modes, and empirical identification criteria. Four classical forms clearly pair a governing mechanism with a defined participant standing:

  - **Hierarchy:** An employee operates within an authority-governed zone of acceptance, where managerial direction secures alignment through formal standard operating procedures (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978).
  - **Market:** An autonomous transacting party evaluates a posted price term backed by an unrestricted right of refusal, relying on the price mechanism as a condensed public signal (Hayek, 1945).
  - **Network:** A partner invests in an elective, revocable relationship grounded in informal social reciprocity and mutual reputational capital (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990).
  - **Classical Co-optation:** An external challenger accepts a conferred administrative seat, transforming outside opposition into structured institutional participation (Selznick, 1949).

Across a managerial zone, a posted market price, an elective relationship, or an institutional seat, established theory articulates how the form operates alongside the formal status it accords the individual.

Coordinative co-optation breaks the pairing, because it does not address *participant standing* at all. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) define the departure by observing that while actors in hierarchies command, in markets contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted. This fifth coordination form operates through automated enrollment: digital platforms deploy algorithms to match unchosen parties, evaluate their ongoing interactions, and unilaterally terminate accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Within it, the enrolled participant functions neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price terms, nor a collaborative partner in an elective network. Selznick's absorbed challenger at least received a seat; the enrolled participant receives a queue position.

That omission exposes a micro-foundational gap. Macro-structural scholarship examines how platform firms leverage informational asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016) and non-portable reputational ratings (Curchod et al., 2020) to enforce administrative control, and leaves unmodeled the behavioral mechanisms through which enrolled individuals achieve day-to-day coordination. Regulatory debates and legal classifications concern the standing a participant ought to hold; organization theory still lacks an account of the individual capacity required to coordinate through an opaque, adaptive intermediary regardless of what standing an arrangement assigns. Eight candidate constructs already circulate in adjacent literatures, and I give each a hearing before coining a new one.

## **Extant Constructs**

Any candidate construct for algorithmic coordination can be read for one thing first: the structural role it assigns the computational intermediary. I define a construct here as a formalized conceptualization of individual capacity, whether operationalized as a functional competence, an infrastructural literacy, or an interpretive sensibility. Adjacent organizational frameworks already incorporate a human counterpart, and they assume relational conditions that algorithmic management eliminates. Relational coordination theory, for instance, conceptualizes shared communication and relational ties among interdependent organizational members (Gittell, 2002). That model presupposes unmediated interactants who directly observe, contact, and negotiate with one another. Because an algorithmic intermediary severs direct mutual visibility, the diagnostic question for each candidate is whether it treats technology as a passive conduit, an autonomous interlocutor, or an opaque third party.

That question carries a classical warrant the comparison would otherwise leave implicit. Simmel (1902) argued that moving from two parties to three changes the sociological form rather than the population: a dyad ends when either member withdraws, while a triad supports positions no dyad can hold — the impartial mediator who reconciles, the *tertius gaudens* who profits from the other two's opposition, and the third who manufactures that opposition in order to profit from it. In each case the relation between the other two is no longer immediate; it is routed, and the router has interests of its own (Simmel, 1950). Which position the third occupies is therefore what determines what the first two can do with each other, and the status a construct accords the intermediary is not a descriptive detail but the thing that fixes the arrangement's form.

Eight theoretical constructs from organizational theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction approach the individual capacity required for algorithmic coordination. I evaluate each against a single structural question: where does the model locate the human counterpart on the far side of the interaction — the client commissioning labor, the peer evaluator whose rating alters administrative standing? To qualify for the comparison, a construct must model individual capacity relative to an algorithmic system while addressing, even partially, the presence of an interdependent human actor. I take the eight in the order of standing they grant the machine, which puts human–machine communication first and gives it the longest hearing: no other program has moved the machine as far toward the position of a party. Table 1 summarizes the eight, and the four analytical boundary conditions stated at the close of this section formalize the criteria I apply across all of them.

### **Human–Machine Communication**

Human–machine communication is not one position on the machine but a sequence of them. Over three decades the field has moved its object outward — from a desktop computer that triggers a user's social reflexes, to a communicative subject in its own right, to a delegate writing on one person's behalf to another, to a participant among several people at once — and each move sets the machine nearer to more humans and nearer to more consequence. I take the generations in that order, because the demarcation this paper draws is a position on the same trajectory rather than a break from it. The third analytical condition — an intermediary that independently evaluates and binds both parties — is where each generation stops, and each stops somewhere different.

#### **Machines as Social Actors: The Dyadic Root**

The field's oldest thesis is that people treat computers as social actors — the computers-are-social-actors program, CASA in the field's own shorthand. Reeves and Nass (1996) ran a long series of experiments substituting a computer for a person in classic social-psychology designs and found the results largely held: participants were polite to machines, reciprocated their disclosures, responded to their flattery, and applied personality and gender stereotypes to them. Nass and Moon (2000) consolidated the program and named its mechanism *mindlessness* — a social script fires on a superficial cue without the user attending to whether the target warrants it — which is why the same participants, asked directly, deny treating a computer as a person. Social response to a machine, on this account, requires no belief that the machine is a social being, and the tradition is careful to say so.

Sundar and Nass (2000) then established that the user's attribution is itself a variable. Participants received identical content from a computer while being told the source was the machine itself, the programmer behind it, the network it drew on, or another person using it, and the same message was evaluated differently under each attribution. The authors call the variable *source orientation* — the user's working answer to the question of who is speaking — and their finding is that the user supplies it rather than the interface settling it. That result is the experimental ground for the first operation this paper defines. An actor reading an unexplained outcome at a gate is doing precisely this work, deciding whether what arrived came from a peer, from a rule, or from the two together, and *interpreting* names the capacity to do it under conditions where no cover story fixes the answer.

What the computers-are-social-actors tradition establishes, it establishes for two positions. The media-equation experiments put one human and one machine in a room and measure the human's response, and across two hundred studies the design does not vary in that respect (Reeves & Nass, 1996). The machine is a social actor *toward* the participant and never a party stationed between the participant and someone else. The tradition also locates its social response below the threshold of deliberation, and a binding verdict is the stimulus that lifts it back above: a determination that prices a person's work is not processed mindlessly, and whatever she brings to it is not the script CASA describes. Sundar and Kim (2019) name the disposition sitting closest to that boundary — the *machine heuristic*, a user's belief that machine judgment, being mechanical, is objective and unbiased — which the construct section takes up as a discriminant-validity threat rather than as a rival account of the arrangement.

#### **The Communicative Subject**

The field named itself around a stronger claim than a measured response. Human–machine communication replaces the transmission conduit by elevating the computational artifact into an autonomous communicative partner, displacing the second human actor from the analytical frame. Guzman and Lewis (2020) formalize the shift by arguing that communicative artificial intelligence systems operate as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). Articulating a programmatic research agenda across conversational agents, social robotics, and automated text generation, they direct the subfield toward "questions of communication as they relate to technologies designed to fulfill the communicator role" (p. 74). Meaning-making, in this formulation, unfolds "within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine" (p. 74). The departure from computer-mediated communication is definitive. Where the default model Guzman and Lewis describe has people exchanging information through a technology that mediates human interaction (p. 73), human–machine communication reconfigures the technical apparatus as an active interlocutor across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions. Guzman and Lewis acknowledge that communicative technologies can blur mediator and communicator functions simultaneously, a distinction that reinforces the premise of this paper: an algorithmic intermediary operates neither as a passive conduit nor as a conventional conversational partner. Inaugurating the subfield's dedicated journal, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) draw the constitutive boundary, defining human–machine communication as the study of meaning-making between humans and machines wherein the technical artifact occupies the position of a communicative subject.

Guzman's (2018) edited volume had already staged the move as a relocation. She traces the assumption her field contests — that communication technology is a channel between humans, an assumption the discipline built into its founding models and carried through every revision of them — and argues that machines designed to occupy the communicator's role break it. One seat is vacated and another taken, within a two-position model. Five years later the field's own inventory shows what the relocation produced. The *SAGE Handbook of Human-Machine Communication* (Guzman, McEwen, & Jones, 2023) runs to sixty-five chapters organized around human–machine meaning-making, partitioning the territory by machine type, by application domain, and by disciplinary neighbor, and the coordination of two humans through a machine that decides between them is neither a chapter nor an organizing category there. Multi-party settings do appear, in the volume's computer-supported-cooperative-work and human–machine teaming material, which is a machine present among several people rather than a machine positioned between two of them as the thing that binds them. A handbook's taxonomy, rather than an inference from one agenda article, is what establishes where the field sets its unit of analysis.

The internal critique mounted by structurational theorists within human–machine communication highlights the limits of isolating person–machine encounters, and it still does not incorporate an interdependent human counterpart. Gibbs et al. (2021) argue that scholarship mischaracterizes human–machine interaction by treating it as an isolated dyad rather than an organizational structuration. In their formulation, computational systems embody institutional rules and resources that human actors draw upon, reproduce, and selectively subvert, distributing agency across the human user, the technical artifact, and the governing institutional order. Targeting the field's methodological individualism, they draw on organizational cases such as algorithmic management to demonstrate that analyzing isolated person–machine interactions obscures the broader forces governing technological deployment.

This structurational turn leaves the vacancy unresolved because it introduces institutional context rather than an interdependent human actor. Structuration theory yields a triad linking the human actor, the computational artifact, and institutional structures; the coordination framework developed here links the focal worker, the algorithmic intermediary, and an unseen human counterpart. Neither formulation accords an analytical position to the specific human actor at the opposite pole of the coordination process, whose reception defines the ultimate purpose of the exchange and who remains structurally inaccessible. An individual may achieve comprehensive mastery within human–machine communication — decoding the system's symbolic cues, sustaining relational rapport with it — and still lack the sensibility required to coordinate interdependent labor through an automated system that evaluates and binds both parties. The capacity human–machine communication theorizes remains, by definition, an orientation directed toward the machine.

In locating algorithmacy within the individual I do not repeat the methodological individualism structurational theorists criticize. Gibbs et al. (2021) caution against reducing structural dynamics to isolated individual attributes; algorithmacy answers that caution as an individual micro-foundation rooted in social antecedents (Felin et al., 2015). The construct reconciles individual action with institutional structure through two properties. First, algorithmacy develops through collective socialization, and empirical research shows that workers acquire platform fluency through informal peer networks, community sensemaking, and shared lore (Sutherland et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2025). Second, while socially cultivated, algorithmacy remains individually held and enacted during live coordination. That individual variation explains why formally equivalent participants, placed in identical algorithmic environments, achieve starkly disparate coordination outcomes. An individual-level construct with social origins satisfies organization theory's micro-foundational requirement without collapsing into atomistic reductionism.

#### **AI-Mediated Communication: The Field Returns to Two Humans**

The field's next generation moved back toward two humans. Rather than seating the machine as the interlocutor, this line of work notices the person on the far side and models the machine as a delegate acting on one party's behalf toward the other. AI-mediated communication reintroduces the human counterpart to mediated interaction, and confines the computational system to the role of a partisan delegate serving a single communicator. Hancock et al. (2020) define it as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 90). The model preserves both human interactants across empirical phenomena such as predictive text engines, automated smart replies, and synthetic media. Its authors characterize the dynamic across five constitutive parameters: magnitude, reflecting the extent of computational alteration; media type, designating the medium of exchange; optimization goal, defining the objective guiding message generation; autonomy, capturing the degree of operation without sender supervision; and role orientation, identifying which communicator the system represents.

A purely structural node count fails to differentiate AI-mediated communication from algorithmacy, because Hancock et al. (2020) already model a triad of two human interactants and a computational agent. Within their design, the receiver occupies a central rather than marginal position: her cognitive inferences constitute the primary dependent variable of the research agenda, while accompanying ethical inquiries address what communicative transparency she is owed. Hancock and colleagues candidly document the vulnerability inherent in the receiver's position. They observe that explicit disclosure of automated assistance remains rare, that recipients typically presume a human counterpart authored the communication, and that the entire arrangement hinges on the unverified assumption that "the message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency" (p. 89).

What the research program found when it actually put an algorithm between two people bears on that assumption directly. Jakesch et al. (2019) had participants judge Airbnb host profiles labeled as human- or machine-written, and suspicion rather than authorship is what cost: with every profile in a set labeled machine-written, trust was unaffected, while in a mixed set the profiles believed to be machine-written were trusted markedly less. They call the pattern the *replicant effect*, an evaluation of provenance made comparatively against whatever the rest of the set is doing rather than absolutely. Mieczkowski et al. (2021) ran pairs through a referential communication task with and without smart replies and found the assisted condition produced shorter, more conventional, more positive language without degrading task success or the partners' perceptions of each other — a study of *grounding*, the joint work of confirming a message landed as intended, in which both parties are present and in contact throughout. Hohenstein and Jung (2020) put a comparable system into a dyadic chat and measured blame: when the conversation went badly, negative attribution shifted toward the system and trust in the human partner rose. Borrowing Elish's (2019) term, they call the machine a *moral crumple zone*, a component positioned to absorb responsibility properly attributable elsewhere.

Hohenstein et al. (2023) close that lineage with its largest study and its sharpest result. Across two experiments, smart replies made conversations faster and more positive and improved how partners evaluated one another; a participant *suspected* of using the system, however, was evaluated more negatively, and the relationship itself suffered. The asymmetry is the finding. The benefit accrues to the interaction, and the cost accrues to an individual, on the basis of a suspicion that may be wrong and is never stated aloud for her to answer. Hancock and colleagues could only presume the receiver's comprehension and acceptance of algorithmic mediation; the experiments show what fills the space when the presumption fails, which is an attribution the suspected party has no route to contest.

The decisive difference between the two frameworks is categorical rather than parametric: AI-mediated communication governs symbolic messages, whereas algorithmacy governs binding institutional verdicts. Hancock and colleagues model an agent that modifies, augments, or generates text. Every dimension of their framework — magnitude, media type, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation — characterizes symbolic message alteration, even when optimization aligns with platform priorities instead of individual sender goals. The algorithmic intermediary in the setting examined here renders authoritative decisions instead: it matches unchosen parties, ranks performance, and terminates accounts. No combination of autonomy or message magnitude transforms an assistant that edits text into an institutional authority binding both interactants to determinations neither commissioned. An editor is not a judge.

Hancock and colleagues' definitional requirement that the agent operate "on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90) underscores the same boundary. In the coordination setting at issue, the algorithmic third party operates on behalf of no one present at the interaction. Their model exposes the counterpart strictly to a modified message; algorithmacy exposes both interactants simultaneously to an authoritative determination. I adopt the optimization goal parameter from Hancock et al. (2020) as the analytical axis separating the two frameworks: their computational agent optimizes for the interpersonal or communicative objectives of a designated communicator — sender or receiver — while the intermediary modeled here optimizes for institutional coordination metrics external to both interactants. I also build on their assessment of receiver vulnerability, in which a receiver's comprehension and acceptance of algorithmic mediation are presumed rather than secured. That presumption is the conceptual foundation for an unseen counterpart barred from occupying an evaluative position altogether.

Read across the whole lineage, the algorithm's effects run through attribution and suspicion. It shapes messages before they are sent and absorbs blame after they land, and in none of these studies does it issue a determination binding both parties before either of them acts. The empirical record confirms the boundary from outside rather than pressing against it, and what it supplies in addition is the mechanism by which a far-side person pays for a system's involvement in work she did not watch being done.

#### **Machines Among Groups**

The delegate is not the only position the field has built for a machine among people. Neighboring work in human–robot interaction has spent a decade putting machines inside groups and measuring what happens to the humans' relations with one another. Jung, Martelaro, and Hinds (2015) designed a task to produce interpersonal violations between two people and had a robot intervene with repair utterances aimed at the violation between them, shifting how the pair perceived each other and the interaction, with the effect depending on how the robot's move was framed. Traeger et al. (2020) ran fifty-one groups of three people and one robot through a collaborative game and varied only the machine's self-presentation: robots that admitted error and expressed uncertainty produced markedly more equal and more responsive conversation among the human members, who spoke more evenly and reported a better group experience. Seeber et al. (2020) set an agenda for machines as teammates rather than tools, distinguishing systems that support a team's work from systems that take a role in it by contributing, facilitating, moderating, or evaluating. Sebo et al. (2020) reviewed the literature entire, organizing it by the robot's function and concluding that a robot in a group poses a research problem distinct from a robot in a dyad.

Two things follow, and they run in opposite directions. Roboticists have built and tested the machine-in-the-middle position, which the demarcation has to accommodate rather than deny: Jung's robot occupied the mediator's seat experimentally in 2015, and the arrangement is not hypothetical. What the systematic review does not contain is the other half. No study in Sebo and colleagues' survey measures a group member's capacity to fare well in the arrangement, and in none of them does the machine's output bind the participants; the robot proposes, facilitates, or discloses, and the humans decide. A field that has asked carefully and at scale what a machine does to the people around it has not yet asked what those people must be able to do when the machine's output settles something for them. That is the vacancy this paper claims, stated here by a neighboring literature's own review rather than by the sort across eight constructs.

Traeger and colleagues' result runs forward into the propositions as well. What their robot varied was the legibility of its own uncertainty, and legibility improved the humans' coordination with each other. An intermediary that admits no error and gives no account of itself is on that evidence doing more than withholding information, which is why Proposition 3 below treats transparency as an institutional lever rather than a fixed perimeter.

#### **Asymmetric Interpretation and the Demarcation**

Beneath all four generations sits an asymmetry that the field's deepest theoretical statement makes explicit. Suchman (2007) argues that plans are resources for action rather than determinants of it, and that human–machine interaction breaks down where a system is designed as though it could read a user's purpose from her conduct. Her expert-help-system study locates the imbalance precisely: the user brings the full situated interpretive resources of a person to the encounter, reading the machine's behavior as meaningful conduct, while the machine holds only a thin, pre-categorized trace of hers. The repair burden falls on the human because she is the only party equipped to carry it, and the system's failures present to her as her own.

Algorithmacy inherits that asymmetry and puts a second interpreter behind it. In Suchman's encounter the thin trace is what the machine holds of the user; in the triad the same thin trace is also what prices the counterpart, so the participant interprets *through* the asymmetry toward a second full interpreter she cannot see and whose reading never returns to her. Suchman's user interprets a machine holding a thin trace of her; the participant here interprets a verdict that fuses that trace with a second person's judgment she cannot see.

Human–machine communication supplies the demarcation algorithmacy needs. Guzman and Lewis (2020) draw the clearest available distinction between the passive transmission conduits of computer-mediated communication and the active communicative partners of their own field, and I adopt that operational distinction while departing from its ontological premise, reconceptualizing the computational apparatus not as an autonomous conversational subject but as an opaque, binding intermediary inside an asymmetric triad. By defining their field around machine-directed meaning-making, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) fix the baseline from which this paper diverges: the question shifts from how individuals communicate with computational systems to how they coordinate interdependent work through them.

Set the generations in sequence and the demarcation reads as a position on the field's own trajectory. The machine begins as a social respondent to one person (Reeves & Nass, 1996; Nass & Moon, 2000), becomes a communicative subject in its own right (Guzman & Lewis, 2020), then a delegate carrying one person's message to another (Hancock et al., 2020), then a participant among several at once (Seeber et al., 2020; Sebo et al., 2020). Each move places the machine nearer to more humans and nearer to more consequence. The triad names the next position on that line: the machine above both humans, reading each of them and returning a determination that binds them both, which no construct in the field yet occupies. Nothing in the field's equipment rules that position out — source orientation, attribution redistribution, and the group-level measures the robotics work has developed are what a study of the triad would reach for first. What is missing is the account of what the person on the far side must be able to do, and the remainder of this paper supplies it.

Human–machine communication defined itself against the conduit tradition it inherited, and that tradition still holds the field's most developed account of what competence in a mediated exchange consists of. It is the next hearing.

### **Computer-Mediated Communication Competence**

Spitzberg's (2006) model of computer-mediated communication competence carries the architecture of interpersonal competence into digital settings intact: the medium enters as a set of parameters conditioning a dyadic exchange, never as a party to it. Conceptualizing computer-mediated communication as "any human symbolic text-based interaction conducted or facilitated through digitally-based technologies" (p. 630), his framework organizes individual capacity across five foundational dimensions: motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and relational outcomes. Motivation captures "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in a given CMC context" (p. 640), while knowledge encompasses "the cognitive comprehension of content and procedural processes involved in conducting appropriate and effective interaction" (p. 641). Behavioral execution rests on four core skill clusters: attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness (p. 638), all conditioned by five discrete facets of context (p. 644). Underpinning the formulation is a theoretical premise: digital mediation recalibrates the speed and bandwidth of message exchange while leaving the fundamental structure of interpersonal competence undisturbed. Media characteristics matter to Spitzberg, and they matter as variables inside a two-person model. The human actors stationed at either terminal remain the sole coordinators and evaluators of the interaction.

Spitzberg locates communicative competence in relational co-construction, not in isolated cognitive faculties. Rooted in the relational communication tradition, his framework establishes that interacting partners jointly produce and evaluate competence. As Spitzberg notes, "competent interactants can facilitate the competence of cointeractants," whereas "an incompetent interactant can diminish a normally competent cointeractant's performance" (p. 650). Communicative acts achieve legitimacy only when evaluated against receiver expectations conditioned by the digital medium. Far from treating the human counterpart as an ancillary variable, the model positions that counterpart as its essential evaluative instrument. Five relational outcome criteria govern the framework: appropriateness, effectiveness (encompassing task achievement and efficiency), coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development (p. 648). These criteria presuppose an unmediated relational architecture in which both interactants observe and perceive one another. Coorientation and relationship development operate as irreducibly bilateral dynamics; appropriateness, defined as the "perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context," requires a conscious human perceiver; and satisfaction, operationalized as "positive affect associated with the fulfillment of positively valenced expectancies," demands an interacting subject capable of affective resonance.

Spitzberg's relational design establishes its own boundary by presupposing a direct connection between a counterpart's appraisal and its communicative consequence. Consider an actor who demonstrates complete mastery across every dimension of the model, including baseline motivation, media knowledge, attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness. Placed within an arrangement where an opaque algorithm evaluates both parties and issues a binding determination, this actor reaches an immediate impasse. The breakdown does not stem from a personal deficit in individual competence, nor does it require the human counterpart to disappear. Instead, the algorithmic intermediary severs the functional link between the counterpart's evaluation and its tangible outcome. The counterpart may still observe the exchange and register a judgment, whether privately or in public view, and that appraisal reaches the focal actor only as an isolated input to an opaque aggregation neither party controls. Interactants consequently cannot engage in the iterative, mutual calibration Spitzberg's criteria require. Grounding theory states why that calibration is unavailable rather than merely difficult. Clark and Brennan (1991) model communication as the joint construction of common ground: a contribution is not complete when it is uttered but when the parties hold mutual evidence that it was understood, and reaching that state — *grounding* — runs through acknowledgment, a relevant next turn, and repair, each with its own cost, with the parties doing the least collaborative effort jointly sufficient to establish understanding. Every one of those forms of evidence is structurally withheld in the triad. The counterpart returns no acknowledgment the actor can read, no next turn addressed to her, and no route to repair, while the verdict that does arrive is a judgment about understanding rather than evidence of it. Grounding cannot close. The arrangement does not merely depress a communicator's competence score; it disconnects the relational evaluative instrument from the authoritative verdict that governs both parties.

I take over Spitzberg's normative evaluative criteria and reject the relational topology his model presupposes. The three constitutive operations defined below specify what an actor cognitively and behaviorally executes; they do not independently establish what constitutes communicative success. Appropriateness and effectiveness remain the most robust standards available for evaluating communicative efficacy. What algorithmacy cannot adopt is the transmission architecture underpinning those criteria — a directly accessible human receiver stationed at the terminal node of a digital channel, whose unmediated appraisal serves as the operational measure of competence.

Under the triad, the two criteria acquire triadic referents. An act is effective when it survives the gate *and* the counterpart decodes the meaning the actor intended — the dual criterion *specifying intent* already implies. An act is appropriate when it fits the counterpart's expectations as the actor has reconstructed them under opacity, which is what *interpreting* produces. Spitzberg's standards survive the move; the evaluator who administers them is what changes. Where his model reads appropriateness off a co-present perceiver's response, algorithmacy reads it off a reconstruction the actor must build and revise without confirmation, which makes the accuracy of that reconstruction an empirical quantity rather than a presupposition. Spitzberg models what competence *is* in a mediated dyad. The strongest account of how communicators *adapt* to what a medium withholds belongs to a different lineage, and it is the next hearing.

### **Social Information Processing and the Hyperpersonal Model**

Walther (1992) dismantled the consensus that text-based exchange is impersonal because it strips nonverbal cues. The reduced-cues theories had mistaken a rate effect for a capacity effect, he argued: communicators adapt, encoding relational information into whatever the channel does carry — word choice, timing, message length — and given enough time they build relationships as personal as face-to-face ones. Social information processing theory states the result compactly — a narrow channel constrains the bandwidth of relational communication, not its ceiling. The engine of the argument is a review: the studies that had produced the impersonal findings were nearly all one-shot laboratory sessions with no anticipated future interaction, a design that removes the very condition the theory says adaptation needs. Four years later Walther (1996) pushed the claim past parity. The *hyperpersonal model* holds that mediated exchange can exceed face-to-face intimacy through four interlocking components: a sender who exploits editability to present a selectively optimized self, a receiver who fills the missing cues with favorable inference, channel properties that make composition strategic, and a behavioral-confirmation loop in which an idealized partner is treated as ideal and behaves accordingly. Against the four analytical conditions, this hearing satisfies individual capacity and an active, interdependent counterpart as robustly as anything else in the comparison, and more robustly than most.

The adaptation thesis is also the closest thing in the literature to *specifying intent*, argued three decades early. Walther's communicators develop, through experience and without instruction, the capacity to convey what a narrow channel was thought unable to carry — which is this paper's acquisition-through-participation property under another name, applied to this paper's second operation. A reviewer who knows the computer-mediated communication literature will ask how algorithmacy is anything but social information processing with an algorithm swapped in for the human receiver, and the question deserves an answer before it is put.

The answer sits in the loop's closing condition. Walther's adaptation works because the party who receives the message is the party who evaluates it and replies: the sender learns what carries because the receiver responds, and every subsequent adjustment is calibrated against feedback from the addressee herself. Selective self-presentation pays off only because the idealizing receiver then treats the sender accordingly, which the sender experiences and can read. In the triad, the party that evaluates is not the party whose understanding matters. The intermediary issues the assessment and the counterpart holds the meaning; neither returns anything resembling a reply, and a verdict is not feedback from anyone. The student at the gate can write her pull-request description a hundred ways, and what the failed verdict tells her is that this one did not clear. Which of her two readers the sentence lost, and where, is not in the signal. The hyperpersonal loop has nowhere to attach — not because the channel is poorer than Walther's, but because the evaluator and the addressee have come apart. Walther's communicator adapts to a channel. The triad's participant answers to one.

I take that boundary as an inheritance rather than a refutation, and the inheritance runs the other way. Walther's evidence is the strongest prior in this literature for expecting participants to develop the capacity at all: people do learn to push meaning through channels built to carry less of it, and algorithmacy predicts the learning continues where Walther's closing conditions no longer hold. Walther (2007) then supplies the rival the empirical design needs. Unpacking the sender side of the hyperpersonal model, he argues that editability and asynchrony free cognitive resources otherwise consumed by managing a live interaction, and that what a communicator does with the freed capacity is what produces the effect. Cognitive slack of that kind is not equally available — a worker holding several jobs or composing in a second language has less of it to redirect into packaging a submission — so accumulated channel experience and available slack predict uneven outcomes without any distinct triadic capacity. Study 3 carries both as measured covariates for that reason (Appendix C).

Experience with a channel is one account of where the capacity comes from. Formal instruction in the technology is another, and the frameworks built on it are the next hearing.

### **AI Literacy**

AI literacy frameworks treat the algorithm as an object of formal technical comprehension, shifting the analytical focus away from interpersonal coordination toward individual system mastery. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace." They structure the domain around five foundational inquiries: what artificial intelligence is, what it can execute, how it operates, how it should be deployed, and how humans perceive its agency. From those questions the authors derive seventeen discrete competencies, including system identification, machine learning representations, algorithmic decision-making, model training workflows, sensor integration, ethical evaluation, and programmability.

Only two of the seventeen competencies reference human actors, and both confine humans to positions outside live interaction. Competency 10 (Human Role in AI) requires learners to "recognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems," while Competency 5 positions human cognition strictly as a comparative baseline against which to evaluate automated performance. Human beings enter this framework solely as upstream software engineers or comparative benchmarks, never as interdependent counterparts in ongoing coordination. Neither competency accords another individual an operational role within the workflow the learner executes.

Long and Magerko's (2020) treatment of collaboration reinforces the boundary by directing every communicative and collaborative action toward the computational artifact. The framework defines its objective as communicating and collaborating "with AI" and targets cognitive misconceptions that "limit people's ability to effectively use, collaborate with, and act as critical consumers of AI." Interpersonal collaboration appears only in auxiliary instructional recommendations, where Design Consideration 11 (Social Interaction) advises educators to "consider designing AI learning experiences that foster social interaction and collaboration." Human peers populate the physical classroom and remain absent from the theoretical competence model. Ceiling performance across all seventeen competencies would still leave a participant unequipped to convey intent to an unseen human counterpart through an opaque, binding system, because all five foundational inquiries interrogate the technical properties of the apparatus rather than the coordination conducted through it.

That omission propagated into subsequent psychometric operationalizations, institutionalizing a dyadic model across empirical research. Ng et al. (2021) synthesized Long and Magerko's seventeen competencies into four overarching dimensions: knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics. Subsequent measurement scales inherited the fourfold taxonomy, operationalizing individual competence strictly as an actor's unilateral transaction with a computational artifact. The interdependent human counterpart was thereby written out of the standard psychometric tradition seeded by the 2020 framework. That architecture is not confined to the research instruments. DigComp 2.2 (Vuorikari et al., 2022), the European Union's flagship competence framework, distributes twenty-one competences across five areas, and its human counterpart lives in the communication-and-collaboration area while its artificial-intelligence material sits in a dedicated appendix: when another person appears the technology is a channel, and when the system appears the counterpart drops out. What that separation costs a framework of policy weight is taken up in the synthesis below.

Algorithmacy diverges from AI literacy in how its dimensions are obtained. Long and Magerko assemble their catalog using scoping criteria grounded in definitional alignment, bibliographic prevalence, and pedagogical utility. That inductive route supplies no principle explaining why nine competencies govern technical mechanics while one addresses normative ethics. The operations of algorithmacy, by contrast, follow from the specific informational deficits an opaque, adaptive, binding arrangement imposes on its participants, and I perform that derivation in the next major section rather than asserting it. I adopt Long and Magerko's literacy genealogy, which anchors the construct in the continuum of human symbolic capacity by tracing literacy from alphabetic text through digital, computational, and data literacies.

### **Algorithmic Competency**

Zhou et al. (2025) provide the first psychometrically validated scale measuring individual capability within algorithmic management, and it sets the empirical benchmark for construct development in this domain. Their multi-stage protocol originated in 99 semi-structured qualitative interviews with Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers, generating 14 candidate items that expert content validation subsequently refined. Across four successive samples spanning an exploratory factor analysis (N=275), a confirmatory factor analysis (N=213), a construct validation study (N=230), and a three-wave longitudinal panel (N=225), the authors validated a second-order, four-factor model embedded within a nomological network of organizational antecedents and performance outcomes. The final 12-item instrument retains an aggregate internal reliability of .85 across four subdimensions, defining algorithmic competency as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2).

Zhou et al.'s operationalization extends beyond declarative technical knowledge by incorporating behavioral navigation and affective disposition. Three of the four validated dimensions capture capacities distinct from cognitive comprehension: Understanding encompasses knowledge of algorithmic management alongside the ability to navigate dynamic operational rules; Embracing captures affective willingness to trust the efficiency and fairness of automated allocations; Leveraging denotes the strategic capacity to exploit algorithmic affordances for personal benefit; and Remediating reflects a worker's ability to correct, circumvent, or supplement algorithmic failures. Zhou and colleagues establish discriminant validity for this multidimensional structure with unusual care. General digital competence correlates moderately with the aggregate scale (*r* = .37, *p* \< .01) and exhibits a non-significant correlation with Embracing (*r* = .04, *p* \> .05), confirming that conventional digital literacy measures fail to capture affective orientations toward automated control.

The theoretical boundary of the instrument lies in the position it assigns the human counterpart, who appears strictly as an external evaluative outcome rather than an active coordination partner. The counterpart surfaces across several scale items, and none positions her as an interlocutor within the competency itself. Item 8 frames the customer as a distant punitive consequence ("I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback"), directing behavioral adaptation toward the platform rules rather than the relational counterpart. Item 11 reduces triadic coordination to an abstract attitudinal belief about matching logistics ("I think the platform AM is highly efficient, such as in customers–workers matching"), an item that resides within the dimension exhibiting the weakest inter-factor correlation and measures no interactive capacity toward the matched individual. Customer-oriented service behavior does enter the authors' structural equation model through Peccei and Rosenthal's (2000) scale, and that modeling choice treats the counterpart as an external downstream outcome of algorithmic coordination, never a constitutive element of it.

The sole contestation mechanism in the scale routes worker grievances through the technical infrastructure, bypassing the counterpart entirely. Item 6 within the Remediating subdimension operationalizes whether a worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve vulnerabilities in AM" (p. 8). The qualitative fieldwork underpinning the item illustrates an asymmetrical dispute process: confronted with an unjustified customer evaluation, a courier compiles supporting evidence and submits an administrative appeal directly to the platform's automated apparatus. The customer generates the initial negative rating; the grievance procedure never engages that customer. Resolving disputes through an automated administrative channel treats the interpersonal relationship as irrelevant to the determination.

Ceiling performance on the twelve-item scale fails to guarantee coordination efficacy because the construct's underlying design remains dyadic. A worker can demonstrate complete mastery across automated dispatch dynamics, express faith in algorithmic matching, exploit platform rules to secure preferred orders, and remediate systemic errors through technical appeals, and still hold no capacity to reconstruct what an unseen counterpart intended or to project communicative legibility toward that individual. The scale cannot see the counterpart because the construct never let her in. Its model bounds the competency before introducing the counterpart, treating the human other as an exogenous dependent variable. Extending the instrument to the counterpart would not be an appended item but a re-derivation: every validated dimension is anchored in the worker–platform dyad, the remediation channel runs to the machine, and the counterpart enters only as an outcome. Rebuilding the construct from a triadic starting point is the task this paper performs. A second transfer limit compounds the first, because the authors bind empirical indicators to commercial gig features such as app-based appeals and order routing, so the scale does not carry into peer-mediated evaluative environments where aggregate scoring rules and decision thresholds are concealed.

I inherit Zhou and colleagues' scale development protocol and build on their empirical findings about construct acquisition. In their structural equation model, the primary organizational antecedents predicting algorithmic competency are social mechanisms — informal peer support systems moderated by collectivist cultural orientations. That evidence supports the socialization proposition directly. Individuals do not master opaque algorithmic systems through isolated trial and error; they cultivate this sensibility through shared community practice, informal lore, and collective peer sensemaking.

### **Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation**

Rahman's (2021) framework of the "invisible cage" conceptualizes how workers experience and navigate algorithmic evaluation systems that replace codified bureaucratic rules with structural indeterminacy. Investigating an online freelancing platform's transition from a transparent five-star rating system to an opaque, percentile-based Job Success Score, Rahman contrasts the fixed, legible procedures of Max Weber's classical iron cage with the shifting opacity of algorithmic management. Five properties define the invisible cage: hidden and fluctuating evaluation criteria, assessments executed at unpredictable intervals, unidentifiable score calculation magnitudes, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority divided between the corporate platform and the individual client (p. 976). Rather than presenting an inductive inventory of platform features, these five properties establish an analytical proof of systemic closure: "even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque" (p. 976).

Rahman analyzes algorithmic work through a labor-process framework of managerial control rather than an organizational theory of coordination capacity. Within a regime of systemic opacity, workers navigate the invisible cage through two behavioral adaptations: experimental reactivity, in which individuals execute trial-and-error adjustments to reverse-engineer scoring criteria and recover fallen metrics, and constrained reactivity, in which workers restrict platform activity to insulate established reputational capital against volatility. Selection between the modes depends on an individual's financial dependence on the platform and prior exposure to negative evaluation shocks. These dynamics impose necessary discipline on the framework developed here, because they demonstrate that individual behavioral adaptations often deepen subordination while entrenching evaluative uncertainty over multi-year horizons.

Rahman's fieldwork documents the human counterpart, and the formal construct excludes her relational agency. To investigate the labor process, Rahman conducted qualitative interviews with 18 clients alongside 80 platform freelancers, recruiting clients through worker referrals because the interface foreclosed direct contact (p. 956). Registered freelancers could access a client's full profile only after submitting a job application, and even then the platform withheld direct contact information (p. 954). The scope of those client interviews remained bounded, serving primarily to examine client evaluation practices and triangulate freelancer accounts (p. 954). Rahman's records document the counterpart's blindness to her own evaluative impact, noting whether individual clients understood how their private ratings altered a freelancer's composite Job Success Score. Because the overarching framework models algorithmic systems through labor discipline and managerial control, that triadic dynamic remains outside the construct. Platform protocols institutionalized the mutual severance from both sides, assuring clients that their evaluative feedback would remain anonymous and never appear directly to the freelancer (p. 960).

Rahman's fifth property acknowledges an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority, and it reduces the counterpart to an interpretive ambiguity in metric calculation rather than an active partner in coordination. Within the invisible cage, a worker confronted with an unexpected score fluctuation cannot discern whether the shift stems from client dissatisfaction or an unannounced recalibration of the platform's baseline. In Rahman's formulation, that uncertainty is an epistemic problem about the behavior of the metric. Algorithmacy addresses a different problem: deciphering the latent expectations of a human counterpart through an opaque intermediary. The one behavioral adaptation in which Rahman's freelancers direct action toward the counterpart reinforces the boundary. Under constrained reactivity, workers attempt to bypass opacity by migrating platform-originated relationships to external channels. That off-platform disintermediation violates contractual terms of service, which shows that within a framework of labor reactivity, reaching the counterpart requires exiting the apparatus entirely and risking termination to restore direct bilateral interaction.

I take three insights from Rahman's framework into the construct. First, shifting and opaque evaluative criteria operate as an enduring environmental condition of algorithmic coordination, which establishes the necessity of the third operation, *keeping track*. Second, Rahman's five properties decompose the informational deficits the arrangement imposes on participants and expose an interpretive problem at the system's boundary: resolving whether to attribute an evaluative shift to machine logic or human discretion. Third, Rahman's demonstration that platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks drive worker behavioral variance introduces a formidable rival hypothesis to any individual-level construct. That third inheritance is also the strongest unanswered charge against this paper. If dependence and shock history explain the variance in coordination outcomes, algorithmacy explains nothing that a labor-process account has not already explained better, and no amount of construct clarity settles the question. Only a design that measures both, in the same participants, can — which is why platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks enter Study 3 as baseline controls rather than as acknowledgments.

### **Gig Literacies**

Sutherland et al. (2020) approach the configuration of algorithmic coordination by incorporating both market sides into their empirical design, and their construct remains bounded by a dyadic struggle over platform autonomy. Their formulation builds on Jarrahi and Sutherland's (2019) conceptualization of algorithmic competencies, which documented the sensemaking, circumventing, and manipulating tactics gig workers deploy against automated systems. That earlier framework established an adversarial dyad centered on worker-versus-platform appropriation, assigning no operational role to the human counterpart whom the algorithm matches to the worker. Sutherland et al. (2020) extend the lineage under the broader umbrella of gig literacies, examining the "critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing" (p. 457) that allow independent contractors to "adapt their skills and work strategies in order to leverage platforms creatively and productively, and as a component of their 'personal holding environment'" (p. 457). They formalize the capacity as platform literacy — "the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470).

To examine these practices, the authors paired in-depth qualitative interviews across 20 freelancers and 19 clients with a dual-sided digital walkthrough in which researchers engaged the platform simultaneously as buyers and sellers, one investigator registering as a client and another as a freelance contractor (pp. 461–463). Client accounts served primarily an evaluative verification function within that design, providing "some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers" (p. 463).

Sutherland et al. categorize worker practices into a fivefold typology spanning administrative navigation, operational efficiency, and interpersonal engagement (pp. 463–469): building reputation through Job Success Scores, platform badges, private feedback channels, and strategic refunds; self-presentation through profile optimization, targeted bidding proposals, and chat interactions; maintaining productivity through personal task routines and third-party software integrations; mitigating transaction risk by leveraging escrow protections, documenting communication trails, vetting prospective clients, and evaluating counterpart trustworthiness; and building relationships through direct interpersonal communication. Within that final dimension, independent contractors deliberately cultivate client rapport "to build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" aimed at securing repeat engagements (p. 468, participant P10).

This relational dimension approaches the interpersonal domain of coordination, and the authors' own evidence shows relationship-building functioning as a mechanism for escaping algorithmic governance rather than mastering coordination within it. When participant P39 confronted an algorithmically depressed rating that could not be rehabilitated within the platform interface, the worker migrated established client relationships off-platform to preserve economic viability (p. 469). In Sutherland et al.'s account, interpersonal rapport is an instrument of disintermediation, extracting the collaboration from the computational system and restoring an unmediated bilateral channel where algorithmic surveillance and scoring no longer apply. That exit strategy marks the boundary of platform literacy. Where gig literacies rely on off-platform evasion to bypass systemic constraints, algorithmacy conceptualizes the capacity required to sustain interdependent coordination and convey intent directly through an opaque, binding intermediary.

Sutherland et al. draw the same boundary from the other side by contrasting freelance marketplaces with deterministic dispatch platforms. They set online talent marketplaces such as Upwork, where participants retain the discretionary capacity to evaluate, select, and converse directly with prospective clients, against automated dispatch systems such as Uber, where algorithms assign labor deterministically (p. 470). Relational cultivation underwrites sustained commercial engagement on Upwork precisely because that platform preserves bilateral agency that deterministic dispatch eliminates. Their framework therefore models competencies enacted *adjacent to* a digital intermediary rather than coordinating action executed *through* an opaque, binding one. Because the model presupposes an environment where market actors can circumvent the apparatus and establish unmediated contact, adding relational indicators to such an instrument captures literacies exercised alongside the platform rather than coordination accomplished under its constraints.

I take two insights from Sutherland and colleagues. First, platform enrollment without institutional standing shifts systemic coordination burdens onto individual participant capacity. Second, workers acquire fluency with opaque evaluative metrics through situated social learning: independent contractors master platform demands through informal peer sensemaking and punitive experiential feedback rather than formalized technical documentation.

### **What These Boundaries Share**

A cross-construct synthesis reveals one limitation running through all eight: no extant framework theorizes an active human counterpart and an opaque, consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. The vacancy does not stem from an outright omission of the second human actor, since AI-mediated communication, social information processing, algorithmic competency, and gig literacies each incorporate a counterpart within their empirical scope. The limitation lies in how each framework couples the intermediary to the counterpart. Where a framework admits a human counterpart, it reduces the computational system to a passive conduit, a channel learned and exceeded, a partisan delegate, or an obstacle to circumvent, never an autonomous authority rendering binding determinations. Where a framework models the intermediary as opaque and binding, it demotes the counterpart to an exogenous rating source or an outcome variable the system measures, never an interdependent actor with whom the focal participant coordinates.

Recent systematic reviews across communication, information systems, and organizational scholarship substantiate the same boundary. The communication discipline's own stocktaking makes the point from further back: Hannawa and Spitzberg's (2015) twenty-two-chapter handbook partitions competence by setting — interpersonal, intercultural, health, organizational, instructional, computer-mediated — and every partition retains a human counterpart whose judgment constitutes the competence, and none is organized around a non-human adjudicator. Analyzing 50 empirical investigations across four research databases, Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) document pervasive conceptual fragmentation within algorithmic literacy research. In a review of 169 peer-reviewed studies, Gagrčin et al. (2026) identify an entrenched absence of unified theoretical frameworks governing user interactions with algorithms. Evaluating empirical research against Nutbeam's (2000) tripartite literacy hierarchy, Iyamu et al. (2026) observe that communicative literacy remains largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and psychometric instruments. Within Nutbeam's model, communicative literacy — operationalized as a clinician explaining algorithmic outputs to a patient whom the system does not simultaneously govern or bind — represents the literature's closest empirical approximation of a second human actor. Expanding survey indicators across empirical domains has consistently failed to unite that second actor with an autonomous, binding intermediary. Confronted with an inherently triadic coordination problem, this scholarship continues to populate the relational space with system-facing operationalizations.

Two policy frameworks with far larger budgets than any research instrument reach the same boundary, and reach it instructively. DigComp 2.2 (Vuorikari et al., 2022) took the European Union a decade to build, and its counterpart and its artificial-intelligence system occupy areas that never meet. UNESCO's (2024) framework for teachers is the sharper near-miss, because it names *human accountability* as a competency in its own right and asks teachers to treat "human accountability in human–AI decision loops" as a legal obligation, landing directly on this paper's vocabulary of withholding. The whole normative program there, though, is to keep that accountability intact against erosion, and its counterpart — the student — appears as the object of a professional's duty rather than as a second party the same system reads and binds. Algorithmacy begins after the erosion, in an arrangement where no framework the participant can invoke restores what the form has already taken.

Visibility labor scholarship approaches the same intersection from cultural and media studies, and stops short of modeling interdependent coordination. Abidin (2016) theorizes the strategic self-presentation followers enact to achieve visibility before a specific social media influencer, examining an identifiable human counterpart while bracketing computational algorithms as analog affective labor. Cotter (2019) integrates both elements, analyzing how Instagram creators pursue human audiences through an opaque, evaluative system by playing what she terms "the visibility game" against inferred algorithmic rules. Despite incorporating human audiences and algorithmic curation together, visibility labor diverges from algorithmacy on three dimensions. First, it theorizes communal practices and occupational subcultures rather than an individual capacity derived from the arrangement's own deficits. Second, its target audience is an aggregate market pursued for attention and engagement rather than an interdependent counterpart collaborating on jointly executed work. Third, the platform algorithm evaluates and binds the creator alone, curating feeds for consumers rather than rendering one mutually binding determination.

The nearest rival to the vacancy claim sits one field over, in the folk-theories and imagined-audience literatures, and the same three dimensions dispose of it. Bucher (2017) develops the *algorithmic imaginary* — the ways ordinary users think about what an algorithm is, what it should be, and how it works — and argues those understandings shape conduct and thereby feed back into the system itself. DeVito et al. (2018) interview users about how folk theories of social media feeds get built, and find them assembled from observation, talk, press coverage, and platform statements, then put to work in the service of self-presentation under conditions where the feed obscures who is actually watching. Litt (2012) names the anticipatory move directly: the imagined audience is the mental construction of unseen others against which a person calibrates a disclosure. Taken together, this line describes a person managing self-presentation to human others through an opaque, adaptive, curating algorithm — a triad, with opacity, adaptivity, and folk-theoretic interpreting all present. The rebuttal is not that the configuration is absent but that its coupling differs on all three counts. The algorithm curates and ranks for the presenter and binds her alone; the audience is an aggregate rather than a party whose intentions must be reconstructed and to whom something must be conveyed; and no work is jointly executed, so nothing turns on whether the two sides arrive at a working alignment. Novelty here survives on *mutual bindingness* and *interdependent joint work*, not on the triad or the opacity, and I state the claim at that width.

Adjacent disciplines have identified triadic configurations, and they analyze the arrangement from system-design, normative, or labor-control perspectives rather than modeling participant-side capacity. In online dispute resolution, Katsh and Rifkin (2001) conceptualize computational infrastructure as an active "fourth party" exerting autonomous influence over human disputants, establishing a setting in which two active human counterparts interact through an authoritative intermediary. Katsh and Rifkin theorize that arrangement from the perspective of system design, while Wing et al. (2021) evaluate its opaque, protection-withholding variants through a normative ethical framework. Neither articulates the participant-level capacity required to navigate it. They supply institutional design benchmarks and governance standards rather than a dimensional model of what an individual must cognitively and behaviorally execute when coordinating through an opaque, binding third party. Human–robot interaction reaches that limit from a third direction, as the hearing above records (Seeber et al., 2020; Sebo et al., 2020).

Organizational and labor-process theorists formalize triadic platform structures while leaving individual coordination sensibilities untheorized. Investigating platform-mediated evaluation, Curchod et al. (2020) demonstrate how automated feedback regimes dismantle bilateral market exchange to produce an asymmetrical triad of platform operators, buyers, and sellers, sustained by a visibility gap that lets buyers evaluate sellers invisibly. Cameron (2024) formalizes the same shape as the "algorithmic labor triangle," detailing how gig workers navigate platform constraints through continuous, bounded choices that generate labor consent. Healy and Pekarek (2025) integrate the triangular framework into labor process theory to explain worker vulnerability and institutional control. These perspectives explain what the triad is, how platforms enforce discipline, and how equilibrium emerges; they do not account for individual variance in coordination capacity. They explain the institutional outcomes the arrangement produces, and they omit the capacity separating an actor who coordinates effectively within it from one who does not.

Communication theory has in fact theorized competence for a three-party arrangement, and it did so from the third party's side. Johnson (2004) traces the life cycle of structural-hole brokerage — emergence, maintenance, dissolution — and argues that maintenance is the demanding phase: a broker must manage the flow of information to keep the two parties from connecting directly, while staying useful enough to both that neither routes around him. What a broker must do to hold that position is modeled in detail; what the party on the far side of him must be able to do is modeled nowhere. Burt (1992) explains why the far side stays untheorized and unserved at once. A structural hole is the gap between two parties who are not connected to each other, and the third who bridges it captures returns that consist precisely in controlling the flow and the timing between them. The platform's withholding of coordination conditions is on that account structural rather than incidental: the asset is the disconnection itself, and it can be held only by whoever sits in the gap.

Four analytical conditions distinguish triadic coordination from adjacent organizational models, and I applied them in every hearing above. First, the construct must operationalize an individual actor's behavioral and interpretive capacity rather than an institutional affordance or communal subculture. Second, the human counterpart must function as an active, interdependent collaborator within the focal coordination episode, which excludes frameworks positioning humans solely as upstream software engineers, informal peer advisors, or passive evaluative recipients. Third, the computational intermediary must independently evaluate and execute binding determinations upon both interactants, which rules out passive transmission conduits, partisan automated delegates, and macro-level design standards lacking participant-side operationalizations. Fourth, the critique must substantiate a genuine vacancy within organizational and communication theory rather than a semantic dispute over taxonomic labels. No candidate meets all four.

Emerging fieldwork already documents the latent capacity operating in naturalistic settings. Manky (2025) demonstrates that ride-hailing drivers in Lima (N=40) interpret opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness and ensure physical safety. That practice reveals a counterpart-directed interpretive sensibility, mobilized for bodily security, that currently lacks formal conceptualization. What organizational scholarship needs is a communicative construct built for the triadic position the empirical work has uncovered and left unequipped.

## **Algorithmacy: A Construct for Coordinative Co-optation**

Construct clarity in organizational theory turns on four things: a precise definition, explicit scope conditions, stated semantic relations to neighboring constructs, and demonstrated theoretical coherence (Suddaby, 2010). The subsections below do that work in order: they fix the construct's boundary against institutional standing, its scope conditions, its relations to four neighboring measures, its three operations, and its ontological category.

Simmel's vocabulary fixes what the intermediary is before the construct specifies what its participants must do. Among the third positions a triad supports, the platform occupies the one Simmel (1950) calls the arbitrator — the third who does not reconcile two parties' positions but decides between them — with one decisive difference. Simmel's arbitrator holds an authority the two parties conferred on him and can withdraw; the platform holds an authority neither party conferred, and the only withdrawal on offer is exit. Conferral is exactly what the arrangement omits, and that omission is where the construct's own boundary starts.

I build algorithmacy as a micro-foundational construct, which requires decoupling an actor's behavioral capacity from her formal institutional standing. Whether an enrolled participant holds the statutory protections of an employee, the unilateral exit rights of a market buyer, or the procedural hearing rights of a co-opted stakeholder is an institutional question of governance and legal design. Algorithmacy operates independently of how that question is resolved. It formalizes the individual capacity an enrolled actor must mobilize to coordinate interdependent action within whatever standing an arrangement assigns. Specifying what an individual can execute cognitively and behaviorally does not prescribe what protections she is normatively owed, which keeps the boundary this article draws a boundary between capacity and standing — not between micro and macro, a distinction the propositions below deliberately cross.

The independence holds because the operations of algorithmacy remain invariant across candidate resolutions of standing. The construct derives from opacity, adaptivity, and bindingness, which are properties of the coordination arrangement, not attributes of participant status. Institutional modifications leaving those three properties intact do not alter the behavioral operations required of the participant. A procedural hearing right that leaves the algorithmic rule undisclosed, or an administrative appeal that cannot overturn a binding determination, alters formal standing without relieving the informational deficits that demand algorithmacy. Governance reforms that eliminate opacity, adaptivity, or bindingness do not produce a standing-endowed participant who no longer needs the capacity; they dissolve the conditions defining coordinative co-optation and move the interaction outside the construct's scope.

### **Scope and Boundary Conditions**

Algorithmacy applies to organizational arrangements where a computational intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding upon interacting parties. The construct ceases to operate, or operates only trivially, in environments governed by static administrative rules or in open markets where transacting parties negotiate terms directly. A posted price accompanied by an unrestricted right of refusal defines the classical market archetype (Hayek, 1945), where price signals coordinate decentralized actors without intermediary evaluation. A codified administrative procedure administered by a designated hierarchical superior defines the formal bureaucracy (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978), where rules remain legible and authority resides in identifiable human offices.

Arrangements fall along a continuum according to how strictly the three properties govern the coordination episode. Online freelance labor platforms occupy a hybrid boundary, because participants retain the discretionary capacity to negotiate terms or migrate collaborations off-platform (Sutherland et al., 2020), mitigating opacity through direct bilateral communication. On-demand dispatch networks, algorithmic piecework queues, and peer-evaluation systems that conceal scoring criteria and decision rules sit at the core of the construct's domain. These scope conditions pick out configurations of coordination, not industrial sectors, which gives empirical researchers diagnostic criteria for identifying both the presence and the absence of algorithmacy across settings.

### **Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation**

I embed algorithmacy in a nomological network of four neighboring constructs and state each relation as a testable prediction. One caveat governs all four. The Paper 3 panel (N ≈ 50) cannot test these latent correlations; they are pre-registered for the pooled sample in Study 2 (Appendix C), and what the panel supplies is descriptive convergence and within-person trajectories.

First, regarding Algorithmic Competency (Zhou et al., 2025), I predict moderate positive correlations between congruent dimensions: *Understanding* with *interpreting*, and *Leveraging* with *specifying intent*. *Embracing* has no theoretical alignment with algorithmacy, because an affective orientation toward platform efficiency is a normative disposition rather than an operational coordination capacity, and the predicted null is the sharpest single test in the network. One disposition in particular makes that test worth running. Sundar and Kim's (2019) machine heuristic — the belief that machine judgment, being mechanical, is objective and unbiased — is a discriminant-validity threat by name, since a participant who holds it can report confidence in her reading of the gate without having developed any interpretive capacity at all. If algorithmacy's scores collapse once heuristic endorsement is partialled out, the construct is measuring a belief.

Second, Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation (Rahman, 2021) runs along an orthogonal axis. Reactivity categorizes degrees of behavioral adaptation versus defensive withdrawal under labor discipline; algorithmacy captures directional communicative orientation and temporal tracking across an asymmetric triad. Discriminant validity requires that latent correlations between the two remain low to moderate, and convergence strong enough to collapse the boundary would indicate a failure of theoretical differentiation.

Third, Computer-Mediated Communication Competence (Spitzberg, 2006) supplies the evaluative criteria of appropriateness and effectiveness that algorithmacy adapts to environments lacking co-present evaluators. I predict that *interpreting* and *specifying intent* correlate moderately and positively with Spitzberg's measures. A correlation approaching zero would indicate that the constructs measure unrelated behavioral domains rather than shared communicative standards enacted under different structural constraints.

Fourth, Gig Literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020) presents a bifurcated pattern. Algorithmacy should correlate weakly or negatively with the *relationship-building* subdimension, which functions as a strategy for off-platform disintermediation rather than mediated coordination. It should correlate positively with the remaining four subdimensions (reputation management, self-presentation, productivity maintenance, and transaction risk mitigation), which reflect active navigation within platform infrastructure rather than evasion of it.

### **Constitutive Operations: The Tripartite Model**

Organization theory has a name for what the participant is short of, and it is not information. Daft and Lengel (1986) separate *uncertainty*, the absence of data, from *equivocality*, the presence of several incompatible readings of the data one already holds, and show that only the second calls for a rich, feedback-bearing channel, because the parties have to negotiate an interpretation rather than acquire a fact. Managers who match the two — rich channels for equivocal messages, lean channels for unequivocal ones — outperform those who do not (Daft et al., 1987). The participant at an opaque gate has ratings, outcomes, and a rejection history; what she lacks is any way to settle which reading of them is right, and media richness theory's own prescription is the choice this arrangement forecloses, since the channel is fixed and is also the judge. Dennis, Fuller, and Valacich (2008) name what goes missing as *convergence* — the process of reaching shared meaning about information both parties already hold — which their theory shows requires the rapid mutual feedback the triad withholds.

Each structural property of the triad imposes one informational deficit, and each deficit demands one operation. Opacity withholds the rule, so the actor must reconstruct it. Bindingness turns every message into evidence before a verdict, so the actor must encode for judge and counterpart at once. Adaptivity unsettles whatever the actor has learned, so the reconstruction has to be maintained rather than achieved once. Three properties, three deficits, three operations:

  - **Interpreting:** Reconstructing, from ambiguous and fragmented system feedback, the underlying actions, constraints, and intentions of both the algorithmic intermediary and the unseen human counterpart — was the failed verdict a peer's downvote, or a shifted threshold?
  - **Specifying intent:** Structuring and encoding communicative action so that intended meaning survives the narrow, standardized signaling channels the intermediary permits, and reaches the counterpart intact — a pull-request description written so the test suite parses it *and* the unseen peer understands why the change matters.
  - **Keeping track:** Detecting temporal shifts, continuous drift, and unannounced recalibrations in the rules that govern and bind both interactants — the commit style that cleared the gate in week four now draws review comments, and nothing was announced.

That mapping is what the phrase "derived from the structure of the triad" means in this paper, and it is the whole of what the phrase claims. Each operation answers one deficit the arrangement imposes. Whether the three exhaust the deficits — or whether evaluative attribution stands as a fourth — is what RQ4 tests, and the answer is not settled by the derivation.

A dyadic competence model restricted to the person–system relationship requires only an interpretive dimension directed at machine feedback, which prior frameworks of algorithmic sensemaking already capture. Triadic coordination requires coupling machine-facing decoding with counterpart-facing encoding and with temporal tracking across a rule that moves. Without a grounding loop to join them, inbound and outbound understanding stop being two halves of one process and become two unaided inferences (Clark & Brennan, 1991), which is why the model splits them rather than treating comprehension as a single competence.

Evaluative attribution — determining whether the platform infrastructure or the human client drove a score change, Rahman's (2021) fifth dimension of opacity — I house provisionally within *interpreting*. Judging whether an outcome stems from technical rules or human discretion is an interpretive task performed on the same composite feedback, not a separate faculty, and that placement is the null hypothesis the measurement model carries. If psychometric testing or field data reveal that actors decode system signals and counterpart intent successfully while displaying a distinct, uncoupled factor for resolving the locus of evaluative authority, attribution emerges as an independent fourth dimension.

The literacy tradition supplies the construct's nomenclature and the limits of the underlying metaphor. Long and Magerko (2020) trace the lineage across textual, digital, computational, and data literacies, and their operationalizations remain confined to person–system dyads. The analogy supplies historical grounding rather than operational content. Printed texts, datasets, and static software interfaces do not evaluate their users or execute binding determinations upon them, so the mechanics of reading or computing do not transfer to an active, evaluating third party. The operations derive instead from the deficits enumerated above, and the lineage marks the point of departure: *keeping track* has no equivalent in traditional literacy frameworks, because conventional media hold still.

### **Why Each Operation Requires the Full Triad**

Each operation earns its place by requiring the computational intermediary and the human counterpart at once, not the technical system alone. *Interpreting* satisfies that test directly. Because opacity precludes separate communication channels, an actor must reconstruct the intermediary's operating rules and the counterpart's latent intentions from the same ambiguous, composite feedback. The student at the gate, reading a failed verdict, cannot ask the gate what it wanted or ask her reviewers what they meant; she has one signal from which to infer two minds. Sundar and Nass (2000) put a version of that problem in a laboratory and found it consequential — the same message is evaluated differently depending on which source the user orients to — with the difference that their participants were told who was speaking and hers is undetermined from her side.

*Specifying intent* satisfies the test through dual-audience encoding constraints. In conventional computer-mediated communication, encoding means compressing meaning to fit a channel's bandwidth. Within an evaluative triad, the channel is simultaneously the authoritative evaluative instrument. A single communicative act — drafting the rationale for a technical contribution, submitting an administrative proposal — must satisfy the intermediary's parsing rules and the counterpart's interpretive expectations concurrently, without unmediated backchannels. The pilot supplies the rival outcome rather than the confirming case: one participant met the intent-specification module by routing her explanation through an external language model to optimize machine parsability, and never addressed the peer at all. Machine-legible, peer-illegible, is precisely the failure mode dual-audience encoding names, and Table B1 lists that pattern as the disconfirming criterion for this operation. The operation is defined by a demand that participants can and do decline.

*Keeping track* requires the triad through an evidentiary mechanism rather than a definitional one. In transparent or inspectable environments where participants can examine source code or explicit rubrics, rule updates generate observable signals and tracking converges with routine technical monitoring. Under opacity, unannounced rule changes produce no notifications. Rule shifts register instead through altered counterpart behavior, unexpected outcomes, or systematic variation in downstream peer ratings. An actor who monitors only platform telemetry misses this category of recalibration entirely, because the primary evidence of algorithmic change surfaces in what the counterpart does next. Duran (1983) named the nearest antecedent: communicative adaptability, the capacity to perceive that a situation has changed and to revise goals and behavior accordingly. His adaptable communicator reads a partner and a setting she can see. The triad's actor reads outcomes from a rule that announces nothing, which is why noticing is part of the operation rather than a precondition for it.

The three operations function as a recursively linked process, not an inventory of discrete traits. A coordination incident typically begins with *interpreting* an ambiguous outcome; that reading informs *specifying intent* in the actor's next communicative act, which must satisfy both technical parsing constraints and counterpart comprehension; *keeping track* then evaluates whether the rules or thresholds have shifted over the episode, generating updated priors that feed back into *interpreting* for the next one.

### **Categorical Delineation: Competency versus Skill and Capability**

Algorithmacy is an individual competency rather than an isolable skill or a firm-level capability, and it occupies a distinct ontological category within organizational theory.

  - **Distinction from Skill:** A skill is a procedural routine that can be codified, standardized, and transmitted through explicit instruction. The governing rules of opaque algorithmic systems remain proprietary, unobservable, and dynamic, which precludes the codification rule-based skill acquisition requires. That cut runs on trainability, and it is not the cut the competence literature draws for itself. Spitzberg (2015) orders the terms by level and attribution instead: skills are repeatable, goal-directed behaviors or behavioral sequences, while competence is the impression that those behaviors were appropriate and effective in context. The two orderings are compatible here, because a molar impression formed by an evaluator is exactly what the triad withholds, and I adopt his rather than substituting mine for it. Bunz and Montez (2015) supply the empirical precedent for the codification claim: surveying two decades of computer-mediated competence constructs and measures, they find a pile of overlapping instruments, none of which survived a change of platform.
  - **Distinction from Capability:** A capability resides in organizational routines and administrative assets a firm owns and deploys (Teece et al., 1997). In platform environments the firm exclusively owns and modifies the matching algorithms, rating systems, and governance parameters; the individual participant holds no ownership or control over the infrastructure.
  - **Phenomenographic Foundation:** Sandberg (2000), examining engine optimizers at the Volvo Car Corporation, demonstrated that competence reflects an actor's qualitative conception of work, not an inventory of independent attributes. He identified three interpretive framings: optimizing separate engine qualities, balancing interacting qualities, or managing the entire process from the customer's holistic perspective. In that final conception the customer is an interpretive orientation toward the task rather than an unseen counterpart reached through an opaque, binding intermediary. Sandberg's finding — that competence lives in the worker's qualitative framing of work — is what algorithmacy extends to mediated triadic coordination.

Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) argue, from the other direction, that interpersonal communication competence is not an isolated individual attribute but a relational quality jointly enacted and evaluated by interacting parties, and Spitzberg (2006) carries that commitment into mediated settings where interactants co-construct competence. Algorithmic coordination disrupts the relational model without eliminating the counterpart's judgment, since she continues to render appraisals visibly or invisibly. She never occupies a position from which mutual competence can be negotiated, because the aggregation converting her appraisal into a binding consequence is withheld from both parties. Where the conditions for relational co-construction are absent, competence reverts from a jointly enacted judgment to an individually held capacity — the interpretive conception of work Sandberg (2000) describes — with the counterpart's appraisal surviving only as an opaque input to the intermediary's verdict. Algorithmacy identifies the interpretive and expressive capacities an individual must supply under that reversion.

### **Foundational Properties: Experiential Acquisition and Uneven Distribution**

Two properties govern the construct's emergence and its empirical manifestation:

  - **Acquisition through Participation:** Because platform operators withhold the decision rules, formal didactic training cannot substitute for experiential immersion. Laupichler et al. (2023) developed the SNAIL scale to evaluate formal AI-literacy coursework, measuring the technical understanding, critical appraisal, and practical application that explicit curricula can teach. Algorithmacy predicts that classroom instruction alone cannot cultivate the capacity; fluency with opaque, adaptive systems requires iterative participation in which actors confront ambiguous feedback under actual evaluative stakes. Jablin and Sias (2001) describe the closest antecedent, and they describe it on this paper's home ground. In their account of organizational assimilation, newcomers acquire communicative competence through participation rather than instruction, inferring what counts as competent from how others respond to them. The minimal pair is the responder: their newcomer reads responses from the same colleagues who evaluate her, while the triad's participant reads an outcome from a rule and never the counterpart whose understanding she is trying to reach.
  - **Uneven Distribution:** Algorithmacy is predicted to be distributed heterogeneously across individuals whom the platform treats as formally equivalent. Whether it does vary — and whether that variance survives controls for platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks (Rahman, 2021) — is what Study 3 measures. Three further rival mechanisms enter alongside those labor-process controls: accumulated channel experience and the cognitive slack a mediated channel frees (Walther, 2007), mindless social scripting of the kind the computers-are-social-actors program documents (Nass & Moon, 2000), and endorsement of the machine heuristic (Sundar & Kim, 2019). Each predicts variance without any triadic capacity, and Study 3 measures all three (Appendix C). The construct predicts the variance; it does not assume it.

### **Theoretical Implications**

Formalizing algorithmacy reorients organizational research on algorithmic management from mechanisms of control to the mechanics of execution. Where labor process theorists conceptualize algorithmic workplaces as contested terrain centered on direction, evaluation, and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020), algorithmacy isolates the competencies required to coordinate through those constraints. The distinction clarifies where interventions land: regulatory mandates requiring algorithmic transparency modify the opacity of the information environment, whereas cultivating algorithmacy enhances an actor's capacity to interpret and act within that environment whatever its transparency.

This micro-foundation supplies the operational engine macro-structural models of platform organizing presuppose. Organizational theorists have detailed how platform designs exploit coordination gaps (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024) and generate consent through algorithmic enrollment (Cameron, 2024; Curchod et al., 2020), and those accounts concern the institutional forces securing compliance rather than the communicative capacities sustaining daily coordination. Specifying the recursive operations of interpreting, specifying intent, and keeping track explains how participants achieve working alignments inside asymmetric triads.

The construct stops at a deliberate boundary. Algorithmacy models the baseline capacity an actor must deploy to coordinate under opacity, adaptivity, and binding evaluation, and it stops short of theorizing formal standing, legal entitlements, normative legitimacy, or collective labor resistance. I isolate this competency from questions of legal classification, which establishes an empirical baseline for investigating how individual capability interacts with institutional governance across platform designs.

Online dispute resolution scholarship supplies the benchmark against which the deficits can be named, because it names the values an institutional intermediary owes its participants. Katsh and Rabinovich-Einy (2017) bring that literature up to platform scale, arguing that networked commerce generates disputes faster than any human process can absorb, so platforms have automated resolution and pushed the automation upstream into prevention. Their description of the result comes close to this paper's own definition: determinations issued at volume by a system, binding on parties who never meet, delivered without reasons and with no route to contest, by a neutral the platform owns. What they do not ask — and what a remedy of better institutional design does not require them to ask — is what a party must be able to do underneath such a system. Extending Katsh and Rifkin's (2001) "fourth party," Wing et al. (2021) point to Wing's (2016) Ethical Principles for Online Dispute Resolution — a field-endorsed framework spanning transparency, accountability, informed participation, and a dozen further values, offered explicitly as guidance rather than enforceable rules. Algorithmic coordination regimes withhold the closest of those values in turn: proprietary decision rules stay unobservable, forfeiting transparency (Rahman, 2021); automated determinations carry no accountable office, forfeiting accountability; and default enrollment substitutes for a participant's informed say in the process, forfeiting informed participation. Contestation is the sharpest absence of all: no principle in Wing's framework, nor anywhere in the wider fourth-party literature, names a participant's right to route a dispute to someone other than the platform itself, and algorithmic coordination regimes exploit exactly that silence, directing contestation to the platform's own apparatus rather than to the counterpart. Curchod et al. (2020) document the consequence, showing marketplace sellers with no appeal channel outside the platform's own rating apparatus, evaluated across a visibility gap by buyers they cannot observe or engage. Elish (2019) accounts for where accountability goes once an arrangement forfeits it. In heavily automated systems the human operator who retains nominal control absorbs the blame for failures properly attributable to the system's design, a pattern Elish calls the *moral crumple zone*, and the mechanism is structural rather than psychological: responsibility attaches to whoever stands visibly nearest the failure, and automation puts a person there while removing her capacity to prevent it. The three operations are the individual-level capacities participants must mobilize because those protections are absent.

### **A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves**

The paradox is this: individual mastery of the arrangement stabilizes the arrangement that burdens individuals. A participant's algorithmacy is a behavioral capacity to operate within a regime rather than an endorsement of its procedural or distributive fairness. Because capacity and fairness vary independently, higher competency does not signal greater welfare or institutional legitimacy. Elish's (2019) hazard applies to the construct and not only to the arrangement it describes: a competency framing that reads variance in outcomes off variance in worker capacity is itself, structurally, a crumple-zone move, and a construct of this kind must not become the attributional pad by which a system's opacity is re-billed as an individual's deficit. Holding capacity and fairness apart is what keeps it from doing that work. That decoupling yields an aggregation mechanism linking micro-level behavior to macro-structural stability. Cameron (2024) demonstrates how on-demand workers navigate continuous, confined choices in ways that manufacture consent, and her model leaves unspecified the individual capacity separating successful navigation from failure. Formalizing algorithmacy translates that insight into a workforce-level distributional mechanism: an enrolled population's collective competency, not isolated individual compliance, determines whether operational frictions trigger visible breakdown or get quietly absorbed by participants. The dynamic fulfills the aggregation requirement expected of micro-foundational organizational theory (Felin et al., 2015).

The mechanism is falsifiable at the collective level. Cohorts exposed to identical task designs, matching gates, and evaluation rubrics but differing in mean algorithmacy should exhibit systematic differences in observable friction, such as voluntary attrition, formal contestation volume, and coordination failures. Identical friction rates across cohorts with divergent mean algorithmacy would falsify the aggregation claim, even if individual competency continued to predict actor-level performance.

Three propositions follow from this tension, and each clarifies why algorithmacy is not an unalloyed individual or organizational benefit:

**Proposition 1.** *Higher algorithmacy improves an individual participant's coordination outcomes while leaving the arrangement's procedural justice unchanged; performance gains concentrate in private navigation rather than in institutional disclosures won, formal appeals granted, or rules rendered legible to peers.*

This synthesizes the micro- and macro-level tensions identified in prior platform research: Rahman (2021) documents how individual reactivity can reinforce subordination, while Sutherland et al. (2020) show informal literacies shifting coordination burdens onto labor. Proposition 1 extends those findings to a formalized competency, where individual mastery compensates for structural deficits without altering the regime's opacity.

**Proposition 2.** *Because algorithmacy is distributed unevenly across participants whom the platform treats as formally equivalent, that variance introduces a secondary axis of inequality among actors whom the organizational form designates as interchangeable.*

The proposition transposes the logic of second-level digital divides (Hargittai, 2002) from general digital literacy to triadic algorithmic coordination. Within formally undifferentiated labor pools, latent differences in algorithmacy generate persistent earnings, safety, and evaluation disparities that platforms attribute to individual merit rather than informational asymmetry.

**Proposition 3.** *Increasing an intermediary's transparency reduces the level of algorithmacy required to coordinate through it, establishing the construct's scope conditions as a malleable institutional lever rather than a fixed analytical perimeter.*

Regulatory mandates enforcing algorithmic transparency or requiring explainable decision logic lower the cognitive and communicative burden imposed on participants. Institutional disclosure policy therefore offers an alternative governance pathway, mitigating coordination friction by redesigning the information environment rather than requiring individuals to develop compensatory competencies.

Taken with the workforce-level stabilization mechanism, these propositions are why algorithmacy is an empirical capacity to be measured. The construct formalizes how participants survive and coordinate inside opaque, binding arrangements, which gives both individual performance and the institutional persistence of algorithmic management a common diagnostic foundation.

### **Dissertation Coherence**

The three papers of this dissertation construct a single cumulative argument, not three independent investigations of a shared domain. Paper 1 establishes the foundation by identifying an unformalized governance arrangement: organizational theory has not theorized coordinative co-optation alongside hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation, leaving unmodeled the behavioral mechanism through which an enrolled actor coordinates within its boundaries. Paper 2 supplies that micro-foundation by developing algorithmacy as the individual capacity required to navigate an opaque, adaptive, mutually binding intermediary. Paper 3 tests the architecture empirically, examining the construct's distribution across formally equivalent participants, its effects on coordination outcomes and collective friction, and its discriminant validity against neighboring measurement traditions. Each paper performs a function the other two presuppose.

## **Empirical Strategy**

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), I separate the core action research project from the formal research project conducted upon it. The core initiative is a longitudinal intervention spanning multiple iterations across distinct institutional settings, in which cyclical evaluations informed pedagogical, gate-mechanism, and instrument redesigns. The study presented in Paper 3 is insider research on that program's mature configuration (Brannick & Coghlan, 2007). In that phase I will execute no further administrative interventions within the focal cohorts, and the data collection protocols will keep pedagogical facilitation separate from participants' reported accounts. Appendix B sets out the full design, the instrument architecture, and the falsification criteria summarized here.

I treat this setting as a strategic research site (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Merton, 1987) for investigating the micro-foundations of triadic coordination. Data collection and analysis follow an abductive protocol (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) built to reconcile observed participant behavior with theoretical categories. The subsections below establish the alignment between the site and the scope conditions of coordinative co-optation, set out the research questions and their instruments, and state the program's current status.

### **Why Hult Instantiates Coordinative Co-optation, Not a Hybrid Form**

A critic might read the site as an ordinary hybrid of pedagogical hierarchy, peer review, and automated grading: traditional bureaucratic authority behind a digital interface. The gate's own properties refuse that reading. The computational gate renders binding determinations on each submission by aggregating peer appraisals, automated test suites, and peer upvotes into a verdict governed by concealed weighting parameters. The outcome is neither a grade assigned through instructor discretion nor a transparent tally of raw votes, and the course instructor holds no vote and no manual override. Neither party occupies the role of a hierarchical superior administering a legible rule (Weber, 1978).

Three properties of the gate carry the instantiation claim, and each is a matter of fact about the system, not an inference from it.

The gate binds both parties. The submitter's progression depends on an aggregated score she cannot inspect, and the verdict also binds the evaluator: her review accuracy and quality feed her own gate score, so a determination she cannot see prices work she performed on both sides of the exchange. Mutual bindingness — the property that separates algorithmacy from all eight constructs in Table 1 — holds at the site rather than being stipulated for it.

The platform assigns reviewers. The algorithm allocates peer reviewers to submissions; participants do not self-select into evaluation queues. Coordinative co-optation is defined by an algorithm that matches unchosen parties (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), and that condition is met here in the same sense it is met on a dispatch platform.

The rule itself moves. Weights and thresholds are retuned over the course of the term, so what the gate rewards in week twelve is not what it rewarded in week two. Beneath that, the codebase against which submissions are evaluated also evolves as participants merge contributions. The two layers differ in kind and in visibility: merge-driven drift is publicly legible in the repository history, which is why the coding protocol for *keeping track* excludes it — detecting a change the repository history announces is technical monitoring, and Table B1 states exactly that as the disconfirming criterion. Adaptivity at this site rests on the rule shift, not the substrate churn.

One parameter of the gate remains undocumented, and I record it in the protocol before fielding: what exit and escalation rights a contested verdict carries. A student's option to drop a course is not the analogue of unilateral account termination, and until the contestation pathway is catalogued the site's correspondence to the form on that dimension is unestablished.

### **Research Questions and Instruments**

I organize the investigation around four research questions, each mapped to a constitutive operation and the boundary condition it tests:

  - **RQ1 (Interpreting):** How do participants decode, reconstruct, and make sense of unexplained or contradictory evaluative outcomes generated by the opaque gate?
  - **RQ2 (Specifying Intent):** How do participants conceptualize and communicate with their peer counterparts — specifically, whether they address the peer as an active, interdependent party or direct communicative action exclusively toward machine legibility?
  - **RQ3 (Keeping Track):** How do participants identify, monitor, and adapt to unannounced shifts and continuous drift in the benchmarks the gate enforces?
  - **RQ4 (Evaluative Attribution):** How do participants attribute perceived evaluative errors across the mediated triad — to algorithmic weighting, to peer reviewer judgment, or to their interaction — and does resolving the locus of evaluative authority function as an independent fourth operation?

RQ4 tests whether evaluative attribution, provisionally housed within *interpreting*, separates empirically from both *interpreting* and *keeping track*. The two comparisons ask different things. Diagnosing longitudinal drift in a rule is a distinct behavioral phenomenon from assigning causal responsibility for one evaluative breakdown: an actor may detect that a standard has moved without diagnosing the locus of failure for a single outcome, and may assign fault for an isolated error without recognizing broader drift. Whether either separation holds is an empirical question the measurement model leaves open.

I built the semi-structured critical incident interview protocol around four corresponding diagnostic modules. Each isolates participant sensemaking, dual-audience encoding, longitudinal tracking, and causal attribution through detailed reconstructions of specific coordination incidents encountered in the platform environment.

### **Instruments and Status**

The multi-method design couples an AI-administered critical incident interview protocol embedded in the development environment with a three-wave longitudinal survey panel administered at weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the semester. Tracking developmental trajectories requires linking individual responses across all three waves without compromising confidentiality, so each participant generates a self-derived alphanumeric identifier at intake and re-enters it across successive waves. The panel is researcher-blind by procedure: no name, token key, or intake record reaches me. With a cohort of roughly fifty I cannot rule out incidental recognition from the details of an incident, and the coding protocol strips identifying particulars for that reason.

The status, plainly. Bentley University's Institutional Review Board determined the study exempt (Protocol 260511078). The formal cohort, roughly fifty students in a sixteen-week Hult course, fields in spring 2027. Two pilot transcripts exist: I read the first informally to check that the modules function, and the second is unread. None of the formal corpus has been collected or coded. The single-cohort panel tracks within-person trajectories and is not powered for factor analysis, which Study 2 takes up (Appendix C). Study 3 carries the rival mechanisms the hearings above named — platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks, accumulated channel experience, mindless social scripting, and machine-heuristic endorsement — as measured covariates rather than as acknowledgments (Appendix C).

The first pilot administration produced an outcome that cuts against the construct rather than for it. Navigating the intent-specification module, the participant routed her explanation through an external language model to optimize machine parsability, and never addressed the peer. Table B1 lists exactly that pattern — consistent evaluative success through isolated technical optimization, without dual-audience encoding — as the disconfirming criterion for *specifying intent*. One incident settles nothing. It does obligate the protocol to treat machine-only routing as a live rival outcome rather than a variant of dual-audience encoding, and I added probes that distinguish the two before the second administration. Formal data collection across the full cohort will test the construct's psychometric structure and process dynamics against that rival.

## **Conclusion and Theoretical Boundaries**

I formulate the theoretical architecture, boundaries, and constitutive operations of algorithmacy here; empirical validation is Paper 3's task. Because the multi-wave field investigation remains prospective, the contribution is an analytical model, a set of formal construct definitions, and a measurement apparatus refined through piloting. Whether the three operations load onto a coherent higher-order factor, hold empirical independence, or separate cleanly from general software literacy and traditional communicative competence are open questions that psychometric testing must resolve (Appendix C). That transition raises a measurement consideration: following Spitzberg and Cupach (1984), competence operates across molar and molecular levels, so operationalization must show that standardized scales preserve the holistic interpretive conception of work (Sandberg, 2000) rather than atomizing it into decontextualized traits, and that a measure pitched at one level licenses no automatic inference about the other.

Neighboring frameworks offer competing explanations that validation must evaluate. Empirical testing must determine whether existing measures of algorithmic sensemaking (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025) capture the variance observed in triadic environments, or whether Sutherland et al.'s (2020) relational gig literacy accounts for worker coordination on its own. Current relational literacy frameworks theorize peer engagement conducted alongside algorithmic systems; algorithmacy models coordination executed through an opaque, evaluative one. Adjacent scholarship continues to accumulate — algorithmic skills (Hong et al., 2026), platform literacies (Scolari et al., 2026), human-AI collaboration dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), metacognitive algorithmic competence (Ayasrah et al., 2026) — and these models remain confined to person-system dyads, treating human counterparts as external peers, offline collaborators, or passive recipients of automated output. The claim advanced here concerns a structural position within an asymmetric, evaluative triad; it is not a taxonomic refinement.

Boundary cases across adjacent disciplines mark the scope conditions:

  - **Clinical Medicine:** Recent models of AI-mediated medical education incorporate the patient alongside diagnostic algorithms ("AI-Mediated Relational Competence in Medical Education," 2026, an unrefereed preprint), and ultimate clinical and legal authority resides with the human physician rather than an autonomous intermediary.
  - **Public Child Welfare:** Models of AI competence in social work address client trust and algorithmic risk scoring (Yang & Liechty, 2026), and frame the algorithmic system as an advisory decision-support tool rather than a binding administrative authority.
  - **Algorithmic Matchmaking:** Interpersonal frameworks in digital dating examine relationship-formation competencies while omitting algorithmic curation (Dredge & Anderson, 2021), whereas computational accounts analyze algorithmic awareness while omitting relational counterpart dynamics (Hu & Zhan, 2024).

These comparisons bound the construct within the coordination of interdependent work, as the Introduction's claim requires, and exclude advisory decision aids, dyadic software tools, and unmediated peer collaboration. The restriction is not a late patch on the definition; it is the second half of the two-part claim stated at the outset — mutual bindingness *and* joint execution — and the boundary cases show what each half excludes on its own.

Algorithmacy isolates the capacity an individual needs inside the triad: to interpret it, to speak through it, and to track it as it moves. Naming that capacity supplies the micro-foundational engine macro-structural models of platform governance presuppose, and it gives researchers a way to ask how individual competence aggregates to stabilize or disrupt an emerging form of organizational control.

## **Appendix A: Comparative Framework of Coordination Forms**

This analytical foundation comes from the first essay of the dissertation program, which evaluated five archetypal coordination forms against a standardized analytical frame to assess the completeness of extant organizational scholarship. Extending the comparative traditions established by Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980), Paper 1 evaluates each coordination form across six core questions: governing mechanism and participant standing, requisite integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, operational instruments, failure modes, and empirical markers. A coordination mechanism is the primary administrative operation securing alignment, paired with the formal standing of the actor that operation reaches. The integrative deliverables map to the three conditions synthesized by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009): predictability, common understanding, and accountability.

The matrix translates classical theory into a contemporary coordination vocabulary. Hayek (1945), for example, demonstrates that the price mechanism bears the primary coordinative burden because it functions as a condensed public signal — Hayek's own figure is a system of telecommunications — that no participant needs to reconstruct privately. In modern coordination terms, that public signal delivers predictability and common understanding at once, while market accountability sits in contractual enforcement and reputational sanction. The four established governance traditions are theoretically complete. Simon (1997) and Weber (1978) together locate the individual in hierarchy through an administrative zone of acceptance; Hayek (1945) defines the market actor through the evaluation of posted prices backed by unconstrained exit rights; Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985) anchor the network participant within elective, revocable partnerships; and Selznick (1949) grounds classical co-optation in conferred positions that transform external opposition into structural participation. Each archetype pairs a governing operation with the formal standing of the actor it coordinates.

The fifth column breaks the pairing. As Stark and Pais (2020) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) observe, digital platforms do not coordinate through command, price discovery, or relational reciprocity; they enroll participants through matching algorithms that pair unchosen parties, monitor performance metrics, and enforce unilateral sanctions. Platform scholarship documents algorithmic matching and surveillance (Möhlmann et al., 2021), administrative direction and disciplinary control (Kellogg et al., 2020), information asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), and non-portable reputational capital (Curchod et al., 2020), and leaves the standing and individual capacity of the enrolled actor untheorized. The participant operates neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price signals, nor a partner in a mutual network alliance. The actor is enrolled, and organizational theory has not yet articulated the competency required to coordinate through the arrangement that enrolls her.

## **Appendix B: Action Research Design, Instrument Architecture, and Empirical Protocol**

**Research Questions and Analytical Mapping**

The empirical program investigates how individuals acquire, develop, and mobilize algorithmacy within opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding coordination regimes. Four research questions organize it, stated in full in the Empirical Strategy section above: RQ1 on *interpreting*, RQ2 on *specifying intent*, RQ3 on *keeping track*, and RQ4 on evaluative attribution.

The four map onto the construct directly. Decoding unexplained outcomes operationalizes *interpreting*; conceptualizing an unseen counterpart captures the dual-audience encoding *specifying intent* demands; detecting unannounced rule shifts captures *keeping track*; and attributing perceived errors tests whether evaluative attribution — provisionally housed within *interpreting* — separates from that operation and, secondarily, from longitudinal tracking. Following Pratt (2009), qualitative rigor depends on alignment between method and the underlying theoretical puzzle rather than adherence to generic templates. I use insider action research (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014), and I treat my dual role as course designer and researcher as an epistemic asset that provides deep institutional access and context-sensitive instrument design.

**The Action Research Program and Institutional Trajectory**

Insider action research proceeds through iterative spirals of four phases — constructing, planning action, taking action, and evaluating action — with each cycle's evaluation informing the next construction (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). The program spans five institutional configurations across two years:

1.  **Secondary School Cohorts (July–August 2025):** The initiative launched in Trinidad and Tobago across two secondary educational institutions.
2.  **Community Youth Cohorts (Autumn–Winter 2025/2026):** Operating as GauntleTT, the program expanded across the constituency of La Brea in partnership with the Member of Parliament, the Ministry of Digital Transformation, local non-governmental organizations, and the community policing division administering the Hearts and Minds youth intervention initiative.
3.  **Public Technical Sprint Cohorts (April–May 2026):** An open-enrollment public iteration operated in Boston, Massachusetts, testing web-based development pipelines.
4.  **Preliminary Business School Cohorts (July–August 2026):** The curriculum transitioned to Hult International Business School, piloting credit-bearing assessment workflows.
5.  **Formal Academic Course (Spring 2027):** A formal, sixteen-week, credit-bearing engineering course at Hult serves as the primary data-collection site for Paper 3.

Within the mature setting, participants engineer production-grade software and submit deliverables as peer-reviewed pull requests. Across each institutional transition, cyclical evaluations drove substantive modifications: site variations altered peer-evaluation networks, gate refinements varied information visibility, and demographic shifts isolated behavioral variance from confounding human-capital baselines.

**The Trinidad Field Setting as a Strategic Research Site**

The early Caribbean cohorts functioned as a strategic research site in Merton's (1987) sense, providing a setting where the focal phenomenon manifested with unusual analytical tractability. As Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) argue, single-case selections are justified when a setting offers revelatory access to an unexamined process. Conventional platform-work research typically examines labor forces characterized by extensive platform tenure and pre-existing human capital differences (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025), where participant sensemaking is heavily confounded by prior domain experience.

Platform labor across the Anglophone Caribbean, by contrast, remains concentrated in traditional logistics, ride-hailing, and hospitality (ILO, 2025), with empirical scholarship on algorithmic coordination in the region remaining sparse (Chigbu, 2026). Studying non-traditional research contexts prevents the misapplication of mature institutional assumptions and allows emergent behavioral phenomena to surface in unobstructed form (Bamberger & Pratt, 2010; Bothello et al., 2019). Because participants in the Trinidad cohorts encountered artificial intelligence coordination interfaces simultaneously and without prior platform conditioning, divergences in how individuals decoded identical gate outcomes could not be attributed to tenure or pre-sorted skill tiers.

Across the cohort iterations, automated evaluation gates withheld information across three operational layers:

  - **Boston Iteration:** Large language models screened initial submissions prior to peer voting, displaying only an aggregated final score to the participant.
  - **Hult International Business School Iteration:** Qualitative peer reviews and upvotes remained publicly inspectable while the underlying aggregate weighting remained concealed.
  - **Trinidad Iteration:** Automated operational logs were not retained, which forced retrospective historical protocols to reconstruct gate mechanics.

Three sites I designed cannot demonstrate that opacity is intrinsic to algorithmic coordination. What the variation supports is narrower and sufficient: the construct's operations were elicited under three different concealment regimes rather than one, so the behaviors recorded do not depend on any single interface.

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), the core action research cycle — the ongoing pedagogical initiative — remains distinct from the thesis research cycle, the formal study presented in Paper 3. The preliminary cohorts provided the ground for developing the construct, calibrating instruments, and refining the protocol; they are the developmental foundation rather than the formal empirical corpus.

**Research Site Architecture and Researcher Positionality**

The formal investigation takes place within a sixteen-week credit-bearing course at Hult International Business School in spring 2027. Students submit technical code to an automated gate that returns determinations without procedural rationales. The gate embodies the scope conditions of coordinative co-optation: the automated system is the opaque intermediary, its unexplained verdict is the opacity participants face, the algorithm assigns reviewers to submissions, the verdict binds submitter and reviewer alike, and the weights and thresholds are retuned as the term proceeds.

The setting is a conservative "hard case" for opacity. Because student participants construct and inspect the codebase itself, the opacity does not come from hidden source code; it comes from concealed aggregation weights and shifting decision thresholds governing peer evaluation. The formal protocol catalogs the institutional escalation rights attached to a contested verdict, the one gate parameter not yet documented.

I manage the dual role through structural safeguards rather than methodological disavowal (Riordan, 1995, as cited in Coghlan & Brannick, 2014; Anteby, 2013). I restrict my instructional role to macro-level course design, and the gate operates autonomously, without instructor voting or manual grading overrides. To remove power asymmetries and instructional demand characteristics (Ferguson et al., 2004; Mercer, 2007), the intake client anonymizes participants before any record reaches me, so I cannot identify who took part in the qualitative protocol. Interviews occur only after evaluative project cycles have closed. I log every protocol version, coding memo, and dated analytic decision in an audit trail (Pratt et al., 2020; Tracy, 2010).

**The In-Editor Interview Protocol and Abductive Coding Framework**

The qualitative arm employs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol administered inside the participant's integrated development environment. An embedded conversational agent guides respondents through four diagnostic modules:

1.  **Decoding Gate Criteria:** Reconstructing the latent rules and peer evaluations behind an unexplained outcome.
2.  **Transmitting Communicative Intent:** Encoding code comments and pull-request documentation to satisfy both computational test suites and human peer reviewers.
3.  **Identifying Unannounced Change:** Detecting shifts in evaluative benchmarks over time without formal platform announcements.
4.  **Attributing Evaluative Errors:** Allocating causal responsibility for perceived evaluative failures across the intermediary, peer reviewers, or their interaction.

Each module applies the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954), prompting participants to detail a complete behavioral episode — operationalized here as the baseline objective, the system determination, the tactical adaptation, and the resolution. If a respondent offers a high-level generalization, the agent prompts for a specific incident; if an initial response is negative, the agent reframes from an alternative angle before closing the module. The protocol omits theoretical terminology to elicit native categories. The agent executes real-time, client-side redaction of personal names, institutions, and geographic identifiers before the participant reviews, edits, and validates the final transcript for transmission.

Analysis follows an abductive logic of inquiry (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) through three iterative moves:

  - **Revisiting:** Returning repeatedly to inscribed transcripts across multiple passes to examine how emergent categories reframe earlier observations.
  - **Defamiliarization:** Treating automated transcription as a technique of analytical estrangement, subjecting self-evident participant explanations to theoretical scrutiny.
  - **Alternative Casing:** Evaluating raw transcripts against competing frameworks (individual skill acquisition, relational gig literacies, dyadic sensemaking) to establish whether the data demand the triadic construct.

The three operations function as sensitizing concepts (Blumer, 1954; Bowen, 2006). Initial coding adheres to first-order, participant-centric terminology (Gioia et al., 2013) before second-order categories are applied. A disconfirming negative case is specified in advance: a participant who consistently resolves gate breakdowns through individual technical mastery alone, without conceptualizing or communicating with the human peer counterpart, is evidence falsifying the necessity of the triadic construct.

**Longitudinal Survey Panel Architecture**

The quantitative arm comprises a three-wave longitudinal survey panel administered at weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the semester. To reconcile within-person tracking with researcher blindness, participants generate a self-derived confidential token at baseline, derived from invariant personal elements, and re-enter it across successive waves (Yurek et al., 2008). The protocol keeps the panel researcher-blind by procedure: no name, token key, or intake record reaches me. With a cohort of roughly fifty and incident narratives rich in particulars, I cannot rule out incidental recognition, and the coding protocol strips identifying details for that reason.

Given a cohort of approximately fifty students, the panel is not powered to perform exploratory or confirmatory factor analyses. The survey serves two purposes instead: tracking within-person developmental trajectories across the semester, and administering Zhou et al.'s (2025) scale at wave 2 as a descriptive convergence check with *interpreting* and *specifying intent*. The same wave carries a short measure of machine-heuristic endorsement, named above as a discriminant threat, which Study 2 tests at scale. I reserve large-sample discriminant testing for Study 2. Bentley University's Institutional Review Board determined the study exempt (Protocol 260511078).

**Preliminary Instrument Piloting Results**

Two pilot administrations of the in-editor protocol exist. I read the first informally to check that the modules function; the second transcript is unread. From the first, three instrument findings follow:

1.  **Elicitation of Concrete Critical Incidents:** The protocol elicited a complete critical incident detailing an adverse gate determination, the participant's retrospective sensemaking, and the perceived absence of contestability channels.
2.  **Identification of Secondary Intermediary Routing:** The participant satisfied the intent-specification module by routing her explanation through an external language model to optimize automated parsability, and never engaged the peer counterpart. Table B1 lists that pattern as the disconfirming criterion for *specifying intent*, so I treat it as a live rival outcome rather than a variant of dual-audience encoding, and I added protocol probes that distinguish direct peer communication from multi-agent intermediary routing.
3.  **Automated Incident Forcing:** I refined the protocol to force incident anchors in place of generalized summaries, and added automated reframing prompts in the tracking and error-attribution modules.

## **Appendix C: Programmatic Research Agenda**

Four sequential studies translate algorithmacy from a conceptual framework into an empirically validated construct, with explicit methodological thresholds and falsification criteria at each analytical level.

**Study 1: Multi-Method Field Validation and Dimensional Boundary Resolution**

The first study executes the multi-method investigation within the primary field setting, coupling the in-editor critical incident protocol with the three-wave panel. Qualitative analysis proceeds not to a grounded-theory saturation metric but to an abductive sufficiency criterion — the threshold at which incremental incidents neither present casings the three operations cannot accommodate *nor* fit a rival framing (skill acquisition, gig literacy, dyadic sensemaking) better than the triadic construct. Fit alone does not stop the analysis; the alternative-casing pass must also come back empty.

This corpus resolves the candidate fourth operation, *evaluative attribution*. Rahman's (2021) "who-influences" dimension will be formalized as an autonomous fourth operation if participant accounts systematically reveal actors who decode observable feedback successfully yet cannot discern whether the platform intermediary or peer evaluators determined the outcome. If participants instead resolve causal responsibility as part of *interpreting* — the operation the construct provisionally houses it within — the tripartite model stands as specified.

**Study 2: Scale Development, Large-Sample Psychometric Testing, and Discriminant Validation**

Study 2 begins by building the instrument the confirmatory model requires. I convert the Study 1 incident corpus into a candidate item pool, one item per recurrent first-order code, following Zhou et al.'s (2025) generation protocol; submit the pool to expert content validation; and fit an exploratory factor analysis on the first pooled cohorts before estimating any confirmatory model. One behavioral alternative to self-report belongs in the item-generation stage. Mieczkowski et al. (2021) run a referential communication task — the paradigm in which two partners must converge on a shared description of an ambiguous figure — through a system offering smart replies, and the task registers directly whether meaning arrived rather than whether a respondent believes it did. The paradigm assumes co-presence and a responding partner, so it measures anything in the triad only after modification that withholds the counterpart's replies.

Pooling then carries a condition the site itself imposes. Because the gate's evaluation rule genuinely retunes over time, pooling cohorts requires either freezing the gate configuration across all pooled cohorts or modeling cohort as a grouping variable and testing configural and metric invariance before pooling. Failure of metric invariance halts pooling, and the cohorts are modeled as separate groups.

With those conditions met, the study establishes large-sample validation by pooling administrations across successive cohorts to a sample of 200 to 250 respondents, matching the methodological baseline Zhou et al. (2025) set. That sample powers a confirmatory factor analysis and evaluates pre-registered latent correlation hypotheses between algorithmacy dimensions and established algorithmic competency subscales:

  - *Interpreting* paired with *Understanding*
  - *Specifying Intent* paired with *Leveraging*
  - An unassigned null baseline paired with *Embracing*

Latent inter-construct correlations in the .30 to .60 range demonstrate convergent yet distinct validity. I evaluate discriminant validity with the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT; Henseler et al., 2015). An HTMT value exceeding the conservative .85 threshold between any proposed operation and a neighboring subscale falsifies the claim of construct distinctiveness on that dimension.

**Study 3: Distributional Inequality and System Stabilization**

The third study tests Proposition 2 and the workforce-level aggregation mechanism across a population of formally equivalent participants. Sample and setting remain to be specified; the constructs and controls are fixed. Measuring baseline algorithmacy alongside technical human capital, task complexity, platform dependence, and prior evaluation shocks — and alongside the three rival mechanisms the communication hearings named: accumulated channel experience and the cognitive slack it frees (Walther, 2007), mindless social scripting (Nass & Moon, 2000), and machine-heuristic endorsement (Sundar & Kim, 2019) — the study examines whether individual variance in algorithmacy accounts for performance differentials among structurally undifferentiated actors once the labor-process and communication-theoretic rivals are controlled. Naming those covariates is what makes the new hearings' inheritance claims cash out as design commitments rather than concessions.

The design also tracks low-scoring cohorts longitudinally to evaluate how the arrangement absorbs coordination friction. Measuring rates of voluntary attrition, formal dispute volume, and the psychological internalization of coordination failures quantifies the operational and human costs an organization incurs when this competency is scarce.

**Study 4: Cross-Sector Generalizability and Transparency Boundary Conditions**

The fourth study evaluates external generalizability by deploying the instruments across naturalistic, non-builder platform environments where the investigative team holds no pedagogical or administrative role. Target settings include on-demand transportation fleets, distributed micro-task queues, and digital freelance marketplaces where algorithmic matching operates without worker agency over system design.

Observing the operational separation of the three dimensions within commercial environments would confirm that algorithmacy is a generalized coordination capacity rather than an artifact of software engineering settings. Comparative field sites exhibiting natural variation in algorithmic disclosure also provide a direct test of Proposition 3, evaluating whether transparency mandates lower the baseline algorithmacy required to sustain coordination.
