# Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation

**Roger B. Hunt III** · Bentley University · rhunt@bentley.edu

*Dissertation Paper 2. Full paper for the Organization Studies / Organization Theory Paper Development Workshop, Universidad de Piura UDEP, Lima, 5–7 October 2026.*

---

# Abstract

Algorithmacy is the competency a person exercises when she coordinates with another person through an algorithm neither of them can see into — an intermediary that evaluates them both and whose verdicts bind them both. Paper 1 of this dissertation showed that this arrangement — coordinative co-optation — remains the least theorized of organization theory's coordination forms, leaving open what standing a participant holds within it: whether she is owed anything like an employee's protections, a buyer's exit right, or a partner's voice. This paper does not resolve that question; it supplies the individual-level micro-foundation a participant needs to act within whatever standing the form eventually settles. We review seven extant competence constructs and show that none simultaneously accounts for an active human counterpart and an opaque, independently consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties: constructs that center the algorithm bracket the counterpart, and constructs that center the counterpart bracket the algorithm. We then formalize algorithmacy as three operations — interpreting, specifying intent, and keeping track — each argued from what the triad specifically withholds, and note a paradox the construct raises rather than resolves: algorithmacy may improve a participant's own outcomes while doing nothing to make the arrangement itself more just, and unevenly distributing it may stabilize coordination that would otherwise register as unfair. We close with a concise empirical strategy — an insider action-research design running an in-editor "harness interview" alongside a three-wave survey panel, revised against an initial developmental transcript and formally fielded as Paper 3 in a specialized software-engineering course at Hult International Business School.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication competency · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# Introduction

Coordination — the collective execution of interdependent work — remains a foundational concern of organization theory. Classic scholarship maps three canonical coordination forms: hierarchy, market, and network, governed respectively by authority, price, and ongoing social relationships. Yet this typology omits an emergent configuration in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) have named the competency a platform worker exercises against the system itself; organization theory has not named the competency she must exercise when a human counterpart remains in the picture, reachable only through that system — whatever her formal standing within the arrangement — and we develop that account here.

This article advances that agenda through three interconnected steps. First, we ground our inquiry in Paper 1 of this dissertation, which evaluated five coordination forms — hierarchy, market, network, classical co-optation (Selznick, 1949), and coordinative co-optation (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024) — across six analytical dimensions: operational mechanism, required deliverables, favoring conditions, primary instruments, failure modes, and empirical identification. That comparative analysis revealed coordinative co-optation as the least theorized form, leaving the question of participant standing open — a structural question this paper does not adjudicate. Second, we examine seven candidate competence constructs and show that none combines an active human counterpart with an opaque, independently consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. To resolve this gap, we formalize algorithmacy to a satisficing standard in Simon's (1997) sense — the construct names the individual capacity Paper 1's open cell requires and stops there — incorporating the counterpart into an algorithmically bound triad. Third, we present a concise empirical strategy — an insider action-research design piloted through developmental cohorts and formally fielded as Paper 3 in a specialized software-engineering course.

Surveying the theoretical landscape reveals candidate constructs that do not, as a set, share a single failure mode. Some include only the system: Long and Magerko (2020) treat the algorithm as an object of technical knowledge with no interlocutor in the frame. Others personify the system into a full interlocutor and lose the human counterpart entirely — Guzman and Lewis (2020) position the machine itself as the communicative partner. Still others include a system and two people at once: Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) frame the computational artifact as a delegate operating on a sender's behalf while both interactants remain in scope, and Zhou et al. (2025) measure how workers navigate the platform while a human evaluator appears as a rated outcome. The shared limit across all of them is narrower and more exact than "each is a person–system dyad": no construct combines an active human counterpart with an opaque, independently consequential intermediary that itself evaluates and binds both parties. Where a counterpart appears, the intermediary is a channel, a delegate, or infrastructure to route around — never an autonomous party rendering a binding verdict. Where the intermediary is opaque and binding, the counterpart is absent, external, or reduced to an outcome the system measures. Organization theory's own dispute-resolution literature named a version of this triad two decades ago — Katsh and Rifkin's (2001) "fourth party" — but from the system's side, not the participant's; What These Boundaries Share, below, returns to that literature directly.

The arrangement is easiest to see in a case this paper later studies: a student submits a pull request to a course's evaluation gate; her peers' reviews and upvotes are public, but the weighting that converts them into a verdict is concealed from everyone — submitter, reviewers, even the instructor, who holds no vote. She is coordinating with people she can see, through a rule she cannot.

We model algorithmacy on the foundational pattern of literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Just as literacy enables participation in communication mediated by text, numeracy equips individuals to navigate situations structured by quantitative data, and oracy governs interaction through spoken discourse, algorithmacy enables sustained participation in coordination structured by an algorithm. We define algorithmacy as the capacity to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, specify intent through it, and track its shifting parameters, allowing an actor to coordinate when a system reads both transacting parties and commits decisions neither controls. In this formulation, opacity means the participant cannot directly observe the governing rule; adaptivity means the rule can shift dynamically; and intermediary refers to the algorithmic system mediating between the actor and her counterpart. Whereas literacy, numeracy, and oracy constitute competencies for a medium, algorithmacy operates as a competency for a coordination form — fulfilling the theoretical requirement identified in Paper 1. We then examine this competency in development through an in-editor "harness interview," wherein participants complete a technical task, encounter an incompletely explained outcome, and articulate how they interpreted the decision and formulated their subsequent actions. Drawing on preliminary piloting, we are iteratively refining this protocol within a specialized software-engineering course at Hult International Business School, where the formally controlled study — Paper 3 — will be fielded.

# The Coordination-Form Gap: Paper 1 in Brief

Paper 1 of this dissertation evaluated five coordination forms — hierarchy, market, network, classical co-optation, and coordinative co-optation — against six questions: how each form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher recognizes it empirically (Appendix A, Table A1, gives the full comparison). Four forms answer the first question with both a governing operation and the formal standing of the party that operation reaches: a zone of acceptance for the employee under hierarchy (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978), a posted term backed by an exit right for the market actor (Hayek, 1945), an elective and revocable relationship for the network participant (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990), a conferred seat for the co-opted party (Selznick, 1949). A zone, a term, a relation, a seat — in each mature form, organization theory names both the operation and the standing of the party it reaches.

Coordinative co-optation's column thins at exactly this point. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) name the mechanism itself — "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" — and Paper 1 builds the fifth form on that mechanism: digital platforms enroll participants through an algorithm that matches unchosen parties, ranks them, and can close accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), which is why the inherited word is co-optation rather than a newly coined term. The enrolled actor is neither an employee under authority, a buyer evaluating terms, nor a partner in a mutual bond — leaving her formal standing an open structural question that Paper 1 does not resolve and that this paper does not resolve either. What Paper 1 does establish is narrower and prior to that question: organization theory has not articulated what individual capacity enables a participant to coordinate within this architecture at all, regardless of what standing she eventually turns out to hold. Seven existing constructs approach that capacity closely enough to warrant formal examination, and closing that narrower gap — not the standing question — is this paper's job.

# Extant Constructs

A construct, within this theoretical framework, designates a formalized conceptualization of participant capacity — whether formulated as an operational competence, an infrastructural literacy, or an interpretive orientation toward an automated system. Theoretical frameworks incorporating a human counterpart already populate adjacent organizational literatures; relational coordination, for example, conceptualizes a shared mode of communicating and relating among interdependent organizational members (Gittell, 2002). Yet relational coordination presupposes the very condition that algorithmic management systematically eliminates — interactants who directly observe, reach, and negotiate with one another. Because the algorithmic intermediary severs that unmediated mutual visibility, evaluating candidate constructs requires examining the precise structural status each framework accords to the computational apparatus. Extant scholarship models individuals interacting with algorithms across divergent theoretical traditions, and that divergence establishes the boundary conditions of what each individual-level capacity can explain.

Seven constructs come close enough to warrant a formal hearing, and we put one question to each: where does the framework locate the human on the far side of the interaction — the client commissioning labor, the peer whose rating alters a worker's standing? A construct earns inclusion here if it models individual capacity vis-à-vis an algorithmic system and makes any claim, however partial, to reach a human counterpart; the four boundary conditions that follow the hearings (What These Boundaries Share, below) state the test each one is held to.

## Computer-Mediated Communication Competence

Formulating one of the earliest systematic accounts of digital interaction, Spitzberg (2006) conceptualizes computer-mediated communication as "any human symbolic text-based interaction conducted or facilitated through digitally-based technologies" (p. 630). His framework imports the classical architecture of interpersonal communication competence intact into mediated settings, organizing participant capacity across motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and relational outcomes. Within this model, motivation represents "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in a given CMC context" (p. 640), while knowledge encompasses "the cognitive comprehension of content and procedural processes involved in conducting appropriate and effective interaction in the computer-mediated context" (p. 641). Behavioral performance rests on four core skill clusters — attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness (p. 638) — all conditioned across five discrete facets of context (p. 644). Underpinning this formulation is a foundational assumption regarding the role of technology: mediation recalibrates the operational parameters of communicative exchange while leaving the fundamental structure of interpersonal competence undisturbed. The digital infrastructure operates as a transmission conduit, leaving the human actors stationed at either terminal as the sole coordinators of the interaction.

Spitzberg's model is not a solitary cognitive faculty lodged in one individual's mind, and reading it as one would make our critique too easy. Rooted in the relational communication tradition, competence in this model is co-constructed interpersonally and evaluated directly by the interacting partner. As Spitzberg notes, "competent interactants can facilitate the competence of cointeractants," whereas "an incompetent interactant can diminish a normally competent cointeractant's performance" (p. 650). Communicative acts reach this evaluative threshold pre-filtered through receiver expectations shaped by the digital medium. Far from an ancillary variable, the human counterpart serves as the essential evaluative instrument of the entire model. Five relational outcome criteria govern the framework — appropriateness, effectiveness (encompassing task achievement and efficiency), coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development (p. 648). These criteria presuppose an unmediated relational architecture. Coorientation and relationship development remain irreducibly two-party dynamics; appropriateness, defined as the "perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context," requires a conscious perceiver; and satisfaction, operationalized as "positive affect associated with fulfillment of positively valenced expectancies," demands an interacting subject in whom that affective resonance can occur.

This relational architecture clarifies the theoretical boundary — though not by making the counterpart disappear. An actor exhibiting mastery across every dimension of Spitzberg's model — from baseline motivation and media knowledge through attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness — placed within an arrangement where an opaque algorithm reads both parties and issues a mutually binding determination experiences a structural impasse. The breakdown does not reflect a behavioral decline in individual competence, and it does not require that the counterpart vanish from view. Rather, the intermediary severs the link between the counterpart's appraisal and its consequence. The counterpart may still observe the exchange and register a judgment — in some arrangements invisibly, in others in public view — but that judgment reaches the focal actor only as one input to an opaque aggregation neither party controls, so the mutual, iterative calibration through which Spitzberg's criteria are administered cannot occur. The algorithmic arrangement does not merely depress the communicator's competence score; it disconnects the relational instrument from the verdict that binds her.

Spitzberg himself reported that the measure's empirical structure came out simpler than the model he had theorized, and characterized research on the framework as nascent (p. 651).

Algorithmacy directly inherits Spitzberg's evaluative axis. While our three constitutive operations define what a participant cognitively and behaviorally executes, they do not define what constitutes communicative efficacy in normative terms — for which appropriateness and effectiveness remain the most robust criteria available in communication theory. What algorithmacy cannot adopt, however, is the structural topology those criteria presuppose — namely, a directly reachable human receiver situated at the terminal node of a transmission channel whose unmediated appraisal serves as the operational measure of competence.

## Human–Machine Communication

The classical transmission model of communication proved untenable once computational architectures were engineered to engage in direct symbolic dialogue with human users. Guzman and Lewis (2020) formalize this paradigm shift by conceptualizing the computational system as an autonomous communicative partner. Articulating a programmatic research agenda rather than an empirical measurement instrument, they argue that communicative artificial intelligence systems are "designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71), positioning the emerging field to focus "on questions of communication as they relate to technologies designed to fulfill the communicator role" (p. 74). Examining conversational agents, social robotics, and automated-writing software, they locate meaning-making "within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine" (p. 74). This departure from computer-mediated communication is deliberate and structurally definitive — where the default communication model they describe has people exchanging information through a technology that mediates human interaction (p. 73), human–machine communication reconfigures the machine itself as an interlocutor, elaborating this position across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions. Guzman and Lewis themselves complicate the clean split, noting that communicative AI can occupy mediator and communicator roles at once; that blurring is grist for our own claim rather than a problem for it, since the algorithmic intermediary we theorize is likewise neither a pure channel nor a pure interlocutor. Inaugurating the subfield's dedicated journal, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) formally define this constitutive analytical unit by establishing that human–machine communication investigates meaning-making between humans and machines wherein the technical artifact occupies the position of a communicative subject.

The dyadic structure represents a deliberate theoretical stipulation of the field rather than an external characterization, and human–machine communication itself supplies the critique hardest to dismiss. Gibbs, Kirkwood, Fang, and Wilkenfeld (2021) argue that scholarship has fundamentally mischaracterized the human–machine encounter as an isolated interpersonal interaction rather than an organizational structuration. In their formulation, computational systems embody institutional rules and resources that human actors draw upon, reproduce, and selectively subvert, distributing agency across the human user, the machine, and the broader institutional architecture that governs their engagement. Directly targeting the field's methodological individualism, they demonstrate through organizational cases — including algorithmic management — that analyzing isolated person–machine dyads obscures the structural forces dictating why and how the technology is deployed.

Yet this structurational turn does not resolve the theoretical vacancy this paper identifies. While structuration introduces institutional rules and resources as an analytical third element, it incorporates an institutional context rather than an interdependent human counterpart. Their formulation yields a triad linking the human actor, the computational artifact, and the institutional structure, whereas our coordination model links the focal worker, the algorithmic intermediary, and the unseen human counterpart. A specific human actor situated on the opposite pole of the coordination process — whose communicative reception defines the ultimate purpose of the exchange yet remains structurally inaccessible — remains absent from both formulations. This distinction yields an explicit theoretical boundary. An individual may exhibit comprehensive mastery within human–machine communication — treating the computational system as an interlocutor, decoding its symbolic cues, and sustaining relational rapport — without ever coordinating interdependent labor with another human through an automated system that simultaneously evaluates and binds both parties. The capacity this field theorizes remains, by structural definition, a competence oriented toward the machine.

A second version of Gibbs and colleagues' critique cuts closer to home: a construct locating the capacity inside an individual might itself repeat the methodological individualism they indict, if the capacity in fact belongs to the institutional arrangement rather than to any one person within it. The construct's two foundational properties answer it. Algorithmacy is socially acquired — the antecedents Zhou and colleagues (2025) and Sutherland and colleagues (2020) report, discussed below, are peer sensemaking and informal social support, not solitary trial-and-error — yet individually held and individually exercised at the moment of coordination, which is what its uneven distribution across formally equivalent participants (Foundational Properties, below) requires. A micro-foundation with social antecedents is not an individualism (Felin, Foss, & Ployhart, 2015); it is the level of analysis at which a structurational account must eventually cash out.

What this literature contributes to our framework is its rigorous demarcation of theoretical boundaries. Guzman and Lewis (2020) articulate the clearest available analytical distinction between the passive transmission channel theorized within computer-mediated communication and the active communicative partner conceptualized within human–machine communication. We adopt this structural distinction while departing from its underlying ontology — reconceptualizing the technical apparatus not as an autonomous conversational subject, but as an opaque, binding intermediary operating within an asymmetric triad. Furthermore, by formalizing their field's constitutive question with foundational precision, Fortunati and Edwards (2020) provide the analytical benchmark against which our theoretical divergence becomes explicit, shifting the focal inquiry from how individuals communicate with computational systems to how they coordinate interdependent action through them.

## AI-Mediated Communication

Synthesizing scholarship across artificial intelligence and computer-mediated communication, Hancock et al. (2020) bring the human counterpart back into view while aligning the computational system with a single communicating party. They define AI-mediated communication as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 90). Both human interactants remain situated within this theoretical scope, illustrated through empirical phenomena such as algorithmic smart replies, predictive text engines, and synthetic media generation. The authors characterize this configuration across five foundational parameters — magnitude, reflecting "the extent of the changes that AI enacts on messages"; media type, designating "the media in which AI operates"; optimization goal, defining "the goal for which AI is optimizing the messages"; autonomy, denoting "the degree to which AI can operate on messages without the sender's supervision"; and role orientation, identifying "the role that the AI is operating on behalf of."

Counting nodes will not separate this framework from ours: by its own definition, AI-mediated communication already specifies a triad — two human interactants and a computational agent. Within this architecture, the receiver occupies a central rather than marginal position — her cognitive inferences constitute a central dependent variable of the empirical agenda, and the accompanying ethical discourse centers on what communicative transparency is owed to her. Hancock and colleagues acknowledge the vulnerability inherent in the receiver's structural position, noting that explicit disclosure remains rare, that recipients typically presume the human sender authored the communication, and that the entire arrangement hinges on an unverified presupposition — namely, that "the message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency" (p. 90).

The decisive difference, however, is categorical rather than parametric. Hancock and colleagues' agent operates on messages — modifying, augmenting, or generating them — and every dimension of their framework (magnitude, media type, optimization goal, autonomy, role orientation) characterizes that operation, including cases where the optimization serves the platform's interest rather than the sender's. The intermediary in our setting renders verdicts: it matches, ranks, and can terminate, and no setting of magnitude, autonomy, or optimization goal converts an agent that rewrites a message into a party that binds both interactants to a determination neither commissioned. Their own definitional requirement that the agent operate "on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90) marks the same boundary from the other side: in our coordination setting, the algorithmic third party operates on behalf of no one present at the interaction. Within their model, the counterpart's exposure is restricted to a symbolic message; within ours, the counterpart and the focal worker are simultaneously exposed to an authoritative, binding determination.

From this literature, we adopt the optimization goal parameter as the definitive analytical axis separating the two frameworks. Whereas their computational agent optimizes for the personal or communicative objectives of a designated sender, the algorithmic intermediary in our model optimizes for institutional coordination metrics external to both interactants. Furthermore, we build upon Hancock and colleagues' candid assessment of receiver vulnerability — wherein the counterpart's comprehension and acceptance of algorithmic mediation are structurally presumed rather than actively secured — which serves as the conceptual precursor to our formulation of an unseen counterpart who remains structurally barred from occupying an evaluative position altogether.

## AI Literacy

A distinct literature shifts analytical focus from communicative interaction to cognitive and technical comprehension, treating the algorithmic architecture as an object of formal domain knowledge. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace." They organize this domain across five foundational conceptual inquiries — interrogating what artificial intelligence is, what it can execute, how it operates, how it should be deployed, and how human actors perceive its agency. From these core questions, the authors derive seventeen discrete competencies spanning system identification, machine learning representations, algorithmic decision-making, model training workflows, sensor and data integration, ethical evaluation, and programmability.

Only two of the seventeen competencies explicitly reference human actors, and their precise operational phrasing reveals the underlying dyadic boundary. Competency 10, designated as Human Role in AI, requires learners to "recognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems," while Competency 5 positions human capabilities strictly as a comparative benchmark against which automated performance is evaluated. Within this architecture, human beings enter solely as upstream software developers or comparative performance baselines rather than as active interlocutors situated within an ongoing interaction. Neither competency accords another individual an operational position within the coordination workflow that the learner conducts.

The framework's conceptualization of collaboration reinforces this structural boundary. Every operational instance of communication or collaboration embedded across the competencies is directed toward the computational artifact — demonstrated by the definitional mandate to communicate and collaborate "with AI" and in the diagnostic framing of misconceptions that "limit people's ability to effectively use, collaborate with, and act as critical consumers of AI." Interpersonal collaboration appears exclusively within the auxiliary instructional guidelines, where Design Consideration 11 (Social Interaction) advises educators to "consider designing AI learning experiences that foster social interaction and collaboration." Other human actors populate the instructional classroom environment yet remain entirely absent from the competence model itself. Ceiling performance across all seventeen competencies still leaves a participant with no capacity to establish communicative legibility or convey intent to an unseen human counterpart through an opaque, binding system, because all five organizing inquiries interrogate the structural properties of the computational system rather than coordination directed toward a human actor reached through it.

This structural limitation subsequently propagated directly into psychometric operationalization. Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) synthesized Long and Magerko's seventeen competencies into four overarching dimensions — knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics. Subsequent empirical instruments inherited this fourfold taxonomy, consistently conceptualizing competence as an individual actor's unilateral, dyadic transaction with a computational artifact. The structural omission of the interdependent human counterpart thus became institutionalized across the broader psychometric tradition seeded by the foundational 2020 framework.

The distinction separating our framework from this tradition centers on constitutive derivation rather than taxonomic inventory. Long and Magerko populate their competency catalog through scoping criteria that evaluate definitional alignment, bibliographic prevalence, and practical utility for system designers and educators — an inductive aggregation that lacks an architectural principle explaining why nine competencies govern technical mechanics while only one addresses normative application. In contrast, the constitutive operations of algorithmacy are derived deductively from the precise information that an opaque, adaptive, and binding coordination arrangement withholds from its participants. What we adopt from Long and Magerko is their overarching literacy genealogy, which anchors the construct within the evolutionary continuum of human symbolic capacity — tracing the trajectory of literacy from alphabetic print through digital, computational, and data literacies to situate algorithmacy within an established historical tradition.

## Algorithmic Competency

Zhou et al. (2025) developed the first psychometrically validated scale within this domain, executing a multi-stage scale development protocol that mirrors the empirical standards our own research program advances. Their empirical methodology originated in ninety-nine semi-structured qualitative interviews conducted with Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers to generate fourteen initial candidate items, subsequently refined through expert content validation. Across successive empirical samples — comprising an exploratory factor analysis (N=275), a confirmatory factor analysis (N=213), a construct validation study (N=230), and a three-wave longitudinal panel (N=225) — the authors established a second-order, four-factor structural model embedded within a nomological network of organizational antecedents and individual performance outcomes. The final instrument retains twelve items distributed evenly across four subdimensions with an aggregate internal reliability of .85, formally defining algorithmic competency as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2).

Dismissing this scale as mere cognitive knowledge of system mechanics misreads the underlying psychometric operationalization. Three of the four dimensions capture behavioral and affective capacities distinct from declarative knowledge. Understanding encompasses sophisticated comprehension of algorithmic management alongside the capacity to navigate its dynamic rules; Embracing captures affective willingness to trust the efficiency and fairness of automated allocations; Leveraging denotes the strategic capacity to exploit algorithmic affordances to personal advantage; and Remediating reflects a worker's ability to correct, circumvent, or supplement algorithmic failures. Zhou and colleagues establish rigorous discriminant validity: general digital competence correlates moderately with the aggregate scale (r=.37, p<.01) yet exhibits a non-significant correlation with Embracing (r=.04, p>.05), consistent with the claim that conventional digital literacy instruments systematically omit affective orientations toward automated control.

The analytical limitation of their instrument is therefore not an absolute omission of the human counterpart, but rather the structural status accorded to her across the scale. The counterpart appears across several empirical roles, yet none positions her as an active interlocutor within the coordination competency itself. Item 8 locates the customer as a distant evaluative consequence rather than a communicative counterpart ("I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback"), where the behavioral object of exploration remains the algorithmic rules rather than the relational counterpart. Similarly, Item 11 references the triadic coordination mechanism strictly as an attitudinal belief regarding administrative matching efficiency ("I think the platform AM is highly efficient, such as in customers–workers matching"), yet this item resides within the dimension exhibiting the weakest inter-factor correlation and captures no interactive capacity toward the matched individual. Furthermore, while customer-oriented service behavior enters their structural equation model as an empirical consequence measured via Peccei and Rosenthal's (1997) scale, this design confirms that the counterpart operates as an external downstream outcome rather than a constitutive element of the focal competency.

The fourth structural role carries the most definitive theoretical significance. Item 6 within the Remediating subdimension operationalizes whether a worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve vulnerabilities in AM" (p. 8). The qualitative fieldwork underpinning this item illustrates an asymmetrical grievance procedure: when confronted with an unjustified customer evaluation, a courier compiles objective documentation — such as audio recordings or timestamped delivery logs — and submits a formal administrative appeal directly to the platform's automated apparatus. This represents the sole mechanism in the rival scale through which a worker actively contests an evaluative verdict. This contestation routes exclusively to the technical infrastructure. Although the customer generates the initial negative rating, the grievance procedure bypasses the counterpart entirely, resolving the dispute through an administrative channel that leaves the interpersonal relationship unaddressed.

A worker could therefore achieve ceiling scores across all twelve items — mastering automated dispatch dynamics, placing faith in algorithmic matching, exploiting platform rules to secure preferred orders, and remediating systemic errors via technical appeals — while possessing no capacity to reconstruct what the human counterpart intended or to project communicative legibility toward that individual. The psychometric instrument cannot detect this coordination failure because the counterpart is incorporated only after the construct is formally bounded, operating strictly as an external dependent variable. Appending an additional survey item cannot resolve this structural absence because the foundational construct definition remains strictly dyadic, linking only the focal worker and the algorithmic apparatus. Furthermore, because their operationalization binds item indicators directly to commercial platform features, the scale cannot transfer to peer-mediated evaluative environments where aggregate scoring tallies and decision criteria are structurally withheld.

What algorithmacy inherits from Zhou and colleagues is their rigorous scale development protocol alongside a vital empirical finding regarding construct acquisition. In their structural equation model, the primary organizational antecedents predicting algorithmic competency are irreducibly social — specifically, informal peer support systems moderated by collectivist cultural orientations. This empirical evidence provides foundational support for our theoretical proposition regarding implicit learning, demonstrating that individuals develop the capacity to navigate opaque algorithmic environments not through isolated trial-and-error, but through shared social practices and collective peer sensemaking.

## Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation

Investigating the structural transformation that occurred when an online freelancing platform replaced its transparent five-star rating system with an opaque, percentile-based Job Success Score, Rahman (2021) conceptualizes the resulting dynamic through the theoretical framework of the invisible cage. This formulation contrasts hidden, fluctuating algorithmic metrics with the classical Weberian iron cage of fixed, codified bureaucratic procedures. Five structural properties constitute this invisible cage (p. 976) — hidden and shifting evaluation criteria, evaluations executed at unpredictable intervals, unidentifiable score calculation magnitudes, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative influence spanning the corporate platform and the individual client. Rather than serving as an inductive inventory, these five properties operate as an analytical proof of systemic closure. As Rahman demonstrates, "even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque" (p. 976).

Rahman examines the empirical terrain our construct addresses through a labor-process framework of managerial control rather than an organizational framework of coordination competence. Within this regime of systemic opacity, workers navigate the invisible cage through two primary behavioral strategies — experimental reactivity, whereby individuals execute trial-and-error adjustments to reverse-engineer and elevate their scores, and constrained reactivity, wherein workers restrict their platform engagement to protect established reputational capital from sudden volatility, with strategic choice governed by platform dependence and prior exposure to negative evaluation shocks. These empirical dynamics impose necessary discipline on our theoretical claims, demonstrating that individual reactivity frequently deepens organizational subordination while entrenching persistent evaluative paranoia over multi-year horizons.

The human counterpart is extensively documented within Rahman's empirical fieldwork yet remains outside his formal theoretical construct. To investigate the labor arrangement, he conducted qualitative interviews with eighteen clients alongside eighty platform freelancers (p. 956), recruiting clients via freelancer referrals because the platform interface systematically foreclosed direct contact — ensuring that "registered freelancers could see a client's full profile only after applying for their job, and even then, TalentFinder withheld their contact information" (p. 954). Rahman defines the methodological purpose of these client interviews with precise analytical boundaries — deploying them to understand client feedback practices and "to triangulate what freelancers said about their experiences working with clients" (p. 954). Indeed, his supplementary empirical documentation systematically records whether each client exhibited cognitive awareness of how qualitative reviews altered a freelancer's composite score. Rahman thus empirically observed the counterpart's structural blindness to her own evaluative impact; yet because his theoretical model centers on labor discipline and administrative control, this relational dynamic remained outside the focal theoretical framework. Platform interface protocols formalized this mutual severance from the opposing direction as well, explicitly assuring clients that their evaluative feedback "will be kept anonymous and never shared directly with the freelancer" (p. 960).

Rahman's fifth structural property brings his framework closest to the theoretical terrain articulated here, yet the surviving analytical distinction remains sharp. The indeterminate locus of evaluative influence incorporates the human counterpart primarily as an interpretive dilemma — namely, that the worker cannot discern whether an unexpected score fluctuation reflects client dissatisfaction or an unannounced recalibration of the platform's algorithmic baseline. Within Rahman's model, uncertainty regarding which entity altered the metric remains an epistemic problem concerning the behavior of the score itself. Within algorithmacy, by contrast, the coordination problem concerns deciphering the latent intentions and communicative expectations of the human counterpart. The sole behavioral instance wherein Rahman's freelancers direct their actions toward the counterpart confirms this structural boundary: under constrained reactivity, workers deliberately migrate platform-originated relationships into external, unmediated communication channels — a disintermediation that directly violates contractual terms of service. Reaching the human counterpart thus necessitates abandoning the algorithmic apparatus entirely, risking administrative termination to restore direct bilateral interaction.

Three foundational insights transition from Rahman's framework into our construct. First, shifting and opaque evaluative criteria operate as an enduring environmental condition of algorithmic coordination, establishing the structural necessity for our third operation — keeping track. Second, his five constitutive properties systematically decompose what the algorithmic architecture withholds from participants, highlighting an interpretive challenge at the boundary of the system: the ambiguity of whether to attribute outcomes to machine logic or human discretion — the candidate fourth operation we return to below. Third, his empirical demonstration that behavioral variance is driven by structural platform dependence and historical evaluation shocks presents a formidable rival hypothesis to individual-level capacity models, establishing an empirical benchmark that any variance attributed to algorithmacy must account for. Rahman ultimately demonstrates this theoretical boundary against his own qualitative evidence: despite incorporating eighteen human clients directly within his fieldwork, his resultant theoretical construct remained strictly dyadic, binding only the focal worker and the algorithmic apparatus.

## Gig Literacies

Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) approach closest to the structural configuration theorized here by incorporating the human counterpart directly into their empirical research design. Their platform literacy has a direct ancestor: Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) first named "algorithmic competencies" as the sensemaking, circumventing, and manipulating behaviors gig workers deploy against the platform, and that earlier construct remains as dyadic as the one Sutherland and colleagues develop from it — worker-versus-algorithm appropriation moves, with no operational position for the counterpart the algorithm also matches the worker to. Conceptualizing gig literacies as "critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing" (p. 457), they argue that independent workers "must adapt their skills and work strategies in order to leverage platforms creatively and productively, and as a component of their 'personal holding environment'" (p. 457). They subsequently formalize this capacity as platform literacy, defining it as "the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470). To investigate this dynamic across both poles of the market, their qualitative design paired in-depth interviews across twenty freelancers and nineteen clients with a dual-sided digital walkthrough wherein the researchers engaged the platform simultaneously as buyers and sellers — one investigator registering as a client and another as a freelance contractor (pp. 461–463). Within this empirical design, client narratives served an explicit evaluative purpose, providing "some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers" (p. 462).

Their empirical findings organize these capacities across five core platform literacies (pp. 463–469) — building reputation through the Job Success Score, platform badges, private client feedback channels, and strategic refunds designed to preempt punitive reviews; self-presentation through profile optimization, targeted proposals, and chat interactions that elevate bid visibility; maintaining productivity via structured routines and external software integrations; mitigating transaction risk by leveraging escrow protections, documenting communication trails, vetting prospective clients, and deploying intuitive heuristics of trustworthiness; and building relationships through direct interpersonal engagement. Within this fifth dimension, participant P10 illustrates how independent workers deliberately cultivate client interactions "to build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" oriented toward securing repeat contractual engagements (p. 467).

This fifth literacy comes closer than any of the other six, and its own evidence is what marks where it stops. The counterpart-directed practices its qualitative evidence documents most explicitly operate as strategies for escaping algorithmic mediation, not for mastering coordination within it. When participant P39 confronted an algorithmically depressed rating that proved impossible to rehabilitate within the platform interface, the worker systematically migrated established client relationships off-platform to preserve commercial viability (p. 469). Interpersonal rapport, within their empirical account, functions primarily to extract the collaboration from the computational apparatus — relocating the working relationship to an unmediated dyadic channel where algorithmic scoring can no longer reach or govern it. This dynamic of structural disintermediation stands in direct opposition to algorithmacy, which conceptualizes the participant capacity required to sustain coordination and convey intent through an opaque, binding intermediary rather than fleeing its architecture.

Sutherland and colleagues delineate this structural boundary explicitly, establishing an analytical distinction directly compatible with our framework. Specifically, they contrast freelance marketplaces such as Upwork — where market participants retain the discretionary capacity to evaluate, select, and converse directly with prospective clients — with automated dispatch platforms such as Uber, wherein algorithmic architectures assign labor deterministically (p. 470). Relational cultivation serves as an empirical foundation for sustained commercial engagements on Upwork precisely because its platform architecture preserves bilateral agency that algorithmic dispatch systems systematically eliminate. Consequently, their framework conceptualizes competencies enacted adjacent to a digital intermediary rather than communicative and coordinating action executed through an opaque, binding triadic system. Because their model presupposes an institutional environment where market actors retain the structural capacity to circumvent the apparatus and establish unmediated bilateral contact, incorporating relational indicators into such an instrument captures literacies exercised alongside the platform rather than coordination accomplished through its constraints.

Two things carry forward from Sutherland and colleagues' account: platform enrollment without institutional standing, which shifts systemic coordination burdens entirely onto participant competence, and the situated acquisition of opaque evaluative metrics, which independent workers learn through informal peer sensemaking and punitive experiential consequences rather than formalized technical documentation.

## What These Boundaries Share

Seven constructs, seven different failures, and one shared shape once Table 1 is read across rather than down. The failure is not that a second human is missing — AI-mediated communication, algorithmic competency, and gig literacies all include one. It is that no construct combines an active human counterpart with an opaque, independently consequential intermediary that itself evaluates and binds both parties. Where a counterpart appears, the intermediary is a channel, a delegate, or infrastructure to route around — never itself an autonomous party rendering a binding verdict. Where the intermediary is opaque and binding, the counterpart is reduced to an outcome the system measures rather than an interlocutor the participant coordinates with.

Systematic reviews across the broader literature confirm this precise limit rather than a cruder one. Synthesizing fifty empirical investigations of algorithmic literacy screened from ninety-six candidate studies across four research databases, Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) identify pervasive conceptual fragmentation; reviewing 169 peer-reviewed publications, Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub (2024) document an entrenched absence of unified theoretical frameworks; and evaluating twelve empirical studies of healthcare professionals against Nutbeam's (2000) tripartite literacy model, Iyamu et al. (2026) report that communicative literacy remains "largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and measurement instruments." Within Nutbeam's (2000) hierarchical model, the communicative dimension — operationalized as an expert explaining algorithmic data to a patient whom the system does not simultaneously evaluate or bind — represents the literature's closest empirical approximation of a second human actor. Yet multiplying survey indicators across dozens of empirical contexts has failed to combine that second actor with a genuinely evaluating, binding intermediary. Confronted with what is fundamentally a triadic coordination challenge, extant scholarship has systematically populated the relational space with system-facing operationalizations.

Theoretical formulations of visibility labor complete this conceptual encirclement from the opposing pole — and the pole holds more than one paper. Abidin (2016) theorizes the strategic self-presentation that followers execute to attain visibility before a specific social media influencer — a tangible, named human counterpart — while deliberately bracketing the computational algorithm outside her analytical scope, defining her investigation as analog affective labor distinct from algorithmic visibility. Cotter (2019) puts both poles in the same frame: Instagram influencers pursuing human audiences through an opaque, evaluating algorithm, playing what she calls "the visibility game" against rules they can only infer. Even this literature stops short of the position our construct occupies. It theorizes practice and communal knowledge rather than a formalized competence with constitutive operations and a measurement program; its audience is an aggregate pursued for attention rather than an interdependent counterpart in jointly executed work; and its algorithm evaluates and binds the creator alone, curating for the audience rather than rendering one determination that binds both. Organizational theory currently possesses no construct that formalizes an individual competence integrating both poles of this triadic coordination challenge.

The triadic structural configuration itself has not escaped scholarly notice across neighboring disciplines, and one of those disciplines supplies the sharpest test of our novelty claim. In online dispute resolution, Katsh and Rifkin (2001) conceptualized the digital infrastructure hosting an interaction as an active "fourth party" that exerts autonomous systemic effects over human disputants — two active human counterparts, coordinating through a computational intermediary that can be exactly as consequential and binding as the one we theorize. Katsh and Rifkin theorized this position from the system-design side, though, and Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021), whom we return to below, theorize its opaque, protections-withholding variant normatively. What neither supplies, and what no construct we review supplies, is the participant-side competency for coordinating inside it — a formalized account of what an individual disputant must be able to do, not a design standard for what the intermediary owes her. Investigating platform-mediated evaluation, Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) demonstrated how automated feedback regimes dismantle traditional bilateral market exchanges to forge an asymmetrical triad comprising platform operators, buyers, and sellers, sustained by a structural visibility gap that enables buyers to evaluate workers invisibly. Cameron (2024) formalized this relational configuration within on-demand platforms as the algorithmic labor triangle, describing in fine behavioral grain how drivers navigate it through constant, confined choices; her account explains what those choices produce — consent, and a stable arrangement — but not what capacity separates the driver who navigates well from the one who does not. Healy and Pekarek (2025) integrated the triangle into labor process theory to explain worker vulnerability and institutional control. These formulations theorize what the triad is and what it produces; none supplies a participant capacity with dimensional structure. The stabilization mechanism developed below (Theoretical Implications) returns to Cameron's mechanism from the competency side.

We establish this theoretical boundary across four explicit analytical conditions. The construct must operationalize an individual actor's behavioral and interpretive capacity. The human counterpart must function as an active, interdependent party within the focal coordination — not an upstream system designer, an informal peer network, or a passive downstream recipient. The computational intermediary must simultaneously evaluate and execute binding determinations upon both interactants, which rules out a transmission channel that merely conveys messages, an automated delegate that assists a focal sender, and a system-design or normative account of what an intermediary owes disputants without a participant-side competency to match. And the critique must substantiate an unpopulated theoretical position within organizational theory rather than a superficial disagreement over taxonomic labels. Evaluated against these criteria, the seven construct hearings alongside the literature's overarching meta-analyses yield a singular conclusion — no extant competence construct accords the interdependent human counterpart a constitutive analytical position.

Concurrently, empirical fieldwork has begun documenting this latent capacity in naturalistic organizational settings. Manky (2025) demonstrates that ride-hailing drivers in Lima (N=40) systematically decode opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness and ensure physical safety — an explicitly counterpart-directed interpretive capacity, mobilized for bodily security, that currently lacks formal theoretical conceptualization. Organizational scholarship therefore requires a communicative capacity formulated specifically for the triadic structural position that empirical research has mapped yet left theoretically unequipped. The next section formalizes that construct.

# Algorithmacy: A Construct for Coordinative Co-optation

Suddaby (2010) establishes four formal criteria for construct clarity in organizational theory: articulating a precise definition, setting explicit scope conditions, specifying semantic relationships with neighboring constructs, and demonstrating theoretical coherence within the broader framework. While we formulated the core definition in the introduction, construct validity demands a systematic account of its boundary conditions, dimensional structure, ontological status, and analytical utility. We develop those components below to establish algorithmacy as a viable micro-foundational construct for organization theory.

One clarification bears on everything that follows. Paper 1 left participant standing in coordinative co-optation open — whether the enrolled actor is owed anything like an employee's protections, a buyer's exit right, or a co-opted party's hearing remains a structural and institutional question this paper does not settle. Algorithmacy does not settle it either. What it supplies is the micro-foundation coordinative co-optation needs regardless of how that structural question resolves: the individual capacity a participant must exercise to act within whatever standing the form eventually assigns her. A construct describing what a person can do is not a claim about what she is owed, and the two should not be read as the same contribution.

That separation is not free, and it holds only for a stated reason. For competency to be independent of standing, the three operations must stay invariant across candidate resolutions of standing — and they do, because they are defined against opacity, adaptivity, and bindingness, which are properties of the arrangement rather than of the participant's formal status. Any resolution of standing that leaves those three properties intact leaves the competency intact: a hearing right that does not disclose the rule, or an appeal channel that does not unwind the verdict, changes nothing the three operations respond to. Resolutions that remove one of the three properties do the opposite — they shrink the arrangement itself out of algorithmacy's scope conditions (above), rather than leaving a standing-endowed participant who no longer needs the competency. That is Proposition 3, below, generalized: transparency is one such resolution, and standing reform is another route to the same effect. Making that route explicit is not a retreat from the construct's relevance; it is what keeps the "regardless of how standing resolves" claim from being an escape hatch rather than an argument.

## Scope Conditions and Boundary Limits

Algorithmacy applies specifically to organizational arrangements where an intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding on interacting parties. The construct does not operate — or operates only trivially — in contexts governed by codified, static administrative rules, or in open market environments where transacting parties negotiate contractual terms directly. A posted market price accompanied by an unrestricted right of refusal represents the classical market archetype (Hayek, 1945). A codified bureaucratic procedure administered by a designated hierarchical superior represents the formal organization (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978).

Online freelance labor platforms occupy a hybrid boundary: while the system algorithmically matches and scores participants, transacting parties frequently retain the capacity to migrate their collaborative relationships off-platform (Sutherland et al., 2020). In contrast, on-demand dispatch networks, algorithmic piecework queues, and peer-evaluation systems that withhold score tallies reside at the core of the construct's domain. Scope conditions define structural configurations rather than specific industrial sectors, fulfilling the exact requirement demanded by Paper 1's open cell: a rigorous account of the capacities an individual must exercise to coordinate within coordinative co-optation, formulated so that empirical researchers can identify its absence.

## Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation

To establish discriminant validity, we map algorithmacy against neighboring constructs. We formalize these semantic relationships as testable empirical predictions across adjacent measurement traditions:

**Algorithmic Competency (Zhou et al., 2025):** We predict that Zhou and colleagues' dimension of Understanding will correlate moderately with interpreting, and their dimension of Leveraging will correlate moderately with specifying intent. Conversely, their dimension of Embracing exhibits no theoretical correspondence within algorithmacy, as an affective attitude toward platform efficiency constitutes a normative disposition rather than an operational coordination capacity.

**Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation (Rahman, 2021):** Rahman's typology operates along an orthogonal theoretical axis. Whereas reactivity categorizes degrees of behavioral engagement versus defensive withdrawal, algorithmacy delineates directional communicative orientation and temporal tracking across an asymmetric triad. Latent correlations high enough to collapse these dimensional boundaries would invalidate the construct's theoretical distinctiveness.

**Computer-Mediated Communication Competence (Spitzberg, 2006):** We predict interpreting and specifying intent will correlate moderately with Spitzberg's appropriateness and effectiveness criteria, which algorithmacy directly inherits (Categorical Delineation, below) but administers without a co-present evaluator. A correlation near zero would suggest the two constructs are measuring unrelated capacities rather than the same evaluative axis under different structural conditions.

**Gig Literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020):** We predict a weak or negative correlation between algorithmacy and platform literacy's relational-building subdimension specifically, since that subdimension is a strategy for exiting algorithmic mediation rather than coordinating through it (Gig Literacies, above); algorithmacy should correlate positively with the remaining four subdimensions, which describe navigating the platform rather than leaving it.

## Constitutive Operations: The Tripartite Model

The operational architecture of algorithmacy comprises three core operations derived directly from the structural demands of the mediated triad rather than aggregated inductively from ad hoc practices:

**Interpreting:** Reconstructing, from ambiguous and fragmented system feedback, the underlying actions, constraints, and intentions of both the algorithmic intermediary and the unseen human counterpart.

**Specifying intent:** Structuring and encoding one's communication so that intended meaning survives the narrow, standardized signaling channels permitted by the intermediary, enabling the counterpart to receive it intact.

**Keeping track:** Detecting shifts and recalibrations in the unannounced rules governing the intermediary that bind both parties over time.

A dyadic competence model restricted to the person–system relationship requires only the interpretive dimension, which Rahman's (2021) framework already captures.

This model explicitly excludes a candidate fourth operation derived from Rahman's fifth dimension of opacity — determining whether the platform firm or the human client drove a change in evaluative score. We subsume this function directly within interpreting, because evaluative ambiguity represents an interpretive failure to parse system mechanics and counterpart behavior simultaneously. This structural boundary remains empirically falsifiable. If field evidence reveals persistent cases where participants successfully decode all available feedback yet remain structurally unable to determine which entity executed the decision, distinguishing the locus of evaluative authority would constitute a distinct fourth operation that our empirical instruments are designed to detect.

The broader literacy tradition provides the construct's nomenclature while setting the analogy's functional boundaries. Long and Magerko (2020) extend this lineage across textual, computational, scientific, and data literacies, yet their operationalizations remain confined to person–system dyads. The analogy supplies lineage and a name, not the operational architecture: texts and numbers do not evaluate their users, so no inventory of reading's or computing's operations transfers onto a mediating party that does. The three operations below are derived instead from what the triad withholds. What the lineage does mark is the point of departure — keeping track has no counterpart among the established literacies, because their media hold still.

### Why Each Operation Requires the Full Triad

Each operation earns its place in the model only if it requires the intermediary and the counterpart together, not the system alone. Interpreting satisfies this test by definition: it reconstructs the intermediary's mechanics and the counterpart's intentions from the same ambiguous feedback, because platform opacity leaves no channel that isolates one from the other.

Specifying intent satisfies it for a less obvious reason. Encoding a message for a narrow channel is ordinary mediated communication, present whenever any channel compresses what can be said; what makes it triadic here is that the channel a participant encodes for is also the instrument evaluating her, so the same act — phrasing a pull request's rationale, say — must satisfy the intermediary's legibility requirements and the counterpart's interpretive ones simultaneously, with no channel available to address either party alone.

Keeping track is the operation most exposed to the objection that it is merely system-facing, because its definition names only the intermediary's rules, and it is the paper's least field-tested claim — no incident in the current pilot material bears on it directly (Instrument Piloting, Appendix B). It requires the counterpart for an evidentiary reason rather than a definitional one. Where the underlying mechanism is genuinely inspectable — Hult's participants can read the codebase their submissions are judged against — a rule shift can register as a direct system signal, and keeping track collapses toward ordinary system monitoring. Where the mechanism is not inspectable, which is the paper's core scope condition (Scope Conditions, above), a shift in the unannounced rule has no direct signal to register as; it becomes visible only as a change in what the counterpart's behavior, output, or evaluation now rewards. A participant confined to system-facing signals in that core case would systematically miss the class of rule change this operation exists to detect. Whether Hult's inspectable codebase pulls keeping track toward system monitoring in practice, or whether the concealed aggregation rule still forces detection through the counterpart even there, is exactly the question the harness interview's third module (Appendix B) is built to answer — and the current pilot has not yet produced an incident that does.

The three operations are, in this sense, recursively linked rather than independent: a change detected through keeping track alters what interpreting must now reconstruct and what specifying intent must now encode for, and the cycle turns again. Figure 1 renders this as a process rather than a static inventory, because the empirical instrument built around the three operations treats them that way — a critical incident begins with interpreting an unexplained outcome, continues through specifying intent in the participant's next action, and closes with keeping track registering whether the rule that produced the outcome has itself moved, which reopens the next incident's interpreting.

This process framing also answers a measurement question this paper would otherwise leave open. The three operations are neither reflective indicators of one underlying latent trait — a participant could plausibly interpret well while specifying intent poorly, since the two draw on different evidentiary bases — nor strictly formative components that simply sum to the construct, since keeping track's outputs feed back into what the other two operations must do next. They are closer to sequential stages in a recursive cycle, which is why Paper 3's qualitative arm (Appendix B) traces critical incidents through the whole cycle rather than scoring each operation in isolation, even though the survey panel — necessarily, for psychometric tractability — treats them as at least partly separable dimensions for discriminant-validity testing. The two strategies are not in tension: the panel tests whether the operations are empirically distinguishable from neighboring constructs, and the qualitative arm tests whether they function as the recursive cycle Figure 1 depicts.

## Categorical Delineation: Competency versus Skill and Capability

Algorithmacy constitutes an individual competency rather than a discrete skill or an organizational capability:

**Distinction from Skill:** A skill represents an isolable procedural routine that can be codified and transmitted through explicit didactic instruction. In contrast, the governing rules of opaque algorithmic systems remain proprietary, unobservable, and dynamic, precluding rule-based instructional codification.

**Distinction from Capability:** A capability resides in organizational routines a firm owns and deploys (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). In platform environments, the platform firm exclusively owns and modifies the matching algorithms, rating systems, and governance parameters; the enrolled individual holds no proprietary capability over the administrative architecture.

**Phenomenographic Foundation:** Sandberg (2000), examining twenty engine optimizers at the Volvo Car Corporation, demonstrated that competence reflects a worker's qualitative conception of work rather than an abstract inventory of independent attributes. Informants optimized either separate technical qualities, interacting qualities, or the entire process from the customer's holistic perspective. In that third conception, the customer represents an interpretive orientation toward the task rather than an unseen counterpart reached through a binding intermediary. Sandberg's phenomenographic finding demonstrates that competence resides in the worker's interpretive framing of work — an insight that algorithmacy extends to mediated triadic coordination.

Conversely, Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) argue that interpersonal communication competence is not an isolated individual attribute but a relational quality jointly enacted and evaluated by interacting parties. Spitzberg (2006) carries this relational commitment into mediated settings, where interactants mutually evaluate and co-construct communicative competence. Algorithmic coordination structurally breaks this relational model, though not by removing the counterpart's judgment — she may render appraisals, visibly or invisibly (Computer-Mediated Communication Competence, above). She never occupies a position from which mutual communicative competence can be negotiated, because the aggregation that converts her appraisal into consequence is withheld from both parties. Where the structural conditions for relational co-construction are absent, competence necessarily reverts from a jointly enacted judgment to an individually held capacity — the interpretive conception of work Sandberg (2000) describes, above — with the counterpart's appraisal surviving only as an opaque input to the binding verdict. Algorithmacy identifies the interpretive and expressive capacities an individual must supply under exactly that reversion.

## Foundational Properties: Experiential Acquisition and Uneven Distribution

Algorithmacy possesses two foundational properties:

**Acquisition through Participation:** Because platform operators withhold the underlying rules, formal didactic training cannot substitute for experiential practice. Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp, and Raupach (2023) developed the SNAIL scale to evaluate formal AI-literacy coursework, measuring declarative knowledge installed through instruction. Algorithmacy predicts that explicit classroom instruction alone cannot produce this competency.

**Uneven Distribution:** Algorithmacy is distributed heterogeneously across individuals whom the platform treats as formally equivalent. If the competency were uniformly distributed, it could not explain the persistent variance in outcomes observed among participants navigating identical algorithmic environments.

## Theoretical Implications

Formalizing algorithmacy transforms how organizational scholars examine algorithmic management. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) conceptualize algorithmic workplaces as contested terrain, analyzing the mechanisms of direction, evaluation, and discipline that systems impose on labor. Algorithmacy shifts analytical focus toward what the individual must execute to sustain coordination. Mandating that platforms disclose their algorithms alters the opacity of the information environment; fostering algorithmacy alters who can interpret that environment.

This inquiry directly bridges macro-structural platform theory and micro-foundational worker agency. Paper 1 named coordinative co-optation on the mechanism Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) identified in *Organization Theory* (The Coordination-Form Gap, above), while Curchod et al. (2020) and Cameron (2024) analyzed in *Administrative Science Quarterly* how algorithmic enrollment reshapes worker subjectivity and structural consent. Algorithmacy supplies the corresponding individual-level competence framework that this macro-structural literature left unresolved.

Our theorizing stops at an aspiration level in Simon's (1997) sense: the threshold is the individual capacity Paper 1's fifth column left unnamed, and we do not theorize past it into the standing question the column also raises (One clarification, above). A comprehensive behavioral account of the enrolled actor would incorporate normative beliefs, institutional rights, and collective resistance strategies.

Finally, scholarship in online dispute resolution articulates the normative obligations owed by the fourth party. Wing et al. (2021), extending Katsh and Rifkin (2001), argue that an institutional intermediary owes disputants procedural transparency, institutional accountability, meaningful contestability, and informed consent. Algorithmic platform coordination operates by systematically withholding these protections — rules remain unobservable, automated determinations lack administrative accountability, contestation channels remain absent, and structural enrollment displaces authentic consent. Curchod et al. (2020) demonstrate the empirical consequences of this design, showing how platform sellers are evaluated across an asymmetric visibility gap by buyers they cannot observe. The three operations of algorithmacy constitute the individual capacities participants must deploy when these institutional protections are absent.

### A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves

A participant's algorithmacy is a capacity to operate within an arrangement, not a judgment on the arrangement's fairness, and the two can move independently. The clearest instance is also the paper's best claim to a genuine aggregation mechanism, and we state it first rather than third. Cameron (2024) shows, in fine behavioral grain, how drivers navigate algorithmic management's constant, confined choices in ways that manufacture consent and stabilize the arrangement (What These Boundaries Share, above); what her account does not supply is the capacity that separates the driver who navigates well from the one who does not. Naming that capacity turns her mechanism into a distributional claim: a workforce's algorithmacy, not any driver's compliance alone, is what determines whether friction surfaces as visible breakdown or is quietly absorbed. That distributional claim is the paper's aggregation obligation as a micro-foundation (Felin, Foss, & Ployhart, 2015) — the clause that lets an individual-level construct say something about how the form holds, not just about how one participant fares within it. The claim is falsifiable at the workforce level, not just the individual one: cohorts matched on task design and gate mechanics but differing in mean algorithmacy should differ in observable friction — churn, appeals, or visible breakdown — and no such difference across otherwise matched cohorts would disconfirm the aggregation mechanism specifically, independent of whether individual algorithmacy still predicts individual outcomes.

Three further propositions follow, none of them tested here, and none of them favorable to reading algorithmacy as an unalloyed good:

**Proposition 1.** Higher algorithmacy improves a participant's own coordination outcomes while leaving the arrangement's procedural justice unchanged: gains concentrate in private navigation rather than in disclosure won, appeals granted, or rules made legible to others. Rahman (2021) documents the individual half of this pattern — reactivity that deepens subordination — and Sutherland et al. (2020) its structural half — literacies that shift coordination burdens onto the participant; the proposition extends both to a competency developed enough to be worth teaching.

**Proposition 2.** Because algorithmacy is unevenly distributed among participants the platform treats as formally equivalent (Foundational Properties, above), that unevenness becomes a new axis of inequality among people the arrangement itself declares interchangeable — a second-level divide (Hargittai, 2002) transposed from general digital-skill gaps onto this specific competency.

**Proposition 3.** Increasing the intermediary's transparency should reduce the algorithmacy required to coordinate through it, which makes the construct's own scope conditions (above) a lever rather than a fixed boundary: transparency mandates are a policy path that does not require developing the competency at all.

These propositions, and the stabilization claim they follow, are the reason the empirical strategy below treats algorithmacy as a capacity to be measured, not a good to be maximized, and they are what the third and fourth studies of the research agenda (Appendix C) are positioned to test directly.

### Dissertation Coherence

The three papers of this dissertation form a single cumulative argument, not three independent studies of a shared topic. Paper 1 identifies the structural gap: organization theory has not theorized coordinative co-optation as fully as hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation, leaving open how a participant takes her place within it. Paper 2 — this paper — supplies the individual competency required to participate in that structure. Paper 3 examines that competency empirically: its distribution across formally equivalent participants, its consequences for coordination outcomes and institutional friction, and its measured performance against neighboring constructs. Each paper does a job the other two cannot do in its place.

# Empirical Strategy: A Concise Summary

The empirical program distinguishes, following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), a core action research project from the thesis project conducted about it. The core project is the two-year initiative itself: the investigator built and repeatedly redesigned the course, the gate, and the instruments across five institutional configurations from Trinidad to Hult, each cycle's evaluation feeding the next cycle's constructing (Appendix B). The thesis project, Paper 3, is insider research on that program's final configuration (Brannick & Coghlan, 2007): it takes no further action within the studied sessions, and its instruments are anonymized precisely to hold the instructor's engagement apart from students' accounts of it. Appendix B gives the full design: the action-research cycle, the site's justification as a strategic research site (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Merton, 1987), researcher positionality, and the abductive analytic procedure (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) governing how transcripts are coded. This section states why the Hult site instantiates coordinative co-optation, the research questions and their instruments, and where the empirical work currently stands.

## Why Hult Instantiates Coordinative Co-optation, Not a Hybrid Form

A reviewer could read the Hult course as an ordinary hybrid of instructor authority, peer evaluation, and automated scoring — hierarchy wearing an algorithmic costume. Several features argue against that reading. The gate renders a single evaluative decision on each submission by aggregating peer reviews and upvotes into a verdict whose underlying weighting is concealed — not a grade the instructor assigns, and not a raw vote count either. That decision binds both sides: the submitter's advancement depends on a tally she cannot see, and the peer evaluators' own votes feed an aggregate that is opaque to them as well, so neither party occupies the position of a Weberian superior administering a known rule. The instructor exercises no vote within the gate (Appendix B). The codebase each cohort evaluates against shifts continuously as peers contribute to it, so the rule environment a submission is judged against is itself moving — the adaptivity condition the site is chosen to instantiate.

Two features of the gate are not yet established with the precision a workshop reviewer is entitled to expect, and we flag rather than guess at them: **whether the algorithm assigns which peers review which submissions**, or whether pairing is left to self-selection; and **what exit or appeal rights** a participant holds if she disputes a verdict. Both are being confirmed for the Paper 3 course design rather than asserted here, and neither claim in this section should be read as settled until they are.

## Research Questions and Instruments

Four questions, one per constitutive operation and the boundary case each is designed to test:

- how participants decode unexplained outcomes generated by the gate (interpreting);
- whether they conceptualize the peer counterpart as an active party to the coordination, or only the gate (specifying intent);
- how they detect and respond to unannounced shifts in what the gate rewards (keeping track);
- how they attribute a perceived evaluative error — to the gate, to peer reviewers, or to both — which tests the candidate fourth operation (Constitutive Operations, above), not keeping track itself.

The fourth question corrects an earlier version of this design, which had mapped error attribution onto keeping track. The two are different phenomena: a participant can detect that a rule has shifted without ever needing to determine who is responsible for a specific bad outcome, and vice versa. The harness interview's four modules (Appendix B) now correspond one-to-one with these four questions, and Table B1 (Appendix B) sets what each module has piloted against what Paper 3 will collect.

## Instruments and Status

A harness interview — an in-editor, AI-administered critical-incident protocol — and a three-wave survey panel (weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the spring 2027 course) together form the design's data collection (Appendix B gives both in full). The panel requires linking one participant's three waves to measure change, which plain anonymity cannot support. Each participant now generates her own confidential code at intake — never known to the investigator — and re-enters it at each wave, preserving anonymity from the researcher's side while permitting within-person linkage across waves. This is a design change, not a caution: it touches the consent language participants see, and should be confirmed with Bentley's IRB before the panel is fielded.

As of this writing, two harness transcripts exist, one of them piloted; neither is coded, and neither is evidence for the construct. One piloting result belongs here rather than waiting for the appendix, because it miniaturizes the construct's stakes: the one fully read respondent satisfied the intent-transmission module by routing an explanation through a second AI system rather than engaging the human counterpart at all — a move the protocol had not anticipated and now explicitly probes for (Appendix B). What else piloting has shown, and what Paper 3 will formally collect, is detailed there.

# Conclusion and Theoretical Boundaries

Algorithmacy is theorized and formulated here, not empirically validated. Because Paper 3's data collection has not yet commenced, this article offers an analytical architecture, a construct definition, and a pair of research instruments refined through piloting rather than through coded data (Appendix B). Whether the three operations cohere into a unified latent construct, maintain empirical independence, and separate cleanly from general software literacy remain open empirical questions that Paper 3 must evaluate. Resolving these questions involves a further methodological consideration: Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) observe that competence operates simultaneously across molar and molecular levels of analysis that cannot be collapsed into one another, so the transition from incident-level qualitative narratives to a standardized psychometric scale must be established systematically at each level of measurement.

Neighboring constructs remain viable alternative explanations. Future empirical testing may reveal that Zhou et al.'s (2025) scale adequately captures the variance we attribute to the missing triadic position, or that Rahman's (2021) model of reactivity fully accounts for worker adaptation under opacity. Similarly, Sutherland et al.'s (2020) relational gig literacy presents a strong alternative. However, we characterize it as operating alongside rather than through binding algorithmic systems; comparative empirical testing has yet to demonstrate algorithmacy's superior predictive power. Sandberg's (2000) phenomenographic findings impose an additional boundary: if competence represents an interpretive conception of work, subsequent psychometric instruments must prove that standardized items preserve this holistic conception rather than fragmenting it into isolated behavioral traits.

Recent scholarship continues to populate adjacent conceptual domains, with contemporary research investigating algorithmic skills (Hong, Cheng, & Liu, 2026), platform work literacies (Scolari, Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés, 2026), human–AI collaboration dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), and metacognitive algorithmic competence (Ayasrah, Al-Rousan, Almulla, & Almulla, 2026). Yet each of these frameworks models competence within a person–system dyad, locating counterparts only as external peers, scheduled offline encounters, or software artifacts. This article advances a theoretical claim about a distinct structural position within an asymmetric triad rather than taxonomic novelty.

Three boundary cases in adjacent fields approach this structural position, and in each instance a specific boundary condition distinguishes it from algorithmacy. In medical education, an unrefereed preprint ("AI-Mediated Relational Competence in Medical Education," 2026) incorporates the patient into an AI-mediated clinical framework, diverging from our model only because ultimate decision-making authority resides with the human physician rather than the algorithmic system. In social work, Yang and Liechty (2026) formulate an AI competence model anchored in client trust and rapport within algorithmic child-welfare risk systems; however, their framework treats the algorithm as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous, binding intermediary. In algorithmic matchmaking, Dredge and Anderson (2021) examine eleven interpersonal competencies that omit the matching algorithm. In contrast, Hu and Zhan (2024) analyze algorithmic awareness while omitting the interpersonal counterpart, even though the underlying platform evaluates both users and unilaterally commits matches. Furthermore, our theoretical framework is explicitly bounded within the organization and coordination of interdependent work; clinical medicine, social welfare administration, and interpersonal dating operate outside this core organizational scope.

Paper 1's analytical classifications provide a comparative baseline that this paper leaves unexamined (Appendix A); the theoretical requirement we address would remain necessary even if coordinative co-optation were more fully theorized across other dimensions. While the Hult course provides a controlled setting to test opacity, whether findings generalize beyond it is what the fourth study in the research agenda (Appendix C) exists to test.

This paper does not measure a population, control for confounding human-capital variables, or assume that acquiring algorithmacy constitutes an unalloyed normative good — the paradox developed above (A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves) is the reason for that caution, not a footnote to it.

This paper provides the theoretical foundation for that broader investigation by formalizing a construct definition that incorporates the human counterpart, systematically reviewing extant frameworks that omit that position, and supplying the micro-foundation Paper 3 measures and Paper 1's structural account still needs.

# Appendix A: Paper 1's Coordination-Form Comparison, in Full

We ground our inquiry in Paper 1 of this dissertation, which evaluated five coordination forms against a uniform analytical architecture and assessed how comprehensively extant scholarship has addressed each foundational dimension. Following the comparative traditions established by Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980), Paper 1 evaluated each form across six core questions: how the form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how an empirical researcher identifies it. Paper 1 defined a form's mechanism — the object of the first question — as the primary operation securing coordination paired with the formal standing of the party reached by that operation. It resolved the second question through the three integrating conditions synthesized by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) — predictability, common understanding, and accountability. Mapping classical theorists onto this modern coordination vocabulary constitutes Paper 1's analytical synthesis. For example, Hayek (1945) provides one of the clearest illustrations, demonstrating how the price mechanism carries the coordinative burden because it operates as a public statistic that no participant must reconstruct privately; Paper 1 interpreted this statistic as delivering predictability and common understanding, while locating market accountability in contract and reputation. The evaluations measure each literature's theoretical completeness rather than the arrangement's empirical prevalence.

The fifth column thins, collapsing precisely where the mechanism inquiry demands an account of participant standing. As Stark and Pais (2020) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) demonstrate, digital platforms neither command, nor contract, nor collaborate; instead, they enroll participants through algorithms that match unchosen parties, evaluate their performance, and unilaterally terminate accounts. While platform scholarship explores algorithmic matching and control (Möhlmann et al., 2021), direction and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020), information asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), and non-portable reputational capital (Curchod et al., 2020), it leaves the enrolled actor's standing unresolved. The participant operates neither as an employee under authority, a buyer evaluating terms, nor a partner in a mutual bond. She is enrolled, yet organization theory has not articulated what individual capacity enables her to coordinate within this architecture. Seven existing constructs approach this capacity closely enough to warrant formal examination — see Extant Constructs, above, and Table 1 there for the summary comparison.

# Appendix B: Full Research Design and Development to Date

## The Research Question

The empirical inquiry investigates how individuals acquire and exercise algorithmacy within opaque, adaptive, and binding coordination arrangements. Formally, we ask:

- how participants decode unexplained outcomes generated by an algorithmic system,
- whether they conceptualize a human counterpart as an active party to the coordination,
- how they detect and respond to unannounced shifts in what the intermediary rewards,
- how they attribute a perceived evaluative error across the system, peer reviewers, or both.

These four inquiries correspond to the construct's constitutive operations and its candidate fourth. Decoding an unexplained outcome captures interpreting in practice; conceptualizing an unseen counterpart operationalizes the relational premise of specifying intent that person–system frameworks neglect; detecting an unannounced shift captures keeping track; and attributing a perceived error tests the candidate fourth operation (locus-of-authority) rather than keeping track, which an earlier version of this design conflated with it. As Pratt (2009) observes, qualitative inquiry admits no generic methodological template — what constitutes empirical sufficiency depends entirely on the nature of the theoretical question posed. The design follows insider action research (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014): the investigator is also the instructor who built and repeatedly redesigned the course this inquiry studies, and the method treats that duality as constitutive of the inquiry rather than a bias to manage down. Because these questions demand observing discrete behavioral occasions where these operations manifest, separate, or intertwine, our research design captures critical incidents across the developmental field sites where the construct originally took shape.

## The Program

Insider action research runs as a spiral of cycles — constructing, planning action, taking action, evaluating action — each cycle's evaluation feeding the next cycle's constructing (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). The initiative's history is that spiral enacted across five institutional configurations, and the same source's concurrent-cycles model supplies the design's grain: the whole program is the long cycle, each cohort a shorter cycle nested inside it, and each evaluated submission or interview shorter still. The program launched in July and August 2025 at two secondary schools in Trinidad and Tobago, then expanded through the following autumn and winter in the constituency of La Brea. Operating as GauntleTT — an intensive, eight-week artificial intelligence engineering curriculum — the initiative partnered with the local Member of Parliament, the national Ministry of Digital Transformation, non-governmental organizations, and the community policing division managing the Hearts and Minds youth gang intervention program. In April and May 2026, an open-enrollment public iteration operated in Boston through cursorboston.com, and the curriculum then transitioned to Hult International Business School in July and August 2026, a public autumn cohort, and a formal, sixteen-week, credit-bearing course beginning in January 2027. Within this final period, participants engineer production-grade software and submit each project as a merged pull request. What each cycle's evaluation changed in the next cycle's constructing is not incidental: a change of site changed who evaluates whom, a change in gate mechanics changed what a participant can see of the evaluation, and a change in population changed what a divergent interpretation could be blamed on. This spring 2027 course serves as the formal empirical research site.

## The Trinidad Field Setting as a Strategic Research Site

The Trinidad cohorts function as what Merton (1987) named a strategic research site — a setting chosen because it exhibits a phenomenon in an unusually tractable form, not because it is convenient to reach. Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) name the same requirement for justifying a single case rather than a representative sample: cases are chosen because they are "unusually revelatory, extreme exemplars, or opportunities for unusual research access," and the Trinidad cohorts meet that standard structurally rather than by convenience. Mainstream platform-work scholarship typically derives its constructs from environments where institutional mechanisms have already pre-sorted participants: Rahman's (2021) freelancers carried years of historical reputation scores, and Zhou and colleagues' (2025) couriers accumulated established delivery routes and tenure, so that observed variance in how workers decode system behavior arrives pre-explained by prior human capital, task specialization, or platform exposure.

The GauntleTT cohorts presented none of these confounding explanations. Platform labor across the Anglophone Caribbean remains emergent and concentrated in transportation, courier delivery, and tourism (ILO, 2025); peer-reviewed scholarship on platform work in Trinidad and Tobago is virtually nonexistent; and a systematic review of algorithmic management identifies only two of 103 empirical studies originating from Latin America and the Caribbean (Chigbu, 2026). An unconventional research context of exactly this kind is a theory-building strategy in its own right, not merely a fallback (Bamberger & Pratt, 2010), and a site read only through constructs formed elsewhere risks showing up as a void rather than as the phenomenon in unobstructed form (Bothello, Nason, & Schnyder, 2019). Without a pre-sorted platform workforce, participants encountered AI-mediated coordination simultaneously, navigating an identical technical curriculum on a synchronized timeline; when two participants confronted an identical unexplained verdict and drew starkly divergent interpretations, that behavioral variance had no conventional explanations to hide behind.

Each cohort iteration evaluated participant submissions through an automated gate, with each configuration withholding information across distinct structural layers. In the Cursor Boston iteration, a large language model screened initial submissions before cohort members voted, with participants observing only their individual evaluative score. At Hult, written peer reviews and upvotes remained public while the underlying aggregate tally remained concealed — an architecture the for-credit course maintains from prior cohorts. In Trinidad, where operational logs were not preserved, we developed a specialized retrospective protocol to reconstruct gate mechanics. By withholding information at three distinct levels — peer scores in Boston, aggregate tallies at Hult, and administrative operational logs in Trinidad — these diverse implementations demonstrate that opacity constitutes a structural property of the coordination arrangement rather than an artifact of a single platform interface.

Insider action research distinguishes the *core* cycle — the organizational initiative itself, which continues on its own terms — from the *thesis* cycle, the inquiry a dissertation conducts about that initiative (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). All iterations preceding the spring 2027 academic course belong to the core cycle: construct development, instrument calibration, and site preparation, not yet the thesis cycle's formal empirical study, which this dissertation reports as Paper 3. The secondary school cohorts, the La Brea initiative, the Boston public program, and the preliminary Hult cohorts provided the empirical ground where the theoretical construct crystallized and where the measurement instruments were designed and piloted. None of it is presented as Paper 3's findings. What this developmental fieldwork yielded instead is an established research site, a harness interview protocol and a survey panel design revised through a first round of piloting, and two harness transcripts — one read for instrument function, the second not yet read.

## The Research Site and Researcher Positionality

Paper 3's formal empirical investigation begins in a credit-bearing course at Hult International Business School in the spring semester of 2027. Students submit technical coursework to an automated evaluation gate that returns decisions without full procedural explanation, directly mapping onto the constitutive elements of our construct definition — the gate functions as the intermediary, its unexplained verdict embodies operational opacity, and the underlying codebase represents the adaptive rule environment, shifting dynamically alongside peer contributions. The parties evaluated on the far side of the gate are student peers whose qualitative reviews and upvotes remain publicly visible. In contrast, their aggregate scoring weights remain withheld, allowing participants to observe the counterpart judging while the decisive scoring mechanism stays out of view. This setting provides a conservative "hard case" test for opacity because participants construct the very technical systems to which they submit work. Opacity, however, constitutes an emergent property of the person–arrangement relationship; what the arrangement withholds even from its builder-participants is the aggregate scoring algorithm and its shifting decision thresholds, even as the underlying source code remains inspectable. Whether the algorithm assigns reviewer pairs, and what exit or appeal rights a participant holds, are being confirmed for the formal course design and are not yet asserted here (Empirical Strategy, above).

Because the investigator serves as course instructor, role duality is not a threat this design manages down but the premise action research is built on: it "require[s] a practitioner of science who is not only an engaged participant, but also incorporates the perspective of the critical and analytical observer, not as a validating instance but as integral to the practice" (Riordan, 1995, as cited in Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). What still needs stating is where that duality stops and observation begins: the "action" this design studies operates at the level of redesigning the course, the gate, and the interview instrument across cohorts, not at the level of any single evaluated session, and the instructor exercises no vote within the peer gate. Participant intake for the harness interview is fully anonymized to neutralize institutional power asymmetries — no records track invitations or identify participating students, establishing the structural separation that Ferguson, Yonge, and Myrick (2004) prescribe for faculty conducting research within their own classrooms. This ethical safeguard entails an operational constraint detailed explicitly in the institutional consent agreement: because harness responses cannot be linked to individual student identifiers, submissions cannot be retracted once entered, rendering the pre-submission participant review step essential. The survey panel's linkage mechanism is necessarily different, and is specified separately below.

Furthermore, interviews occur exclusively after evaluative project cycles have formally closed. Brannick and Coghlan (2007) supply the practical grammar for holding that duality inside insider research, while Anteby (2013) shows that deep personal involvement and analytical distance operate as complementary dimensions rather than opposing poles, with instructional engagement grounding the empirical validity of the instrument's vocabulary. Although Mercer (2007) notes the residual demand characteristics inherent in student-instructor research, methodological trustworthiness is secured through an auditable research trail (Pratt, Kaplan, & Whittington, 2020) aligned with foundational qualitative quality criteria (Tracy, 2010), maintaining interview protocols in a public repository while securely archiving raw responses.

## The Harness Interview Protocol and Abductive Design

The harness interview serves as the primary qualitative instrument for gathering empirical evidence. The protocol prompts participants to complete a designated technical task, receive an outcome that the system leaves incompletely explained, and articulate both their retrospective interpretation of that verdict and their subsequent behavioral response. Four structured modules organize the inquiry — decoding gate requirements, transmitting communicative intent, identifying unannounced rule modifications, and managing perceived evaluative errors — corresponding one-to-one with the four research questions above. Each module operationalizes the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954), requiring an end-to-end narration of a specific occasion that includes the participant's baseline task, the system's output, the immediate tactical response, and the ultimate resolution. If an initial response yields a high-level generalization, the protocol prompts the participant to anchor her account in a concrete occurrence; similarly, an initial negative response triggers a single reframing prompt from an alternative angle before the module concludes. The protocol excludes all formal theoretical nomenclature, ensuring that respondents formulate accounts using native conceptual categories rather than mimicking researcher-provided frameworks, thereby preserving the authentic structure of participant sensemaking.

The instrument is administered through a situated, in-editor architecture. An embedded artificial intelligence assistant conducts the interview sequentially within the participant's software development environment. The agent executes automated, client-side anonymization in real time, redacting personal names, organizational affiliations, specific geographic localities, and calendar dates before the participant reviews the generated transcript. After the interview, the participant inspects, edits, or deletes text at her discretion before formal submission. Anonymized data are then transmitted to an independent third-party endpoint that programmatically rejects any transcript lacking explicit participant validation, ensuring the technical pipeline enforces ethical verification deterministically alongside individual consent.

The instrument reproduces, in miniature, the structure it studies: the participant coordinates with an unseen researcher through an AI intermediary whose probing behavior she cannot inspect. We treat this as a validity consideration rather than an ornament — an automated interviewer's session-to-session variance is uncontrolled, and an account performed for a machine may differ from one performed for a person — and the full prompt-and-probe sequence of every session is logged so that instrument behavior can be examined alongside participant responses when Paper 3's corpus is analyzed. The transcript this produces is also, for the same reason, a co-produced and edited artifact rather than a raw record: the participant reviews, edits, and can delete text before submission, and the agent redacts as it writes. Revisiting, below, returns to the transcript as inscribed, not to an unmediated observation, and that distinction is worth stating plainly rather than assuming away.

We designed three tailored protocols to capture distinct organizational vantage points. The student protocol gathers experiential accounts from individuals participating in the instructional cohorts; the operations protocol collects operational reflections from instructional staff and institutional partner organizations; and the self protocol captures retrospective testimony from the primary investigator to reconstruct the historical gate mechanics of the Trinidad cohorts, administered by an automated agent programmed to restrict inquiries to factual operational events rather than post hoc theoretical interpretations.

Action research names the design and the practitioner's relation to it; it does not on its own specify how a transcript gets analyzed, and Coghlan and Brannick (2014) license borrowing a qualitative technique for that purpose once its compatibility with the design is checked. The analytical architecture here follows an abductive logic of inquiry (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012). Abduction here means a cultivated theoretical position rather than a blank slate or a fixed a priori framework: the analyst reads broadly enough across candidate theories, before and during fieldwork, that a transcript can register as surprising, then works that surprise through three named moves. Revisiting returns to the same inscribed observation as often as the record allows — a transcript reread after a later interview, a code reconsidered after a discard — because its relevance changes with each pass; the harness interview's own reframing prompt, which returns to a thin or negative answer from an alternative angle before a module closes, performs the same move inside a single session. Defamiliarization treats the act of transcription itself as a technique of estrangement, holding what a participant renders obvious open to a second look. Alternative casing works a transcript against more than one theoretical account in turn, and it is why a participant who reports full transparency about the gate counts as evidence against the construct rather than as noise set aside.

The three constitutive operations of algorithmacy function within this design as sensitizing concepts in Blumer's (1954) sense — directions along which to look, not fixed benchmarks a transcript must match, open to revision or discard by what the data show. Bowen (2006) supplies the applied precedent for taking that openness seriously rather than treating it as a hedge: of three sensitizing concepts he specified in advance for a study of Jamaican antipoverty projects, one survived into the emergent theory and two were discarded outright, a result he reports as evidence of rigor rather than of failure. First-cycle coding stays in the participant's own terms before any operation is applied, the discipline Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013) formalize as first-order analysis: the two methods are compatible rather than competing, since Gioia and colleagues describe their own procedure, once coding turns back to the literature, as itself a shift from induction toward a form of abductive research. What the design declines is the full data-structure apparatus built on that discipline, which even its authors caution against using as a template outside the large informant sets it was built for. The same logic sets the negative case boundary: a participant who successfully decodes and satisfies the gate through individual technical mastery alone, without engaging or conceptualizing the human counterpart, is the disconfirming instance the three operations must be able to produce if they are to survive contact with the transcripts.

## The Longitudinal Survey Panel

The survey panel serves as the quantitative component of the research design, executing a three-wave longitudinal assessment administered across the sixteen-week semester at weeks 1, 4, and 16 — capturing an initial baseline, an evaluation following the first build-and-review cycle, and a concluding assessment. Because measuring change requires linking one participant's three waves, and the harness interview's full anonymity cannot support that linkage, the panel uses a separate mechanism: each participant generates a confidential, self-chosen code at intake — known only to her, never recorded against her identity by the investigator — and re-enters it at each subsequent wave. This preserves the same anonymity from the researcher's side that the harness interview provides while permitting within-person linkage across waves; the consent language covering the panel states this mechanism explicitly and separately from the harness interview's consent, and both should be confirmed with Bentley's IRB before the panel is fielded. Self-generated codes carry a known cost — participants misremember them, and unmatched waves are routine in this literature (Yurek, Vasey, & Havens, 2008) — so the code is derived from stable personal elements rather than left to free recall, and the realized linkage rate is reported as a design outcome rather than assumed.

The course enrolls approximately fifty students a semester, which sets what the panel can and cannot do. Zhou et al.'s (2025) own scale-development samples ran 213 to 275 per analysis — four to five times what one cohort supplies — so this panel does not attempt a fresh exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis, and Paper 3 does not claim one. What a cohort of this size can support is narrower and still useful: the second wave administers Zhou et al.'s (2025) instrument as a first, descriptive check on convergence with interpreting and specifying intent, and the three waves together describe within-person change over the semester rather than establish latent-factor dimensionality. Formal discriminant-validity testing against Zhou et al.'s scale, at a sample this design can actually power, is the second study of the research agenda (Appendix C), not this panel. The qualitative harness and the survey panel serve complementary analytical functions even at this smaller scale: the harness captures critical behavioral incidents and participants' native cognitive conceptions in their own words, while the panel describes how those same operations move over one semester for one cohort. The Bentley University Institutional Review Board issued an exempt determination for the panel's original three-wave design on 11 May 2026 (Protocol 260511078); the self-generated-code linkage mechanism is a change to that design and needs its own confirmation. Together, the self-protocol narrative, the harness interviews, and the survey panel answer to what Reason and Marshall (1987, as cited in Coghlan & Brannick, 2014) call research's three audiences: the self-protocol is for the investigator's own learning, the harness and survey findings are for the program, feeding the next cycle's constructing, and the dissertation itself is for the field this paper addresses.

## Instrument Piloting: Two Pre-Qualitative Responses

Two harness interview transcripts exist as of this writing, and both belong to the core cycle's piloting, not the thesis cycle's formal study. The first is a student-protocol response read informally for instrument function; the second, recorded 22 August 2026, has not yet been read at all. Neither has entered the abductive procedure specified above — no sensitizing-concept coding, no second coder, no discard log, no saturation claim — and neither counts as evidence for or against the three constitutive operations. Their job at this stage is narrower: telling us whether the harness elicits material an abductive coder could later work with, and where it does not.

On that narrower test, the one fully read transcript did three things worth reporting, none of them a finding about algorithmacy. It produced a critical incident with the concreteness the design needs — a specific adverse gate outcome, the rationale the respondent offered for it, and the absence of any channel to contest it — rather than the generalized summary the protocol later had to guard against. It also surfaced a gap the original instrument had not anticipated: a respondent can satisfy a module's letter by routing an explanation through a second AI system rather than by engaging the human counterpart, and nothing in the protocol distinguished the two. And in two of the four modules — unannounced rule changes and perceived evaluative errors — it accepted a general summary in place of a specific occasion, material too thin for the design's own commitment to concrete, revisitable observation.

Each of these is a piloting result, and each changed the instrument rather than advanced a claim about the construct. The routing gap became an explicit distinction the protocols now probe for; the two permissive modules were tightened to require a specific behavioral incident before closing, with an automated reframing prompt when a respondent offers a brief or negative answer. What the pilot demonstrates is that the harness, run once, produces text an abductive coder can work with and exposes its own weak points — an argument for the instrument's readiness, not for what participants' accounts will show once coded.

## What Paper 3 Will Do

The thesis cycle's formal empirical work is Paper 3, not this article. Where this paper offers a construct, a research design, and two harness transcripts — one read informally for instrument function, the second not yet read at all — Paper 3 offers coded data, a blind second coder, a stopping-rule claim, and a first descriptive check against Zhou and colleagues' (2025) scale — the properly controlled analysis the spring 2027 Hult course exists to supply, at the scale one cohort can support.

Paper 3's qualitative arm applies the abductive procedure specified above to a corpus gathered across all three protocols through the concluding weeks of the course: critical incidents in which participants reconstruct peer voters' expectations, instances of strategic specification in code submissions aimed at unseen peers, occasions on which participants detect an uncodified shift in the evaluation rule, and accounts of how evaluative errors get attributed across the system, the peer reviewers, or both. A second coder blind to this paper's derivation of the three operations performs an independent first-order pass in participant-centric terms before either coder applies the operations as sensitizing concepts; divergences between the two passes are logged rather than adjudicated toward agreement, since the abductive procedure treats a divergence as a candidate surprise, not an error. A discard log records any sensitizing concept the transcripts fail to support. Sufficiency, following the abductive logic specified above, is reached when a module's incidents stop generating a casing the derived operations cannot already accommodate — not a fixed sample size, and not the grounded-theory saturation rule this design's stopping point is sometimes mistaken for. # Appendix C: A Research Agenda

Four programmatic studies translate algorithmacy from a theoretical proposal into an empirically validated construct, each establishing definitive falsification criteria. The first runs on the spring 2027 Hult cohort alongside the survey panel described above, and together they constitute Paper 3; the second returns to the same course across the semesters following spring 2027 to reach the sample the discriminant test needs; the third and fourth extend beyond it, to a broader population and to settings outside the author's own institutional stake.

The first study executes the qualitative investigation initiated in the spring 2027 course, gathering critical incidents across the three interview protocols until each module's incidents stop generating a casing the derived operations cannot already accommodate — the abductive sufficiency criterion specified above, not grounded-theory saturation — across all modules in Table B1 (Appendix B). This empirical corpus will formally resolve the candidate fourth operation. Specifically, Rahman's (2021) "who-influences" dimension will be established as an autonomous fourth operation if transcripts systematically reveal participants who decode all observable system feedback yet remain structurally incapable of discerning whether the platform firm or peer evaluators drove an outcome; if no such empirical pattern emerges, the tripartite architecture stands as specified.

The second study conducts the formal psychometric discriminant test the panel above cannot power on its own. One semester of the credit-bearing Hult course supplies roughly fifty respondents, a quarter of Zhou et al.'s (2025) 213-to-275-per-analysis scale-development samples, so this study pools wave-2 administrations of an algorithmacy scale alongside Zhou et al.'s (2025) algorithmic competency scale across successive semesters of the same course until the pooled sample reaches that range. We evaluate pre-registered latent correlation predictions established before data collection — specifically testing Understanding against interpreting, Leveraging against specifying intent, and Embracing against an unassigned null baseline. Correlations in the .30-to-.60 range on each pairing will support the construct's discriminant validity; a heterotrait-monotrait ratio exceeding .85 (Henseler, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2015) on any pairing collapses that theoretical boundary and disconfirms the construct's distinctiveness on that dimension.

The third study investigates the institutional distribution and selection dynamics of the competency across a population of formally equivalent participants — the study positioned to test the stabilization mechanism and Propositions 1 and 2 above. Measuring algorithmacy across this baseline tests whether individual variance in the competency predicts coordination success after controlling for baseline technical skill, task complexity, and platform tenure. This design also addresses the structural selection question by tracking low-scoring participants longitudinally, documenting how the organizational form absorbs coordination friction — whether through elevated worker churn, voluntary platform exit, or the psychological internalization of systemic failures as individual shortcomings — and clarifying the institutional costs an organization incurs when the competency is scarce.

The fourth study evaluates external generalizability by deploying the instrument in conventional, non-builder platform settings where the author holds no institutional stake — such as on-demand ride-hailing fleets, piecework micro-task queues, or online freelance marketplaces where clients remain anonymous, and workers exercise no agency over system design. Observing the manifestation and empirical separation of the three operations across these naturalistic settings will demonstrate that algorithmacy operates as a generalizable coordination construct rather than an artifact of instructional software environments, and is also where Proposition 3 (transparency reducing required algorithmacy) is testable against sites that vary naturally in disclosure.
