## Abstract

Algorithmacy, the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls, is the competency through which a person integrates herself into a newer form of coordination, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. In this paper we begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, tracing its finding that this newer form is less developed than hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation. We present a review of extant constructs, arranged according to what each construct makes of the algorithm. We then propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, sufficient because it includes the counterpart, not only the system. We end by reporting early qualitative research using a harness interview, including several responses obtained to date, and by locating the continuing inquiry in a specialized course at Hult University, where we refine the instrument toward subsequent quantitative assessment.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication competency · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# Introduction

Coordination — the process by which people collectively perform interdependent work — remains a central concern of organization theory. We now have a substantial body of knowledge about the forms through which that work is coordinated. That knowledge describes three familiar forms: hierarchy, market, and network, which coordinate by authority, by price, and by ongoing relationship. However, this picture does not yet include the newer form in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm, leaving us without an account of how a person integrates herself into that form. We develop that account here.

This paper has three main purposes. We begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, including its comparison of those three forms on a common set of questions (how each form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher would recognize it). Paper 1 also examined classical co-optation, in which an organization facing opposition brings the opponent inside and confers a seat. Moreover, it examined coordinative co-optation, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm that matches them, ranks them, and can close their accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). After comparing those five forms, Paper 1 found coordinative co-optation less developed than the other four, and we proceed from there. Finally, we review extant constructs that do not satisfy that account, propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, and describe our early empirical work with participants.

As we examined the literature, we identified several candidates. For example, we found that some of these constructs treat the algorithm as a channel between two people (Spitzberg, 2006). In some cases the algorithm is treated as a communicative partner, or as an agent that modifies or transmits the speaker's message (Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). And while knowledge of the system and the capacity to navigate it are indeed indispensable in this kind of work (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), they are conceptually separate from coordination with a human counterpart. In a few instances we also drew on research that treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021), which approaches the problem and nevertheless stops at the system. Each construct places a person on one side of a relation and a system on the other, and leaves the human counterpart — a client, an evaluator, or another participant — outside the competence it specifies.

We then define algorithmacy on the same pattern as literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Literacy is the ability to read and write, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through text. Numeracy is the ability to use and interpret quantitative information, so that a person can participate in situations that run through number. Oracy is the ability to speak and listen, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through speech. Algorithmacy is the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it, so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls. By opaque we mean that the person cannot observe the rule; by adaptive, that the rule may change; by intermediary, the algorithm that mediates between the person and her counterpart. Literacy, numeracy, and oracy are competences for a medium. Algorithmacy is a competence for a coordination. That is why we argue that it fulfills the requirements Paper 1 defined. After presenting that definition, we turn to our work with participants who are acquiring algorithmacy. This empirical work remains at an early stage. We offer a qualitative, narrative account of what we have observed thus far, based on a harness interview in which participants complete a task, receive an outcome that is not fully explained, and are asked how they interpreted that outcome and what they did next. We have obtained several responses and are refining the interview iteratively, in order to improve its clarity and its usefulness to participants, until the instrument can be administered at greater scale and can support quantitative assessment. The continuing site for this inquiry is a specialized course at Hult University.

# Paper 1 of This Dissertation

We begin with Paper 1 of this dissertation, which set five forms of coordination on one set of questions and graded how fully each form's literature had answered them. Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980) had already compared two or more forms on named dimensions; Paper 1 followed that practice with six questions: how the form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher would recognize it. Paper 1 defined a form's mechanism, the first question's object, as the operation that secures the form together with the standing of the party that operation reaches, and answered the second question with the three integrating conditions Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) assembled: predictability, common understanding, and accountability. Simon, Weber, Hayek, Powell, and Granovetter never wrote in that vocabulary, and laying their accounts onto it is Paper 1's own work. Hayek (1945) is the sharpest case: he showed why the price can carry the load, because it is a public statistic no participant has to reconstruct in private, and Paper 1 read that statistic as delivering predictability and common understanding, locating the market's accountability in contract and reputation. The grades record how fully a literature had answered each question, not whether the arrangement it names is real or common. We carry the result forward as Table 1.

The four older columns are full. Paper 1, drawing on Simon (1997) and Weber (1978), placed a person in hierarchy by her acceptance of a zone of acceptance — the range of orders a subordinate will follow; drawing on Hayek, in the market by her reading of a posted term she remains free to refuse; drawing on Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985), in the network by her investment in a partner she chose and can withdraw from; and drawing on Selznick (1949), in classical co-optation by a conferred seat that converts opposition into participation. A zone, a term, a relation, a seat: in each answered form, the party the operation reaches holds a standing the literature can name.

The fifth column thins, and it is empty exactly where the mechanism question asks for standing. Stark and Pais (2020) argue that platforms neither contract, command, nor collaborate as their primary operation: they enroll participants through an algorithm, and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) develop that enrollment into an organizational form with an object of management and a direction of accountability of its own. The algorithm matches parties who do not choose each other, ranks them, and can close their accounts. The participant is neither an employee who accepted a zone of authority, nor a buyer free to take the same terms elsewhere, nor a partner who entered a relation she can revise. She is enrolled.

Enrollment answers the operation half of the mechanism question, and the platform sources answer much of the rest. Möhlmann, Zalmanson, Henfridsson, and Gregory (2021) describe matching joined to control, Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) direction and evaluation, Rosenblat and Stark (2016) the information asymmetries, and Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) ratings that stay where they were earned. What none of those sources says — the open half-cell in Table 1 — is what standing the enrolled party holds and what she must be able to do to take part. Seven constructs already in print come near enough to that ability to deserve a hearing, and we turn to them now.

# Extant Constructs

A construct, here, is a named account of that ability: a competence, a literacy, or a way of interpreting the system. Constructs that include the counterpart already exist on another shelf: relational coordination names a quality of communicating and relating among parties doing interdependent work (Gittell, 2002), and it presupposes exactly what this arrangement removes, parties who can see, reach, and answer one another. We therefore ask of each construct what it makes of the algorithm, because the algorithm is what stands where that mutual visibility used to be. Seven accounts describe people working with algorithms, and they do not make the same thing of it. That difference is the line each competence can draw.

We hear seven: computer-mediated communication competence treats the computer as a channel between two people (Spitzberg, 2006); human–machine communication treats the system as a communicative partner (Guzman & Lewis, 2020); AI-mediated communication treats it as an agent on a speaker's side (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020); AI literacy, as an object of knowledge (Long & Magerko, 2020); algorithmic competency, as an object of navigation (Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025); reactivity under opaque evaluation, as an object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021); and gig literacies, as a marketplace to use and to work around (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, & Nelson, 2020). We take them in that order, asking in each hearing where the person on the far side — the client who hires, the evaluator whose judgement moves a score — then sits.

## Computer-Mediated Communication Competence

The first answers came from communication research, which met the computer as a channel. Spitzberg (2006) treats that computer as a medium between two people, and computer-mediated communication competence as the ability to use it so that a message reaches a counterpart. He carries a five-part model of interpersonal competence into mediated interaction (motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and outcomes), and every part faces the partner: motivation is the ratio of approach to avoidance in interacting with her, knowledge is of what makes the interaction appropriate and effective for her, and the four skill clusters — attentiveness, composure, coordination, expressiveness — are skills toward her. Five outcome criteria close the model (appropriateness, effectiveness — subsuming task achievement and efficiency — coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development), and two of them are administered by the partner: appropriateness is legitimacy as she perceives it, satisfaction her affect. Mediation, on this account, changes the parameters of competence rather than its structure. The parties on either end remain the coordinators.

He offered the model as a typology of competence between people, and wrote a measure to test it; the hypothesized structure did not appear as drawn. Nothing in that model gives the medium a stake of its own. The channel constrains the parties; it does not interpret them, rank them, or commit a decision neither of them controls. The competence he specifies ends when the message has reached a person, and it does not begin again when a system ranks both sides and commits a decision.

## Human–Machine Communication

That channel picture could not hold once machines were built to talk with a person. Guzman and Lewis (2020) treat the system as a communicative partner. Their article is an agenda rather than a measure. Communicative AI is “designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects” (p. 71); conversational agents, social robots, and automated-writing software are their examples; and meaning-making occurs “within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine” (p. 74). They leave the channel picture: computer-mediated communication theorizes technology as a mediator of human interaction, and human–machine communication theorizes the machine as a communicator.

The agenda has three parts, and in all three the other pole is the device. The functional, relational, and metaphysical parts all aim at the person–machine pair. Guzman and Lewis still allow machines to mediate; what they refuse is to relegate technology to only that role. A counterpart reached through that machine is outside the work the agenda names.

## AI-Mediated Communication

A further move kept a human counterpart in view and put the machine on one party's side. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) treat the system as an agent that operates on a communicator's behalf. Their formal definition of AI-mediated communication is “mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals.” Both humans are inside that definition; smart replies, predictive text, and emerging video manipulation are the agent's worked examples. The article's parameters are magnitude, medium, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation — the parameters of a delegate.

That agent still sits on a party's side of the exchange and produces messages that the sender or the receiver could in principle have produced. The counterpart remains an addressee, and any competence the agenda implies sits with the speaker or her delegate. Even a fully autonomous agent, on their definition, still operates on behalf of a communicator. A rating that can close an account does not.

## AI Literacy

A different literature asked what a person must know about the system itself. Long and Magerko (2020) treat that system as an object of knowledge. AI literacy, in their definition, is “a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace.” They organize that literature under five questions: what AI is, what it can do, how it works, how it should be used, and how people perceive it. Seventeen competencies follow, overwhelmingly about knowing the system: recognizing AI and distinguishing general from narrow AI under the first question; strengths, weaknesses, and imagined futures under the second; representations, decision-making, machine learning, data literacy, and sensors under the third; ethics alone under the fourth; programmability alone under the fifth. Every competency is a relation between a person and a technology. The one that names humans at all (competency 10, *Human Role in AI*) names them as the system's makers.

No competency names another person as a party to an interaction the learner is conducting. Their second clause, communicate and collaborate with AI, puts the machine in that person's chair, a dyad by their own wording. Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) compressed the seventeen into know and understand AI, use and apply AI, evaluate and create AI, and ethical issues; later instruments inherit those four cells, and every cell is a relation between a person and a technology.

## Algorithmic Competency

Twelve items, four dimensions, five samples of Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers, an overall reliability of .85: Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) built what they call the first scale of algorithmic competency — a worker's understanding of the platform algorithms that assign and evaluate work, together with the ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms. The construct treats the system as an object of navigation, and its ancestry is stated. Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) named algorithmic competencies for gig workers (sensemaking, circumventing, manipulating); the same line runs through the gig literacies heard below; and Zhou and colleagues built their first three dimensions from that 2019 inventory.

The four dimensions are not a knowledge test: Understanding, Embracing, Leveraging, and Remediating. Understanding is a grasp of how algorithmic management works; Embracing, a willingness to trust the system's efficiency; Leveraging, using the system strategically for the worker's benefit — each still of the apparatus. Remediating is repairing the system's deficiencies, and it holds the one place in the scale where anyone contests an outcome: the item built from a courier answering a customer's unfair rating routes the evidence “to the AM.”

The counterpart appears in their items as a rating source (“I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback”), a matching function inside a belief about the platform's efficiency, or a repair crew in WeChat groups, and in their model as customer-oriented service behaviour, an outcome measured separately. Their site fixes those items; they do not transfer to a peer gate of the kind described below, where the tally is withheld. Navigating the apparatus is what the scale measures; coordinating with the person the apparatus matched is not.

## Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation

The 2015 Success Score on a high-skill freelancing platform replaced a transparent five-star system with an opaque hundred-point number; Rahman (2021) built the invisible cage from what workers did next. He treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation: a score whose criteria are hidden and can shift without notice, set against the Weberian iron cage, whose rules are fixed and knowable. Five components make the cage invisible: hidden and shifting evaluation criteria, execution at uncertain intervals, unidentifiable magnitude, uncertain impact on reputation, and an unspecified influence on the score, client or platform — that last the counterpart appearing as a failure of interpretation.

Workers answered the cage in two ways: experimental reactivity tests tactics to raise the score, constrained reactivity limits engagement to protect it, and which form a worker enacts turns on platform dependence and evaluation setbacks. The counterpart was in his field the whole time. Rahman interviewed eighteen clients alongside eighty freelancers, and the platform had already decided what their judgements would do: TalentFinder told clients that feedback “will be kept anonymous and never shared directly with the freelancer.” That is the counterpart's judgement, routed through the system and withheld from the person it judges: the position this paper describes, documented as an interface decision in the platform's own words. A worker can hold a fully serviceable theory of the score and still have no way to answer a rating she cannot see.

## Gig Literacies

The nearest account put the counterpart in the sample. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) call their gig literacies “critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing” (p. 457): using the resources the platform provides and working around its imposed structures. They interviewed twenty Upwork freelancers and nineteen clients, and the clients' narratives “provided some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers” (p. 463).

The findings are five literacies (pp. 463–469): building reputation (the Job Success Score, badges, and refunds), self-presentation (profiles and chat), maintaining productivity (routines and third-party tools), mitigating transaction risk (escrow and vetting), and building relationships. Under that fifth literacy, workers try to “build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship” for repeat business (p. 468); P39, unable to recover from an early bad rating, continued work off-platform with clients first met on Upwork. The construct remains freelancer skills beside the platform, not through a binding intermediary, and a site the client can leave cannot confirm it. They mark the scope themselves: on Upwork the worker can choose and talk to the client; on Uber the algorithm assigns (p. 470).

## What These Boundaries Share

Seven constructs, seven boundaries, one shape: a person on one side, a system on the other. The field has audited itself and found the same. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) reviewed fifty algorithmic-literacy studies, ninety-six screened across four databases; Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub (2024) cover one hundred and sixty-nine and find no cohesive framework; Iyamu, Wheelans, Haag, Roe, and Chang (2026) chart twelve health-worker studies against Nutbeam's model (its communicative level, explaining AI-mediated information to a patient the system does not also judge, is the field's nearest slot for a second human) and report communicative literacy “largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and measurement instruments.” Adding items has not moved that line in fifty studies. The field was handed the slot and filled it with system-facing measurement.

Visibility labour closes the pincer from the other side. Abidin (2016) names the work a follower performs to become visible to a specific influencer — a real, named counterpart — and sets the algorithm outside the account by her own choice, marking her subject as analogue affective labour apart from studies of algorithmic visibility. Every construct that centres the algorithm has no counterpart; the one account with a counterpart has no algorithm. Nobody has both.

The position itself is not unnoticed. Katsh and Rifkin (2001) named the technology that hosts an online dispute the fourth party, a participant with effects of its own. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020), cited earlier for ratings that stay on site, set customer reviews “in place of traditional dyadic exchanges” and named the triad those reviews enact — platform operator, buyers, sellers — held open by a visibility gap that lets the buyer judge unseen. Cameron (2024) drew the same figure for on-demand work as the algorithmic labor triangle, and Healy and Pekarek (2024) draw it again in the study of work and employment. All four are theories of power, consent, or vulnerability.

None of the four writes a competence for the party the triangle traps, and that is the claim this paper defends, bounded four ways: it concerns constructs of a party's capacity; the counterpart must be a party to the interaction, not the system's makers, a worker's peers, or a patient downstream; the intermediary must read both parties and bind them, where a channel carries, a delegate serves, and the fourth party works inside a disclosed process; and the claim is about a position, never a name. Names in this field are cheap and getting cheaper. On those conditions the seven hearings and the field's own audits return one verdict: no competence yet written gives the counterpart a position. Practice has meanwhile entered the position from the other side: Manky (2026) reports forty Lima ride-hailing drivers who use the platform's own data to read the passenger, a counterpart-facing capacity framed as security, with no construct yet written for it. What the arrangement still lacks is a competence written for the position the field has already mapped. The next section writes one.

# Algorithmacy

Suddaby (2010) asks four things of a construct that claims such a position: a precise definition, scope conditions, semantic relations to its neighbours, and coherence with the larger argument. We gave the definition in the introduction; clarity, as he insists, is not validity. We set out the other three here, then what the construct buys.

## Scope Conditions

The construct applies where an intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and binding on both parties, the conditions we have already named. It does not apply, or applies only trivially, where the rule is published and static, or where the parties meet and revise terms without it. A posted price with a walk-away right is the market case Paper 1 already described. A published office rule with a named superior is the hierarchy case. Upwork-style freelance markets sit on the edge: the platform matches and scores, but the client can still take the relation off-site. Ride-hail and a peer gate that withholds its tally sit nearer the center. Scope is a claim about arrangements, not about industries. These are also the terms Paper 1's open cell demands: an account of what a person must be able to do in order to take part, stated so that a researcher could recognize its absence.

## Semantic Relations

The neighbours occupy the positions those hearings described; we state the relations as predictions. We expect Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's Understanding to correlate moderately with interpreting, and their Leveraging with specifying intent; their Embracing has no counterpart in the definition, because an attitude toward the platform's efficiency is not an operation performed through it. Rahman's reactivity is orthogonal on a different axis: he cuts by engagement, this construct cuts by direction and time. Correlations high enough to collapse those distinctions would take the construct with them.

## The Three Operations, and Why Not Four

The closing clause of the definition requires the three operations; we did not collect them from a list of practices. To interpret is to reconstruct, from outcomes, what the system and the unseen counterpart are doing. To specify intent is to make what one meant survive the few signals the system will carry, so the counterpart can receive it. To keep track is to notice that a rule binding both parties has moved when nobody announced it. A competence that stopped at the system would need only the first, and Rahman already describes that first.

The count faces one live fourth candidate in the paper's own sources: Rahman's fifth cage component, who influences the evaluation — client or platform. We keep it inside interpreting, because uncertainty about which party moved the score is a failure to reconstruct what the system and the counterpart are doing. Our refusal is falsifiable in the field. Incidents in which a participant has interpreted everything the arrangement shows and still cannot tell which party to interpret would make who-influences an operation of its own, and the instrument we describe below can collect them.

The literacy family supplies the name and then marks the limit of the analogy. Long and Magerko (2020) run the family argument themselves (literacy for written language, then digital, computational, scientific, and data literacies by extension), and their construct still runs person-to-system, so shelf membership settles nothing. What the family does settle is the count: each older member carries two operations, one receptive and one expressive — reading and writing, speaking and listening, interpreting number and using it — because text and number hold still while a person reads them. Keeping track is what a competence acquires when the medium is also a moving party.

## Competency, Not Skill, Not Capability

This is a competency rather than a skill or a capability. A skill can be taught as a rule, and the rule here is undisclosed. A capability is held by the party that owns the routine (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997), and the platform holds the ratings, the matching, and the rule. Sandberg (2000) showed, with twenty engine optimizers drawn from a department of about fifty at the Volvo Car Corporation, that competence is the worker's way of conceiving the work, not a list of attributes identified independently of it. Six of his informants optimized separate qualities, ten optimized interacting qualities, and four optimized from the customer's perspective. The customer in that third conception is an orientation of the job, not a party reached through a binding intermediary. That result stands; the rationalistic definition he wrote the article to refute does not.

Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) went further in the other direction: for them competence is not a property of a person at all but a quality of a relationship, produced jointly and judged by both parties, and Spitzberg (2006) carries the commitment into mediated settings, where co-interactants raise or lower one another's competence. That standard is the one this arrangement cannot meet. The counterpart never reaches a position from which she could judge, and what the construct names is what a party supplies when the relational test has nobody to administer it. The construct belongs with these accounts, and its index is the form, whatever tool implements it.

## Properties: Acquired by Participation, Unevenly Held

The competency is acquired by participation: there is no published rule to instruct from, and if explicit teaching produced the same distribution as use, the property would be wrong. Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp, and Raupach (2023) built the SNAIL scale to evaluate whether AI-literacy courses teach; its declared use is what a course can install, and algorithmacy predicts that no course could install it. The competency is also uneven among people the arrangement treats as equivalent. If it were uniform, it would explain none of the differences in outcome that appear among participants facing the same system.

## What the Construct Buys

The construct, if it is real, changes what a coordination researcher can ask. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) frame these workplaces as contested terrain: direction, evaluation, and discipline are what the system does to workers. Algorithmacy asks what the person must be able to do, and the two questions have different remedies. A rule the platform is forced to publish changes what there is to interpret; a competency changes who can interpret it.

The question of what the person must be able to do joins a conversation already in print. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) named the form in *Organization Theory*, and Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) and Cameron (2024) traced, in *Administrative Science Quarterly*, what enrollment does to the parties it holds. This paper answers, in the same register, the competence question that work left open.

If participation requires the competency and the arrangement cannot teach it, the form's viability depends on how the competency is distributed, and a form full of low-algorithmacy participants selects for something — churn, exit, or coordination failures absorbed as personal ones — a question Paper 1's comparative frame can now receive.

The construct also satisfices, in Simon's (1997) sense of the word we used in the abstract: it names an aspiration level and stops when the level is met. The level is Paper 1's open cell, and the construct clears it because it is the first competence that includes the counterpart. A fuller account of the person in this form would add what she believes, what she is owed, and what she can refuse.

The online dispute resolution literature that named the fourth party also wrote its duties: Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021), with Katsh and Rifkin (2001) behind them, hold that such a party owes the parties before it transparency, accountability, contestability, and consent. This form withholds near-exactly that list: the rule cannot be observed, the determination answers to no one, no channel carries a contest, and enrollment stands in for consent — a design standard read in negative. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) supply the interpreting operation's published antecedent: sellers judged across a visibility gap by buyers they cannot see. The operations are what a person supplies while those requirements go unmet; how people begin to supply them is the early empirical question.

# Research Design and Development to Date

## The Research Question

The empirical question that closed the last section can be put formally. How do people acquire and exercise algorithmacy: how do they work out an outcome the arrangement leaves unexplained, whether they treat any other person as party to the situation, and what they do when an outcome seems wrong? We derive the three clauses from the three operations. Working out an unexplained outcome is interpreting, exercised on an occasion; whether another person figures in the working-out is what specifying intent presupposes and what a person–system competence never asks; and what someone does when an outcome seems wrong is where keeping track meets a verdict that binds her. Pratt (2009) argued that qualitative research has no boilerplate: what counts as enough is a function of the question asked. This question asks for occasions, incidents in which the operations appear and either separate or run together, and the design below is built to collect them, starting from the programme in which the construct formed.

## The Programme, and What It Was For

The programme is older than the study, and it has run in a different arrangement roughly every season for two years. It began in July and August 2025 with two schools in Trinidad and Tobago. Through the following fall and winter it ran in La Brea, with the MP for that constituency, the government's AI Ministry, NGO partners, and the police unit that runs Hearts and Minds, whose concern is keeping young people out of gangs. Those cohorts ran as GauntleTT, an intensive AI engineering programme of eight weeks at a time. In April and May 2026 a general open version ran in Boston, its public face at cursorboston.com. In July and August 2026 the programme ran with Hult in Boston; it runs again this fall, open and public; and in the spring semester of 2027 it becomes a course for credit. In its full-semester form the Hult programme runs sixteen weeks, and participants build production software and submit each project as a merged pull request. That spring 2027 course is the study site.

Those Trinidad cohorts are where the construct was formed, and where a construct forms is a claim about where its theory comes from. The platform-work literature forms its constructs at sites the arrangement has already sorted: Rahman's freelancers carried years of scores, Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's couriers carried routes and tenure, and at sites like those, any difference in how two workers read the same system arrives pre-explained — by prior skill, by task mix, by hours on the platform. GauntleTT held none of those explanations. It could not have: platform work in the Anglophone Caribbean is recent, limited, and concentrated in transport, delivery, and tourism (ILO, 2025); we found no peer-reviewed journal study of platform labour in Trinidad and Tobago; and a systematic review of algorithmic management counts two of 103 empirical studies from the whole of Latin America and the Caribbean (Chigbu, 2026). There was no platform-sorted workforce for a cohort to be drawn from. Participants arrived new to AI-mediated coordination and worked one curriculum on one compressed calendar, so when two of them met the same unexplained verdict and made different things of it, the difference had nowhere ordinary to hide. A site like that is what Merton (1987) called a strategic research site, one that exhibits the phenomenon to be explained in accessible form, and choosing one is the move Bamberger and Pratt (2010) reclaimed for organizational scholarship: unconventional contexts and samples yield constructs that conventional sites cannot. Bothello, Nason, and Schnyder (2019) add, in *Organization Studies*, what the reverse reading costs: a site read only through constructs formed elsewhere shows up as a void, and this cohort, read through the platform-work constructs above, would have shown up as having nothing — no scores, no tenure, no task history — when what it held was the phenomenon unobstructed. What the cohort made visible was the competency forming: interpreting, specifying, and keeping track, already uneven among people whom every enrollment variable treated as equivalent. That visibility is what the formation contributes. It is not evidence of the construct's structure: it grounds the instrument's language, because we wrote the protocols to ask for occasions of the kind the teaching showed, and it bears on whether that language says what participants mean. Evidence has to come from a site where consent and analysis run on the record.

Each cohort programme ran submitted work through a gate, and the gates differ in what they withhold. At Cursor Boston a language model screens each submission and the cohort then votes, with each participant seeing only her own score. At Hult the written reviews and the upvotes are public and the tally is withheld. For Trinidad no record survives of how the gate ran, and we wrote one of the interview protocols below to recover it. Three arrangements withhold at three different layers — the peers' scores at Boston, the count at Hult, the record itself in Trinidad — so what the definition calls opacity is a property of the arrangement rather than an artefact of one design.

Everything before the spring 2027 course is development. The two schools, the La Brea work, the Boston open version, the summer cohort with Hult, and the public cohort running now are where the construct was formed and where the instrument was built and tested. None of it is the study, and none of it is reported below as findings. What those two years produced is a site, two instruments, and two test responses.

## The Site, and the Researcher's Position

The study site is the formal course at Hult University, beginning in the spring semester of 2027. Participants will submit work to a gate that returns an outcome that is not fully explained: the gate is the definition's intermediary, its unexplained verdict the opacity, and its effective rule the adaptive element, because the codebase the gate tests against moves with other participants' work. The parties the gate reads on the far side are peers whose written reviews and upvotes are public and whose tally is withheld — a counterpart who can be watched judging while the judgement that decides stays out of sight. The site is a hard case for opacity: participants build the systems they also submit to. Opacity, though, is a property of the person–arrangement pair, and what the arrangement withholds even from its builders is the tally and the criteria behind it. The code they can read.

The investigator will teach the course, and that position invites the standing objection: role duality, and consent that may not be free. The investigator casts no vote in the peer gate. Intake is anonymous, and that anonymity is what answers the objection: no record exists of who was invited or who took part, so the investigator does not learn whether any particular person responded — the separation Ferguson, Yonge, and Myrick (2004) prescribe for faculty studying their own students. The protection carries a cost the consent form states plainly. Nothing links a file to the person who sent it, so a response cannot be withdrawn once it is submitted, which is why the review step before submission is not a formality. No one is interviewed about a project whose outcome is still open, and the programmes a participant is asked about have concluded or are concluding. Brannick and Coghlan (2007) treat role duality as a dynamic insider research manages; Anteby (2013) holds that involvement and distance are not ends of one continuum — the involvement that formed the construct also grounds the instrument's language. Mercer (2007) names the residue no design removes — participants may still be managing a teacher's expectations. Pratt, Kaplan, and Whittington (2020) distinguish an audit trail held for a committee from a deposit; Tracy's (2010) sincerity and ethics are the criteria the trail answers to, and the protocol lives in a public repository while the raw responses do not.

## The Harness Interview

The harness interview is the instrument the study's evidence will come through. A participant completes a task, receives an outcome that is not fully explained, and is asked how she interpreted it and what she did next. Four blocks organize the questions — working out what the gate wanted, getting work across, noticing unannounced change, and what happens when an outcome seems wrong — and each block requires one incident: a specific occasion, narrated end to end, with what she was doing, what happened, what she did next, and how it came out. A characterization is where an answer starts; the interview asks which time. A closing "no" gets one reframe from another angle before the block moves on. The interviewer introduces no theoretical vocabulary at any point, because a person handed a category will answer with the category, and the answer stops being evidence of how she conceives the work. The words she lands on are the data.

What makes the instrument unusual is where it runs and who conducts it. The interview is conducted by an AI assistant in the participant's own editor, one question at a time, and the assistant anonymises as it writes: names, organizations, localities, and exact dates are replaced before the participant ever reads the text. The participant then reviews the file, edits or deletes what she chooses, and approves it, and submission is anonymous, to a third-party endpoint that refuses anything still marked unreviewed. Approval is enforced by the pipeline as well as asked of the person.

We wrote three protocols to carry the same rules to three positions. A student protocol reaches people who took part in one of the cohort programmes; an operations protocol reaches the staff and partner organizations who ran one; a self protocol reaches the investigator, because the record of how the Trinidad gate ran survives nowhere else and has to be recovered as testimony, under an agent instructed to keep the author on events and off interpretation.

The design is abductive (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012): we enter with a derived construct and hold the three operations as sensitizing concepts, which start inquiry and do not sort it (Bowen, 2006). First-cycle coding, when the files support it, will stay in the participant's words (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013); we decline the data-structure template (Mees-Buss, Welch, & Piekkari, 2022) and treat the design as bricolage (Pratt, Sonenshein, & Feldman, 2022). The negative case is a participant who obtains an answer from the gate by individual skill alone.

## The Survey Panel

The survey panel is the second instrument, and it carries the design's quantitative half. Three waves run across the sixteen-week course, at weeks 1, 4, and 16: a baseline, a wave after the first build-and-review cycle, and a wave at the close. The second wave carries a discriminant block adapted from Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's (2025) scale, with a latent-correlation threshold we fixed before any data exist, so the predictions registered in the construct section meet data on terms set in advance: Understanding against interpreting, Leveraging against specifying intent, Embracing against nothing. Between them the two instruments divide the question: the harness collects incidents and conceptions, how people describe the work in their own words; the panel tests structure and discrimination, whether the three operations cohere, separate, and stay distinguishable from the scale they most resemble. Bentley IRB issued an exempt determination on 11 May 2026, protocol 260511078, for the three-wave panel.

We report the two responses as tests of the instrument, and testing taught three things. The first lesson is that the blocks can collect what a person–system competence would miss. On working out what the gate wanted, the test participant received an outcome she did not contest, named the reason, and found no channel for a contest — an incident of forbearance with no forum. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's couriers hold an appeal channel and it reaches the apparatus; this participant found no channel at all. A person–system reading would file the incident as finished adaptation, a reading that includes the counterpart has to file it as a missing position, and the block caught the material on which those readings divide.

The second lesson is that the blocks can separate tool skill from the competency. On getting work across, the same participant pasted the gate's feedback into another system and asked for an explanation, delegating the interpretive work to a tool. That is useful conduct, not algorithmacy, and it leaves the counterpart where she was. An instrument that confused the two would collect fluency and call it competence; this block held them apart.

The third lesson concerned the interview itself. On noticing unannounced change, and on outcomes that seem wrong, the first file was thin: answers and questions sat at roughly the same length, because the interview was accepting summaries where it should have been asking for the occasion. We deepened the protocols: the occasion is now required in every block, and a closing answer gets one reframe from another angle.

The spring collection is set against the four blocks. On working out what the gate wanted, the study will collect incidents in which a participant reconstructs what the voters wanted, and the same gate described from the operations side. On getting work across, it will collect incidents of specification proper: a submission shaped so the unseen peers can receive what its author meant. On noticing unannounced change, it will collect occasions: a submission on which the effective rule had moved unannounced, and what the participant did next. On outcomes that seem wrong, it will collect incidents naming whom the wrongness was taken to concern — the system, the voters, or both.

The student protocol runs across the later weeks of the course as projects close, under the rule already stated; the operations and self protocols carry no course clock and can run at once. Collection aims at the disconfirming file as hard as the confirming one: the negative case remains the participant who gets what she needs from the gate by individual skill alone, and the blocks are written so that she can appear. What counts as enough follows Pratt's rule from the opening of this section: enough is a function of the question, and the question asks whether the three operations appear in incidents and whether they separate. Block coverage, on that rule, is the aspiration level, and Table 2 sets it out.

# A Research Agenda

Four studies would carry the construct from proposal to test, and each has a result that would count.

The first is the study the spring course opens: interviews across the three protocols until every block of Table 2 carries incidents. The finished corpus would also settle the fourth-operation question the construct section refused. Rahman's who-influences candidate becomes an operation of its own if files accumulate in which a participant has interpreted everything the arrangement shows and still cannot tell which party to interpret; if no such file appears, the three-operation count stands as drawn.

The second is the discriminant test against Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's scale, administered to one population alongside an instrument built on this paper's definition, with the correlation predictions we registered in the construct section held fixed before anyone looks: Understanding moderate against interpreting, Leveraging against specifying intent, Embracing against nothing. Moderate correlations would separate the construct from the scale it most resembles; correlations high enough to collapse the distinctions would end it.

The third is a distribution study. The construct's properties predict a competency uneven among people the arrangement treats as equivalent, so measure it across one such population and test whether the unevenness predicts coordination outcomes after prior skill, task mix, and hours are controlled. The same design reaches the selection question: follow the low scorers and record what the form does with them — churn, exit, or coordination failures absorbed as personal ones — because whichever it is tells a comparative researcher what the form spends when the competency is scarce.

The fourth takes the instrument to a site this paper holds no stake in: a ride-hail fleet, a piecework queue, or a freelance platform whose clients stay unmet, where the intermediary binds both parties and no participant has built the system she submits to. Incidents there in which the three operations appear and separate would show the construct travels beyond the course that is refining its instrument.

# What This Paper Cannot Say

Algorithmacy is proposed rather than demonstrated. No finding is claimed in this paper, because data collection for the study has not begun. What the paper offers on the empirical side is a design and an instrument that testing has already changed once. Whether the three operations cohere as one competency, whether they separate from one another, and whether they separate from general knowledge of software remain open questions; the early interviews can refine the instrument, and they cannot close those questions. Closing them carries a further concession: Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) hold that competence is at once molar and molecular, levels that do not collapse into one another, and the design moves from incident-level interviews toward a scale. That move will have to be earned level by level.

The neighbouring constructs remain live. A later study might show that Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's scale already captures the variance we attribute to a missing position, and it might show the same of Rahman's account of reactivity. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson's fifth literacy is the hardest neighbour on the counterpart; we have argued that it is a literacy beside the platform, not through it, and no study yet shows that a measure built on this paper's definition outperforms any of them. Sandberg's result sets a further limit. If competence is a conception of work, a later standardized scale will have to show that it still measures that conception.

The shelf is also still filling: this year alone has added algorithmic skills (Hong, Cheng, & Liu, 2026), platform work literacies (Scolari, Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés, 2026), Human-AI Collaboration Dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), and AI-algorithmic competence (Ayasrah, Al-Rousan, Almulla, & Almulla, 2026), each of which, so far as its published account shows, still runs person-to-system — the nearest counterpart in any of them is a peer, a face-to-face encounter the app schedules, or the tool itself. The claim this paper defends is about a position, never about a name. Names in this field are cheap, and they are getting cheaper.

Nearer cases exist than those four, and a single condition excludes each of them. Every counterpart-inclusive construct we have found demotes the intermediary to a tool, a delegate, a channel, or a threat, so the third scope condition — the intermediary reads both parties and binds them — carries the claim wherever a counterpart appears. Three cases are close enough to decide it. A competence for medical education, circulating as an unrefereed preprint submitted in June 2026 ("AI-Mediated Relational Competence in Medical Education," 2026), writes the patient into its sub-competencies with AI mediating the clinical encounter, and stays outside the claim only because accountable human decision-making is itself one of those sub-competencies: the clinician, not the system, commits the decision. Yang and Liechty (2026) build an AI competency for social work that begins from trust, power, and rapport in the worker–client relationship, in a field whose child-welfare risk models do read both parties and bind them; so far as its account is visible, the chapter hands the worker the algorithm as a tool, and only a full reading can settle whether it stays one. Intimacy platforms hold the pincer this paper drew for labour: Dredge and Anderson (2021) give eleven social competencies with a counterpart and no algorithm, Hu and Zhan (2024) give algorithm awareness with no counterpart, and the matcher between them reads both parties and commits matches neither controls. We have read none of the three in full. Their ground marks a second limit: this paper states its claim for the coordination of work, and medicine, social work, and intimacy lie outside the terrain the review has tested. Each of the three literatures is one construct away from meeting the third condition, and whichever crosses first is the case that would decide the claim.

Paper 1's grades are a frame this paper leaves untested; the requirement we took up would still stand if coordinative co-optation were more fully answered, or if the form were not distinct. We argued the Hult course's fit as a hard case for opacity. Whether findings from this course travel to ride-hail, piecework, or freelance platforms is what the agenda's fourth study would settle.

We have not measured a population, defeated rival explanations that run on prior skill, task mix, or hours, or shown that a rise in this competency is a good outcome. If scores rose while the form stayed as it is, what would have risen is the amount of unpaid coordination work the participants absorb. Whether that is welcome depends on facts about the arrangement that this construct does not measure.

A later paper can take the instrument to scale. This one offers a definition that includes the counterpart, a review of constructs that do not, and a pair of instruments built to hear from people who are learning to take part.
