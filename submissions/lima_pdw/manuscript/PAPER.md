## Abstract

Algorithmacy is the competency through which an individual integrates into coordinative co-optation—an emerging organizational form where a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. We define this competency as the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, specify intent through it, and track its unannounced shifts, enabling sustained participation in coordination where a system evaluates both transacting parties and commits decisions neither party controls. Grounded in Paper 1 of this dissertation, which demonstrated that coordinative co-optation remains far less theorized than hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation, we review seven extant competence constructs and show how each reduces interaction to a dyadic person–system relation that leaves the human counterpart outside. We then formalize algorithmacy as a satisficing construct that brings the counterpart back into the analytical frame. Finally, we detail an abductive research design featuring an in-editor "harness interview" calibrated across developmental cohorts, locating ongoing empirical inquiry in a specialized software engineering course at Hult International Business School to refine the instrument for subsequent measurement.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication competency · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# Introduction

Coordination—the collective execution of interdependent work—remains a foundational concern of organization theory. Classic scholarship maps three canonical coordination forms: hierarchy, market, and network, governed respectively by authority, price, and ongoing social relationships. Yet this typology omits an emergent configuration in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. Because organization theory lacks an account of how an individual takes her place within this algorithmic architecture, we develop that structural and behavioral account here.

This article advances that agenda through three interconnected steps. First, we ground our inquiry in Paper 1 of this dissertation, which evaluated five coordination forms—hierarchy, market, network, classical co-optation (Selznick, 1949), and coordinative co-optation (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024)—across six analytical dimensions: operational mechanism, required deliverables, favoring conditions, primary instruments, failure modes, and empirical identification. That comparative analysis revealed coordinative co-optation as the least theorized form, leaving the question of participant standing open. Second, we examine seven candidate competence constructs and show that each reduces interaction to a dyadic person–system relation that leaves the human counterpart outside the analytical frame. To resolve this blind spot, we formalize algorithmacy as a satisficing construct that incorporates the counterpart into an algorithmically bound triad. Third, we present an abductive research design and report calibration findings from an in-editor harness interview administered to early developer cohorts, establishing the empirical foundation for longitudinal measurement.

Surveying the theoretical landscape reveals several candidate constructs, yet each ultimately restricts its analytical gaze to a person–system dyad. Early communication models conceptualize the technology as a passive transmission channel between two human actors (Spitzberg, 2006). Subsequent perspectives personify the apparatus, framing the algorithm as an autonomous communicative partner (Guzman & Lewis, 2020) or as a delegate generating messages on a speaker's behalf (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). While technical comprehension and navigational fluency are vital for navigating platform environments (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), these competencies address system mechanics rather than interpersonal coordination. Even frameworks that treat the algorithm as an opaque object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021) stop at the boundary of the technical apparatus. Consequently, extant constructs uniformly position the individual on one side of a relation and the system on the other, stranding the human counterpart—a hiring client, a peer evaluator, or an interdependent collaborator—outside the competence they specify.

We model algorithmacy on the foundational pattern of literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Just as literacy enables participation in communication mediated by text, numeracy equips individuals to navigate situations structured by quantitative data, and oracy governs interaction through spoken discourse, algorithmacy enables sustained participation in coordination structured by an algorithm. We define algorithmacy as the capacity to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, specify intent through it, and track its shifting parameters, allowing an actor to coordinate when a system reads both transacting parties and commits decisions neither controls. In this formulation, opacity means the participant cannot directly observe the governing rule; adaptivity means the rule can shift dynamically; and intermediary refers to the algorithmic system mediating between the actor and her counterpart. Whereas literacy, numeracy, and oracy constitute competencies for a medium, algorithmacy operates as a competency for a coordination form—fulfilling the theoretical requirement identified in Paper 1. We then examine this competency in development through an in-editor "harness interview," wherein participants complete a technical task, encounter an incompletely explained outcome, and articulate how they interpreted the decision and formulated their subsequent actions. Drawing on preliminary calibration responses, we are iteratively refining this protocol within a specialized software engineering course at Hult International Business School to prepare the instrument for large-scale quantitative assessment.

# Paper 1 of This Dissertation

We ground our inquiry in Paper 1 of this dissertation, which evaluated five coordination forms against a uniform analytical architecture and assessed how comprehensively extant scholarship has addressed each foundational dimension. Following the comparative traditions established by Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980), Paper 1 evaluated each form across six core questions: how the form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how an empirical researcher identifies it. Paper 1 defined a form's mechanism—the object of the first question—as the primary operation securing coordination paired with the formal standing of the party reached by that operation. It resolved the second question through the three integrating conditions synthesized by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009)—predictability, common understanding, and accountability. Mapping classical theorists onto this modern coordination vocabulary constitutes Paper 1's analytical synthesis. For example, Hayek (1945) provides one of the clearest illustrations, demonstrating how the price mechanism carries the coordinative burden because it operates as a public statistic that no participant must reconstruct privately; Paper 1 interpreted this statistic as delivering predictability and common understanding, while locating market accountability in contract and reputation. The evaluations measure each literature's theoretical completeness rather than the arrangement's empirical prevalence.

The four established columns demonstrate theoretical completeness. Paper 1 synthesized Simon (1997) and Weber (1978) to locate the individual in hierarchy through a zone of acceptance; drew on Hayek (1945) to define the market actor through the evaluation of a posted term backed by an exit right; Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985) to anchor the network participant in an elective, revocable relationship; and Selznick (1949) to ground classical co-optation in a conferred seat that transforms external opposition into institutional participation. A zone, a term, a relation, a seat—in each mature form, organization theory names both the governing operation and the formal standing of the party it reaches.

The fifth column thins, collapsing precisely where the mechanism inquiry demands an account of participant standing. As Stark and Pais (2020) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) demonstrate, digital platforms neither command, nor contract, nor collaborate; instead, they enroll participants through algorithms that match unchosen parties, evaluate their performance, and unilaterally terminate accounts. While platform scholarship explores algorithmic matching and control (Möhlmann et al., 2021), direction and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020), information asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), and non-portable reputational capital (Curchod et al., 2020), it leaves the enrolled actor's standing unresolved. The participant operates neither as an employee under authority, a buyer evaluating terms, nor a partner in a mutual bond. She is enrolled, yet organization theory has not articulated what individual capacity enables her to coordinate within this architecture. Seven existing constructs approach this capacity closely enough to warrant formal examination.

# Extant Constructs

A construct, in this context, designates a formalized account of participant capacity—whether conceptualized as a competence, a literacy, or an interpretive orientation toward an automated system. Frameworks that incorporate a human counterpart already occupy adjacent literature; relational coordination, for example, defines a shared mode of communicating and relating among interdependent workers (Gittell, 2002). Yet relational coordination presupposes the very condition that platform mediation eliminates—interactants who directly observe, reach, and respond to one another. Because the algorithm replaces that mutual visibility, we evaluate what status each candidate construct accords the computational intermediary. Existing scholarship models individuals working alongside algorithms through divergent lenses, and that divergence establishes the theoretical boundary of what each competence can explain.

We examine seven candidate perspectives in sequence. Computer-mediated communication competence treats the system as a transmission channel between two individuals (Spitzberg, 2006). Human–machine communication conceptualizes the apparatus as an interactive communicative partner (Guzman & Lewis, 2020). AI-mediated communication frames the computational artifact as an agent operating on a speaker's behalf (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). AI literacy approaches the technology as an object of formal knowledge (Long & Magerko, 2020). Algorithmic competency defines the platform as an object of strategic navigation (Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025). Reactivity under opaque evaluation analyzes the algorithm as an object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021). Finally, gig literacies treat the platform as an infrastructure to leverage and circumvent (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, & Nelson, 2020). Across each evaluation, we pose a single structural question: where does the human counterpart on the opposite side—the client who commissions work, or the evaluator whose rating alters a score—theoretically reside?

## Computer-Mediated Communication Competence

Communication research initially approached digital technology as a transmission channel. Spitzberg (2006) conceptualizes the computer as a conduit between two individuals, defining computer-mediated communication competence as the capacity to navigate that medium so that a message successfully reaches a counterpart. He translates a five-part model of interpersonal competence—motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and outcomes—into mediated environments, orienting every dimension toward the human partner. Motivation governs the balance of approach and avoidance toward her; knowledge captures what renders interaction appropriate and effective for her; and the four behavioral skill clusters (attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness) focus entirely on interpersonal alignment. Five outcome criteria complete the model—appropriateness, effectiveness (subsuming task achievement and efficiency), coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development—with the partner directly evaluating appropriateness through perceived legitimacy and satisfaction through affective response. On this account, mediation modifies the operational parameters of communication rather than its underlying structure. The human actors at either terminal remain the exclusive coordinators.

Although Spitzberg formulated the model as an interpersonal typology and developed an empirical measure to evaluate it, the hypothesized factor structure did not emerge cleanly in testing. More critically, nothing in the framework grants the technical medium an autonomous stake in the interaction. The channel may constrain the transacting parties, but it does not interpret their actions, rank their performance, or commit decisions beyond their control. The competence Spitzberg specifies ends the moment a message reaches the human receiver, leaving unaddressed any coordination setting in which an algorithmic system evaluates both sides and makes binding determinations.

## Human–Machine Communication

The transmission model collapsed once computational systems were engineered to engage in direct dialogue with human users. Guzman and Lewis (2020) advance this shift by conceptualizing the machine as an autonomous communicative partner. Framing a conceptual agenda rather than an empirical scale, they argue that communicative artificial intelligence systems are "designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). Examining conversational agents, social robotics, and automated-writing software, they locate meaning-making "within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine" (p. 74). This formulation departs decisively from computer-mediated communication—whereas the transmission model treats technology as a conduit for human interaction, human–machine communication positions the machine itself as an interlocutor.

This agenda develops across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions, each establishing the person–machine dyad as its focal analytical unit. While Guzman and Lewis acknowledge that machines may still mediate human exchanges, they reject confining technology to an intermediary function. Yet in elevating the device to a communicative partner, the framework strands the human counterpart: anyone reached on the far side of that technical apparatus remains entirely outside the scope of the interaction the agenda theorizes.

## AI-Mediated Communication

A subsequent theoretical development brought the human counterpart back into view while aligning the computational system with one communicating party. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) conceptualize the system as an algorithmic agent operating on a human communicator's behalf, formally defining AI-mediated communication as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 89). Both human participants remain within this analytical scope, illustrated through algorithmic smart replies, predictive text engines, and synthetic video generation. The framework's core parameters—magnitude, medium, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation—characterize an algorithmic delegate rather than an autonomous arbiter.

This computational agent resides firmly on one party's side of the exchange, generating or refining messages that the human sender or receiver could theoretically author herself. The counterpart remains a conventional addressee, and any communicative competence the model implies resides with the human speaker or her digital proxy. Even when operating with high autonomy, an agent in this framework functions strictly on behalf of a focal communicator. An algorithmic rating system that can unilaterally terminate a participant's account operates under an entirely different structural logic.

## AI Literacy

A distinct literature shifts focus from communicative interaction to technical comprehension, treating the algorithmic architecture as an object of formal knowledge. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to evaluate AI technologies critically; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace" (p. 2). They organize the domain around five core inquiries: what artificial intelligence is, what tasks it executes, how its underlying mechanisms operate, how society ought to govern it, and how humans perceive its functions. Across the seventeen discrete competencies derived from this framework, the focus centers overwhelmingly on system comprehension—spanning the identification of AI systems, the analysis of machine learning representations and data sensors, ethical considerations, and programmability. Each competency describes a unilateral relationship between an individual and a technological artifact; the only item referencing human actors (Competency 10, "Human Role in AI") conceptualizes them strictly as upstream system designers.

No competency in this taxonomy incorporates a human counterpart as an interdependent participant in an ongoing coordination process. The mandate to "communicate and collaborate effectively with AI" positions the machine directly in the interlocutor's seat, re-inscribing a person–system dyad by definition. When Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) synthesized these seventeen competencies into four overarching categories—knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and ethical issues—subsequent measurement instruments inherited this identical four-cell architecture. Every resulting operationalization models competence as an individual's unilateral engagement with a computational artifact, leaving the triadic counterpart outside the analytical frame.

## Algorithmic Competency

Drawing on five empirical samples of ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers in China, Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) developed a twelve-item, four-dimension psychometric instrument with an overall reliability of .85, introducing what they term the first validated scale of algorithmic competency. They define this construct as a worker's comprehension of the platform algorithms that allocate and evaluate labor, coupled with the capacity to adapt to and navigate those systems. The construct treats the computational infrastructure as an object of navigation, directly inheriting its theoretical lineage from Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019)—who identified sensemaking, circumventing, and manipulating as foundational gig-worker competencies—and adapting those behaviors into its core subscales.

The four validated dimensions—Understanding, Embracing, Leveraging, and Remediating—measure behavioral adaptation rather than formal declarative knowledge. Understanding captures a cognitive grasp of algorithmic management; Embracing reflects a willingness to trust systemic efficiency; Leveraging denotes the strategic manipulation of system parameters for individual benefit; and Remediating addresses worker efforts to repair technical deficiencies. Remediating is the only instance in the scale in which an actor contests an institutional outcome, operationalized through items measuring how a courier routes evidence directly to algorithmic management to overturn an unfair customer evaluation.

Within these scale items, the human counterpart appears only peripherally—as an anonymous source of performance ratings ("I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback"), an abstract matching variable embedded within beliefs about platform efficiency, or an external peer network mobilized for troubleshooting on WeChat. In their structural model, customer-oriented service behavior operates strictly as an externally measured outcome rather than an intrinsic dimension of competence. Because their empirical setting firmly anchors these items to platform mechanics, the scale does not transfer to peer-evaluation environments where score tallies remain withheld. The instrument rigorously measures how workers navigate an automated apparatus; it does not measure how they coordinate with the human counterpart the apparatus matched them with.

## Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation

When an online freelancing platform replaced its transparent five-star rating system with an opaque, hundred-point Job Success Score, Rahman (2021) theorized the resulting labor dynamic through the invisible cage. He treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation, contrasting its hidden, fluctuating metrics with the Weberian iron cage of fixed, codified administrative rules. Five structural properties constitute this invisible cage—hidden and shifting evaluation criteria, evaluations executed at uncertain intervals, unidentifiable score magnitude, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative influence spanning the client and the platform. In that final dimension, the human counterpart surfaces precisely as an interpretive failure.

Workers responded to this opacity through two divergent behavioral strategies. Experimental reactivity tested tactical adjustments to elevate the score, whereas constrained reactivity limited platform engagement to protect established metrics from volatility, with the chosen response turning on platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks. The human counterpart remained present throughout Rahman's field setting—comprising eighteen clients interviewed alongside eighty freelancers—yet the platform's interface explicitly severed interpersonal feedback loops, assuring clients that their reviews "will be kept anonymous and never shared directly with the freelancer." The system thus captured the counterpart's judgment and deliberately withheld it from the evaluated worker. Documented in the platform's own interface language, this asymmetric structure leaves a worker able to maintain a fully serviceable theory of the algorithm while having no mechanism to address a rating she cannot observe.

## Gig Literacies

The closest empirical candidate incorporated the human counterpart directly into the research design. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) conceptualize gig literacies as "critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing" (p. 457), encompassing the capacity to leverage platform affordances while developing workarounds to navigate structural constraints. Drawing on qualitative interviews with twenty Upwork freelancers and nineteen hiring clients, they utilized client accounts to provide "some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers" (p. 463).

Their empirical taxonomy identifies five core literacies (pp. 463–469)—building reputation through badges, refunds, and Job Success Scores; optimizing self-presentation across profiles and proposals; maintaining productivity via personal routines and third-party tools; mitigating transaction risk through escrow services and client vetting; and building interpersonal relationships. Under this fifth literacy, workers actively cultivate "a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" to secure repeat business (p. 468). For instance, participant P39, unable to rehabilitate an algorithmically depressed rating, migrated established Upwork clients off-platform to maintain commercial viability. Yet this construct ultimately captures freelancer competencies deployed beside the platform rather than communicative action executed through a binding, opaque intermediary. Because their model depends on an environment where parties can bypass the system and interact directly, it cannot account for coordination where off-platform exit is impossible. The authors explicitly mark this scope boundary themselves, contrasting Upwork—where workers can evaluate, select, and converse directly with clients—with platforms such as Uber, where the algorithm assigns labor deterministically (p. 470).

## What These Boundaries Share

Across seven constructs and seven theoretical boundaries, an identical topology emerges—an individual on one side of a relation and an automated system on the other. Systematic reviews of the field confirm this structural boundary. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) synthesized fifty empirical studies of algorithmic literacy (screened from ninety-six across four databases) and identified pervasive conceptual fragmentation; Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub (2024) reviewed 169 publications and documented an absence of unified theoretical models; and Iyamu, Wheelans, Haag, Roe, and Chang (2026) evaluated twelve health-worker studies against Nutbeam's literacy model, reporting communicative literacy as "largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and measurement instruments." In Nutbeam's hierarchy, the communicative level—explaining AI-mediated data to a patient whom the system does not simultaneously evaluate—represents the field's nearest approximation of a second human actor. Yet expanding survey items across dozens of studies has not moved that boundary. The field was handed a relational slot and systematically populated it with system-facing operationalizations.

Theoretical accounts of visibility labor close the conceptual pincer from the opposite direction. Abidin (2016) conceptualizes the deliberate self-presentation a follower performs to become visible to a specific social media influencer—an authentic, named counterpart—while consciously setting the algorithm outside her analytical frame, defining her inquiry as analog affective labor distinct from algorithmic visibility. Extant constructs that center the algorithm bracket the human counterpart, whereas the primary framework that centers the counterpart brackets the algorithm. Organization theory currently offers no construct that integrates both.

The triadic structural position itself has not gone unnoticed. In dispute resolution, Katsh and Rifkin (2001) conceptualized the technology hosting an online interaction as a "fourth party" exerting autonomous systemic effects. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) showed how customer reviews replace traditional dyadic exchanges, creating an asymmetrical triad—comprising platform operators, buyers, and sellers—sustained by a visibility gap that lets buyers evaluate workers invisibly. Cameron (2024) formalized this configuration in on-demand labor as the algorithmic labor triangle, while Healy and Pekarek (2024) integrated it into labor process theory to explain worker precarity. Yet all four frameworks operate strictly as structural theories of power, institutional consent, or systemic vulnerability.

None of these four frameworks articulates an actionable competence for the actor caught within that structural triad. We defend this theoretical boundary across four conditions: the construct must delineate an individual's behavioral and interpretive capacity; the counterpart must operate as an active party to the focal coordination rather than as an upstream designer, peer network, or downstream recipient; the intermediary must simultaneously evaluate and bind both interacting parties (where a transmission channel carries, a delegate assists, and a fourth party operates within a disclosed procedural framework); and the claim addresses a theoretical position rather than taxonomic terminology. Evaluated against these conditions, the seven hearings and the field's own meta-reviews yield a uniform conclusion—no extant competence construct accords the counterpart an analytical position. Concurrently, empirical practice has begun documenting this capacity in the field. Manky (2026), for instance, demonstrates that forty ride-hailing drivers in Lima actively decode platform metrics to assess passenger safety—a counterpart-facing capacity deployed for physical security that currently lacks formal conceptualization. Organization theory requires a competence formulated specifically for the triadic position the field has already mapped. The next section develops that construct.

# Algorithmacy: A Construct for Coordinative Co-optation

Suddaby (2010) establishes four formal criteria for construct clarity in organizational theory: articulating a precise definition, setting explicit scope conditions, specifying semantic relationships with neighboring constructs, and demonstrating theoretical coherence within the broader framework. While we formulated the core definition in the introduction, construct validity demands a systematic account of its boundary conditions, dimensional structure, ontological status, and analytical utility. We develop those components below to establish algorithmacy as a viable micro-foundational construct for organization theory.

## Scope Conditions and Boundary Limits

Algorithmacy applies specifically to organizational arrangements where an intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding on interacting parties. The construct does not operate—or operates only trivially—in contexts governed by codified, static administrative rules, or in open market environments where transacting parties negotiate contractual terms directly. A posted market price accompanied by an unrestricted right of refusal represents the classical market archetype (Hayek, 1945). A codified bureaucratic procedure administered by a designated hierarchical superior represents the formal organization (Weber, 1978; Simon, 1997).

Online freelance labor platforms occupy a hybrid boundary: while the system algorithmically matches and scores participants, transacting parties frequently retain the capacity to migrate their collaborative relationships off-platform (Sutherland et al., 2020). In contrast, on-demand dispatch networks, algorithmic piecework queues, and peer-evaluation systems that withhold score tallies reside at the core of the construct's domain. Scope conditions define structural configurations rather than specific industrial sectors, fulfilling the exact requirement demanded by Paper 1's open cell: a rigorous account of the capacities an individual must exercise to coordinate within coordinative co-optation, formulated so that empirical researchers can identify its absence.

## Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation

To establish discriminant validity, we map algorithmacy against neighboring constructs. We formalize these semantic relationships as testable empirical predictions across adjacent measurement traditions:

**Algorithmic Competency (Zhou et al., 2025):** We predict that Zhou and colleagues' dimension of Understanding will correlate moderately with interpreting, and their dimension of Leveraging will correlate moderately with specifying intent. Conversely, their dimension of Embracing exhibits no theoretical correspondence within algorithmacy, as an affective attitude toward platform efficiency constitutes a normative disposition rather than an operational coordination capacity.

**Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation (Rahman, 2021):** Rahman's typology operates along an orthogonal theoretical axis. Whereas reactivity categorizes degrees of behavioral engagement versus defensive withdrawal, algorithmacy delineates directional communicative orientation and temporal tracking across an asymmetric triad. Latent correlations high enough to collapse these dimensional boundaries would invalidate the construct's theoretical distinctiveness.

## Constitutive Operations: The Tripartite Model

The operational architecture of algorithmacy comprises three core operations derived directly from the structural demands of the mediated triad rather than aggregated inductively from ad hoc practices:

**Interpreting:** Reconstructing, from ambiguous and fragmented system feedback, the underlying actions, constraints, and intentions of both the algorithmic intermediary and the unseen human counterpart.

**Specifying intent:** Structuring and encoding one's communication so that intended meaning survives the narrow, standardized signaling channels permitted by the intermediary, enabling the counterpart to receive it intact.

**Keeping track:** Detecting shifts and recalibrations in the unannounced rules governing the intermediary that bind both parties over time.

A dyadic competence model restricted to the person–system relationship requires only the interpretive dimension, which Rahman's (2021) framework already captures.

This model explicitly excludes a candidate fourth operation derived from Rahman's fifth dimension of opacity—determining whether the platform firm or the human client drove a change in evaluative score. We subsume this function directly within interpreting, because evaluative ambiguity represents an interpretive failure to parse system mechanics and counterpart behavior simultaneously. This structural boundary remains empirically falsifiable. If field evidence reveals persistent cases where participants successfully decode all available feedback yet remain structurally unable to determine which entity executed the decision, distinguishing the locus of evaluative authority would constitute a distinct fourth operation that our empirical instruments are designed to detect.

The broader literacy tradition provides the construct's nomenclature while setting the analogy's functional boundaries. Long and Magerko (2020) extend this lineage across textual, computational, scientific, and data literacies, yet their operationalizations remain confined to person–system dyads. The literacy analogy establishes operational symmetry. Established literacies encompass two core operations—one receptive and one expressive (reading and writing, listening and speaking, interpreting quantitative data and computing with it)—because texts and numbers remain static artifacts during user engagement. Keeping track becomes a distinct operational requirement when the mediating infrastructure operates as an autonomous, dynamically moving party.

## Categorical Delineation: Competency versus Skill and Capability

Algorithmacy constitutes an individual competency rather than a discrete skill or an organizational capability:

**Distinction from Skill:** A skill represents an isolable procedural routine that can be codified and transmitted through explicit didactic instruction. In contrast, the governing rules of opaque algorithmic systems remain proprietary, unobservable, and dynamic, precluding rule-based instructional codification.

**Distinction from Capability:** A capability resides in organizational routines a firm owns and deploys (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). In platform environments, the platform firm exclusively owns and modifies the matching algorithms, rating systems, and governance parameters; the enrolled individual holds no proprietary capability over the administrative architecture.

**Phenomenographic Foundation:** Sandberg (2000), examining twenty engine optimizers at the Volvo Car Corporation, demonstrated that competence reflects a worker's qualitative conception of work rather than an abstract inventory of independent attributes. Informants optimized either separate technical qualities, interacting qualities, or the entire process from the customer's holistic perspective. In that third conception, the customer represents an interpretive orientation toward the task rather than an unseen counterpart reached through a binding intermediary. Sandberg's phenomenographic finding demonstrates that competence resides in the worker's interpretive framing of work—an insight that algorithmacy extends to mediated triadic coordination.

Conversely, Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) argue that interpersonal communication competence is not an isolated individual attribute but a relational quality jointly enacted and evaluated by interacting parties. Spitzberg (2006) carries this relational commitment into mediated settings, where interactants mutually evaluate and co-construct communicative competence. Algorithmic coordination structurally breaks this relational model. The human counterpart never occupies an evaluative position where mutual communicative competence can be openly negotiated. Algorithmacy identifies the interpretive and expressive capacities an individual must supply when the structural conditions for relational co-construction are absent.

## Foundational Properties: Experiential Acquisition and Uneven Distribution

Algorithmacy possesses two foundational properties:

**Acquisition through Participation:** Because platform operators withhold the underlying rules, formal didactic training cannot substitute for experiential practice. Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp, and Raupach (2023) developed the SNAIL scale to evaluate formal AI-literacy coursework, measuring declarative knowledge installed through instruction. Algorithmacy predicts that explicit classroom instruction alone cannot produce this competency.

**Uneven Distribution:** Algorithmacy is distributed heterogeneously across individuals whom the platform treats as formally equivalent. If the competency were uniformly distributed, it could not explain the persistent variance in outcomes observed among participants navigating identical algorithmic environments.

## Theoretical Utility

Formalizing algorithmacy transforms how organizational scholars examine algorithmic management. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) conceptualize algorithmic workplaces as contested terrain, analyzing the mechanisms of direction, evaluation, and discipline that systems impose on labor. Algorithmacy shifts analytical focus toward what the individual must execute to sustain coordination, yielding distinct theoretical and institutional implications. Mandating that platforms disclose their algorithms alters the opacity of the information environment; fostering algorithmacy alters who can interpret that environment.

This inquiry directly bridges macro-structural platform theory and micro-foundational worker agency. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) defined coordinative co-optation in *Organization Theory*, while Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) and Cameron (2024) analyzed in *Administrative Science Quarterly* how algorithmic enrollment reshapes worker subjectivity and structural consent. Algorithmacy supplies the corresponding individual-level competence framework that this macro-structural literature left unresolved.

Furthermore, if effective participation requires algorithmacy and algorithmic arrangements cannot explicitly instruct it, the institutional viability of the organizational form depends on how this competency is distributed across its participant base. A platform populated by participants with low algorithmacy necessarily generates institutional friction—driving labor churn, platform exit, or the internal absorption of systemic coordination failures as personal deficiencies—posing questions that Paper 1's comparative architecture can now formally evaluate.

The construct also satisfies Simon's (1997) formal behavioral sense: it establishes an aspiration threshold and terminates once that theoretical requirement is satisfied. That threshold is defined by the unresolved cell in Paper 1, which algorithmacy satisfies by integrating the human counterpart into the competence model. A comprehensive behavioral account of the enrolled actor would incorporate normative beliefs, institutional rights, and collective resistance strategies.

Finally, scholarship in online dispute resolution articulates the normative obligations owed by the fourth party. Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021), extending Katsh and Rifkin (2001), argue that an institutional intermediary owes disputants procedural transparency, institutional accountability, meaningful contestability, and informed consent. Algorithmic platform coordination operates by systematically withholding these protections—rules remain unobservable, automated determinations lack administrative accountability, contestation channels remain absent, and structural enrollment displaces authentic consent. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) demonstrate the empirical consequences of this design, showing how platform sellers are evaluated across an asymmetric visibility gap by buyers they cannot observe. The three operations of algorithmacy constitute the individual capacities participants must deploy when these institutional protections are absent. How individuals develop and execute these operations constitutes the central empirical inquiry.

# Research Design and Development to Date

## The Research Question

The empirical inquiry investigates how individuals acquire and exercise algorithmacy within opaque, adaptive, and binding coordination arrangements. Formally, we ask:

- how participants decode unexplained outcomes generated by an algorithmic system,
- whether they conceptualize a human counterpart as an active party to the coordination,
- how they respond when an automated decision appears erroneous.

These three inquiries correspond directly to the construct's constitutive operations. Decoding an unexplained outcome captures interpreting in practice; conceptualizing an unseen counterpart operationalizes the relational premise of specifying intent that person–system frameworks neglect; and responding to perceived evaluative errors captures keeping track when confronted with a binding systemic determination. As Pratt (2009) observes, qualitative inquiry admits no generic methodological template—what constitutes empirical sufficiency depends entirely on the nature of the theoretical question posed. Because this question demands observing discrete behavioral occasions where these operations manifest, separate, or intertwine, our research design captures critical incidents across the developmental field sites where the construct originally took shape.

## The Program

The research design emerged from an instructional engineering initiative that evolved across diverse institutional configurations over a two-year developmental trajectory. The program launched in July and August 2025 at two secondary schools in Trinidad and Tobago, then expanded through the following autumn and winter in the constituency of La Brea. Operating as GauntleTT—an intensive, eight-week artificial intelligence engineering curriculum—the initiative partnered with the local Member of Parliament, the national Ministry of Digital Transformation, non-governmental organizations, and the community policing division managing the Hearts and Minds youth gang intervention program. In April and May 2026, an open-enrollment public iteration operated in Boston through cursorboston.com. The curriculum then transitioned to Hult International Business School in Boston in July and August 2026, followed by a public autumn cohort, culminating in a formal, sixteen-week, credit-bearing course in the spring semester of 2027. Within this semester-long framework, participants engineer production-grade software and submit each project as a merged pull request. This spring 2027 course, beginning in January, serves as the formal empirical research site.

## The Trinidad Field Setting as a Strategic Research Site

The Trinidad cohorts provided the generative environment where the construct of algorithmacy took shape, and where a construct originates constitutes a fundamental claim regarding its theoretical foundations. Mainstream platform-work scholarship typically derives its constructs from environments where institutional mechanisms have already pre-sorted participants. Rahman's (2021) freelancers carried years of historical reputation scores, while Zhou and colleagues' (2025) couriers accumulated established delivery routes and tenure. In such mature environments, observed variance in how workers decode system behavior arrives pre-explained by prior human capital, task specialization, or platform exposure.

The GauntleTT cohorts presented none of these confounding explanations. Platform labor across the Anglophone Caribbean remains emergent and concentrated in transportation, courier delivery, and tourism (ILO, 2025); peer-reviewed scholarship on platform work in Trinidad and Tobago is virtually nonexistent; and a systematic review of algorithmic management identifies only two of 103 empirical studies originating from Latin America and the Caribbean (Chigbu, 2026). Without a pre-sorted platform workforce, participants encountered AI-mediated coordination simultaneously, navigating an identical technical curriculum on a synchronized timeline; when two participants confronted an identical unexplained verdict and drew starkly divergent interpretations, that behavioral variance had no conventional explanations to hide behind.

Each cohort iteration evaluated participant submissions through an automated gate, with each configuration withholding information across distinct structural layers. In the Cursor Boston iteration, a large language model screened initial submissions before cohort members voted, with participants observing only their individual evaluative score. At Hult, written peer reviews and upvotes remained public while the underlying aggregate tally remained concealed—an architecture the for-credit course maintains from prior cohorts. In Trinidad, where operational logs were not preserved, we developed a specialized retrospective protocol to reconstruct gate mechanics. By withholding information at three distinct levels—peer scores in Boston, aggregate tallies at Hult, and administrative operational logs in Trinidad—these diverse implementations demonstrate that opacity constitutes a structural property of the coordination arrangement rather than an artifact of a single platform interface.

All iterations preceding the spring 2027 academic course represent construct development, instrument calibration, and site preparation. The secondary school cohorts, the La Brea initiative, the Boston public program, and the preliminary Hult cohorts provided the empirical ground where the theoretical construct crystallized and where the measurement instruments were designed and piloted. Consequently, none of these preliminary iterations constitutes the formal empirical study, and none is presented as formal empirical findings. Rather, these two years of developmental fieldwork yielded an established research site, two calibrated instruments, and two preliminary test responses.

## The Research Site and Researcher Positionality

The formal empirical investigation begins in a credit-bearing course at Hult International Business School in the spring semester of 2027. Students submit technical coursework to an automated evaluation gate that returns decisions without full procedural explanation, directly mapping onto the constitutive elements of our construct definition—the gate functions as the intermediary, its unexplained verdict embodies operational opacity, and the underlying codebase represents the adaptive rule environment, shifting dynamically alongside peer contributions. The parties evaluated on the far side of the gate are student peers whose qualitative reviews and upvotes remain publicly visible. In contrast, their aggregate scoring weights remain withheld, allowing participants to observe the counterpart judging while the decisive scoring mechanism stays out of view. This setting provides a conservative "hard case" test for opacity because participants construct the very technical systems to which they submit work. Opacity, however, constitutes an emergent property of the person–arrangement relationship; what the arrangement withholds even from its builder-participants is the aggregate scoring algorithm and its shifting decision thresholds, even as the underlying source code remains inspectable.

Because the investigator serves as course instructor, the research design explicitly addresses the methodological challenges of role duality and potentially coercive consent. The instructor exercises no vote within the peer gate. Participant intake is fully anonymized to neutralize institutional power asymmetries—no records track invitations or identify participating students, establishing the structural separation that Ferguson, Yonge, and Myrick (2004) prescribe for faculty conducting research within their own classrooms. This ethical safeguard entails an operational constraint detailed explicitly in the institutional consent agreement. Because survey responses cannot be linked to individual student identifiers, submissions cannot be retracted once entered, rendering the pre-submission participant review step essential.

Furthermore, interviews occur exclusively after evaluative project cycles have formally closed. Brannick and Coghlan (2007) frame role duality as an internal dynamic to manage within insider research, while Anteby (2013) shows that deep personal involvement and analytical distance operate as complementary dimensions rather than opposing poles, with instructional engagement grounding the empirical validity of the instrument's vocabulary. Although Mercer (2007) notes the residual demand characteristics inherent in student-instructor research, methodological trustworthiness is secured through an auditable research trail (Pratt, Kaplan, & Whittington, 2020) aligned with foundational qualitative quality criteria (Tracy, 2010), maintaining interview protocols in a public repository while securely archiving raw responses.

## The Harness Interview Protocol and Abductive Design

The harness interview serves as the primary qualitative instrument for gathering empirical evidence. The protocol prompts participants to complete a designated technical task, receive an outcome that the system leaves incompletely explained, and articulate both their retrospective interpretation of that verdict and their subsequent behavioral response. Four structured modules organize the inquiry—decoding gate requirements, transmitting communicative intent, identifying unannounced rule modifications, and managing perceived evaluative errors. Each module operationalizes the critical incident technique, requiring an end-to-end narration of a specific occasion that includes the participant's baseline task, the system's output, the immediate tactical response, and the ultimate resolution. If an initial response yields a high-level generalization, the protocol prompts the participant to anchor her account in a concrete occurrence; similarly, an initial negative response triggers a single reframing prompt from an alternative angle before the module concludes. Crucially, the protocol excludes all formal theoretical nomenclature, ensuring that respondents formulate accounts using native conceptual categories rather than mimicking researcher-provided frameworks, thereby preserving the authentic structure of participant sensemaking.

The instrument is administered through a situated, in-editor architecture. An embedded artificial intelligence assistant conducts the interview sequentially within the participant's software development environment. The agent executes automated, client-side anonymization in real time, redacting personal names, organizational affiliations, specific geographic localities, and calendar dates before the participant reviews the generated transcript. After the interview, the participant inspects, edits, or deletes text at her discretion before formal submission. Anonymized data are then transmitted to an independent third-party endpoint that programmatically rejects any transcript lacking explicit participant validation, ensuring the technical pipeline enforces ethical verification deterministically alongside individual consent.

We designed three tailored protocols to capture distinct organizational vantage points. The student protocol gathers experiential accounts from individuals participating in the instructional cohorts; the operations protocol collects operational reflections from instructional staff and institutional partner organizations; and the self protocol captures retrospective testimony from the primary investigator to reconstruct the historical gate mechanics of the Trinidad cohorts, administered by an automated agent programmed to restrict inquiries to factual operational events rather than post hoc theoretical interpretations.

The analytical architecture follows an abductive logic of inquiry (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012), employing the three operations of algorithmacy as sensitizing concepts that guide empirical observation without predetermining categorical boundaries (Bowen, 2006). First-cycle qualitative coding adheres to participant-centric in vivo terminology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013). However, rather than forcing emergent findings into a standardized data-structure template (Mees-Buss, Welch, & Piekkari, 2022), we embrace qualitative bricolage to maintain contextual richness and theoretical flexibility (Pratt, Sonenshein, & Feldman, 2022). To safeguard against confirmation bias, the design incorporates a formal negative case boundary—defined as a participant who successfully decodes and satisfies the gate through individual technical mastery alone, without engaging or conceptualizing the human counterpart.

## The Longitudinal Survey Panel

The survey panel serves as the quantitative component of the research design, executing a three-wave longitudinal assessment administered across the sixteen-week semester at weeks 1, 4, and 16—capturing an initial baseline, an evaluation following the first build-and-review cycle, and a concluding assessment. The second wave incorporates a discriminant validity module adapted from Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's (2025) algorithmic competency scale, evaluated against pre-registered latent correlation thresholds to test our theoretical predictions on terms established before data collection—specifically evaluating Understanding against interpreting, Leveraging against specifying intent, and Embracing against an unassigned null baseline. The qualitative harness and the longitudinal panel thus serve complementary analytical functions: the harness captures critical behavioral incidents and participants' native cognitive conceptions in their own words, whereas the panel evaluates latent-factor dimensionality, internal coherence, and discriminant separation from neighboring constructs. The Bentley University Institutional Review Board issued an exempt determination for this three-wave panel on 11 May 2026 (Protocol 260511078).

## Preliminary Instrument Calibration, Target Data Collection, and Table 2

Primary empirical data collection for the formal study initiates during the spring 2027 semester. Two harness interview transcripts currently exist from preliminary instrument calibration—an initial student protocol analyzed at the critical incident level, and a second response recorded on 22 August 2026 awaiting formal coding.

Reporting these calibration interviews as tests of instrument functionality yields three distinct methodological insights. First, the structured modules successfully capture empirical phenomena that person–system competence models systematically overlook. Within the module exploring how participants decoded gate requirements, the test respondent received an adverse evaluation, articulated the underlying technical rationale, and identified a complete absence of contestation channels, revealing an incident of structured forbearance without an institutional forum. Whereas couriers in Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's (2025) framework access formal appeal channels routed directly to the administrative apparatus, this participant encountered no forum for redress. A dyadic person–system model would categorize this behavior as completed adaptation; an algorithmacy framework recognizes it as a structural failure of positionality within an asymmetric triad. The interview block successfully captured the precise empirical material on which these theoretical interpretations diverge.

Second, the instrument effectively differentiates general software tool fluency from the specific coordination competency of algorithmacy. In navigating the module on transmitting intent, the participant copied automated feedback from the gate into an external large language model to generate an explanation, delegating the interpretive task to a secondary software artifact. While this reflects effective technical problem-solving, it leaves the unseen human counterpart unaddressed. An instrument conflating general digital literacy with algorithmacy would capture this tool fluency and misclassify it as coordination competence, whereas our protocol successfully isolates and separates the two behaviors.

Third, preliminary testing exposed specific modules requiring methodological deepening. In the modules addressing unannounced rule changes and perceived evaluative errors, the initial transcript yielded thin, generalized summaries rather than detailed critical incidents because the conversational agent accepted descriptive overviews where concrete occasions were required. In response, we deepened the interview protocols to require a specific behavioral incident for every module, programming the conversational agent to automatically reframe the question from an alternative angle whenever a respondent offers a brief or negative reply.

Target data collection during the spring 2027 semester systematically evaluates all four modules. For decoding gate requirements, the study will collect critical incidents in which participants reconstruct peer voters' expectations alongside complementary accounts from system operators. To capture communicative intent, it will record instances of strategic specification in which participants structure code submissions so unseen peers can receive their design rationale intact. To identify unannounced change, it will record specific occasions when participants detect uncodified shifts in the effective evaluation rule and execute adaptive strategies. For managing perceived errors, it will document how participants attribute evaluative mistakes across the system, peer reviewers, or their intersection.

The student protocol executes dynamically across the concluding weeks of the course as technical projects close, whereas the operations and retrospective self-protocols proceed independently of the academic calendar. The data collection protocol actively pursues disconfirming evidence, operationalized through the negative case of a participant who successfully satisfies the gate through isolated technical mastery without engaging or conceptualizing the human counterpart. Conforming to Pratt's (2009) criteria, empirical sufficiency is achieved when gathered incidents demonstrate theoretical saturation across all four structural blocks. # A Research Agenda

Four programmatic studies translate algorithmacy from a theoretical proposal into an empirically validated construct, each establishing definitive falsification criteria.

The first study executes the qualitative investigation initiated in the spring 2027 course, gathering critical incidents across the three interview protocols until reaching theoretical saturation across all modules in Table 2. This empirical corpus will formally resolve the candidate fourth operation. Specifically, Rahman's (2021) "who-influences" dimension will be established as an autonomous fourth operation if transcripts systematically reveal participants who decode all observable system feedback yet remain structurally incapable of discerning whether the platform firm or peer evaluators drove an outcome; if no such empirical pattern emerges, the tripartite architecture stands as specified.

The second study conducts a formal psychometric discriminant test by co-administering an algorithmacy scale alongside Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's (2025) algorithmic competency scale within a shared sample. We evaluate pre-registered latent correlation predictions established before data collection—specifically testing Understanding against interpreting, Leveraging against specifying intent, and Embracing against an unassigned null baseline. Moderate latent correlations will support the construct's discriminant validity, whereas high collinearity that collapses these theoretical boundaries will disconfirm the construct's distinctiveness.

The third study investigates the institutional distribution and selection dynamics of the competency across a population of formally equivalent participants. Measuring algorithmacy across this baseline tests whether individual variance in the competency predicts coordination success after controlling for baseline technical skill, task complexity, and platform tenure. This design also addresses the structural selection question by tracking low-scoring participants longitudinally, documenting how the organizational form absorbs coordination friction—whether through elevated worker churn, voluntary platform exit, or the psychological internalization of systemic failures as individual shortcomings—and clarifying the institutional costs an organization incurs when the competency is scarce.

The fourth study evaluates external generalizability by deploying the instrument in conventional, non-builder platform settings where the authors hold no institutional stake—such as on-demand ride-hailing fleets, piecework micro-task queues, or online freelance marketplaces where clients remain anonymous, and workers exercise no agency over system design. Observing the manifestation and empirical separation of the three operations across these naturalistic settings will demonstrate that algorithmacy operates as a generalizable coordination construct rather than an artifact of instructional software environments.

# Conclusion and Theoretical Boundaries

Algorithmacy is theorized and formulated here, not empirically validated. Because primary data collection for the main study has not yet commenced, this article offers an analytical architecture, a construct definition, and a pair of research instruments refined through preliminary calibration. Whether the three operations cohere into a unified latent construct, maintain empirical independence, and separate cleanly from general software literacy remain open empirical questions that subsequent field testing must evaluate. Resolving these questions involves a further methodological consideration. As Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) observe, competence operates simultaneously across molar and molecular levels of analysis that cannot be collapsed into one another. The transition from incident-level qualitative narratives to a standardized psychometric scale must therefore be established systematically at each level of measurement.

Neighboring constructs remain viable alternative explanations. Future empirical testing may reveal that Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's (2025) scale adequately captures the variance we attribute to the missing triadic position, or that Rahman's (2021) model of reactivity fully accounts for worker adaptation under opacity. Similarly, Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson's (2020) relational gig literacy presents a strong alternative. However, we characterize it as operating alongside rather than through binding algorithmic systems; comparative empirical testing has yet to demonstrate algorithmacy's superior predictive power. Sandberg's (2000) phenomenographic findings impose an additional boundary: if competence represents an interpretive conception of work, subsequent psychometric instruments must prove that standardized items preserve this holistic conception rather than fragmenting it into isolated behavioral traits.

Recent scholarship continues to populate adjacent conceptual domains, with contemporary research investigating algorithmic skills (Hong, Cheng, & Liu, 2026), platform work literacies (Scolari, Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés, 2026), human–AI collaboration dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), and metacognitive algorithmic competence (Ayasrah, Al-Rousan, Almulla, & Almulla, 2026). Yet each of these frameworks models competence within a person–system dyad, locating counterparts only as external peers, scheduled offline encounters, or software artifacts. This article advances a theoretical claim about a distinct structural position within an asymmetric triad rather than taxonomic novelty.

Three boundary cases in adjacent fields approach this structural position, and in each instance a specific boundary condition distinguishes it from algorithmacy. In medical education, an unrefereed preprint ("AI-Mediated Relational Competence in Medical Education," 2026) incorporates the patient into an AI-mediated clinical framework, diverging from our model only because ultimate decision-making authority resides with the human physician rather than the algorithmic system. In social work, Yang and Liechty (2026) formulate an AI competence model anchored in client trust and rapport within algorithmic child-welfare risk systems; however, their framework treats the algorithm as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous, binding intermediary. In algorithmic matchmaking, Dredge and Anderson (2021) examine eleven interpersonal competencies that omit the matching algorithm. In contrast, Hu and Zhan (2024) analyze algorithmic awareness while omitting the interpersonal counterpart, even though the underlying platform evaluates both users and unilaterally commits matches. Furthermore, our theoretical framework is explicitly bounded within the organization and coordination of interdependent work; clinical medicine, social welfare administration, and interpersonal dating operate outside this core organizational scope.

Paper 1's analytical classifications provide a comparative baseline that this paper leaves unexamined; the theoretical requirement we address would remain necessary even if coordinative co-optation were more fully theorized across other dimensions. While the Hult software engineering course provides an ideal empirical setting to test opacity under controlled conditions, whether findings from this setting generalize to on-demand transportation, micro-task crowdsourcing, or freelance labor remains an empirical question for the broader research agenda.

Finally, this paper does not measure a population, control for confounding human-capital variables, or assume that acquiring algorithmacy constitutes an unalloyed normative good. If individual algorithmacy scores rise while underlying platform structures remain unchanged, the volume of uncompensated coordination labor absorbed by workers increases. Whether that outcome is institutionally desirable depends on structural arrangements outside this construct's measurement scope.

This paper provides the theoretical foundation for that broader investigation by formalizing a construct definition that incorporates the human counterpart, systematically reviewing extant frameworks that omit that position, and introducing a multi-method research design to investigate how individuals learn to coordinate within algorithmically managed organizations.
