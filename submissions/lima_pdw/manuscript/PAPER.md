# **Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation**

**Roger B. Hunt III** · Bentley University · rhunt@bentley.edu

*Dissertation Paper 2. Full paper for the Organization Studies / Organization Theory Paper Development Workshop, Universidad de Piura UDEP, Lima, 5–7 October 2026.*

## **Abstract**

Algorithmacy names the interpretive sensibility an individual exercises when coordinating with another person through an opaque, adaptive algorithmic intermediary that evaluates and binds them both. Organizational scholarship has formalized the governance dynamics of hierarchies, markets, networks, and classical co-optation; coordinative co-optation — enrollment by an algorithm that matches unchosen parties, scores their exchange, and can revoke their accounts — remains theoretically incomplete, leaving the enrolled actor's standing and capacity unmodeled. Rather than adjudicating legal or institutional standing, this article supplies the behavioral micro-foundation coordination inside that arrangement requires. A structured comparative evaluation of eight candidate constructs reveals a persistent omission: models centering the algorithmic system bracket the human counterpart, while models centering interpersonal interaction treat the technological artifact as a transmission channel or an external tool. No construct models individual capacity where the intermediary evaluates and binds both parties engaged in interdependent joint work. I formalize algorithmacy through three constitutive operations, each answering one deficit the triad imposes: *interpreting* the intermediary's rules and the unseen counterpart's intentions from the same composite feedback; *specifying intent* so that meaning survives constrained channels and reaches the counterpart intact; and *keeping track* of the rule's unannounced shifts. An empirical strategy pairs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol with a three-wave longitudinal survey panel in a software-engineering field setting.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication sensibility · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

## **Introduction**

A student submits her code for review. Her classmates read it, comment on it, and vote, all in public where she can watch. An algorithm none of them can inspect then weighs those inputs by a concealed formula and returns a verdict: pass or fail. Her instructor holds no vote and cannot override the outcome, and the weights themselves are retuned as the term runs. To get through, she must coordinate with people she can see through a rule she cannot — reading the verdict for what her peers meant, writing an explanation that a test suite will parse and a person will still understand, and noticing when the hidden rule has moved.

Arrangements of this shape now carry a growing share of everyday work, and organization theory lacks an account of how individuals coordinate with one another inside them. Classical scholarship identifies three canonical coordination forms: hierarchies governed by managerial authority, markets governed by price mechanisms, and networks governed by ongoing social relationships. Digital platforms introduce a distinct configuration by using algorithms to enroll, evaluate, and match participants, and the research that has followed workers into them (Zhou et al., 2025) leaves the human counterpart outside the analytical frame.

Fragments of the sensibility this student exercises are already documented elsewhere. Bucher (2017) shows that ordinary users build working theories of an opaque feed algorithm — an *algorithmic imaginary* — and act on what they believe it wants. DeVito et al. (2018) find those folk theories assembled from many sources and put to work in the service of self-presentation. Litt (2012) names the *imagined audience*, the mental picture of unseen others against which a person calibrates a message she cannot watch land. Manky (2025) watches ride-hailing drivers in Lima read opaque platform metrics to judge whether a passenger is safe to accept. In each case an opaque, adaptive system stands between a person and human others; in none of them does that system return a single determination binding both sides of a jointly executed task. The defensible claim is therefore narrower than the one the phenomenon invites: no construct models the individual capacity to coordinate interdependent joint work with a human counterpart through an intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. That position stands empty.

I model algorithmacy on the developmental lineage of literacy, numeracy, and oracy — the last of these named by Wilkinson (1965), who argued that speaking and listening deserved the standing schooling already granted reading and counting. Literacy enables participation where text mediates communication, numeracy equips individuals for environments structured by quantitative data, and oracy governs interaction through spoken discourse. Algorithmacy enables sustained participation in coordination structured by an algorithm: the sensibility required to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary *and* the counterpart reached through it, to specify intent through the channels that intermediary permits, and to track its rule as that rule shifts. Three terms in the definition carry the structure. Opacity means the participant cannot observe the governing rule directly; adaptivity means the rule itself moves over time; intermediary designates the algorithmic system standing between the focal actor and her human counterpart. The term is not algocracy: Aneesh (2009) uses that word for a mode of organization — rule by code, set beside bureaucracy and the market — whereas algorithmacy names the individual capacity a person exercises inside such an arrangement (see also Danaher, 2016).

Paper 1 names that arrangement *coordinative co-optation*, building the compound term on the mechanism Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) themselves identify — "on platforms they are co-opted" — and a reader's intuition for co-optation will misfire without a gloss. In Selznick's (1949) classical sense, an organization absorbs an external challenger by conferring a seat on him: opposition becomes participation, and the seat carries a formal standing. The platform confers nothing of the kind. It does not hire you, buy from you, or befriend you — it enrolls you, on terms it sets, scores, and can revoke.

Naming this capacity matters because its distribution is consequential and currently invisible. Algorithmacy is held unevenly by participants the platform treats as formally equivalent, so it sorts identical workers into winners and losers while the arrangement attributes the outcome to merit — the claim Proposition 2 formalizes below. An inequality with no name cannot be measured, taught, or compensated for.

The article proceeds in three moves. I first summarize Paper 1, which sets coordinative co-optation beside the classical archetypes (Appendix A). I then evaluate eight candidate constructs from organization theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction, none of which accounts at once for an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary, and build algorithmacy as a bounded behavioral construct: it defines the sensibility the triad demands of a participant and stops there, leaving institutional justice to the governance literatures equipped to judge it (Suddaby, 2010). Third, I present an empirical strategy built on an insider action-research design in a software-engineering course (Appendix B).

## **The Coordination-Form Gap**

Mature organization theory defines a coordination form by specifying both its primary operational mechanism and the formal standing of the participants it governs. Four classical forms pair a governing mechanism with a defined participant standing. In a hierarchy, an employee operates within an authority-governed zone of acceptance, where managerial direction secures alignment through formal procedures (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). In a market, an autonomous transacting party evaluates a posted price term backed by an unrestricted right of refusal (Hayek, 1945). In a network, a partner invests in an elective, revocable relationship grounded in informal reciprocity and mutual reputational capital (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). In classical co-optation, an external challenger accepts a conferred administrative seat, transforming outside opposition into structured institutional participation (Selznick, 1949).

Coordinative co-optation breaks the pairing, because it does not address *participant standing* at all. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) define the departure by observing that while actors in hierarchies command, in markets contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted. This fifth coordination form operates through automated enrollment: digital platforms deploy algorithms to match unchosen parties, evaluate their ongoing interactions, and unilaterally terminate accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Within it, the enrolled participant functions neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price terms, nor a collaborative partner in an elective network. Selznick's absorbed challenger at least received a seat; the enrolled participant receives a queue position.

That omission exposes a micro-foundational gap. Macro-structural scholarship examines how platform firms leverage informational asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016) and non-portable reputational ratings (Curchod et al., 2020) to enforce administrative control, and leaves unmodeled the behavioral mechanisms through which enrolled individuals achieve day-to-day coordination. Regulatory debates concern the standing a participant ought to hold; organization theory still lacks an account of the individual capacity required to coordinate through an opaque, adaptive intermediary regardless of what standing an arrangement assigns. Eight candidate constructs already circulate in adjacent literatures, and I give each a hearing before coining a new one.

## **Extant Constructs**

Any candidate construct for algorithmic coordination can be read for one thing first: the structural role it assigns the computational intermediary. A construct here is a formalized conceptualization of individual capacity, and the diagnostic question for each candidate is whether it treats technology as a passive conduit, an autonomous interlocutor, or an opaque third party.

That question carries a classical warrant the comparison would otherwise leave implicit. Simmel (1902) argued that moving from two parties to three changes the sociological form rather than the population: a dyad ends when either member withdraws, while a triad supports positions no dyad can hold — the impartial mediator who reconciles, the *tertius gaudens* who profits from the other two's opposition, and the third who manufactures that opposition in order to profit from it. In each case the relation between the other two is no longer immediate; it is routed, and the router has interests of its own (Simmel, 1950). Which position the third occupies is therefore what determines what the first two can do with each other, and the status a construct accords the intermediary is not a descriptive detail but the thing that fixes the arrangement's form.

Eight theoretical constructs from organizational theory, communication studies, and human-computer interaction approach the individual capacity required for algorithmic coordination. I evaluate each against a single structural question — where does the model locate the human counterpart on the far side of the interaction? — and I take the eight in the order of standing they grant the machine, which puts human–machine communication first and gives it the longest hearing: no other program has moved the machine as far toward the position of a party. Table 1 summarizes the eight, and the four analytical conditions stated at the close of this section formalize the criteria I apply across all of them.

### **Human–Machine Communication**

Human–machine communication is not one position on the machine but a sequence of them. Over three decades the field has moved its object outward — from a desktop computer that triggers a user's social reflexes, to a communicative subject in its own right, to a delegate writing on one person's behalf to another, to a participant among several people at once — and each move sets the machine nearer to more humans and nearer to more consequence. I take the generations in that order, because the demarcation this paper draws is a position on the same trajectory rather than a break from it. The third analytical condition — an intermediary that independently evaluates and binds both parties — is where each generation stops, and each stops somewhere different.

#### **From Social Actor to Communicative Subject**

The field's oldest thesis is that people treat computers as social actors: Reeves and Nass (1996) substituted a computer for a person in classic social-psychology designs and found the results largely held, and Nass and Moon (2000) named the mechanism *mindlessness*, a social script firing on a superficial cue. Sundar and Nass (2000) then established that the user's attribution is itself a variable. Participants received identical content from a computer while being told the source was the machine itself, the programmer behind it, the network it drew on, or another person using it, and the same message was evaluated differently under each attribution. The authors call the variable *source orientation* — the user's working answer to the question of who is speaking — and their finding is that the user supplies it rather than the interface settling it. That result is the experimental ground for the first operation this paper defines. An actor reading an unexplained outcome at a gate is deciding whether what arrived came from a peer, from a rule, or from the two together, and *interpreting* names the capacity to do it under conditions where no cover story fixes the answer.

What the tradition establishes, it establishes for two positions: the machine is a social actor *toward* the participant and never a party stationed between the participant and someone else. It also locates its social response below the threshold of deliberation, and a binding verdict is the stimulus that lifts it back above. Sundar and Kim (2019) name the disposition sitting closest to that boundary — the *machine heuristic*, a user's belief that machine judgment, being mechanical, is objective and unbiased — which the construct section takes up as a discriminant-validity threat.

The field then named itself around a stronger claim than a measured response. Guzman and Lewis (2020) formalize the shift by arguing that communicative artificial intelligence systems operate as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71), directing the subfield toward "questions of communication as they relate to technologies designed to fulfill the communicator role" (p. 74). Where the default model they describe has people exchanging information through a technology that mediates human interaction (p. 73), human–machine communication reconfigures the technical apparatus as an active interlocutor. Guzman and Lewis acknowledge that communicative technologies can blur mediator and communicator functions, which reinforces the premise of this paper: an algorithmic intermediary operates neither as a passive conduit nor as a conversational partner. The relocation is one seat vacated and another taken within a two-position model (Guzman, 2018), and the field's own inventory shows what it produced: the *SAGE Handbook of Human-Machine Communication* (Guzman, McEwen, & Jones, 2023) runs to sixty-five chapters, and the coordination of two humans through a machine that decides between them is neither a chapter nor an organizing category there.

The structurational critique mounted from inside the field does not fill that seat either. Gibbs et al. (2021) argue that treating human–machine interaction as an isolated dyad obscures the institutional rules the system embodies, and their triad links actor, artifact, and institution; the framework developed here links the focal worker, the intermediary, and an unseen human counterpart. An individual may achieve comprehensive mastery within human–machine communication — decoding the system's cues, sustaining rapport with it — and still lack the sensibility required to coordinate interdependent labor through a system that evaluates and binds both parties. The capacity the field theorizes remains, by definition, an orientation directed toward the machine.

#### **AI-Mediated Communication: The Field Returns to Two Humans**

The field's next generation moved back toward two humans, and confined the computational system to the role of a partisan delegate serving a single communicator. Hancock et al. (2020) define AI-mediated communication as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 90). The model preserves both human interactants across predictive text, smart replies, and synthetic media, and characterizes the dynamic across five parameters: magnitude, media type, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation. A purely structural node count therefore fails to differentiate the framework from algorithmacy, because Hancock and colleagues already model two humans and a computational agent. Within their design the receiver occupies a central position — her inferences are the research agenda's primary dependent variable — and the authors document her vulnerability candidly: disclosure of automated assistance is rare, recipients presume a human authored the message, and the arrangement hinges on the unverified assumption that "the message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency" (p. 89).

What the program found when it put an algorithm between two people runs through attribution and suspicion. Jakesch et al. (2019) had participants judge Airbnb host profiles labeled as human- or machine-written; with every profile labeled machine-written, trust was unaffected, while in a mixed set the profiles believed machine-written were trusted markedly less — the *replicant effect*, a provenance judgment made comparatively. Hohenstein and Jung (2020) put a smart-reply system into a dyadic chat and found that when the conversation went badly, blame shifted toward the system and trust in the human partner rose; borrowing Elish's (2019) term, they call the machine a *moral crumple zone*, a component positioned to absorb responsibility properly attributable elsewhere. Hohenstein et al. (2023) close the lineage with its sharpest result: smart replies made conversations faster and more positive, and a participant *suspected* of using them was evaluated more negatively. The benefit accrues to the interaction and the cost to an individual, on the basis of a suspicion never stated aloud for her to answer. In none of these studies does the algorithm issue a determination binding both parties before either acts; what the record supplies is the mechanism by which a far-side person pays for a system's involvement in work she did not watch being done.

The decisive difference between the two frameworks is categorical rather than parametric: AI-mediated communication governs symbolic messages, whereas algorithmacy governs binding institutional verdicts. Every dimension of Hancock's framework characterizes message alteration, even when optimization aligns with platform priorities. The intermediary in the setting examined here matches unchosen parties, ranks performance, and terminates accounts, and no combination of autonomy or message magnitude transforms an assistant that edits text into an authority binding both interactants to determinations neither commissioned. An editor is not a judge. The requirement that the agent operate "on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90) marks the same boundary from the other side: the algorithmic third party operates on behalf of no one present. I adopt Hancock's optimization-goal parameter as the axis separating the two frameworks — their agent optimizes for a designated communicator's goals, the intermediary here for institutional metrics external to both — and I build on their account of a receiver whose acceptance of algorithmic mediation is presumed rather than secured.

#### **Machines Among Groups**

The delegate is not the only position the field has built for a machine among people. Jung, Martelaro, and Hinds (2015) had a robot intervene with repair utterances aimed at a violation between two people, shifting how the pair perceived each other; Traeger et al. (2020) ran fifty-one groups of three people and one robot through a collaborative game and found that robots which admitted error and expressed uncertainty produced markedly more equal and more responsive conversation among the humans; Seeber et al. (2020) set an agenda for machines as teammates, and Sebo et al. (2020) reviewed the literature entire. Roboticists have built and tested the machine-in-the-middle position, which the demarcation has to accommodate rather than deny. What the systematic review does not contain is the other half. No study in Sebo and colleagues' survey measures a group member's capacity to fare well in the arrangement, and in none of them does the machine's output bind the participants; the robot proposes, facilitates, or discloses, and the humans decide. Traeger's result runs forward into the propositions as well: what the robot varied was the legibility of its own uncertainty, and legibility improved the humans' coordination with each other, which is why Proposition 3 below treats transparency as an institutional lever rather than a fixed perimeter.

#### **Asymmetric Interpretation and the Demarcation**

Beneath all four generations sits an asymmetry that the field's deepest theoretical statement makes explicit. Suchman (2007) argues that plans are resources for action rather than determinants of it, and that human–machine interaction breaks down where a system is designed as though it could read a user's purpose from her conduct. The user brings the full situated interpretive resources of a person to the encounter, while the machine holds only a thin, pre-categorized trace of hers, and the repair burden falls on the human because she is the only party equipped to carry it. Algorithmacy inherits that asymmetry and puts a second interpreter behind it. In the triad the same thin trace is also what prices the counterpart, so the participant interprets *through* the asymmetry toward a second full interpreter she cannot see and whose reading never returns to her.

Set the generations in sequence and the demarcation reads as a position on the field's own trajectory. The machine begins as a social respondent to one person (Reeves & Nass, 1996; Nass & Moon, 2000), becomes a communicative subject in its own right (Guzman & Lewis, 2020), then a delegate carrying one person's message to another (Hancock et al., 2020), then a participant among several at once (Seeber et al., 2020; Sebo et al., 2020). Each move places the machine nearer to more humans and nearer to more consequence. The triad names the next position on that line: the machine above both humans, reading each of them and returning a determination that binds them both, which no construct in the field yet occupies. I adopt Guzman and Lewis's distinction between the conduits of computer-mediated communication and the communicative partners of their own field while departing from its ontological premise, reconceptualizing the apparatus as an opaque, binding intermediary inside an asymmetric triad. Nothing in the field's equipment rules that position out; what is missing is the account of what the person on the far side must be able to do. Human–machine communication defined itself against the conduit tradition it inherited, and that tradition still holds the field's most developed account of what competence in a mediated exchange consists of. It is the next hearing.

### **Computer-Mediated Communication Competence**

Spitzberg's (2006) model of computer-mediated communication competence carries the architecture of interpersonal competence into digital settings intact: the medium enters as a set of parameters conditioning a dyadic exchange, never as a party to it. His framework organizes individual capacity across motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and relational outcomes (p. 638), and the premise underneath is that digital mediation recalibrates the speed and bandwidth of message exchange while leaving the structure of interpersonal competence undisturbed. The human actors stationed at either terminal remain the sole coordinators and evaluators of the interaction.

Spitzberg locates competence in relational co-construction, and the counterpart is his essential evaluative instrument: "competent interactants can facilitate the competence of cointeractants" (p. 650), and his five outcome criteria — appropriateness, effectiveness, coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development (p. 648) — presuppose an architecture in which both interactants perceive one another.

That relational design establishes its own boundary. Consider an actor who demonstrates complete mastery across every dimension of the model, placed within an arrangement where an opaque algorithm evaluates both parties and issues a binding determination. The breakdown does not stem from a deficit in competence, nor does it require the counterpart to disappear. The intermediary severs the link between the counterpart's evaluation and its consequence: her appraisal reaches the focal actor only as an isolated input to an opaque aggregation neither party controls. Grounding theory states why the mutual calibration Spitzberg's criteria require is unavailable rather than merely difficult. Clark and Brennan (1991) model communication as the joint construction of common ground: a contribution is complete not when it is uttered but when the parties hold mutual evidence that it was understood, and reaching that state — *grounding* — runs through acknowledgment, a relevant next turn, and repair. Every one of those forms of evidence is structurally withheld in the triad. The counterpart returns no acknowledgment the actor can read, no next turn addressed to her, and no route to repair, while the verdict that does arrive is a judgment about understanding rather than evidence of it. Grounding cannot close.

I take over Spitzberg's normative criteria and reject the relational topology his model presupposes. Appropriateness and effectiveness remain the most robust standards available for evaluating communicative efficacy; what algorithmacy cannot adopt is a directly accessible human receiver whose unmediated appraisal serves as the measure of competence. Under the triad the two criteria acquire triadic referents. An act is effective when it survives the gate *and* the counterpart decodes the meaning the actor intended — the dual criterion *specifying intent* already implies. An act is appropriate when it fits the counterpart's expectations as the actor has reconstructed them under opacity, which is what *interpreting* produces. Spitzberg's standards survive the move; the evaluator who administers them is what changes, and the accuracy of the actor's reconstruction becomes an empirical quantity rather than a presupposition. Spitzberg models what competence *is* in a mediated dyad. The strongest account of how communicators *adapt* to what a medium withholds belongs to a different lineage.

### **Social Information Processing and the Hyperpersonal Model**

Walther (1992) dismantled the consensus that text-based exchange is impersonal because it strips nonverbal cues. Communicators adapt, he argued, encoding relational information into whatever the channel does carry — word choice, timing, message length — and given enough time they build relationships as personal as face-to-face ones: a narrow channel constrains the rate of relational communication, not its ceiling. Walther (1996) then pushed the claim past parity. The *hyperpersonal model* holds that mediated exchange can exceed face-to-face intimacy through a sender who exploits editability to present an optimized self, a receiver who fills the missing cues with favorable inference, and a behavioral-confirmation loop in which an idealized partner behaves accordingly. Against the four analytical conditions, this hearing satisfies individual capacity and an active, interdependent counterpart as robustly as anything in the comparison. The adaptation thesis is also the closest thing in the literature to *specifying intent*, argued three decades early: Walther's communicators develop, through experience and without instruction, the capacity to convey what a narrow channel was thought unable to carry. A reviewer who knows this literature will ask how algorithmacy is anything but social information processing with an algorithm swapped in for the human receiver.

The answer sits in the loop's closing condition. Walther's adaptation works because the party who receives the message is the party who evaluates it and replies: the sender learns what carries because the receiver responds, and every adjustment is calibrated against feedback from the addressee herself. In the triad, the party that evaluates is not the party whose understanding matters. The intermediary issues the assessment and the counterpart holds the meaning; neither returns anything resembling a reply, and a verdict is not feedback from anyone. The student at the gate can write her pull-request description a hundred ways, and what the failed verdict tells her is that this one did not clear. Which of her two readers the sentence lost, and where, is not in the signal. The hyperpersonal loop has nowhere to attach — not because the channel is poorer than Walther's, but because the evaluator and the addressee have come apart. Walther's communicator adapts to a channel. The triad's participant answers to one.

I take that boundary as an inheritance, and it runs the other way too. Walther's evidence is the strongest prior in this literature for expecting participants to develop the capacity at all, and Walther (2007) supplies the rival the empirical design needs: editability and asynchrony free cognitive resources, and what a communicator does with the freed capacity produces the effect. Cognitive slack of that kind is not equally available, so channel experience and available slack predict uneven outcomes without any distinct triadic capacity, and Study 3 carries both as measured covariates (Appendix C). Experience with a channel is one account of where the capacity comes from. Formal instruction in the technology is another.

### **AI Literacy**

AI literacy frameworks treat the algorithm as an object of formal technical comprehension. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace," and derive seventeen competencies from five foundational inquiries into what artificial intelligence is, what it can do, how it works, how it should be used, and how people perceive it. Only two of the seventeen reference human actors, and both confine humans to positions outside live interaction: Competency 10 requires learners to "recognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems," and Competency 5 positions human cognition as a comparative baseline. Interpersonal collaboration appears only in an auxiliary design consideration advising educators to "foster social interaction and collaboration." Human peers populate the physical classroom and remain absent from the competence model. The omission propagated: Ng et al. (2021) synthesized the seventeen into four dimensions that subsequent scales inherited, each operationalizing competence as a unilateral transaction with an artifact, and DigComp 2.2 (Vuorikari et al., 2022) keeps its human counterpart in one competence area and its artificial-intelligence material in an appendix — when another person appears the technology is a channel, and when the system appears the counterpart drops out.

Algorithmacy diverges from AI literacy in how its dimensions are obtained. Long and Magerko assemble their catalog by scoping criteria of definitional alignment, bibliographic prevalence, and pedagogical utility, a route that supplies no principle explaining why nine competencies govern technical mechanics and one addresses ethics. The operations of algorithmacy follow instead from the specific informational deficits an opaque, adaptive, binding arrangement imposes, and I perform that derivation in the next section rather than asserting it. What I adopt is their literacy genealogy, which anchors the construct in the continuum of human symbolic capacity.

### **Algorithmic Competency**

Zhou et al. (2025) provide the first psychometrically validated scale measuring individual capability within algorithmic management, and it sets the empirical benchmark for construct development in this domain. From 99 interviews with Chinese ride-hailing drivers and couriers, through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, a validation study, and a three-wave panel, they validate a second-order, four-factor, twelve-item instrument, defining algorithmic competency as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2). The four dimensions — Understanding, Embracing, Leveraging, and Remediating — extend beyond declarative knowledge into affective disposition and behavioral navigation, and general digital competence correlates only moderately with the aggregate scale (*r* = .37).

The theoretical boundary of the instrument lies in the position it assigns the human counterpart, who appears strictly as an external evaluative outcome. Item 8 frames the customer as a distant punitive consequence ("I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback"), directing adaptation toward the platform rules rather than the relational counterpart. Item 11 reduces triadic coordination to an attitudinal belief about matching logistics ("I think the platform AM is highly efficient, such as in customers–workers matching") and measures no interactive capacity toward the matched individual. The sole contestation mechanism, Item 6, asks whether a worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve vulnerabilities in AM" (p. 8): confronted with an unjustified rating, a courier compiles evidence and appeals to the platform's automated apparatus, and the grievance never engages the customer who generated the rating. Customer-oriented service behavior enters the authors' structural model, through Peccei and Rosenthal's (2000) scale, only as a downstream outcome.

Ceiling performance on the twelve items therefore fails to guarantee coordination efficacy because the construct's design is dyadic. A worker can master dispatch dynamics, trust algorithmic matching, exploit platform rules, and remediate errors through technical appeals, and still hold no capacity to reconstruct what an unseen counterpart intended or to project legibility toward her. The scale cannot see the counterpart because the construct never let her in, and extending the instrument would be a re-derivation rather than an appended item: every validated dimension is anchored in the worker–platform dyad. Rebuilding the construct from a triadic starting point is the task this paper performs. I inherit Zhou and colleagues' development protocol and one of their findings: the primary antecedents of algorithmic competency in their structural model are social — informal peer support moderated by collectivist orientation — which supports the socialization property stated below. Individuals do not master opaque systems through isolated trial and error; they cultivate the sensibility through shared practice and collective sensemaking.

### **Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation**

Rahman's (2021) "invisible cage" conceptualizes how workers navigate algorithmic evaluation systems that replace codified bureaucratic rules with structural indeterminacy. Studying a freelancing platform's transition from a transparent five-star rating to an opaque, percentile-based Job Success Score, Rahman defines the cage by five properties: hidden and fluctuating criteria, assessments executed at unpredictable intervals, unidentifiable score magnitudes, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority divided between the platform and the individual client (p. 976). Together they establish systemic closure: "even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque" (p. 976). Workers respond through *experimental reactivity*, trial-and-error adjustment to reverse-engineer the score, or *constrained reactivity*, restricting activity to insulate reputational capital, with the choice driven by financial dependence on the platform and prior exposure to evaluation shocks. Those dynamics discipline the framework developed here, because they show individual adaptation often deepening subordination.

Rahman's fieldwork documents the human counterpart, and the formal construct excludes her relational agency. He interviewed 18 clients alongside 80 freelancers (p. 956), and his records show clients blind to how their private ratings altered a freelancer's composite score, with the platform assuring them that feedback would never appear directly to the freelancer (p. 960). Because the framework models algorithmic systems through labor discipline and managerial control, that triadic dynamic remains outside the construct. The fifth property reduces the counterpart to an ambiguity in metric calculation: a worker confronted with a score drop cannot discern whether it stems from client dissatisfaction or an unannounced recalibration, and in Rahman's formulation that uncertainty is an epistemic problem about the metric. Algorithmacy addresses a different problem, deciphering the expectations of a human counterpart through an opaque intermediary. The one adaptation in which Rahman's freelancers direct action toward the counterpart reinforces the boundary: under constrained reactivity they migrate relationships to external channels, in violation of the terms of service. Reaching the counterpart requires exiting the apparatus.

I take three insights from Rahman's framework into the construct. Shifting and opaque criteria are an enduring environmental condition of algorithmic coordination, which establishes the necessity of the third operation, *keeping track*. The five properties expose an interpretive problem at the system's boundary — whether to attribute an evaluative shift to machine logic or human discretion. And Rahman's demonstration that dependence and shock history drive behavioral variance is the strongest unanswered charge against this paper. If those two explain the variance in coordination outcomes, algorithmacy explains nothing a labor-process account has not already explained better, and no amount of construct clarity settles the question. Only a design that measures both, in the same participants, can — which is why platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks enter Study 3 as baseline controls rather than as acknowledgments.

### **Gig Literacies**

Sutherland et al. (2020) incorporate both market sides into their empirical design, and their construct remains bounded by a dyadic struggle over platform autonomy. Building on Jarrahi and Sutherland's (2019) account of the sensemaking, circumventing, and manipulating tactics gig workers deploy against automated systems, they formalize *platform literacy* as "the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470). Interviews with 20 freelancers and 19 clients and a dual-sided platform walkthrough (pp. 461–463) yield a fivefold typology spanning reputation, self-presentation, productivity, transaction risk, and relationship building, the last of which has contractors cultivating rapport "to build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" for repeat engagements (p. 468).

That relational dimension approaches the interpersonal domain of coordination, and the authors' own evidence shows relationship-building functioning as an escape from algorithmic governance rather than mastery within it. When participant P39 confronted an algorithmically depressed rating that could not be rehabilitated in the interface, the worker migrated established client relationships off-platform (p. 469). Rapport is an instrument of disintermediation, extracting the collaboration from the system and restoring an unmediated bilateral channel where scoring no longer applies. Sutherland et al. draw the same boundary from the other side by contrasting talent marketplaces such as Upwork, where participants can evaluate, select, and converse directly with clients, against dispatch systems such as Uber, where algorithms assign labor deterministically (p. 470). Their framework models competencies enacted *adjacent to* a digital intermediary rather than coordination executed *through* an opaque, binding one, and adding relational indicators to such an instrument would capture literacies exercised alongside the platform rather than under its constraints. I take two insights from them: platform enrollment without institutional standing shifts coordination burdens onto individual capacity, and workers acquire fluency with opaque metrics through situated social learning rather than documentation.

### **What These Boundaries Share**

One limitation runs through all eight: no extant framework theorizes an active human counterpart and an opaque, consequential intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties. The vacancy does not stem from omitting the second human actor, since AI-mediated communication, social information processing, algorithmic competency, and gig literacies each incorporate a counterpart. The limitation lies in how each framework couples the intermediary to the counterpart. Where a framework admits a human counterpart, it reduces the computational system to a passive conduit, a channel learned and exceeded, a partisan delegate, or an obstacle to circumvent, never an authority rendering binding determinations. Where a framework models the intermediary as opaque and binding, it demotes the counterpart to an exogenous rating source or an outcome variable, never an interdependent actor with whom the focal participant coordinates. Systematic reviews reach the same boundary from inside the algorithmic-literacy literature (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025; Iyamu et al., 2026).

The nearest rival to the vacancy claim sits one field over, in the folk-theories and imagined-audience literatures, and three dimensions dispose of it. Bucher's (2017) *algorithmic imaginary*, DeVito et al.'s (2018) folk theories of feeds, and Litt's (2012) imagined audience together describe a person managing self-presentation to human others through an opaque, adaptive, curating algorithm — a triad, with opacity, adaptivity, and folk-theoretic interpreting all present. Visibility labor reaches the same intersection from media studies, with Cotter's (2019) Instagram creators playing "the visibility game" against inferred algorithmic rules before a human audience (see also Abidin, 2016). The rebuttal is not that the configuration is absent but that its coupling differs on all three counts. The algorithm curates and ranks for the presenter and binds her alone; the audience is an aggregate rather than a party whose intentions must be reconstructed and to whom something must be conveyed; and no work is jointly executed, so nothing turns on whether the two sides arrive at a working alignment. Novelty here survives on *mutual bindingness* and *interdependent joint work*, not on the triad or the opacity, and I state the claim at that width.

Adjacent disciplines have identified triadic configurations and analyzed them from the system's side. Online dispute resolution theorized the infrastructure as an active "fourth party" (Katsh & Rifkin, 2001; see also Wing et al., 2021), and organizational theorists formalize the same shape — Curchod et al.'s (2020) asymmetrical triad sustained by a visibility gap, Cameron's (2024) "algorithmic labor triangle" — explaining what the triad is and how platforms enforce discipline while omitting the capacity separating an actor who coordinates effectively within it from one who does not.

Four analytical conditions distinguish triadic coordination from adjacent organizational models, and I applied them in every hearing above. First, the construct must operationalize an individual actor's behavioral and interpretive capacity rather than an institutional affordance or communal subculture. Second, the human counterpart must function as an active, interdependent collaborator within the focal coordination episode, which excludes frameworks positioning humans solely as upstream software engineers, informal peer advisors, or passive evaluative recipients. Third, the computational intermediary must independently evaluate and execute binding determinations upon both interactants, which rules out passive transmission conduits, partisan automated delegates, and macro-level design standards lacking participant-side operationalizations. Fourth, the critique must substantiate a genuine vacancy within organizational and communication theory rather than a semantic dispute over taxonomic labels. No candidate meets all four.

Fieldwork already documents the latent capacity: Manky (2025) shows ride-hailing drivers in Lima (N=40) interpreting opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness — a counterpart-directed interpretive sensibility, mobilized for bodily security, that lacks formal conceptualization. What organizational scholarship needs is a communicative construct built for the triadic position the empirical work has uncovered and left unequipped.

## **Algorithmacy: A Construct for Coordinative Co-optation**

Construct clarity in organizational theory turns on four things: a precise definition, explicit scope conditions, stated semantic relations to neighboring constructs, and demonstrated theoretical coherence (Suddaby, 2010). The subsections below do that work in order. Simmel's vocabulary fixes what the intermediary is before the construct specifies what its participants must do. Among the third positions a triad supports, the platform occupies the one Simmel (1950) calls the arbitrator — the third who does not reconcile two parties' positions but decides between them — with one decisive difference. Simmel's arbitrator holds an authority the two parties conferred on him and can withdraw; the platform holds an authority neither party conferred, and the only withdrawal on offer is exit.

That omission is where the construct's own boundary starts. I build algorithmacy as a micro-foundational construct, which requires decoupling an actor's behavioral capacity from her formal institutional standing. Whether an enrolled participant holds the protections of an employee, the exit rights of a market buyer, or the hearing rights of a co-opted stakeholder is a question of governance, and algorithmacy operates independently of how it is resolved, because the operations derive from opacity, adaptivity, and bindingness — properties of the arrangement, not of participant status. A hearing right that leaves the rule undisclosed alters standing without relieving the deficits that demand the capacity; reforms that eliminate the three properties dissolve coordinative co-optation and move the interaction outside the construct's scope. The boundary this article draws runs between capacity and standing — not between micro and macro, a distinction the propositions below deliberately cross. Locating algorithmacy within the individual does not repeat the methodological individualism Gibbs et al. (2021) criticize: the construct is a micro-foundation with social antecedents (Felin et al., 2015), acquired through peer networks and enacted individually.

Those same three properties fix the scope conditions. Algorithmacy applies where a computational intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding, and it ceases to operate under static administrative rules or in open markets where parties negotiate directly (Hayek, 1945; Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). Online freelance platforms occupy a hybrid boundary, because participants can negotiate terms or migrate off-platform (Sutherland et al., 2020); dispatch networks, piecework queues, and peer-evaluation systems that conceal scoring rules sit at the core. The conditions pick out configurations of coordination, not industrial sectors.

### **Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation**

I embed algorithmacy in a nomological network of four neighboring constructs and state each relation as a testable prediction, pre-registered for the pooled sample in Study 2 (Appendix C) because the Paper 3 panel (N ≈ 50) cannot test latent correlations. Against Algorithmic Competency (Zhou et al., 2025), I predict moderate positive correlations between congruent dimensions — *Understanding* with *interpreting*, *Leveraging* with *specifying intent* — and a null with *Embracing*, an affective disposition rather than a coordination capacity. That predicted null is the sharpest single test in the network, and Sundar and Kim's (2019) machine heuristic makes it worth running: a participant who believes machine judgment is objective can report confidence in her reading of the gate without having developed any interpretive capacity. If algorithmacy's scores collapse once heuristic endorsement is partialled out, the construct is measuring a belief. Against Reactivity (Rahman, 2021), discriminant validity requires low-to-moderate latent correlations; against Computer-Mediated Communication Competence (Spitzberg, 2006), *interpreting* and *specifying intent* should correlate moderately and positively; against Gig Literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020), algorithmacy should correlate weakly or negatively with *relationship-building*, an off-platform strategy, and positively with the four subdimensions that reflect navigation within the infrastructure.

### **Constitutive Operations: The Tripartite Model**

Organization theory has a name for what the participant is short of, and it is not information. Daft and Lengel (1986) separate *uncertainty*, the absence of data, from *equivocality*, the presence of several incompatible readings of the data one already holds, and only the second calls for a rich, feedback-bearing channel. The participant at an opaque gate has ratings, outcomes, and a rejection history; what she lacks is any way to settle which reading is right, and the channel that could settle it is fixed and is also the judge. Dennis, Fuller, and Valacich (2008) name what goes missing as *convergence*.

Each structural property of the triad imposes one informational deficit, and each deficit demands one operation. Opacity withholds the rule, so the actor must reconstruct it. Bindingness turns every message into evidence before a verdict, so the actor must encode for judge and counterpart at once. Adaptivity unsettles whatever the actor has learned, so the reconstruction has to be maintained rather than achieved once. Three properties, three deficits, three operations:

  - **Interpreting:** Reconstructing, from ambiguous and fragmented system feedback, the underlying actions, constraints, and intentions of both the algorithmic intermediary and the unseen human counterpart — was the failed verdict a peer's downvote, or a shifted threshold?
  - **Specifying intent:** Structuring and encoding communicative action so that intended meaning survives the narrow, standardized signaling channels the intermediary permits, and reaches the counterpart intact — a pull-request description written so the test suite parses it *and* the unseen peer understands why the change matters.
  - **Keeping track:** Detecting temporal shifts, continuous drift, and unannounced recalibrations in the rules that govern and bind both interactants — the commit style that cleared the gate in week four now draws review comments, and nothing was announced.

That mapping is what the phrase "derived from the structure of the triad" means in this paper, and it is the whole of what the phrase claims. Whether the three exhaust the deficits — or whether evaluative attribution, deciding whether the platform or the human client drove a score change (Rahman, 2021), stands as a fourth — is what RQ4 tests; I house attribution provisionally within *interpreting*, and that placement is the null hypothesis the measurement model carries. Without a grounding loop to join them, machine-facing decoding and counterpart-facing encoding become two unaided inferences rather than two halves of one process (Clark & Brennan, 1991), which is why the model splits *interpreting* from *specifying intent*.

Each operation earns its place by requiring the intermediary and the counterpart at once. *Interpreting* does so directly: the student at the gate cannot ask the gate what it wanted or ask her reviewers what they meant; she has one signal from which to infer two minds. *Specifying intent* does so through dual-audience encoding, since the channel is also the evaluative instrument and a single act must satisfy the intermediary's parsing rules and the counterpart's expectations concurrently. The pilot supplies the rival outcome rather than the confirming case: one participant met the intent-specification module by routing her explanation through an external language model to optimize machine parsability, and never addressed the peer at all. Machine-legible, peer-illegible, is precisely the failure mode dual-audience encoding names, and Table B1 lists that pattern as the disconfirming criterion for this operation. *Keeping track* requires the triad through an evidentiary mechanism: under opacity, rule shifts register through altered counterpart behavior and systematic variation in peer ratings, so an actor who monitors only platform telemetry misses the recalibration entirely. Duran (1983) named the nearest antecedent, communicative adaptability, but his communicator reads a partner and a setting she can see; the triad's actor reads outcomes from a rule that announces nothing, which is why noticing is part of the operation rather than a precondition for it.

The three operations function as a recursively linked process, not an inventory of discrete traits: a coordination incident begins with *interpreting* an ambiguous outcome, that reading informs *specifying intent* in the actor's next act, and *keeping track* then evaluates whether the rules have shifted, generating updated priors that feed back into *interpreting* for the next one. The operations are therefore neither reflective indicators of a single latent trait nor formative components of a static index; the survey establishes that they are distinct from neighboring constructs, and the critical incident technique shows that they operate as the loop Figure 1 depicts.

### **Competency, Not Skill or Capability**

Algorithmacy is an individual competency rather than an isolable skill or a firm-level capability. A skill is a procedural routine that can be codified and transmitted through instruction, and the rules of opaque systems remain proprietary and dynamic. Spitzberg (2015) orders the terms by level and attribution instead — skills are repeatable, goal-directed behaviors, competence the impression that they were appropriate and effective in context — and I adopt his ordering, because a molar impression formed by an evaluator is exactly what the triad withholds. A capability resides in routines and assets a firm owns and deploys (Teece et al., 1997), and the firm alone owns the matching algorithms and rating systems. Sandberg (2000), examining engine optimizers at Volvo, demonstrated that competence reflects an actor's qualitative conception of work rather than an inventory of attributes; that finding is what algorithmacy extends to mediated triadic coordination. Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) argue from the other direction that interpersonal competence is a relational quality jointly enacted and evaluated by interacting parties, and algorithmic coordination disrupts that model without eliminating the counterpart's judgment: she continues to render appraisals but never occupies a position from which mutual competence can be negotiated. Where the conditions for relational co-construction are absent, competence reverts from a jointly enacted judgment to an individually held capacity, and algorithmacy identifies the interpretive and expressive capacities an individual must supply under that reversion.

### **Acquisition and Distribution**

Two properties govern the construct's emergence and its empirical manifestation. First, algorithmacy is acquired through participation. Because operators withhold the decision rules, formal training cannot substitute for immersion, and algorithmacy predicts that the classroom instruction Laupichler et al.'s (2023) SNAIL scale evaluates cannot alone cultivate the capacity. Jablin and Sias (2001) describe the closest antecedent: newcomers acquire communicative competence through participation rather than instruction, inferring what counts as competent from how others respond to them. The minimal pair is the responder — their newcomer reads responses from the colleagues who evaluate her, while the triad's participant reads an outcome from a rule and never the counterpart she is trying to reach. Second, algorithmacy is predicted to be distributed unevenly across individuals the platform treats as formally equivalent. Whether it does vary — and whether that variance survives controls for platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks (Rahman, 2021), accumulated channel experience and cognitive slack (Walther, 2007), mindless social scripting (Nass & Moon, 2000), and machine-heuristic endorsement (Sundar & Kim, 2019) — is what Study 3 measures (Appendix C). Each rival predicts variance without any triadic capacity. The construct predicts the variance; it does not assume it.

### **Theoretical Implications**

Formalizing algorithmacy reorients organizational research on algorithmic management from mechanisms of control to the mechanics of execution. Where labor process theorists conceptualize algorithmic workplaces as contested terrain centered on direction, evaluation, and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020), algorithmacy isolates the competencies required to coordinate through those constraints. The distinction locates interventions: transparency mandates modify the information environment, whereas cultivating algorithmacy enhances an actor's capacity to act within it whatever its transparency. The micro-foundation also supplies the operational engine macro-structural models of platform organizing presuppose. Organizational theorists have detailed how platform designs exploit coordination gaps (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024) and generate consent through enrollment (Cameron, 2024; Curchod et al., 2020); those accounts concern the institutional forces securing compliance rather than the communicative capacities sustaining daily coordination. Online dispute resolution names what the arrangement withholds — transparency, accountability, and informed participation (Wing, 2016) — and algorithmic regimes forfeit each while directing contestation to the platform's own apparatus rather than to the counterpart (Curchod et al., 2020; Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy, 2017). The three operations are the individual-level capacities participants must mobilize because those protections are absent.

### **A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves**

The paradox is this: individual mastery of the arrangement stabilizes the arrangement that burdens individuals. Capacity and fairness vary independently, so higher competency does not signal greater welfare or legitimacy. Elish's (2019) hazard applies to the construct and not only to the arrangement it describes: a framing that reads variance in outcomes off variance in worker capacity is itself, structurally, a crumple-zone move, and holding capacity and fairness apart is what keeps the construct from re-billing a system's opacity as an individual's deficit. The decoupling also yields an aggregation mechanism: where Cameron (2024) shows workers manufacturing consent through confined choices, an enrolled population's collective competency determines whether frictions trigger visible breakdown or get quietly absorbed (Felin et al., 2015). Cohorts exposed to identical gates but differing in mean algorithmacy should therefore differ in attrition, contestation volume, and coordination failures, and identical friction rates would falsify the claim.

Three propositions follow from this tension, and each clarifies why algorithmacy is not an unalloyed individual or organizational benefit.

**Proposition 1.** *Higher algorithmacy improves an individual participant's coordination outcomes while leaving the arrangement's procedural justice unchanged; performance gains concentrate in private navigation rather than in institutional disclosures won, formal appeals granted, or rules rendered legible to peers.* It extends to a formalized competency what Rahman (2021) and Sutherland et al. (2020) document for reactivity and informal literacies.

**Proposition 2.** *Because algorithmacy is distributed unevenly across participants whom the platform treats as formally equivalent, that variance introduces a secondary axis of inequality among actors whom the organizational form designates as interchangeable.* It transposes the logic of second-level digital divides (Hargittai, 2002) to triadic coordination.

**Proposition 3.** *Increasing an intermediary's transparency reduces the level of algorithmacy required to coordinate through it, establishing the construct's scope conditions as a malleable institutional lever rather than a fixed analytical perimeter.* Disclosure policy is therefore an alternative governance pathway to compensatory competency.

## **Empirical Strategy**

### **Site and Design**

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), I separate the core action research project — a longitudinal intervention in which cyclical evaluations informed pedagogical, gate-mechanism, and instrument redesigns — from the formal research project conducted upon it. Paper 3 is insider research on that program's mature configuration (Brannick & Coghlan, 2007): I will execute no further interventions within the focal cohorts, and the protocols keep facilitation separate from participants' reported accounts. Appendix B sets out the full design, the instrument architecture, and the falsification criteria summarized here.

A critic might read the site as an ordinary hybrid of pedagogical hierarchy, peer review, and automated grading, and three properties of the gate refuse that reading. The gate binds both parties: the submitter's progression depends on an aggregated score she cannot inspect, and the evaluator's review accuracy feeds her own gate score, so a determination she cannot see prices work she performed on both sides of the exchange. Mutual bindingness — the property that separates algorithmacy from all eight constructs in Table 1 — holds at the site rather than being stipulated for it. The platform assigns reviewers, so participants do not self-select into evaluation queues, which is the matching of unchosen parties that defines coordinative co-optation (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). And the rule itself moves: weights and thresholds are retuned over the term, and the instructor holds no vote and no override, so no identifiable office administers a legible rule (Weber, 1978). The codebase against which submissions are evaluated also evolves as participants merge contributions, but that drift is publicly legible in the repository history, which is why the coding protocol for *keeping track* excludes it — detecting a change the history announces is technical monitoring, and Table B1 states exactly that as the disconfirming criterion. One parameter remains undocumented, and I record it in the protocol before fielding: what exit and escalation rights a contested verdict carries. Until the contestation pathway is catalogued, the site's correspondence to the form on that dimension is unestablished.

### **Questions, Instruments, and Status**

Four research questions map to the constitutive operations and the boundary condition they test. RQ1 (*interpreting*): how do participants decode and make sense of unexplained or contradictory outcomes generated by the opaque gate? RQ2 (*specifying intent*): do participants address the peer as an active, interdependent party, or direct communicative action exclusively toward machine legibility? RQ3 (*keeping track*): how do participants identify and adapt to unannounced shifts in the benchmarks the gate enforces? RQ4 (evaluative attribution): how do participants attribute perceived evaluative errors across the triad — to algorithmic weighting, to peer judgment, or to their interaction — and does resolving the locus of authority function as an independent fourth operation? An actor may detect that a standard has moved without diagnosing the locus of failure for a single outcome, and the measurement model leaves that separation open.

The multi-method design couples an AI-administered critical incident interview protocol, embedded in the development environment and built around four diagnostic modules corresponding to the research questions, with a three-wave survey panel at weeks 1, 4, and 16. Each participant generates a self-derived identifier at intake and re-enters it across waves, so the panel is researcher-blind by procedure; with a cohort of roughly fifty I cannot rule out incidental recognition, and the coding protocol strips identifying particulars for that reason. The status, plainly: Bentley University's Institutional Review Board determined the study exempt (Protocol 260511078); the formal cohort, roughly fifty students in a sixteen-week Hult course, fields in spring 2027; two pilot transcripts exist, one read informally and one unread; none of the formal corpus has been collected or coded. The single-cohort panel tracks within-person trajectories and is not powered for factor analysis, which Study 2 takes up, and Study 3 carries the rival mechanisms the hearings named as measured covariates (Appendix C). The first pilot administration produced the outcome that cuts against the construct — machine-only routing of the explanation, with the peer never addressed — and it obligates the protocol to treat that pattern as a live rival outcome rather than a variant of dual-audience encoding. I added probes that distinguish the two before the second administration.

## **Conclusion and Theoretical Boundaries**

I formulate the theoretical architecture, boundaries, and constitutive operations of algorithmacy here; empirical validation is Paper 3's task. Because the field investigation remains prospective, the contribution is an analytical model, a set of construct definitions, and a measurement apparatus refined through piloting. Whether the three operations load onto a coherent higher-order factor, hold empirical independence, or separate cleanly from general software literacy and traditional communicative competence are open questions that psychometric testing must resolve (Appendix C), and following Spitzberg and Cupach (1984), a measure pitched at the molecular level licenses no automatic inference about the molar conception of work Sandberg (2000) describes. Validation must also determine whether existing measures of algorithmic sensemaking (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025) capture the variance observed in triadic environments, or whether Sutherland et al.'s (2020) relational gig literacy accounts for coordination on its own. The claim advanced here concerns a structural position within an asymmetric, evaluative triad, not a taxonomic refinement, and its two halves — mutual bindingness *and* joint execution — together exclude advisory decision aids, dyadic software tools, and unmediated peer collaboration.

Algorithmacy isolates the capacity an individual needs inside the triad: to interpret it, to speak through it, and to track it as it moves. Naming that capacity supplies the micro-foundational engine macro-structural models of platform governance presuppose, and it gives researchers a way to ask how individual competence aggregates to stabilize or disrupt an emerging form of organizational control.

## **Appendix A: Comparative Framework of Coordination Forms**

This analytical foundation comes from the first essay of the dissertation program, which evaluated five archetypal coordination forms against a standardized analytical frame to assess the completeness of extant organizational scholarship. Extending the comparative traditions established by Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980), Paper 1 evaluates each coordination form across six core questions: governing mechanism and participant standing, requisite integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, operational instruments, failure modes, and empirical markers. A coordination mechanism is the primary administrative operation securing alignment, paired with the formal standing of the actor that operation reaches. The integrative deliverables map to the three conditions synthesized by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009): predictability, common understanding, and accountability.

The matrix translates classical theory into a contemporary coordination vocabulary. Hayek (1945), for example, demonstrates that the price mechanism bears the primary coordinative burden because it functions as a condensed public signal — Hayek's own figure is a system of telecommunications — that no participant needs to reconstruct privately. In modern coordination terms, that public signal delivers predictability and common understanding at once, while market accountability sits in contractual enforcement and reputational sanction. Table A1 assesses the structural completeness of each theoretical tradition rather than the empirical prevalence of the form.

| **Analytical Dimension** | **Hierarchy** | **Market** | **Network** | **Classical Co-optation** | **Coordinative Co-optation** |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **How it operates: Mechanism & standing** | Authority; participant operates within an institutional zone of acceptance (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). | Price; transacting party evaluates a posted term backed by an unconstrained right of refusal (Hayek, 1945). | Ongoing relational ties; actor invests in an elective, revocable partnership (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). | Institutional absorption; an external opponent is brought inside to assume a formal seat, converting opposition into participation (Selznick, 1949). | Enrollment; an opaque algorithm matches unchosen parties, evaluates actions, and exercises unilateral account termination (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). **Table A1.** *Comparative Analysis of Five Coordination Forms Across Six Foundational Dimensions.*

The four established governance traditions are theoretically complete. Simon (1997) and Weber (1978) together locate the individual in hierarchy through an administrative zone of acceptance; Hayek (1945) defines the market actor through the evaluation of posted prices backed by unconstrained exit rights; Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985) anchor the network participant within elective, revocable partnerships; and Selznick (1949) grounds classical co-optation in conferred positions that transform external opposition into structural participation. Each archetype pairs a governing operation with the formal standing of the actor it coordinates.

The fifth column breaks the pairing. As Stark and Pais (2020) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) observe, digital platforms do not coordinate through command, price discovery, or relational reciprocity; they enroll participants through matching algorithms that pair unchosen parties, monitor performance metrics, and enforce unilateral sanctions. Platform scholarship documents algorithmic matching and surveillance (Möhlmann et al., 2021), administrative direction and disciplinary control (Kellogg et al., 2020), information asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), and non-portable reputational capital (Curchod et al., 2020), and leaves the standing and individual capacity of the enrolled actor untheorized. The participant operates neither as an employee bound by hierarchical authority, a market buyer evaluating transparent price signals, nor a partner in a mutual network alliance. The actor is enrolled, and organizational theory has not yet articulated the competency required to coordinate through the arrangement that enrolls her.

## **Appendix B: Action Research Design, Instrument Architecture, and Empirical Protocol**

**Research Questions and Analytical Mapping**

The empirical program investigates how individuals acquire, develop, and mobilize algorithmacy within opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding coordination regimes. Four research questions organize it, stated in full in the Empirical Strategy section above: RQ1 on *interpreting*, RQ2 on *specifying intent*, RQ3 on *keeping track*, and RQ4 on evaluative attribution.

The four map onto the construct directly. Decoding unexplained outcomes operationalizes *interpreting*; conceptualizing an unseen counterpart captures the dual-audience encoding *specifying intent* demands; detecting unannounced rule shifts captures *keeping track*; and attributing perceived errors tests whether evaluative attribution — provisionally housed within *interpreting* — separates from that operation and, secondarily, from longitudinal tracking. Following Pratt (2009), qualitative rigor depends on alignment between method and the underlying theoretical puzzle rather than adherence to generic templates. I use insider action research (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014), and I treat my dual role as course designer and researcher as an epistemic asset that provides deep institutional access and context-sensitive instrument design.

**The Action Research Program and Institutional Trajectory**

Insider action research proceeds through iterative spirals of four phases — constructing, planning action, taking action, and evaluating action — with each cycle's evaluation informing the next construction (Coghlan & Brannick, 2014). The program spans five institutional configurations across two years:

1.  **Secondary School Cohorts (July–August 2025):** The initiative launched in Trinidad and Tobago across two secondary educational institutions.
2.  **Community Youth Cohorts (Autumn–Winter 2025/2026):** Operating as GauntleTT, the program expanded across the constituency of La Brea in partnership with the Member of Parliament, the Ministry of Digital Transformation, local non-governmental organizations, and the community policing division administering the Hearts and Minds youth intervention initiative.
3.  **Public Technical Sprint Cohorts (April–May 2026):** An open-enrollment public iteration operated in Boston, Massachusetts, testing web-based development pipelines.
4.  **Preliminary Business School Cohorts (July–August 2026):** The curriculum transitioned to Hult International Business School, piloting credit-bearing assessment workflows.
5.  **Formal Academic Course (Spring 2027):** A formal, sixteen-week, credit-bearing engineering course at Hult serves as the primary data-collection site for Paper 3.

Within the mature setting, participants engineer production-grade software and submit deliverables as peer-reviewed pull requests. Across each institutional transition, cyclical evaluations drove substantive modifications: site variations altered peer-evaluation networks, gate refinements varied information visibility, and demographic shifts isolated behavioral variance from confounding human-capital baselines.

**The Trinidad Field Setting as a Strategic Research Site**

The early Caribbean cohorts functioned as a strategic research site in Merton's (1987) sense, providing a setting where the focal phenomenon manifested with unusual analytical tractability. As Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) argue, single-case selections are justified when a setting offers revelatory access to an unexamined process. Conventional platform-work research typically examines labor forces characterized by extensive platform tenure and pre-existing human capital differences (Rahman, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025), where participant sensemaking is heavily confounded by prior domain experience.

Platform labor across the Anglophone Caribbean, by contrast, remains concentrated in traditional logistics, ride-hailing, and hospitality (ILO, 2025), with empirical scholarship on algorithmic coordination in the region remaining sparse (Chigbu, 2026). Studying non-traditional research contexts prevents the misapplication of mature institutional assumptions and allows emergent behavioral phenomena to surface in unobstructed form (Bamberger & Pratt, 2010; Bothello et al., 2019). Because participants in the Trinidad cohorts encountered artificial intelligence coordination interfaces simultaneously and without prior platform conditioning, divergences in how individuals decoded identical gate outcomes could not be attributed to tenure or pre-sorted skill tiers.

Across the cohort iterations, automated evaluation gates withheld information across three operational layers:

  - **Boston Iteration:** Large language models screened initial submissions prior to peer voting, displaying only an aggregated final score to the participant.
  - **Hult International Business School Iteration:** Qualitative peer reviews and upvotes remained publicly inspectable while the underlying aggregate weighting remained concealed.
  - **Trinidad Iteration:** Automated operational logs were not retained, which forced retrospective historical protocols to reconstruct gate mechanics.

Three sites I designed cannot demonstrate that opacity is intrinsic to algorithmic coordination. What the variation supports is narrower and sufficient: the construct's operations were elicited under three different concealment regimes rather than one, so the behaviors recorded do not depend on any single interface.

Following Coghlan and Brannick (2014), the core action research cycle — the ongoing pedagogical initiative — remains distinct from the thesis research cycle, the formal study presented in Paper 3. The preliminary cohorts provided the ground for developing the construct, calibrating instruments, and refining the protocol; they are the developmental foundation rather than the formal empirical corpus.

**Research Site Architecture and Researcher Positionality**

The formal investigation takes place within a sixteen-week credit-bearing course at Hult International Business School in spring 2027. Students submit technical code to an automated gate that returns determinations without procedural rationales. The gate embodies the scope conditions of coordinative co-optation: the automated system is the opaque intermediary, its unexplained verdict is the opacity participants face, the algorithm assigns reviewers to submissions, the verdict binds submitter and reviewer alike, and the weights and thresholds are retuned as the term proceeds.

The setting is a conservative "hard case" for opacity. Because student participants construct and inspect the codebase itself, the opacity does not come from hidden source code; it comes from concealed aggregation weights and shifting decision thresholds governing peer evaluation. The formal protocol catalogs the institutional escalation rights attached to a contested verdict, the one gate parameter not yet documented.

I manage the dual role through structural safeguards rather than methodological disavowal (Riordan, 1995, as cited in Coghlan & Brannick, 2014; Anteby, 2013). I restrict my instructional role to macro-level course design, and the gate operates autonomously, without instructor voting or manual grading overrides. To remove power asymmetries and instructional demand characteristics (Ferguson et al., 2004; Mercer, 2007), the intake client anonymizes participants before any record reaches me, so I cannot identify who took part in the qualitative protocol. Interviews occur only after evaluative project cycles have closed. I log every protocol version, coding memo, and dated analytic decision in an audit trail (Pratt et al., 2020; Tracy, 2010).

**The In-Editor Interview Protocol and Abductive Coding Framework**

The qualitative arm employs an in-editor critical incident interview protocol administered inside the participant's integrated development environment. An embedded conversational agent guides respondents through four diagnostic modules:

1.  **Decoding Gate Criteria:** Reconstructing the latent rules and peer evaluations behind an unexplained outcome.
2.  **Transmitting Communicative Intent:** Encoding code comments and pull-request documentation to satisfy both computational test suites and human peer reviewers.
3.  **Identifying Unannounced Change:** Detecting shifts in evaluative benchmarks over time without formal platform announcements.
4.  **Attributing Evaluative Errors:** Allocating causal responsibility for perceived evaluative failures across the intermediary, peer reviewers, or their interaction.

Each module applies the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954), prompting participants to detail a complete behavioral episode — operationalized here as the baseline objective, the system determination, the tactical adaptation, and the resolution. If a respondent offers a high-level generalization, the agent prompts for a specific incident; if an initial response is negative, the agent reframes from an alternative angle before closing the module. The protocol omits theoretical terminology to elicit native categories. The agent executes real-time, client-side redaction of personal names, institutions, and geographic identifiers before the participant reviews, edits, and validates the final transcript for transmission.

Analysis follows an abductive logic of inquiry (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012) through three iterative moves:

  - **Revisiting:** Returning repeatedly to inscribed transcripts across multiple passes to examine how emergent categories reframe earlier observations.
  - **Defamiliarization:** Treating automated transcription as a technique of analytical estrangement, subjecting self-evident participant explanations to theoretical scrutiny.
  - **Alternative Casing:** Evaluating raw transcripts against competing frameworks (individual skill acquisition, relational gig literacies, dyadic sensemaking) to establish whether the data demand the triadic construct.

The three operations function as sensitizing concepts (Blumer, 1954; Bowen, 2006). Initial coding adheres to first-order, participant-centric terminology (Gioia et al., 2013) before second-order categories are applied. A disconfirming negative case is specified in advance: a participant who consistently resolves gate breakdowns through individual technical mastery alone, without conceptualizing or communicating with the human peer counterpart, is evidence falsifying the necessity of the triadic construct.

**Longitudinal Survey Panel Architecture**

The quantitative arm comprises a three-wave longitudinal survey panel administered at weeks 1, 4, and 16 of the semester. To reconcile within-person tracking with researcher blindness, participants generate a self-derived confidential token at baseline, derived from invariant personal elements, and re-enter it across successive waves (Yurek et al., 2008). The protocol keeps the panel researcher-blind by procedure: no name, token key, or intake record reaches me. With a cohort of roughly fifty and incident narratives rich in particulars, I cannot rule out incidental recognition, and the coding protocol strips identifying details for that reason.

Given a cohort of approximately fifty students, the panel is not powered to perform exploratory or confirmatory factor analyses. The survey serves two purposes instead: tracking within-person developmental trajectories across the semester, and administering Zhou et al.'s (2025) scale at wave 2 as a descriptive convergence check with *interpreting* and *specifying intent*. The same wave carries a short measure of machine-heuristic endorsement, named above as a discriminant threat, which Study 2 tests at scale. I reserve large-sample discriminant testing for Study 2. Bentley University's Institutional Review Board determined the study exempt (Protocol 260511078).

**Preliminary Instrument Piloting Results**

Two pilot administrations of the in-editor protocol exist. I read the first informally to check that the modules function; the second transcript is unread. From the first, three instrument findings follow:

1.  **Elicitation of Concrete Critical Incidents:** The protocol elicited a complete critical incident detailing an adverse gate determination, the participant's retrospective sensemaking, and the perceived absence of contestability channels.
2.  **Identification of Secondary Intermediary Routing:** The participant satisfied the intent-specification module by routing her explanation through an external language model to optimize automated parsability, and never engaged the peer counterpart. Table B1 lists that pattern as the disconfirming criterion for *specifying intent*, so I treat it as a live rival outcome rather than a variant of dual-audience encoding, and I added protocol probes that distinguish direct peer communication from multi-agent intermediary routing.
3.  **Automated Incident Forcing:** I refined the protocol to force incident anchors in place of generalized summaries, and added automated reframing prompts in the tracking and error-attribution modules.

## **Appendix C: Programmatic Research Agenda**

Four sequential studies translate algorithmacy from a conceptual framework into an empirically validated construct, with explicit methodological thresholds and falsification criteria at each analytical level.

**Study 1: Multi-Method Field Validation and Dimensional Boundary Resolution**

The first study executes the multi-method investigation within the primary field setting, coupling the in-editor critical incident protocol with the three-wave panel. Qualitative analysis proceeds not to a grounded-theory saturation metric but to an abductive sufficiency criterion — the threshold at which incremental incidents neither present casings the three operations cannot accommodate *nor* fit a rival framing (skill acquisition, gig literacy, dyadic sensemaking) better than the triadic construct. Fit alone does not stop the analysis; the alternative-casing pass must also come back empty.

This corpus resolves the candidate fourth operation, *evaluative attribution*. Rahman's (2021) "who-influences" dimension will be formalized as an autonomous fourth operation if participant accounts systematically reveal actors who decode observable feedback successfully yet cannot discern whether the platform intermediary or peer evaluators determined the outcome. If participants instead resolve causal responsibility as part of *interpreting* — the operation the construct provisionally houses it within — the tripartite model stands as specified.

**Study 2: Scale Development, Large-Sample Psychometric Testing, and Discriminant Validation**

Study 2 begins by building the instrument the confirmatory model requires. I convert the Study 1 incident corpus into a candidate item pool, one item per recurrent first-order code, following Zhou et al.'s (2025) generation protocol; submit the pool to expert content validation; and fit an exploratory factor analysis on the first pooled cohorts before estimating any confirmatory model. One behavioral alternative to self-report belongs in the item-generation stage. Mieczkowski et al. (2021) run a referential communication task — the paradigm in which two partners must converge on a shared description of an ambiguous figure — through a system offering smart replies, and the task registers directly whether meaning arrived rather than whether a respondent believes it did. The paradigm assumes co-presence and a responding partner, so it measures anything in the triad only after modification that withholds the counterpart's replies.

Pooling then carries a condition the site itself imposes. Because the gate's evaluation rule genuinely retunes over time, pooling cohorts requires either freezing the gate configuration across all pooled cohorts or modeling cohort as a grouping variable and testing configural and metric invariance before pooling. Failure of metric invariance halts pooling, and the cohorts are modeled as separate groups.

With those conditions met, the study establishes large-sample validation by pooling administrations across successive cohorts to a sample of 200 to 250 respondents, matching the methodological baseline Zhou et al. (2025) set. That sample powers a confirmatory factor analysis and evaluates pre-registered latent correlation hypotheses between algorithmacy dimensions and established algorithmic competency subscales:

  - *Interpreting* paired with *Understanding*
  - *Specifying Intent* paired with *Leveraging*
  - An unassigned null baseline paired with *Embracing*

Latent inter-construct correlations in the .30 to .60 range demonstrate convergent yet distinct validity. I evaluate discriminant validity with the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT; Henseler et al., 2015). An HTMT value exceeding the conservative .85 threshold between any proposed operation and a neighboring subscale falsifies the claim of construct distinctiveness on that dimension.

**Study 3: Distributional Inequality and System Stabilization**

The third study tests Proposition 2 and the workforce-level aggregation mechanism across a population of formally equivalent participants. Sample and setting remain to be specified; the constructs and controls are fixed. Measuring baseline algorithmacy alongside technical human capital, task complexity, platform dependence, and prior evaluation shocks — and alongside the three rival mechanisms the communication hearings named: accumulated channel experience and the cognitive slack it frees (Walther, 2007), mindless social scripting (Nass & Moon, 2000), and machine-heuristic endorsement (Sundar & Kim, 2019) — the study examines whether individual variance in algorithmacy accounts for performance differentials among structurally undifferentiated actors once the labor-process and communication-theoretic rivals are controlled. Naming those covariates is what makes the new hearings' inheritance claims cash out as design commitments rather than concessions.

The design also tracks low-scoring cohorts longitudinally to evaluate how the arrangement absorbs coordination friction. Measuring rates of voluntary attrition, formal dispute volume, and the psychological internalization of coordination failures quantifies the operational and human costs an organization incurs when this competency is scarce.

**Study 4: Cross-Sector Generalizability and Transparency Boundary Conditions**

The fourth study evaluates external generalizability by deploying the instruments across naturalistic, non-builder platform environments where the investigative team holds no pedagogical or administrative role. Target settings include on-demand transportation fleets, distributed micro-task queues, and digital freelance marketplaces where algorithmic matching operates without worker agency over system design.

Observing the operational separation of the three dimensions within commercial environments would confirm that algorithmacy is a generalized coordination capacity rather than an artifact of software engineering settings. Comparative field sites exhibiting natural variation in algorithmic disclosure also provide a direct test of Proposition 3, evaluating whether transparency mandates lower the baseline algorithmacy required to sustain coordination.
