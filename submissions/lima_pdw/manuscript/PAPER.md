## Abstract

Algorithmacy, the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls, is the competency through which a person integrates herself into a newer form of coordination, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. In this paper we begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, tracing its finding that this newer form is less developed than hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation. We present a review of extant constructs, arranged according to what each construct makes of the algorithm. We then propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, sufficient because it includes the counterpart, not only the system. We end by reporting early qualitative research using a harness interview, including several responses obtained to date, and by locating the continuing inquiry in a specialized course at Hult University, where we refine the instrument toward subsequent quantitative assessment.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication competency · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# Introduction

Coordination — the process by which people collectively perform interdependent work — remains a central concern of organization theory. We now have a substantial body of knowledge about the forms through which that work is coordinated. That knowledge describes three familiar forms: hierarchy, market, and network, which coordinate by authority, by price, and by ongoing relationship. However, this picture does not yet include the newer form in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm, leaving us without an account of how a person integrates herself into that form. We develop that account here.

This paper has three main purposes. We begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, including its comparison of those three forms on a common set of questions (how each form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher would recognize it). Paper 1 also examined classical co-optation, in which an organization facing opposition brings the opponent inside and confers a seat. Moreover, it examined coordinative co-optation, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm that matches them, ranks them, and can close their accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). After comparing those five forms, Paper 1 found coordinative co-optation less developed than the other four, and we proceed from there. Finally, we review extant constructs that do not satisfy that account, propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, and describe our early empirical work with participants.

As we examined the literature, we identified several candidates. For example, we found that some of these constructs treat the algorithm as a channel between two people (Spitzberg, 2006). In some cases the algorithm is treated as a communicative partner, or as an agent that modifies or transmits the speaker's message (Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). And while knowledge of the system and the capacity to navigate it are indeed indispensable in this kind of work (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), they are conceptually separate from coordination with a human counterpart. In a few instances we also drew on research that treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021), which approaches the problem and nevertheless stops at the system. Each construct places a person on one side of a relation and a system on the other, and leaves the human counterpart — a client, an evaluator, or another participant — outside the competence it specifies.

We then define algorithmacy on the same pattern as literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Literacy is the ability to read and write, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through text. Numeracy is the ability to use and interpret quantitative information, so that a person can participate in situations that run through number. Oracy is the ability to speak and listen, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through speech. Algorithmacy is the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it, so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls. By opaque we mean that the person cannot observe the rule; by adaptive, that the rule may change; by intermediary, the algorithm that mediates between the person and her counterpart. Literacy, numeracy, and oracy are competences for a medium. Algorithmacy is a competence for a coordination. That is why we argue that it fulfills the requirements Paper 1 defined. After presenting that definition, we turn to our work with participants who are acquiring algorithmacy. This empirical work remains at an early stage. We offer a qualitative, narrative account of what we have observed thus far, based on a harness interview in which participants complete a task, receive an outcome that is not fully explained, and are asked how they interpreted that outcome and what they did next. We have obtained several responses and are refining the interview iteratively, in order to improve its clarity and its usefulness to participants, until the instrument can be administered at greater scale and can support quantitative assessment. The continuing site for this inquiry is a specialized course at Hult University.

# Paper 1 of This Dissertation

Paper 1 asked the same six questions of each coordination form it examined. How does the form operate? What must it deliver? What conditions favor it? What instruments does it employ? How does it fail? How would a researcher recognize it? Those questions were taken from scholarship that already places two or more forms on named dimensions (Powell, 1990; Williamson, 1991; Bradach & Eccles, 1989; Ouchi, 1980; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009), and they were fixed before the co-optation sources were coded. The grades report how completely a literature has answered each question. They do not report whether the arrangement a literature names is real or common.

On those questions the three familiar forms are well developed. Hierarchy operates by authority. It delivers predictability by rule, common understanding by shared premises, and accountability by a chain of command (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). It is favored where tasks are interdependent and the parties already hold membership, and its instruments are offices, decision premises, and the employment relation. It fails when rules cannot cover the work, or when the zone of acceptance is withdrawn. A researcher recognizes it by a named superior, a published rule, and a party who can be asked.

The market operates by price. It delivers predictability and common understanding cheaply, because both arrive with a public statistic that no participant has to reconstruct privately (Hayek, 1945). Accountability arrives through contract and reputation. The form is favored where goods are comparable and parties can walk away, and it fails when the price is no longer a sufficient statistic. A researcher recognizes it by a posted term and a party free to refuse the trade.

The network operates by ongoing relationship. It delivers accountability by reputation and sanction, predictability by continuing relation, and common understanding by shared norms (Powell, 1990; Granovetter, 1985). It is favored where trust can be built and where neither price nor command will carry the work. Its instruments are reciprocity, reputation, and membership. It fails when the relation is broken or opportunism outruns sanction. A researcher recognizes it by partners who chose each other and who can withdraw.

Paper 1 also examined classical co-optation. Selznick (1949) described an organization that, facing opposition, brings the opponent inside and confers a seat. The opponent is absorbed so that opposition is converted into participation. The form operates by that absorption. What it must deliver is a graded standing: the co-opted party can be heard, even if the hearing is managed. The instruments are boards, committees, and other seats. The form fails when the seat is empty of influence, or when absorption does not quiet the opposition. A researcher recognizes it by a party who was outside, is now inside, and holds a position the organization can name. Later work in resource dependence and comparative politics developed the same operation. Paper 1 found that literature as developed as the three familiar forms.

Coordinative co-optation is a newer claim. Stark and Pais (2020) argue that platforms do not contract, command, or collaborate as their primary operation. They enroll participants through an algorithm. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) develop the claim as an organizational form with its own object of management and its own direction of accountability. The algorithm matches parties who do not choose each other, ranks them, prices their work, and can close their accounts. The participant is not an employee, not a buyer free to take the same terms elsewhere, and not a partner who entered a relation she can revise. She is enrolled.

Paper 1 found this last form less developed than the other four. The platform sources describe the arrangement in detail — matching and control (Möhlmann, Zalmanson, Henfridsson, & Gregory, 2021), direction and evaluation (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020), information asymmetries (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), ratings that cannot be carried to another site (Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, & Neysen, 2020) — and they do not yet answer the six questions in the form's own terms with the completeness the older literatures reached. One cell in particular remains open. The older forms all describe, even when they do not name, how a person integrates herself into the coordination: the employee accepts a zone of authority, the trader reads a price, the partner invests in a relation, the co-opted opponent takes a seat. Coordinative co-optation describes what the algorithm does to the parties. It does not describe what the person must be able to do in order to take part.

That is the requirement we take up. Paper 1 treats forms. This paper treats the person who has to find her place in one of them.

# Extant Constructs

Several constructs already describe what people do with algorithms. We arrange them by what each makes of the algorithm, because that choice sets the boundary of the competence each construct can specify.

Spitzberg (2006) treats the algorithm, or any computer-mediated channel, as a medium between two people. Computer-mediated communication competence is the ability to use that medium so that a message reaches a counterpart. The two humans remain the coordinators. The channel may constrain what can be said, and it does not interpret the parties, rank them, or commit a decision neither of them controls. A person who is competent in that sense has learned a medium. She has not learned to take part in a coordination that runs through a system which acts on both sides.

Guzman and Lewis (2020) treat the system as a communicative partner. Human–machine communication studies the dyad of person and machine. The counterpart, if one exists beyond the machine, is outside the relation the construct names. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) treat the system as an agent that modifies or transmits the speaker's message. AI-mediated communication keeps the human counterpart in view as an addressee, and it still locates competence in what the speaker, or her agent, sends. The system works for one party. It does not sit between two parties as an intermediary that reads both and decides.

Long and Magerko (2020) treat the system as an object of knowledge. AI literacy is a set of competencies for understanding what artificial intelligence is, what it can do, and how to work with it. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) treat the system as an object of navigation. Their algorithmic-competency scale, developed with on-demand platform workers, measures understanding of the algorithms that assign and evaluate work, and the ability to adapt to and move through those algorithms. Both constructs are indispensable in this kind of work. Neither specifies a competence for coordination with a human counterpart. Knowledge of the system can be complete, and the other party still does not appear inside the measure. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's twelve items name the platform, the assignment, the evaluation, and the worker's adaptation. They do not name a client, an evaluator, or another participant as a party the worker must reach.

Rahman (2021) treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation. Workers under opaque evaluation construct folk theories of the system, anticipate its judgments, and adjust their conduct. That work is close to the first operation in our definition: interpreting an opaque intermediary. It remains work on the system. A worker who has a serviceable theory of the evaluation has not yet been asked whether she coordinated with anyone through it. The counterpart appears as a source of ratings, not as a party the competence includes.

Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) come nearer still. Gig literacies name the competencies freelancers build in order to survive on platforms, and they read enrollment without standing as a burden the person has to carry. The construct is collected from what workers report doing. It lists practices. It does not specify a competence whose object is coordination with a counterpart through an intermediary that reads both sides.

Each of these constructs is itself a boundary. The channel construct draws a line around a medium. The partner construct draws a line around a machine. The agent construct draws a line around a speaker and her delegate. The knowledge and navigation constructs draw a line around a system. The interpretation construct draws a line around a theory of that system. In every case a person stands on one side and a system on the other. The human counterpart — a client, an evaluator, a rider, a reviewer, another participant — is outside the competence specified. Adding items to an AI-literacy inventory, or to Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's scale, will not move that line. The missing piece is a position, not a variable.

# Algorithmacy

The definition in the introduction is built on the same pattern as literacy, numeracy, and oracy, and it is sufficient because every clause is required for participation in this form.

To interpret an opaque intermediary is to reconstruct, from outcomes, what the system and the unseen counterpart are doing. The person cannot observe the rule, so she reads backward from a rating, a rejection, a match, or a silence. That reconstruction is not knowledge of how the software works in general. It is a working account of what this gate, this week, did with her and with the other party.

To specify intent through that intermediary is the reverse direction. The person has to make what she meant survive a channel that will accept only certain signals — a profile, a submission, a message in a required format, a rating of her own. Something is lost in the compression. Part of the competency is knowing what can be lost and still reach the counterpart.

To keep track of an adaptive intermediary is to notice that the rule has moved when nobody announced it. What passed last month fails this month. The person who detects the shift early is coordinating under a different model than the person still acting on last month's outcomes.

Those three operations include the counterpart. Interpretation is of a situation that already contains another party. Specification is aimed at being received by that party, through the system. Tracking is of a rule that binds both. A competence that stopped at the system would need only the first operation, and only in the form Rahman describes: a theory of the algorithm. Algorithmacy requires all three, and it requires them so that a person can participate in coordination, not so that she can operate a tool.

The closing clause of the definition states the coordination this competency is for. The system reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls. That is what distinguishes coordinative co-optation from a channel, a partner, or an agent. A channel does not decide. A partner can be answered. An agent works for one side. An intermediary of this kind matches, ranks, and can close an account, and it does so on objectives neither participant set.

This is a competency, not a skill and not a capability. A skill can be taught as a rule, and the rule here is undisclosed. What a course can teach is the interface. What it cannot teach is what this gate rewards this month. A capability is held by the party that owns the routine (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). The platform holds the ratings, the graph, and the rule. The coordinated party owns none of those. Calling the competency a capability would attribute to her a possession the form is defined by denying. Sandberg (2000) treats competence as a way of conceiving and enacting work in a role. Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) treat communication competence as what a party is able to do in a coordination, as against what she did on any one occasion. Algorithmacy belongs with those accounts, and with literacy, numeracy, and oracy: a competence for taking part, learned by taking part, indexed to a form rather than to a particular tool.

Two properties follow, and both are claims about a distribution rather than about any single person. The competency is acquired by participation, not by instruction, because there is no published rule to instruct. And it is uneven among people the arrangement treats as equivalent. If it were uniform, it would explain none of the differences in outcome that appear among participants facing the same system. If explicit teaching produced the same distribution as use, the first property would be wrong.

# Early Empirical Work

We are studying people who are acquiring this competency, not people who already hold a finished one. The construct was formed while teaching. GauntleTT, an intensive AI engineering cohort in Trinidad and Tobago, is where the questions first became sharp: what does the gate want, why did it change, who can I ask. Formation bears on whether the language of the instrument says what participants mean. It is not evidence of the construct's structure, and it is not the study site.

The continuing site is a specialized course at Hult University. Participants build production software and submit work through a gate. The gate returns an outcome that is not fully explained. Advancement depends on peers who read work the submitter cannot watch them read. The platforms under construction change because other participants change them, so the rule a person faces is not the rule she faced the week before. Those three features — an unexplained outcome, an unseen evaluator, and a moving rule — are the conditions the definition names as opaque, intermediary, and adaptive.

The principal instrument is a harness interview. A participant completes a task, receives an outcome that is not fully explained, and is asked how she interpreted that outcome and what she did next. The interview then follows the three operations: what she reconstructed about the gate and about the other party, how she packed her next attempt so that it might be received, and whether she noticed a change she had not been told about. A further question asks what she did when an outcome seemed wrong, whom she approached, and what happened. That last question is the one a construct that includes the counterpart has to be able to hear. A person who can obtain an answer from the gate by individual skill alone, with no other party involved, would show a competence that stops at the system. A person who finds no forum, and treats the outcome as simply the way the arrangement is, would show the counterpart still out of reach.

We have obtained several responses. The work remains qualitative and narrative. We are not reporting a completed analysis, and we are not claiming that the early material confirms the construct. We are reporting what the first interviews have made visible enough to refine the instrument.

One participant, in the Hult course, described an outcome she did not contest. She named the reason: no channel was legible as the place for a contest, so she concluded that this was the way the arrangement worked. Forbearance with no forum is what a person-system competence would treat as a finished adaptation, and what a competence that includes the counterpart has to treat as a missing position. The same participant read the gate by pasting its feedback into another system and asking for an explanation. The interpretive work was delegated to a tool. That is useful conduct, and it is not algorithmacy. It is a way of operating on the system that leaves the counterpart where she was.

The transcripts also ran thin. Answers and questions sat at roughly the same length, which told us the interview was not yet drawing out the reconstruction we need. We deepened the protocols in response: more follow-up on what was tried after an unexplained outcome, more insistence on who else was in the situation, and a second pass when a closing answer arrives too quickly. We will keep refining the interview for clarity and for usefulness to participants, until it can be administered at greater scale and can support quantitative assessment. A companion three-wave instrument is already built and approved (Bentley IRB, exempt determination 11 May 2026, protocol 260511078). It is not yet fielded. The qualitative protocol is the live one.

The investigator teaches the course he is studying. Advancement is decided by the peer gate, in which he casts no vote. Consent records and withdrawals are held outside the course. No participant is interviewed about a project whose outcome is still open.

# What This Paper Cannot Say

Algorithmacy is proposed, not demonstrated. Whether the three operations cohere as one competency, whether they separate from one another, and whether they separate from general knowledge of software are open questions. The early interviews can refine the instrument. They cannot close those questions.

The neighbouring constructs remain live. A later study might show that Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's scale, or Rahman's account of reactivity, already captures the variance we attribute to a missing position. We have argued that those constructs stop at the system. We have not shown that a measure built on our definition outperforms them.

Paper 1's grades are a frame, not a finding of this paper. Should coordinative co-optation turn out to be more fully developed than Paper 1 allowed, the requirement we took up would still stand: an account of how a person integrates herself into the form. Should the form turn out not to be distinct, the same requirement would still stand for whatever arrangement enrolls people through an algorithm that reads both parties and commits decisions they do not control.

The Hult course is a hard case for opacity, because the participants build the systems they also submit to. We have said why the gate, the private vote, and the moving codebase still withhold the rule. That argument is about site fit. It is not a claim that findings from this course travel to ride-hail, piecework, or freelance platforms without a further study.

We have not measured a population. We have not defeated rival explanations that run on prior skill, task mix, or hours. We have not shown that a rise in this competency is a good outcome. If scores rose while the form stayed as it is, what would have risen is the amount of unpaid coordination work the participants absorb. Whether that is welcome depends on facts about the arrangement that this construct does not measure.

A later paper can take the instrument to scale. This one offers a definition that includes the counterpart, a review of constructs that do not, and an early look at people who are learning to take part.
