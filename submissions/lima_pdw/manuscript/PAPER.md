## Abstract

Algorithmacy, the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls, is the competency through which a person integrates herself into a newer form of coordination, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm. In this paper we begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, tracing its finding that this newer form is less developed than hierarchy, market, network, or classical co-optation. We present a review of extant constructs, arranged according to what each construct makes of the algorithm. We then propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, sufficient because it includes the counterpart, not only the system. We end by reporting early qualitative research using a harness interview, including several responses obtained to date, and by locating the continuing inquiry in a specialized course at Hult University, where we refine the instrument toward subsequent quantitative assessment.

**Keywords:** coordination forms · algorithmic management · communication competency · platform work · co-optation · organizational form

# Introduction

Coordination — the process by which people collectively perform interdependent work — remains a central concern of organization theory. We now have a substantial body of knowledge about the forms through which that work is coordinated. That knowledge describes three familiar forms: hierarchy, market, and network, which coordinate by authority, by price, and by ongoing relationship. However, this picture does not yet include the newer form in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm, leaving us without an account of how a person integrates herself into that form. We develop that account here.

This paper has three main purposes. We begin by reviewing Paper 1 of this dissertation, including its comparison of those three forms on a common set of questions (how each form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher would recognize it). Paper 1 also examined classical co-optation, in which an organization facing opposition brings the opponent inside and confers a seat. Moreover, it examined coordinative co-optation, in which a platform enrolls participants through an algorithm that matches them, ranks them, and can close their accounts (Stark & Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). After comparing those five forms, Paper 1 found coordinative co-optation less developed than the other four, and we proceed from there. Finally, we review extant constructs that do not satisfy that account, propose algorithmacy as a new, satisficing construct, and describe our early empirical work with participants.

As we examined the literature, we identified several candidates. For example, we found that some of these constructs treat the algorithm as a channel between two people (Spitzberg, 2006). In some cases the algorithm is treated as a communicative partner, or as an agent that modifies or transmits the speaker's message (Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). And while knowledge of the system and the capacity to navigate it are indeed indispensable in this kind of work (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), they are conceptually separate from coordination with a human counterpart. In a few instances we also drew on research that treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation (Rahman, 2021), which approaches the problem and nevertheless stops at the system. Each construct places a person on one side of a relation and a system on the other, and leaves the human counterpart — a client, an evaluator, or another participant — outside the competence it specifies.

We then define algorithmacy on the same pattern as literacy, numeracy, and oracy. Literacy is the ability to read and write, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through text. Numeracy is the ability to use and interpret quantitative information, so that a person can participate in situations that run through number. Oracy is the ability to speak and listen, so that a person can participate in communication that runs through speech. Algorithmacy is the ability to interpret an opaque, adaptive intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it, so that a person can participate in coordination that runs through a system which reads both parties and commits decisions neither of them controls. By opaque we mean that the person cannot observe the rule; by adaptive, that the rule may change; by intermediary, the algorithm that mediates between the person and her counterpart. Literacy, numeracy, and oracy are competences for a medium. Algorithmacy is a competence for a coordination. That is why we argue that it fulfills the requirements Paper 1 defined. After presenting that definition, we turn to our work with participants who are acquiring algorithmacy. This empirical work remains at an early stage. We offer a qualitative, narrative account of what we have observed thus far, based on a harness interview in which participants complete a task, receive an outcome that is not fully explained, and are asked how they interpreted that outcome and what they did next. We have obtained several responses and are refining the interview iteratively, in order to improve its clarity and its usefulness to participants, until the instrument can be administered at greater scale and can support quantitative assessment. The continuing site for this inquiry is a specialized course at Hult University.

# Paper 1 of This Dissertation

Paper 1 of this dissertation asked whether the literatures on coordination already say how a person takes her place in each form. Powell (1990), Williamson (1991), Bradach and Eccles (1989), and Ouchi (1980) had already set two or more forms on named dimensions. Paper 1 followed that comparative practice with one set of questions: how the form operates, what it must deliver, what conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, and how a researcher would recognize it. Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) supplied the three integrating conditions that answer what a form must deliver — predictability, common understanding, and accountability. Those questions were fixed before any co-optation source was read. What Paper 1 then recorded, as grades, was how fully a literature had answered each question, not whether the arrangement that literature names is real or common.

The oldest of those accounts is hierarchy. Hierarchy operates by authority. Simon (1997) and Weber (1978) argued that this authority delivers predictability by rule, common understanding by shared premises, and accountability by a chain of command. A person takes her place in that form by accepting a zone of acceptance, the range of orders a subordinate will follow. Offices, decision premises, and the employment relation are what make that acceptance visible. The form is favored where tasks are interdependent and the parties already hold membership. The arrangement fails when the rules cannot cover the work, or when the zone is withdrawn. A researcher knows it by a named superior, a published rule, and a party who can be asked.

Where authority is not the instrument, the market operates by price. Hayek (1945) showed that predictability and common understanding travel cheaply in that form, because both arrive with a public statistic that no participant has to reconstruct in private, and that accountability arrives through contract and reputation. A person takes her place by reading the posted term and remaining free to refuse the trade. The form is favored where goods are comparable and parties can walk away. It fails when the price no longer carries the information a party needs.

Where neither price nor command will carry the work, the network operates by ongoing relationship. Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985) argued that accountability then arrives by reputation and sanction, predictability by continuing relation, and common understanding by shared norms. A person takes her place by investing in a partner she chose and can withdraw from. Reciprocity, reputation, and membership are the instruments. The form is favored where trust can be built. It fails when the relation is broken or opportunism outruns sanction.

A fourth literature already names a further way of taking a place. Selznick (1949) described an organization that, facing opposition, brings the opponent inside and confers a seat, so that opposition is converted into participation. Classical co-optation operates by that absorption. What it delivers is a graded standing: the co-opted party can be heard, even if the hearing is managed. Boards, committees, and other seats are the instruments. The form fails when the seat is empty of influence or when absorption does not quiet the opposition. A researcher knows it by a party who was outside, is now inside, and holds a position the organization can name. Later studies of resource dependence and of comparative politics developed the same operation. Paper 1 found that literature as fully answered as the three familiar forms.

A newer claim then appeared. Stark and Pais (2020) argue that platforms do not contract, command, or collaborate as their primary operation, because they enroll participants through an algorithm. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) develop that claim as an organizational form, with an object of management and a direction of accountability of its own. The algorithm matches parties who do not choose each other, ranks them, prices their work, and can close their accounts. The participant is neither an employee who accepted a zone of authority, nor a buyer free to take the same terms elsewhere, nor a partner who entered a relation she can revise. She is enrolled.

Paper 1 found this last form less fully answered than the other four. The platform sources already describe the arrangement at length: Möhlmann, Zalmanson, Henfridsson, and Gregory (2021) on matching and control, Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) on direction and evaluation, Rosenblat and Stark (2016) on information asymmetries, and Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) on ratings that cannot be carried to another site. Those sources still do not answer the six questions in the form’s own terms with the completeness the older literatures reached. One cell remains open. The older forms already say how a person takes her place — by accepting a zone, reading a price, investing in a relation, or taking a seat. Coordinative co-optation says what the algorithm does to the parties. It does not say what the person must be able to do in order to take part.

Paper 1 treated forms. This paper treats the person who has to find her place in one of them.

# Extant Constructs

Once that cell is open, the question is whether anyone has already named what the person must be able to do. A construct, here, is a named account of that ability — a competence, a literacy, or a way of interpreting the system. Several such accounts already describe people working with algorithms. They do not make the same thing of the algorithm. That difference is the line each competence can draw.

Computer-mediated communication competence treats the computer as a channel between two people, and asks whether a message sent through that channel was appropriate and effective for a human partner (Spitzberg, 2006). Human–machine communication treats the system as a communicative partner, a machine built to talk with a person (Guzman & Lewis, 2020). AI-mediated communication treats it as an agent that modifies, augments, or generates a speaker’s message (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). AI literacy treats it as an object of knowledge: what a person must know in order to evaluate, use, and work with AI technologies (Long & Magerko, 2020). Algorithmic competency treats it as an object of navigation: a worker’s understanding of the platform algorithms that assign and evaluate work, and her ability to adapt to those algorithms (Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025). Reactivity under opaque evaluation treats it as an object of interpretation, a score whose criteria are hidden and can shift without notice (Rahman, 2021). Gig literacies treat it as a marketplace to use and to work around, including by taking the relation off the platform (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, & Nelson, 2020). We take them in that order. Each hearing asks what the construct makes of the algorithm, and where the human counterpart — a client, an evaluator, or another participant — then sits.

## Computer-Mediated Communication Competence

The first answers came from communication research, which met the computer as a channel. Spitzberg (2006) treats that computer as a medium between two people. Computer-mediated communication competence is the ability to use that medium so that a message reaches a counterpart. He carries a five-part model of interpersonal competence into mediated interaction: motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and outcomes. Motivation is the ratio of approach to avoidance — still a person’s stance toward interaction with a partner. Knowledge is of the content and the procedures that make that interaction appropriate and effective for the partner. Skills — attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness — are skills toward that partner. Context, whether cultural, chronological, relational, environmental, or functional, is the situation of that interaction. The outcomes that matter are appropriateness and effectiveness for a human partner. Mediation, on this account, changes the parameters of competence rather than its structure. The parties on either end remain the coordinators.

He offered the model as a typology of competence between people, and wrote a measure to test it; the hypothesized structure did not appear as drawn. Nothing in that model gives the medium a stake of its own. The channel constrains the parties; it does not interpret them, rank them, or commit a decision neither of them controls. The competence he specifies ends when the message has reached a person, and it does not begin again when a system ranks both sides and commits a decision.

## Human–Machine Communication

That channel picture could not hold once machines were built to talk with a person. Guzman and Lewis (2020) treat the system as a communicative partner. Their article is an agenda rather than a measure. For more than a century, they write, communication theory formed around how people exchange messages with one another, and technology was relegated to the role of medium. Communicative AI is “designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects” (p. 71). Conversational agents, social robots, and automated-writing software are their examples. Meaning-making occurs “within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine” (p. 73). They leave the channel picture: computer-mediated communication theorizes technology as a mediator of human interaction, and human–machine communication theorizes the machine as a communicator.

The agenda has three parts, and in all three the other pole is the device. The functional, relational, and metaphysical parts all aim at the person–machine pair, including social roles designed into the device — therapist, assistant, “robot journalist” — each aimed at the person rather than at a counterpart. Guzman and Lewis still allow machines to mediate; they refuse to relegate technology to only that role. A counterpart reached through that machine is outside the work the agenda names.

## AI-Mediated Communication

A further move kept a human counterpart in view and put the machine on one party’s side. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) treat the system as an agent that operates on a communicator’s behalf. AI-mediated communication is “interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals.” A sender, a receiver, and an agent stand between them; smart replies, predictive text, and emerging video manipulation are the agent’s worked examples. The article’s parameters are magnitude, medium, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation — the parameters of a delegate.

That agent still sits on a party’s side of the exchange and produces messages that the sender or the receiver could in principle have produced. The counterpart remains an addressee, and any competence the agenda implies sits with the speaker or her delegate. Even a fully autonomous agent, on their definition, still operates on behalf of a communicator. A rating that can close an account does not.

## AI Literacy

A different literature asked what a person must know about the system itself. Long and Magerko (2020) treat that system as an object of knowledge. AI literacy, in their definition, is “a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace.” They organize that literature under five questions: what AI is, what it can do, how it works, how it should be used, and how people perceive it. Seventeen competencies follow, overwhelmingly about knowing the system: recognizing AI, understanding intelligence, and distinguishing general from narrow AI under the first question; strengths, weaknesses, and imagining future AI under the second; representations, decision-making, machine learning, data literacy, and sensors under the third; ethics under the fourth; programmability among them as well. Every competency is a relation between a person and a technology, and none names another person.

Their second clause, communicate and collaborate with AI, puts the machine in that person’s chair — a dyad by their own wording. Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) compressed those competencies into know and understand AI, use and apply AI, evaluate and create AI, and ethical issues, and later instruments inherit those four cells. Adding items stays inside cells already defined without a counterpart. Distinguishing artifacts that use AI, describing how a model is trained, and identifying bias in a dataset leave unasked whether the person on the other side of a match received what was meant.

## Algorithmic Competency

Platform work then named a competency against the apparatus that assigns the work. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) treat the system as an object of navigation. Their construct, algorithmic competency, is a worker’s understanding of the platform algorithms that assign and evaluate work, together with the ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms. They built what they call the first scale of that construct from Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers: twelve items, four dimensions, reliability of .85.

The four dimensions are not a knowledge test: Understanding, Embracing, Leveraging, and Remediating. Understanding is a grasp of how algorithmic management works — still of the apparatus. Embracing is a willingness to trust the system’s efficiency — still of the apparatus. Leveraging is using the system strategically for the worker’s benefit — still of the apparatus. Remediating is repairing or supplementing the system’s deficiencies — still of the apparatus.

The counterpart appears in their items as a rating source (“I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback”), a matching function inside a belief about the platform’s efficiency, or a repair crew in WeChat groups — and in their model as customer-oriented service behaviour, an outcome measured separately. Their site fixes those items; they do not transfer to a review gate that votes in private. Navigating the apparatus is what the scale measures; coordinating with the person the apparatus matched is not. We predict a moderate correlation between their Understanding and interpreting, and between their Leveraging and specifying intent; their Embracing has no counterpart in the definition already given. Adding items will not move a counterpart already coded as an outcome.

## Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation

A still nearer account asked how workers interpret a score they cannot see. Rahman (2021) treats the algorithm as an object of interpretation: the invisible cage hides the criteria and shifts them without notice. He sets that construct against the Weberian iron cage, whose rules are fixed and knowable. The case is the 2015 Success Score on a high-skill freelancing platform; the opacity was deliberate, adopted to prevent gaming and to preserve differentiation.

Five components make the cage invisible: hidden and shifting evaluation criteria, execution at uncertain intervals, unidentifiable magnitude, uncertain impact on reputation, and an unspecified influence on the score, client or platform — that last the counterpart named as a failure of interpretation. Experimental reactivity tests tactics to raise the score; constrained reactivity limits engagement to protect it; which form a worker enacts turns on platform dependence and evaluation setbacks. His cut is engagement; ours is direction and time; the cuts are orthogonal, and dependence is a rival explanation to a competency. A serviceable theory of the score leaves unasked whether anyone was reached through the system.

## Gig Literacies

The nearest account put the counterpart in the sample. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) call their gig literacies “critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing” (p. 457): using the resources the platform provides and working around its imposed structures. Later they call the same thing a platform literacy: know-how that uses the platform while keeping autonomy from its structures (p. 470). They interviewed twenty Upwork freelancers and nineteen clients; clients were used “to provide some verification as to which literacies or skills were apparent” to them (p. 463).

The findings are five literacies (pp. 463–469): building reputation (the Job Success Score, badges, and refunds), self-presentation (profiles and chat), maintaining productivity (routines and third-party tools), mitigating transaction risk (escrow and vetting), and building relationships. Under that fifth literacy, workers try to “build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship” for repeat business (p. 468); P39, unable to recover from an early bad rating, continued work off-platform with clients first met on Upwork. They mark the scope themselves: on Upwork the worker can choose and talk to the client; on Uber the algorithm assigns (p. 470). The construct remains freelancer skills beside the platform, not through a binding intermediary, and cannot be confirmed on a site that lets the client leave.

## What These Boundaries Share

Each of these constructs is itself a boundary: computer-mediated communication competence around a medium (Spitzberg, 2006), human–machine communication around a machine (Guzman & Lewis, 2020), AI-mediated communication around a speaker and her delegate (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020), AI literacy around what a person knows about a technology (Long & Magerko, 2020), algorithmic competency around navigation of an apparatus (Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, & Hou, 2025), reactivity around a theory of an opaque score (Rahman, 2021), and gig literacies around survival on, and escape from, a marketplace platform (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, & Nelson, 2020). In every case a person stands on one side and a system on the other. Adding items will not move that line. The missing piece is a position, not a variable.

# Algorithmacy

The definition that occupies that position already stands in the introduction. Suddaby (2010) asks four things of a new construct: a precise definition, scope conditions, semantic relations to neighbours, and coherence with the argument. Clarity, as he insists, is not validity; this section is conceptual, and scope, neighbours, and coherence are what remain to be set.

The construct applies where an intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and binding on both parties — the conditions already named. It does not apply, or applies only trivially, where the rule is published and static, or where the parties meet and revise terms without it. A posted price with a walk-away right is the market case Paper 1 already described. A published office rule with a named superior is the hierarchy case. Upwork-style freelance markets sit on the edge: the platform matches and scores, but the client can still take the relation off-site. Ride-hail and a review gate that votes in private sit nearer the center. Scope is a claim about arrangements, not about industries.

The neighbours occupy the positions just described. They measure adjacent things; if they already measured the same thing, the semantic relation would collapse. Adding items will not produce the position.

The three operations are required by the closing clause of the definition, not collected from a list of practices. To interpret is to reconstruct, from outcomes, what the system and the unseen counterpart are doing. To specify intent is to make what one meant survive the few signals the system will carry, so the counterpart can receive it. To keep track is to notice that a rule binding both parties has moved when nobody announced it. A competence that stopped at the system would need only the first, and Rahman already describes that first.

This is a competency rather than a skill or a capability. A skill can be taught as a rule, and the rule here is undisclosed. A capability is held by the party that owns the routine (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997), and the platform holds the ratings, the matching, and the rule. Sandberg (2000) showed, with twenty engine optimizers at Volvo, that competence is the worker’s way of conceiving the work, not a list of attributes identified independently of it. Six of his informants optimized separate qualities, ten optimized interacting qualities, and four optimized from the customer’s perspective. The customer in that third conception is an orientation of the job, not a party reached through a binding intermediary. That result stands; the rationalistic definition he wrote the article to refute does not. Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) treat communication competence as what a party is able to do in a coordination, as against what she did on any one occasion. The construct belongs with those accounts, and it is indexed to a form rather than to a particular tool.

The competency is acquired by participation, not by instruction: there is no published rule to instruct, and if explicit teaching produced the same distribution as use, that property would be wrong. It is uneven among people the arrangement treats as equivalent. If it were uniform, it would explain none of the differences in outcome that appear among participants facing the same system.

# Early Empirical Work

Once the construct is named, the question is how people beginning to acquire it work out an unexplained outcome, whether they treat anyone else as in the situation, and what they do when the outcome seems wrong. Pratt (2009) argued that qualitative research has no boilerplate, and that what counts as enough is a function of the question, not of a magic number of interviews. We report early work, and we claim neither saturation nor a completed analysis.

The construct was formed in teaching GauntleTT, an intensive AI engineering cohort in Trinidad and Tobago; that formation is not the study site.

The continuing site is a specialized course at Hult University. Participants submit work to a gate that returns an outcome that is not fully explained. Their advancement depends on peers they cannot watch. The codebase moves because other participants change it. Those three features are the conditions the definition names as opaque, intermediary, and adaptive. The site is a hard case for opacity, because the participants build the systems they also submit to. We argue fit from the gate, the private vote, and the moving rule, not from employment status.

The investigator teaches the course, casts no vote in the peer gate, and keeps consent outside the course; no one is interviewed about a project whose outcome is still open. Pratt, Kaplan, and Whittington (2020) distinguish an audit trail held for a committee from a deposit: the protocol lives in a public repository, and the raw responses do not.

The instrument is a harness interview in which a participant completes a task, receives an outcome that is not fully explained, and is asked how she interpreted that outcome and what she did next. One incident is required for each block of questions. A closing “no” gets one reframe from another angle. The interviewer introduces no theoretical vocabulary. Three protocols are in use: student, operations, and self.

Analysis is abductive (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012): we entered with a derived construct, treat the three operations as sensitizing concepts, keep first-cycle coding in the participant’s words (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), decline the data-structure template (Mees-Buss, Welch, & Piekkari, 2022), and treat the design as bricolage (Pratt, Sonenshein, & Feldman, 2022). The negative case is a participant who obtains an answer from the gate by individual skill alone.

We have obtained several responses. The first inspectable incident is from the student protocol in the Hult course. We organize what is visible so far by the interview’s own blocks, not by the three operations as if they had been found.

On working out what the gate wanted, that participant received an outcome she did not contest, named the reason, and found no channel for a contest: an incident of forbearance with no forum. A person–system competence would treat it as finished adaptation; a competence that includes the counterpart has to treat it as a missing position.

On getting work across, the same participant pasted the gate’s feedback into another system and asked for an explanation. The interpretive work was delegated to a tool. That is useful conduct, not algorithmacy, and it leaves the counterpart where she was.

On noticing unannounced change, and on what happens when an outcome seems wrong, the first file is thin. The thinness is itself a finding about the instrument: answers and questions sat at roughly the same length, because the interview was accepting summaries where it should have been asking for the occasion. We deepened the protocols.

We will keep refining the interview until it can be administered at greater scale. A companion three-wave instrument for that scale is already built and approved. Bentley IRB issued an exempt determination on 11 May 2026, protocol 260511078. That instrument is not yet fielded; the qualitative protocol is the live one.

# What This Paper Cannot Say

Algorithmacy is proposed rather than demonstrated. Whether the three operations cohere as one competency, whether they separate from one another, and whether they separate from general knowledge of software remain open questions. The early interviews can refine the instrument; they cannot close those questions.

The neighbouring constructs remain live. A later study might show that Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou’s scale already captures the variance we attribute to a missing position, and it might show the same of Rahman’s account of reactivity. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson’s fifth literacy is the hardest neighbour on the counterpart. We have argued that it is a literacy beside the platform, not through it. We have not shown that a measure built on our definition outperforms any of them. Sandberg’s result also sets a limit we have not resolved. If competence is a conception of work, a later standardized scale will have to show that it still measures that conception.

Paper 1’s grades are a frame rather than a finding of this paper; the requirement we took up would still stand if coordinative co-optation were more fully answered, or if the form were not distinct.

The Hult course is a hard case for opacity. We have said why the gate, the private vote, and the moving codebase still withhold the rule. That argument is about site fit, not a claim that findings from this course travel to ride-hail, piecework, or freelance platforms without a further study.

We have not measured a population, defeated rival explanations that run on prior skill, task mix, or hours, or shown that a rise in this competency is a good outcome. If scores rose while the form stayed as it is, what would have risen is the amount of unpaid coordination work the participants absorb. Whether that is welcome depends on facts about the arrangement that this construct does not measure.

A later paper can take the instrument to scale. This one offers a definition that includes the counterpart, a review of constructs that do not, and an early look at people who are learning to take part.
