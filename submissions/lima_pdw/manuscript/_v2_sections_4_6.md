# Algorithmacy

Suddaby (2010) asks four things of a construct that claims such a position: a precise definition, scope conditions, semantic relations to its neighbours, and coherence with the larger argument. The definition already stands in the introduction; clarity, as he insists, is not validity. This section sets the other three, then what the construct buys.

## Scope Conditions

The construct applies where an intermediary is opaque, adaptive, and binding on both parties — the conditions already named. It does not apply, or applies only trivially, where the rule is published and static, or where the parties meet and revise terms without it. A posted price with a walk-away right is the market case Paper 1 already described. A published office rule with a named superior is the hierarchy case. Upwork-style freelance markets sit on the edge: the platform matches and scores, but the client can still take the relation off-site. Ride-hail and a peer gate that votes in private sit nearer the center. Scope is a claim about arrangements, not about industries. These are also the terms Paper 1's open cell demands: an account of what a person must be able to do in order to take part, stated so that a researcher could recognize its absence.

## Semantic Relations

The neighbours occupy the positions §3 described; the relations can be stated as predictions. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's Understanding should correlate moderately with interpreting, and their Leveraging with specifying intent; their Embracing has no counterpart in the definition, because an attitude toward the platform's efficiency is not an operation performed through it. Rahman's reactivity is orthogonal on a different axis: he cuts by engagement, this construct cuts by direction and time. Correlations high enough to collapse those distinctions would take the construct with them.

## The Three Operations, and Why Not Four

The three operations are required by the closing clause of the definition, not collected from a list of practices. To interpret is to reconstruct, from outcomes, what the system and the unseen counterpart are doing. To specify intent is to make what one meant survive the few signals the system will carry, so the counterpart can receive it. To keep track is to notice that a rule binding both parties has moved when nobody announced it. A competence that stopped at the system would need only the first, and Rahman already describes that first.

The count faces one live fourth candidate in the paper's own sources: Rahman's fifth cage component, who influences the evaluation — client or platform. It stays inside interpreting, because uncertainty about which party moved the score is a failure to reconstruct what the system and the counterpart are doing. The refusal is falsifiable in the field. Incidents in which a participant has interpreted everything the arrangement shows and still cannot tell which party to interpret would make who-influences an operation of its own, and the instrument §5 describes can collect them.

The literacy family supplies the name and then marks the limit of the analogy. Long and Magerko (2020) run the family argument themselves — literacy for written language, then digital, computational, scientific, and data literacies by extension — and their construct still runs person-to-system, so shelf membership settles nothing. What the family does settle is the count: each older member carries two operations, one receptive and one expressive — reading and writing, speaking and listening, interpreting number and using it — because text and number hold still while a person reads them. Keeping track is what a competence acquires when the medium is also a moving party.

## Competency, Not Skill, Not Capability

This is a competency rather than a skill or a capability. A skill can be taught as a rule, and the rule here is undisclosed. A capability is held by the party that owns the routine (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997), and the platform holds the ratings, the matching, and the rule. Sandberg (2000) showed, with twenty engine optimizers drawn from a department of about fifty at the Volvo Car Corporation, that competence is the worker's way of conceiving the work, not a list of attributes identified independently of it. Six of his informants optimized separate qualities, ten optimized interacting qualities, and four optimized from the customer's perspective. The customer in that third conception is an orientation of the job, not a party reached through a binding intermediary. That result stands; the rationalistic definition he wrote the article to refute does not.

Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) went further in the other direction: for them competence is not a property of a person at all but a quality of a relationship, produced jointly and judged by both parties, and Spitzberg (2006) carries the commitment into mediated settings, where co-interactants raise or lower one another's competence. That standard is the one this arrangement cannot meet. The counterpart never reaches a position from which she could judge, and what the construct names is what a party supplies when the relational test has nobody to administer it. The construct belongs with these accounts, and its index is the form, whatever tool implements it.

## Properties: Acquired by Participation, Unevenly Held

The competency is acquired by participation: there is no published rule to instruct from, and if explicit teaching produced the same distribution as use, the property would be wrong. Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp, and Raupach (2023) built the SNAIL scale to evaluate whether AI-literacy courses teach; its criterion is what a course can install, and algorithmacy predicts that no course could install it. The competency is also uneven among people the arrangement treats as equivalent. If it were uniform, it would explain none of the differences in outcome that appear among participants facing the same system.

## What the Construct Buys

The construct, if it is real, changes what a coordination researcher can ask. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) frame these workplaces as contested terrain: direction, evaluation, and discipline are what the system does to workers. Algorithmacy asks what the person must be able to do, and the two questions have different remedies. A rule the platform is forced to publish changes what there is to interpret; a competency changes who can interpret it.

If participation requires the competency and the arrangement cannot teach it, the form's viability depends on how the competency is distributed, and a form full of low-algorithmacy participants selects for something — churn, exit, or coordination failures absorbed as personal ones — a question Paper 1's comparative frame can now receive.

The construct also satisfices, in Simon's (1997) sense of the word the abstract uses: it names an aspiration level and stops when the level is met. The level is Paper 1's open cell, and the construct clears it because it is the first competence that includes the counterpart. A fuller account of the person in this form would add what she believes, what she is owed, and what she can refuse.

The online dispute resolution literature that named the fourth party also wrote its duties: Wing, Martinez, Katsh, and Rule (2021), with Katsh and Rifkin (2001) behind them, hold that such a party owes the parties before it transparency, accountability, contestability, and consent. This form withholds near-exactly that list — the rule cannot be observed, the determination answers to no one, no channel carries a contest, and enrollment stands in for consent — a design standard read in negative. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) supply the interpreting operation's published antecedent: sellers judged across a visibility gap by buyers they cannot see. The operations are what a person supplies while those requirements go unmet; how people begin to supply them is the early empirical question.

# Early Empirical Work

Once the construct is named, the question is how people beginning to acquire it work out an unexplained outcome, whether they treat anyone else as in the situation, and what they do when the outcome seems wrong. Pratt (2009) argued that qualitative research has no boilerplate: what counts as enough is a function of the question asked. No analysis is complete; this is early work.

The construct was formed in teaching GauntleTT, an intensive AI engineering cohort in Trinidad and Tobago; that formation is not the study site.

The continuing site is a specialized course at Hult University. Participants submit work to a gate that returns an outcome that is not fully explained: the gate is the definition's intermediary, its unexplained verdict the opacity, and its effective rule the adaptive element, because the codebase the gate tests against moves with other participants' work. The parties the gate reads on the far side are peers who vote in private — a counterpart who judges without being seen judging. The site is a hard case for opacity: participants build the systems they also submit to. Opacity, though, is a property of the person–arrangement pair, and what the arrangement withholds even from its builders is the vote and the criteria behind it. The code they can read.

The investigator teaches the course, and that position invites the standing objection: role duality, and consent that may not be free. The investigator casts no vote in the peer gate. Consent is held outside the course by a third party who receives withdrawals, and the investigator does not learn who declined until grades are final — the separation Ferguson, Yonge, and Myrick (2004) prescribe for faculty studying their own students. No one is interviewed about a project whose outcome is still open. Brannick and Coghlan (2007) treat role duality as a dynamic insider research manages; Anteby (2013) holds that involvement and distance are not ends of one continuum — the involvement that formed the construct also grounds the instrument's language. Mercer (2007) names the residue no design removes — participants may still be managing a teacher's expectations. Pratt, Kaplan, and Whittington (2020) distinguish an audit trail held for a committee from a deposit — Tracy's (2010) sincerity and ethics are the criteria the trail answers to — and the protocol lives in a public repository while the raw responses do not.

The instrument is a harness interview: a participant completes a task, receives an outcome that is not fully explained, and is asked how she interpreted it and what she did next. One incident is required for each block of questions. A closing "no" gets one reframe from another angle. The interviewer introduces no theoretical vocabulary. Three protocols are written: student, operations, and self.

The design is abductive (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012): we enter with a derived construct and hold the three operations as sensitizing concepts, which start inquiry and do not sort it (Bowen, 2006). First-cycle coding, when the files support it, will stay in the participant's words (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013); we decline the data-structure template (Mees-Buss, Welch, & Piekkari, 2022) and treat the design as bricolage (Pratt, Sonenshein, & Feldman, 2022). No coding cycle is complete. The negative case is a participant who obtains an answer from the gate by individual skill alone.

Two responses have come in. The first comes from the student protocol in the Hult course and is inspectable at the incident level; the second was uploaded on 22 August and awaits reading. The operations and self protocols have not returned a file.

On working out what the gate wanted, the first participant received an outcome she did not contest, named the reason, and found no channel for a contest: an incident of forbearance with no forum. Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's couriers hold an appeal channel and it reaches the apparatus; this participant found no channel at all. A person–system competence would treat the incident as finished adaptation; a competence that includes the counterpart has to treat it as a missing position.

On getting work across, the same participant pasted the gate's feedback into another system and asked for an explanation. The interpretive work was delegated to a tool. That is useful conduct, not algorithmacy, and it leaves the counterpart where she was.

On noticing unannounced change, and on what happens when an outcome seems wrong, the first file is thin. The thinness is itself a finding about the instrument: answers and questions sat at roughly the same length, because the interview was accepting summaries where it should have been asking for the occasion. We deepened the protocols.

We will keep refining the interview until it can be administered at greater scale; a companion three-wave instrument for that scale is built, approved, and not yet fielded, and the qualitative protocol is the live one. Bentley IRB issued an exempt determination on 11 May 2026, protocol 260511078.

# What This Paper Cannot Say

Algorithmacy is proposed rather than demonstrated. Whether the three operations cohere as one competency, whether they separate from one another, and whether they separate from general knowledge of software remain open questions; the early interviews can refine the instrument, and they cannot close those questions. Closing them carries a further concession: Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) hold that measurement at one level licenses no inference at another, and §5's design moves from incident-level interviews toward a scale. That move will have to be earned level by level.

The neighbouring constructs remain live. A later study might show that Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou's scale already captures the variance we attribute to a missing position, and it might show the same of Rahman's account of reactivity. Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson's fifth literacy is the hardest neighbour on the counterpart; we have argued that it is a literacy beside the platform, not through it, and we have not shown that a measure built on our definition outperforms any of them. Sandberg's result sets a further limit. If competence is a conception of work, a later standardized scale will have to show that it still measures that conception.

The shelf is also still filling: this year alone has added algorithmic skills (Hong, Cheng, & Liu, 2026), platform work literacies (Scolari, Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés, 2026), Human-AI Collaboration Dynamics (Mahy & Li, 2026), and AI-algorithmic competence (Ayasrah, Al-Rousan, Almulla, & Almulla, 2026), each of which, so far as its published account shows, still runs person-to-system — the nearest counterpart in any of them is a peer, a face-to-face encounter the app schedules, or the tool itself. The claim this paper defends is about a position, never about a name. Names in this field are cheap, and they are getting cheaper.

Paper 1's grades are a frame this paper leaves untested; the requirement we took up would still stand if coordinative co-optation were more fully answered, or if the form were not distinct. §5 argued the Hult course's fit as a hard case for opacity. Whether findings from this course travel to ride-hail, piecework, or freelance platforms is a question for a further study.

We have not measured a population, defeated rival explanations that run on prior skill, task mix, or hours, or shown that a rise in this competency is a good outcome. If scores rose while the form stayed as it is, what would have risen is the amount of unpaid coordination work the participants absorb. Whether that is welcome depends on facts about the arrangement that this construct does not measure.

A later paper can take the instrument to scale. This one offers a definition that includes the counterpart, a review of constructs that do not, and an early look at people who are learning to take part.
