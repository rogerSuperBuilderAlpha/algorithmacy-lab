# Extant Constructs

A construct, in this context, designates a formalized account of participant capacity—whether conceptualized as a competence, a literacy, or an interpretive orientation toward an automated system. Frameworks that incorporate a human counterpart already occupy adjacent literature; relational coordination, for example, defines a shared mode of communicating and relating among interdependent workers (Gittell, 2002). Yet relational coordination presupposes the very condition that platform mediation eliminates—interactants who directly observe, reach, and respond to one another. Because the algorithm replaces that mutual visibility, we evaluate what status each candidate construct accords the computational intermediary. Existing scholarship models individuals working alongside algorithms through divergent lenses, and that divergence establishes the theoretical boundary of what each competence can explain.

We examine seven candidate perspectives in sequence, taking each on its strongest reading before asking what it cannot reach. Across every hearing we pose a single structural question: where does the human counterpart on the opposite side—the client who commissions work, or the evaluator whose rating alters a score—theoretically reside?

## Computer-Mediated Communication Competence

Spitzberg (2006) took mediation as his subject before anyone else in this literature, and his framework deserves a hearing on its own terms. He defines computer-mediated communication as "any human symbolic text-based interaction conducted or facilitated through digitally-based technologies," then carries the interpersonal architecture into it intact: motivation, knowledge, skills, context, and outcomes. Motivation is "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in a given CMC context" (p. 640). Knowledge is "the cognitive comprehension of content and procedural processes involved in conducting appropriate and effective interaction in the computer-mediated context." Four skill clusters carry the behavioral load: attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness (p. 638). Five facets of context condition all of it (p. 644). Underneath sits a claim about what mediation does—it alters the operational parameters of competence and leaves its structure alone, because the parties at either terminal remain the coordinators.

We must refuse that claim, and refusing it requires first correcting a reading of Spitzberg that would make the refusal cheap. His is not a model of a faculty lodged in a single head. Competence in his tradition is produced between people and judged by the partner. "Competent interactants can facilitate the competence of cointeractants," he writes, and "an incompetent interactant can diminish a normally competent cointeractant's performance" (p. 650). Messages reach that judgment already filtered through receivers' expectations for messages in the medium. The counterpart is no late addition to this framework; she is its instrument of measurement. Five outcome criteria close the model: appropriateness, effectiveness (including task achievement and efficiency), coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development (p. 648). The arrangement they presuppose is unambiguous. Coorientation and relationship development are irreducibly two-party. Appropriateness, defined as "perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context," requires a perceiver; satisfaction, defined as "positive affect associated with fulfillment of positively valenced expectancies," requires someone in whom that affect occurs.

That architecture sharpens the test. Consider a participant competent on every dimension Spitzberg specifies, from motivation and media knowledge through attentiveness, composure, coordination, and expressiveness, placed in an arrangement where a system reads her work and her counterpart's and commits a determination binding on both. Her competence has not declined. The damage runs deeper into the model than a decline: her counterpart cannot perceive the message, assess its legitimacy, or register an affective response to it, and the criteria that would score her therefore have nobody to administer them. The arrangement has not lowered her score. It has removed the instrument.

Spitzberg reported the model's empirical difficulties himself, which is more than most construct papers manage. Negatively worded items attenuated his subscales, the context and message factors proved less multidimensional than he had drawn them, and a factor analysis of all items returned "four reliable factors that roughly parallel motivation, knowledge, skills, and outcomes" instead of the structure he had proposed (p. 651). "Research on the CMC competence model is nascent," he wrote, in the article proposing it.

Algorithmacy inherits his evaluative axis. Our three operations specify what a participant does; they do not specify what performing them well amounts to, and appropriateness and effectiveness remain the best available answer to that question. What we cannot inherit is the arrangement those criteria presuppose: a receiver at the far end of a channel, reachable, whose judgment constitutes the measure.

## Human–Machine Communication

The transmission model collapsed once engineers built computational systems that hold direct dialogue with human users. Guzman and Lewis (2020) advance that shift by conceptualizing the machine as an autonomous communicative partner. Framing a conceptual agenda, with no empirical scale attached, they argue that communicative artificial intelligence systems are "designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71), and that the field "focuses on questions of communication as they relate to technologies designed to fulfill the communicator role" (p. 73). Examining conversational agents, social robotics, and automated-writing software, they locate meaning-making "within a communication context in which at least one of the interaction partners is a machine" (p. 74). The departure from computer-mediated communication is deliberate and explicit: where computer-mediated communication "theorizes technology as a mediator of human interaction" (p. 72), human–machine communication positions the machine itself as an interlocutor, and their agenda develops that position across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions (p. 74). Fortunati and Edwards (2020), inaugurating the field's journal, state the constitutive unit outright, as a definition: human–machine communication studies meaning-making between humans and machines, and the machine holds the position of a communicative subject.

The dyad is therefore the field's stipulation, not our characterization of it—and the strongest objection to our hearing comes from inside the field. Gibbs, Kirkwood, Fang, and Wilkenfeld (2021) argue that human–machine communication has been theorizing the encounter as an interaction when it should be theorizing it as a structuration: machines carry rules and resources that humans draw on, reproduce, and occasionally subvert, and agency distributes across the human, the machine, and the institutional arrangement that brought them together. Their explicit target is the field's individualism, on the grounds that scholarship examining one person and one machine cannot see the organizational structures determining what the machine is doing there. They work the argument through organizational cases, algorithmic management among them.

Gibbs and colleagues do not close the position this paper identifies, and the reason is precise. Structuration introduces rules and resources, an institutional third term. It does not introduce a party. Their triad comprises the human, the machine, and the structure; ours comprises the worker, the counterpart, and the system. A specific human on the other side of the coordination, whose reception is the point of the exchange and whom the participant never reaches, appears in neither formulation. The test follows from that distinction. A person may be fully competent at human–machine communication—treating the system as a partner, reading its cues, sustaining the relation—and never once have coordinated with another human through a system that reads them both and decides. The competence this field would specify, were it to specify one, is competence with the machine.

What we inherit from this literature is its boundary work. Guzman and Lewis draw the cleanest available distinction between the channel that computer-mediated communication theorizes and the partner that human–machine communication theorizes, and we adopt that distinction while declining their ontology. Fortunati and Edwards make the field's question explicit enough to quote, which allows us to state our own difference as a difference of question.

## AI-Mediated Communication

Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) brought the human counterpart back into view while aligning the computational system with one communicating party. They integrate the artificial intelligence and computer-mediated communication literatures to define AI-mediated communication as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals" (p. 90). Both human participants sit inside that definition, and the phenomena they examine—algorithmic smart replies, predictive text engines, synthetic video—keep them there. Five parameters characterize the arrangement, each stated in their own vocabulary: magnitude, "the extent of the changes that AI enacts on messages"; media type, "the media in which AI operates"; optimization goal, "the goal for which AI is optimizing the messages"; autonomy, "the degree to which AI can operate on messages without the sender's supervision"; and role orientation, "the role that the AI is operating on behalf of" (Table 1, p. 91).

This is the most dangerous hearing in the section, and any argument that counts positions will fail here. AI-mediated communication is a three-party arrangement with two humans in it, by its own definition, and the receiver is not a residual figure within it: her inference is the dependent variable of the research program, and the ethical discussion concerns largely what is owed to her. Hancock and colleagues are candid about how exposed her position is. Disclosure, they observe, is the exception, with the partner presumably assuming the sender produced the message, and the arrangement rests on an assumption that may not hold—"the message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency."

The discrimination therefore turns on standing rather than on number, and their own parameters supply it. Take a person fully competent at AI-mediated communication: she calibrates how much to delegate, knows what her smart replies optimize for, and reads correctly when a counterpart's message has been shaped by an agent. Place her before a system delegated by neither party, optimizing for neither party's goal, and committing a decision binding on both. Every parameter loses its referent. Role orientation has no role to name. Optimization goal has no principal. Autonomy is calibrated against a sender's supervision that does not exist in this arrangement. The parameters do not merely fail to describe the case; they cannot be filled in for it, because the construct is defined by the phrase *on behalf of*, and our third party acts on behalf of no one at the table. In their framework the counterpart's exposure is to a message. In ours, her exposure—and the participant's—is to a determination.

We inherit the optimization-goal parameter as the cleanest statement of that difference: their agent optimizes for a party's goal, and ours optimizes for neither. We also inherit their candor about the receiver, whose acceptance the arrangement assumes and does not secure, which stands as the near neighbor of a counterpart who never reaches a position from which she could judge at all.

## AI Literacy

A distinct literature shifts focus from communicative interaction to technical comprehension, treating the algorithmic architecture as an object of formal knowledge. Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to evaluate AI technologies critically; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace" (p. 2), and organize the domain around five questions: what artificial intelligence is, what it can do, how it works, how it should be used, and how people perceive it. Seventeen competencies follow from those questions, spanning the identification of AI systems, machine learning representations, decision-making, training steps, data and sensors, ethics, and programmability.

Two of the seventeen name human beings, and the exact wording matters because a reviewer holding the table will check it. Competency 10, *Human Role in AI*, reads: "Recognize that humans play an important role in programming, choosing models, and fine-tuning AI systems." Competency 5 names human skills as the alternative to using AI. Humans enter this framework as the system's makers or as a benchmark against which the system is measured, and neither competency gives another person a position within an interaction the learner is conducting.

The framework's treatment of collaboration confirms the pattern and can be checked in both directions. Every instance of communicating or collaborating in the competencies points at the system: the definitional clause reads "with AI," and their background discussion of misconceptions concerns what "limit[s] people's ability to effectively use, collaborate with, and act as critical consumers of AI." Collaboration among people appears in the paper's design considerations, where Design Consideration 11, *Social Interaction*, advises designers to "consider designing AI learning experiences that foster social interaction and collaboration." Other humans occupy the pedagogy and never the competencies. A learner may satisfy all seventeen—distinguishing AI artifacts, arguing about intelligence, explaining representations, decisions, machine learning steps, data, sensors, ethics, and programmability, and recognizing that humans built the system—and hold no capacity whatever for making herself legible to a human counterpart through a system that reads them both. The framework would score her at ceiling, because all five of its organizing questions are questions about the system, and not one asks about a person reached through it.

That ceiling propagated into measurement. Ng, Leung, Chu, and Qiao (2021) compressed the seventeen competencies into four categories: knowing and understanding, using and applying, evaluating and creating, and ethics. Subsequent instruments inherited those four cells. Every one models competence as an individual's unilateral engagement with a computational artifact, so the omission propagated into a measurement tradition well beyond the 2020 paper that seeded it.

The contrast we inherit concerns derivation rather than count. Long and Magerko admit a competency by asking whether it reflects their definition, whether numerous sources support it, and whether it serves designers and educators. Nothing in that rule excludes an eighteenth competency, and nothing explains why nine belong under *how AI works* and one under *how AI should be used*. Our own facets are admitted by what the coordination form withholds. We also inherit their literacy-family argument, which traces literacy from written language to its digital, computational, scientific, and data descendants—the shelf this paper and theirs both stand on, and a lineage neither of us invented.

## Algorithmic Competency

Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025) built the first validated scale in this family, and they built it the way our own program intends to. Ninety-nine semi-structured interviews with Chinese ride-hailing drivers and food-delivery couriers generated fourteen candidate items; expert content validation reduced them; an exploratory factor analysis at N = 275 and a confirmatory analysis at N = 213 settled a second-order four-factor model; a validity study at N = 230 and a three-wave design at N = 225 placed the construct within a nomological net of antecedents and consequences. Twelve items survived, three per dimension, at an overall reliability of .85. They define algorithmic competency as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms."

A hearing that files this as knowledge of how the system works has read the label and not the items. Three of the four dimensions measure something other than knowledge. Understanding covers a sophisticated comprehension of algorithmic management and the ability to navigate its dynamic nature; Embracing captures a willingness to trust the efficiency and accuracy of that management; Leveraging denotes the capacity to use it strategically or advantageously; and Remediating concerns a worker's ability to address or supplement its deficiencies. Their discriminant work deserves stating plainly, because it is more careful than most: digital competence correlates at .37 with the scale overall but at .04, not significant, with Embracing—a null they predicted, on the grounds that digital-competence measures omit attitude.

The question was therefore never whether the counterpart is absent from their instrument. She is present, in four distinct roles. Not one of them makes her a party to the competency. Item 8 places her behind a score: "I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback." The object of that verb is the rules. Item 11 names the triad's own mechanism inside a belief—"I think the platform AM is highly efficient, such as in customers–workers matching"—yet it sits in the dimension correlating most weakly with the rest, and it asks nothing about any capacity toward the person matched. Customer-oriented service behavior enters their structural model as an outcome variable on Peccei and Rosenthal's (1997) measure, so within their own design the counterpart matters empirically, receives measurement, and still sits outside the construct.

The fourth role is the one that matters most here. Remediating item 6 reads "I can use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve vulnerabilities in AM," and the interview behind it is explicit: a courier who receives an unfair customer rating assembles evidence such as recordings and appeals to the algorithmic management. This is the only place in the rival scale where a worker contests an outcome. The contest runs to the apparatus. The customer originates the grievance and never receives it.

A worker may therefore score at ceiling across all twelve items—understanding dispatch, trusting the matching, exploiting the rules for the orders she wants, and repairing the system's failures through peer networks and the appeals function—while holding no capacity whatever for working out what the person on the other side of the match wanted, or for making herself legible to that person. Nothing in the scale would detect the failure. The counterpart enters their design only once the construct is complete, as a dependent variable, and a thirteenth item would not move her, because the definition names two terms: the worker and the algorithm. Their empirical setting anchors the items to platform mechanics, and the scale does not transfer to peer-evaluation environments where the tally itself is withheld.

We inherit their derivation discipline, and one empirical finding this paper has been asserting without support. The antecedents of algorithmic competency in their data are social: peer support, moderated by collectivism. That is the acquisition account our own claim about implicit learning requires and has not previously had.

## Reactivity under Opaque Evaluation

When an online freelancing platform replaced its transparent five-star rating with an opaque hundred-point Job Success Score, Rahman (2021) theorized the resulting dynamic through the invisible cage, contrasting hidden and fluctuating metrics with the Weberian iron cage of fixed, codified administrative rules. Five properties constitute that cage (p. 976): hidden and shifting evaluation criteria, evaluations executed at uncertain intervals, unidentifiable score magnitude, ambiguous reputational consequences, and an indeterminate locus of evaluative influence spanning the client and the platform. The five function as a proof of closure rather than as a list. As he puts it, "even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque."

Rahman describes the situation this construct answers, with more evidence than we will have, and describes it as a problem of control rather than of competence. His workers responded through two strategies: experimental reactivity, which tested tactical adjustments to raise the score, and constrained reactivity, which limited engagement to protect established metrics from volatility, with the choice turning on platform dependence and prior evaluation shocks. His outcome should discipline our claims, because reactivity entrenches the platform's hold, and the paranoia he documents persists years after the change.

The counterpart is in his field and remains outside his construct, and the record of that exclusion is unusually complete. He interviewed eighteen clients alongside eighty freelancers (p. 956), recruiting them through freelancer referral because the platform blocked the direct route—"registered freelancers could see a client's full profile only after applying for their job, and even then, TalentFinder withheld their contact information." He states their analytic purpose precisely: to understand client perspectives on providing feedback and "to triangulate what freelancers said about their experiences working with clients." His online appendix records, for each client, whether that client indicated awareness of how her actions affected freelancers' evaluation scores. Rahman thus measured the counterpart's own ignorance of her effect and left it outside the theory, because his theory concerns control. The platform's interface language documents the same severance from the other direction: clients were assured their feedback "will be kept anonymous and never shared directly with the freelancer."

His fifth component brings this literature closest to the position we name, and the distinction that survives is fine. Who influences the evaluation is the counterpart appearing as a failure of interpretation—the worker cannot determine whether the client or the platform moved the score. Not knowing which party moved the score is a problem about the score. Ours is a problem about the party. The one place his workers act toward the counterpart confirms the boundary: constrained reactivity takes platform-met clients off the platform, against a user agreement barring off-platform payment for two years on pain of deactivation. Reaching the counterpart requires leaving the arrangement, at risk of the account.

Three things pass from Rahman into this construct. Shifting criteria become an environmental condition, which restates our third operation as a property of the world. The five components decompose what is withheld, and the fifth names a failure our three operations do not cover: not knowing which party to interpret. Finally, his explanation of divergence by structural position and score history rather than by competence stands as a rival to any variance claim we make, and any such claim must survive it. He proves the boundary against himself, having had eighteen clients in the field while the construct still emerged with two terms.

## Gig Literacies

Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, and Nelson (2020) come closest of the seven, because they built the counterpart into the research design. They conceptualize gig literacies as "critical literacies… emerging around online freelancing" (p. 457), holding that workers "must adapt their skills and work strategies in order to leverage platforms creatively and productively, and as a component of their 'personal holding environment'." Later they name the object a platform literacy: "the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470). Their design interviewed twenty freelancers and nineteen clients and walked the platform from both sides, one author registered as a client and one as a freelancer (pp. 461–463). Client narratives, they write, "provided some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact valued in gig workers" (p. 463).

The five literacies below are their headings, quoted from their findings sections (pp. 463–469): building reputation through the Job Success Score, badges, private client feedback, and refunds that forestall bad reviews; self-presentation across profiles, proposals, and the chat that bumps a bid; maintaining productivity through routines and third-party tools; mitigating transaction risk through escrow, logged conversations, client vetting, and a gut feeling for who is trustworthy; and building relationships. Under the fifth, participant P10 engages the client "to build a rapport and a little longer lasting relationship" for repeat business (p. 468).

That fifth literacy is the strongest case any neighbor makes against this paper, and it also contains the turn. The most counterpart-directed practice these authors document is a strategy for escaping mediation. Participant P39, unable to rehabilitate an algorithmically depressed rating, migrated established clients off the platform to remain commercially viable (p. 468). Rapport, on their evidence, secures repeat business by moving the relation somewhere the score cannot follow it, which is the opposite of the competency we name.

The authors mark the boundary themselves, and mark it in a form we can adopt without adjustment. They contrast Upwork, where a worker can evaluate, select, and converse directly with clients, against platforms such as Uber, where the algorithm assigns labor deterministically (p. 470). Relationship building is foundational to longer engagements on their site precisely because Upwork leaves the parties more agency than a dispatch platform does. Their construct therefore captures freelancer competencies deployed beside an intermediary rather than communicative action executed through a binding one, and it depends on an environment where the parties can bypass the system and reach one another directly. Adding a relationship item to such a scale still measures a literacy exercised beside the platform.

We inherit two things from them: enrollment without standing, treated as a burden that falls on the worker's competence, and the situated learning of an opaque rating, which their freelancers acquire from peers and from consequences, never from documentation.

## What These Boundaries Share

Seven constructs yield seven boundaries and one topology: an individual on one side of a relation and an automated system on the other. The field's own stocktakings confirm the pattern at scale. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) synthesized fifty empirical studies of algorithmic literacy, screened from ninety-six across four databases, and identified pervasive conceptual fragmentation; Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub (2024) reviewed 169 publications and documented an absence of unified theoretical models; and Iyamu, Wheelans, Haag, Roe, and Chang (2026) evaluated twelve health-worker studies against Nutbeam's literacy model, reporting communicative literacy as "largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and measurement instruments." In Nutbeam's hierarchy the communicative level—explaining AI-mediated data to a patient whom the system does not simultaneously evaluate—represents the field's nearest approximation of a second human actor. Expanding survey items across dozens of studies has not moved that boundary. The field was handed a relational slot and populated it with system-facing operationalizations.

Theoretical accounts of visibility labor close the conceptual pincer from the opposite direction. Abidin (2016) conceptualizes the deliberate self-presentation a follower performs to become visible to a specific social media influencer—an authentic, named counterpart—while consciously setting the algorithm outside her analytical frame, defining her inquiry as analog affective labor distinct from algorithmic visibility. Constructs that center the algorithm bracket the human counterpart; the framework that centers the counterpart brackets the algorithm. Organization theory currently offers no construct that integrates both.

The triadic structural position itself has not gone unnoticed. In dispute resolution, Katsh and Rifkin (2001) conceptualized the technology hosting an online interaction as a "fourth party" exerting autonomous systemic effects. Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, and Neysen (2020) showed how customer reviews replace traditional dyadic exchanges, creating an asymmetrical triad—platform operators, buyers, and sellers—sustained by a visibility gap that lets buyers evaluate workers invisibly. Cameron (2024) formalized this configuration in on-demand labor as the algorithmic labor triangle, while Healy and Pekarek (2024) integrated it into labor process theory to explain worker precarity. All four operate as structural theories of power, institutional consent, or systemic vulnerability, and none articulates an actionable competence for the actor caught inside the triad.

We defend this boundary across four conditions: the construct must delineate an individual's behavioral and interpretive capacity; the counterpart must operate as an active party to the focal coordination rather than as an upstream designer, peer network, or downstream recipient; the intermediary must simultaneously evaluate and bind both interacting parties, where a transmission channel carries, a delegate assists, and a fourth party operates within a disclosed procedural framework; and the claim addresses a theoretical position rather than taxonomic terminology. Evaluated against these conditions, the seven hearings and the field's own meta-reviews yield a uniform conclusion: no extant competence construct accords the counterpart an analytical position. Empirical practice has meanwhile begun documenting the capacity in the field. Manky (2026) demonstrates that forty ride-hailing drivers in Lima decode platform metrics to assess passenger safety—a counterpart-facing capacity, deployed for physical security, that currently lacks formal conceptualization. Organization theory requires a competence formulated for the triadic position the field has already mapped. The next section develops that construct.

# Three corrections folded into this rewrite

**1. Hancock's page anchor was wrong.** The current text quotes the body definition—"mediated
communication between people…communication or interpersonal goals"—and anchors it to p. 89. That
anchor belongs to the abstract variant ("interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent…").
The body definition, introduced with "We integrate these AI and CMC conceptualizations to define AI-MC
as," sits on **p. 90**, and Table 1 with the parameters begins on p. 91
(`steelmans/hancock2020.md`, corrected 2026-08-26 against the version of record). The rewrite cites
p. 90 and p. 91.

**2. "The only item referencing human actors" overstates Long and Magerko.** Competency 5 names human
skills as the alternative to using AI, so Competency 10 is not the only competency in which human
beings appear. A reviewer with the ACM table checks this in seconds. The rewrite names both, which
costs one clause and produces the stronger claim—no competency gives another person a position in an
interaction the learner is conducting—and adds the check in the other direction: Design Consideration
11 puts collaboration among people in the pedagogy, never in the competencies.

**3. "They utilized client accounts to provide some verification" conflates two methods.** Sutherland
and colleagues ran a platform walkthrough with one author registered as a client, and separately
interviewed nineteen clients whose narratives supplied the verification. The current sentence reads as
though the walkthrough accounts produced the corroboration. The rewrite separates them.
