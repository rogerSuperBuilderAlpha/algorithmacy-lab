# Steelman — the field's own stocktakings

Not a construct hearing. Three reviews that audit the whole algorithmic- and AI-literacy field, read
so that §3 can say what it currently only implies: not "we read seven constructs and none has the
counterpart," but **the field has audited itself and the position is empty across it**. The 19 August
review named this the cheapest available upgrade and it is the one this memo delivers.

| Review | Scope | Read from |
|---|---|---|
| Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum (2025), *NMS* 27(2), 681–701 | 96 screened, **50 reviewed**, four databases | Full text, author-deposited (DuEPublico), 22 pp |
| Iyamu, Wheelans, Haag, Roe & Chang (2026), *Frontiers in Public Health* 14 | Seven databases, Jan 2020 – May 2025, **12 included** | Full text, gold OA, 16 pp |
| Gagrčin, Naab & Grub (2024), *NMS* 28(1), 423–447 | **169 studies** | **Abstract only.** Both repository copies returned HTML on 19 August |

## What they claim, in their words

**Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum** set out to "define algorithmic literacy, based on existing research,
review current issues in algorithmic literacy, and propose an agenda." They find the field carrying two
competing definitions and quote both. DeVito (2021: 3):

> the capacity and opportunity to be aware of both the presence and impact of algorithmically-driven
> systems on self- or collaboratively-identified goals, and the capacity and opportunity to crystalize
> this understanding into a strategic use of these systems to accomplish said goals

Dogruel and colleagues (2021: 4):

> being aware of the use of algorithms in online applications, platforms, and services, knowing how
> algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making as well as having the
> skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations

They name the field's problem as "twofold: (1) What is algorithmic literacy? and (2) How do we assess
this?" and observe that the measurement problem has no clean floor, because "the secretive nature of
algorithms makes it difficult to assess literacy about them" — the researcher cannot score correctness
when the algorithm is unknown to her too.

**Iyamu and colleagues** chart AI and algorithmic literacy among health workers against **Nutbeam's**
three levels, and the third level is the one that matters here: "functional: basic understanding and
use; critical: evaluation and ethics; **communicative: interacting with AI systems and explaining
AI-mediated information**," elsewhere given as "interaction with AI systems, **including supporting
patients to do so**."

**Gagrčin and colleagues**, at abstract depth, report that research on how individuals understand
algorithms "lacks a cohesive framework" across 169 studies.

## Best version

Take the field at its strongest and two of its moves are real.

DeVito's definition contains **"self- or collaboratively-identified goals,"** which is the only place in
either field definition where another person appears at all. And Nutbeam's communicative level, as
Iyamu and colleagues import it, is explicitly about a **second human**: explaining AI-mediated
information to a patient, supporting a patient to interact with a system. Anyone claiming the field has
no room for another person has not read these two.

The field also knows it is fragmented and says so. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum, Gagrčin and colleagues,
and Iyamu and colleagues all reach the same verdict independently — proliferating constructs, measures
that do not talk to each other, no agreement on whether literacy is one construct or several. A paper
proposing yet another construct into that field owes an argument for why it is not more of the same.
**§4's admission rule is that argument**, and §3 should hand off to it here rather than leaving the
reader to notice the problem.

## Where the counterpart is, and is not

Two findings, and the second is the one to put in the paper.

**Word-level, and checkable.** In Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum's 22 pages, "interpersonal" appears zero
times and "communicative" appears zero times. In Iyamu and colleagues' 16 pages, "interpersonal"
appears zero times. Neither review contains the word "counterpart." These are review articles whose job
is to name what the field studies.

**Structural, and stronger.** Iyamu and colleagues did not merely omit the communicative level — **they
looked for it and found the field had left it empty**:

> Across studies, competencies aligned mainly with functional and critical dimensions of DHL,
> particularly awareness, use, evaluation, and ethics, while communicative literacies were infrequently
> assessed.

> In contrast, communicative literacy was largely absent from both conceptual frameworks and
> measurement instruments.

And the single study that came closest reframed the level into a person–system relation on its way
past:

> more limited attention to communicative literacy which was described in relation to integrating AI
> systems in workflows and collaborating effectively with them rather than competencies related to
> explaining AI-mediated information, negotiating uncertainty, or supporting patient and community
> decision making

That is the paper's charge occurring inside somebody else's systematic review. A framework supplied a
slot for the human-facing competence; the literature filled it with more system-facing measurement.
Twelve studies, seven databases, five years.

## The test, at field level

A person could satisfy the field's own definitions completely — aware of algorithmic presence and
impact, knowing how algorithms work, able to evaluate algorithmic decision-making, skilled at coping
with and influencing algorithmic operations, and strategic in using systems toward her goals — and have
no capacity to make herself legible to a counterpart through a system that reads them both and commits
a decision binding on both. **Fifty studies, and the ability is not among them.**

## What we inherit

Three things, and the third costs the paper something.

**The negative result itself**, which is worth a sentence with numbers in it: two systematic reviews and
a scoping review, 50 and 169 and 12 studies, four and seven databases, and no construct in any of them
gives the counterpart a position. That converts §3's close from a claim about seven readings into a
documented property of a field.

**The measurement problem, stated by the field rather than by us.** Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum's point
that a researcher cannot establish how correct a user is, because the algorithm is unknown to her too,
is a direct argument for a competency defined on what a person *does* rather than on what she *knows* —
which is what the locked definition already does. Citing them for it turns a limitation into a warrant.

**And a scope condition we now have to write.** Nutbeam's communicative level is real prior art on a
second human being in the picture, so §3 cannot say the field never mentions another person. The
distinction that survives is the paper's own: explaining AI-mediated information **to** a patient is a
competence exercised toward someone the system is not also judging, and the clinician's account of the
system is not a determination binding on both parties. Say that, and the negative result gets sharper
rather than weaker.

## What adding items will not get

Both field definitions have two terms — a user and an algorithm — and every dimension proposed under
them (awareness, knowledge, evaluation, coping, influence, strategic use) is a relation between those
two. Iyamu and colleagues supply the demonstration: hand the field a framework with a communicative
level already in it, and the instruments still come back functional and critical. **The field was given
the slot and did not fill it**, which is the strongest available evidence that the missing element is a
position rather than a variable.
