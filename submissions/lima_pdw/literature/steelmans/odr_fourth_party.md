# Steelman — the fourth party (online dispute resolution)

An eighth hearing, and the one §3 does not currently have. The 19 August review's finding 3 named this
literature as the prior-art trap under §3's close; the review was right, and the concession is worth
more than the sentence it costs.

**Read depth, stated plainly.** Written from the arm's own cards — `cards/katsh2001.md`
(`extended_preview`: Jossey-Bass chapters 1–4, plus Bol's review in *Artificial Intelligence and Law*)
and `cards/wing2021.md` (`full_text`, read during the 18 August sweep) — not from a fresh read on 19
August. Wing et al. is closed access and no repository holds it; the 2001 book needs a library.
**Nothing below should be quoted in print without re-checking against the sources.** The analysis is
the arm's; the wording is not the authors'.

## Their claim

**Katsh and Rifkin (2001)** founded online dispute resolution on the claim that when a dispute moves
online, the technology is not a neutral conduit. It shapes what can be said, when, to whom, and with
what consequence, and it does so with enough independent effect to warrant treating it as a party. They
name it **the fourth party** — after the two disputants and the human third-party neutral — and argue
that as its capabilities grow it takes over functions belonging to the third, until in some designs no
human neutral remains and the system resolves the dispute itself. The fourth party can *displace* the
third.

**Wing, Martinez, Katsh and Rule (2021)** revisit that claim twenty years on, with machine learning in
the systems. Their argument is that the displacement has happened: fourth parties now triage,
recommend, and in some designs decide. Their contribution is a set of ethical design requirements —
transparency about the system's role, accountability for its determinations, contestability, protection
against embedded bias, informed consent to the process — and a normative claim that a fourth party
exercising decisional power without those safeguards produces outcomes that are efficient and
illegitimate.

## Best version, and why it is the strongest version available to us

This is a peer-reviewed literature, running for two decades, whose subject is **a non-human party that
commits binding determinations in a multi-party arrangement**, with the position named and theorised.
Any sentence of ours implying that nobody has put a system in that seat dies against it.

But run the two literatures side by side and they are asking different questions of the same
arrangement:

| | Fourth party (ODR) | Algorithmacy |
|---|---|---|
| Object | The **system's obligations** | The **party's capacity** |
| Asks | What must the system provide for the outcome to be legitimate? | What must a person be able to do? |
| Answer | Transparency, accountability, contestability, consent | Interpret, specify intent, keep track |
| Presumes | A **disclosed** process the parties entered | A process disclosed to nobody, entered by taking work |

Read that way the fourth-party literature is our **strongest antecedent and our complement**, not our
rival. Wing and colleagues name what the arrangement owes; §4 names substantially the same list as what
this arrangement withholds; the operations are what a person supplies when the requirements go unmet
and nobody is going to meet them. The concession costs a paragraph and buys an antecedent that has been
developing the position for twenty years.

## What they make the algorithm

A **party**, and this is the only neighbour that says so outright. Not a channel, not a partner, not a
delegate, not an object of knowledge or navigation or interpretation. A fourth party with standing,
which in some designs decides.

## Hunt for the counterpart

Present, named, and structurally protected — which is exactly the difference.

In ODR the other disputant is a party to a disclosed process. She is known to be there, the process is
known to both, and the fourth party's determination is one both can in principle contest, because
contestability is a design requirement the literature exists to specify. Wing and colleagues treat the
absence of contestability as an ethical failure of the system.

In the arrangement §4 describes, the counterpart is not protected and not reachable, and the absence of
contestability is not a design failure anyone is accountable for. It is the ordinary operation of the
form. Our first respondent's incident is the clean illustration: she did not contest an outcome
because no channel read as the place for it. In an ODR system that would be a violated requirement. Here
it is Tuesday.

## The test

Yes, and the failure runs in an unusual direction. A person who fully satisfies what the ODR literature
asks of a *party* — she consents to the process, understands the system's role, contests what she
disputes — is exercising capacities that presuppose a system meeting its obligations. Strip the
obligations and those capacities have nothing to attach to. **The ODR literature specifies the
competence of a participant in a legitimate process; ours specifies what a person supplies when the
process is not one.**

## What we inherit

**The position itself, and the vocabulary for it.** "Fourth party" is a better-established name than
anything the platform literature offers for a system with standing, and citing it lets §4 say that the
seat is recognised and that what is new is the competence written for someone sitting across from it.

**Their four requirements as our four withholdings.** Transparency, accountability, contestability and
consent map almost one to one onto what the form withholds. That correspondence is worth stating
because it makes the withholding list principled rather than assembled — it is the negation of a
published design standard.

**A second route made real.** §6's institutional-remedy route now has a named, developed design
literature behind it, which turns a rhetorical balance into a genuine alternative.

## What adding items will not get

An ODR measure, if anyone wrote one, would measure whether a *system* met its obligations, or whether a
*process* was legitimate. Neither is a competence of a person. And the literature's founding presumption
— that the parties entered a disclosed process — is the thing that fails in our case, so no amount of
refinement inside it reaches the arrangement where nobody disclosed anything.

## For §3

The review already drafted the insert. What this memo adds is the reason it strengthens rather than
concedes: **ODR asks what the system owes, and answers for its design; the question here begins where
that presumption fails.** Put the concession in §3 and the fourth-party requirements in §4 as the
negation list, and the same literature does two jobs.
