# Steelman — Hancock, Naaman & Levy (2020)

Read 2026-08-19 from the publisher's full text, *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 25(1),
89–100, doi:10.1093/jcmc/zmz022 (Oxford Academic). The article is not open access; page anchors for
the body quotations still need the PDF.

## Their claim, in their words

**The article carries two statements of the definition, and we are quoting the weaker one.**

The abstract:

> interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by
> modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals

The formal definition, in the "Conceptualizing AI-MC" section, introduced with *"We integrate these AI
and CMC conceptualizations to define AI-MC as"*:

> mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a
> communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or
> interpersonal goals

§3 quotes the abstract. That is not an error — both sentences are the authors' — but the body version
is the one to use, for a reason given below.

Their parameters, each verbatim:

- **Magnitude:** *"The extent of the changes that AI enacts on messages."*
- **Media type:** *"The media in which AI operates (e.g., text, audio, video)."*
- **Optimization goal:** *"The goal for which AI is optimizing the messages."*
- **Autonomy:** *"The degree to which AI can operate on messages without the sender's supervision."*
- **Role orientation:** *"The role that the AI is operating on behalf of (e.g., sender vs. receiver)."*
- **Synchronicity:** AI involvement in synchronous versus asynchronous exchange.

An agenda and a definitional apparatus, not a measure and not a competence construct.

## Best version

This is the most dangerous hearing in §3 and the arm has said so before; the full text confirms why.

AI-MC is, on its own definition, a **three-party arrangement with two humans in it** — the body version
says "between people" outright. Its research programme is about what happens to the *receiver*: whether
she can tell, what she attributes to the sender, what trust survives. They are explicit that disclosure
is the exception — *"Commonly, the involvement of AI is not disclosed, with the partner presumably
assuming that the message was produced by the sender"* — and that the arrangement rests on an assumption
that may not hold: *"The message receiver is assumed to understand and accept that agency."*

So a claim that AI-MC "inherits a dyad" will not survive a reviewer who works in this literature.
Neither will position-counting of any kind. §3's current close is already better than that — it locates
the difference in standing rather than in number — and the hearing should run entirely on standing.

## What they make the algorithm

A **delegate**. The agent acts *on behalf of* a communicator; role orientation asks whose side it is on;
optimization goal asks whose goal it pursues. Every parameter presupposes a principal.

## Hunt for the counterpart

Present by construction, and this is the construct where she is most present: the receiver is a named
party, her inference is the dependent variable of the agenda, and the ethical section is largely about
what is owed her. The definition itself puts communication "between people."

What she never is, in AI-MC, is **subject to the agent**. The agent shapes a message she receives; it
does not rate her, rank her, match her, or close her account. Her exposure is to a *message*. In our
arrangement her exposure is to a *determination*, and so is the sender's.

## The test

Yes — and here the test has to be run carefully, because a sympathetic reviewer will say AI-MC already
covers our case. It does not, and the reason is in their own parameters. Take a person fully competent
at AI-MC: she calibrates how much to delegate, knows what her smart replies optimize for, and reads
correctly when a counterpart's message has been shaped by an agent. Now put her in front of a system
that is delegated by neither party, optimizes for neither party's goal, and commits a decision binding
on both. **Every one of their parameters loses its referent.** Role orientation has no role to name.
Optimization goal has no principal. Autonomy is measured relative to a sender's supervision that does
not exist here. The parameters do not merely fail to describe our case; they cannot be filled in for it.
That is a stronger result than "the agent is on a party's side," and it is entirely theirs.

## What we inherit

The **optimization-goal parameter**, which is the cleanest available way to state our difference: their
agent optimizes for a party's goal, ours for neither. Also their honesty about the receiver's position —
they raise the problem of an agent whose involvement is undisclosed and whose acceptance is assumed,
which is the near neighbour of our counterpart who never reaches a position from which she could judge.

## What adding items will not get

An AI-MC measure, if anyone wrote one, would ask about delegation, disclosure, calibration, and
attribution of authorship. Add items indefinitely and they stay indexed to a principal, because the
construct is defined by the phrase *on behalf of*. Our third party acts on behalf of no one at the
table. There is no item that turns a delegate into an adjudicator.

## Housekeeping for the manuscript

- §3's Hancock hearing quotes the abstract definition. Switching to the body definition costs nothing,
  is the authors' own formal statement, and pre-empts the "you flattened a triad" objection by putting
  "between people" in our own quotation.
- The parameters as §3 lists them — magnitude, medium, optimization goal, autonomy, role orientation —
  are correct and in the article's own vocabulary. Synchronicity is a sixth and can be left out.
