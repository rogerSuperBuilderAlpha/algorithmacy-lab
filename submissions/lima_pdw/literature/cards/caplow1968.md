## What it argues

Caplow formalises Simmel's triad into a predictive theory of coalitions. Given three parties with unequal power, which two will ally against the third is determined by the power distribution, and he enumerates the types — including the counterintuitive cases where the weakest two combine to dominate the strongest, and where the strongest party's advantage becomes a liability. The book extends the analysis to organisational hierarchies, families, and international relations, and its governing claim is that **triads are inherently unstable and resolve into a coalition of two against one**.

## Relation to the argument

The structural prediction is directly relevant and mildly uncomfortable for the paper. If triads resolve into two-against-one, then the arrangement §7 describes should resolve too, and it is worth asking which coalition forms. The obvious answer is platform-and-counterpart against worker: the system's determinations are made in the counterpart's idiom (the rider's rating, the client's review) and the worker faces both. That would be a sharper statement of §6's distributive claim than "a form that converts unequal private capacity into unequal outcomes," and it is a genuinely triadic account of why the worker's position is what it is.

It also names a testable alternative. Coalitions between workers and counterparts against the platform do form — the driver who asks the rider to cancel, the freelancer who takes a client off-platform — and Caplow's framework predicts when. That is a mechanism for §6's two routes and one the paper does not currently have.

## Caution

Caplow's parties are all strategic actors capable of choosing allies, which the algorithm is not, and his power distributions are known to the players, which the platform's is not. The formalism assumes three-person games and does not extend cleanly to a party that is a rule rather than an agent. 1968 sociology, largely superseded by network exchange theory.
