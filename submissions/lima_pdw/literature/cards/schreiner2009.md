## What it argues

Schreiner, Kale and Corsten separate two questions the alliance-capability literature had been running together: *how* a firm develops alliance capability, and *what* the capability actually consists of. The first had received most of the attention; they take the second. Building on prior research and their own fieldwork, they conceptualize alliance management capability as a multidimensional construct with three distinct but related skills, all of them concerned with managing an individual alliance once it is up and running: **coordination**, **communication**, and **bonding**. They then test the conceptualization in a framework linking the capability to alliance-level and firm-level outcomes, using survey and secondary data on interfirm relationships between software service providers and three major global software vendors. The construct holds up and shows predictive validity for certain alliance outcomes.

## Relation to the argument

§4 cites this beside Ritter and Gemünden as the second named competency in the network column, and it earns the place by being the *decomposed* version. Where network competence answers "does the party have what the form demands," alliance management capability answers "of what does the demand consist" — and the three components map recognizably onto the network's integrating problem: coordination for predictability, communication for common understanding, bonding for the accountability that no contract supplies. That mapping is a template the co-optation column can borrow when §7 derives algorithmacy's three facets; the paper should be aware it is walking a road already walked.

The setting is also worth noting for the fourth column. The data come from providers in an asymmetric relationship with three dominant global vendors — closer to a platform's provider side than most alliance research, though the parties are firms with contracts.

## Caution

**The level problem is worse here than in Ritter and Gemünden, and it is in the title.** The construct is called a *capability*, the abstract's opening sentence is "a firm's alliance capability provides competitive advantage," and the outcomes are measured at the alliance and firm level. §8's table assigns capability to "the firm that owns the routine," and §9 uses exactly that assignment to dispose of AI as an organizing capability. §4 cites Schreiner, Kale and Corsten as the second named competency standing "beside" network competence in the network column; on the manuscript's own taxonomy it is not a competency at all. Either §4 acknowledges that both of its network-column precedents are firm-level and argues that the *move* rather than the construct is what it inherits, or a reviewer points out that the paper's warrant and its dismissal of Stelmaszak and colleagues run on the same argument in opposite directions.

Abstract depth only: I have not verified the item construction, the three-factor structure, or which outcomes the capability did and did not predict. The abstract itself hedges — support is "general," and the capability impacts "certain" outcomes — so the manuscript should not cite this as a clean confirmation. Unit of analysis is the firm; respondents are managers reporting on their own alliances, with the endogeneity that implies. And the construct is squarely managerial: it describes what a party does to *run* a relationship it entered voluntarily, which is not the situation of a worker who reconstructs an unstated rule from outcomes.
