## What it argues

A preregistered online experiment assigned **"occupation-specific, incentivized writing tasks to 453 college-educated professionals"** and randomly exposed half to ChatGPT. **"The average time taken decreased by 40% and output quality rose by 18%."** The sentence that matters for this cluster is the next one: **"Inequality between workers decreased."** Exposure also changed subsequent behaviour — treated workers were **"2 times as likely to report using it in their real job 2 weeks after the experiment and 1.6 times as likely 2 months after."**

The compression is the point. Noy and Zhang report explicitly that the tool narrowed the spread of performance across workers rather than widening it, which is the same directional result Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond find on a very different population and design.

## Relation to the argument

Third independent confirmation of the novice-levelling pattern §6 concedes, and the cleanest one to cite for it, because it is randomised and states the inequality result in one clause. Its evidential role in the manuscript is negative: it is the study that most directly contradicts a naive reading of §1. If the same tool at the same intensity *compresses* outcomes among equivalently trained professionals, then the platform variance §1 opens on cannot be attributed to generative AI exposure as such, and the burden shifts to something about the coordination form rather than the technology.

That is usable. §1's claim is not that technology produces dispersion; it is that dispersion persists among workers on the same system and is not explained by intensity of use. Noy and Zhang help establish the *negative* half of that — measured tool use does not straightforwardly generate the spread — while leaving the positive half unsupported.

## Caution

Short online tasks with recruited professionals, not sustained work in an organisation; the outcomes are task time and blind-graded quality, not earnings or exit. "Inequality between workers decreased" is a within-experiment result on task scores and must not be reported as a claim about labour-market inequality. Two-week and two-month follow-ups are self-reported. Do not cite for platform work, algorithmic management, or heterogeneity that survives skill — this paper is evidence that skill explains a great deal of it.
