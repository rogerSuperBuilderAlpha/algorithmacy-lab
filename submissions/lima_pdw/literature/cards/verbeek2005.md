## What it argues

Verbeek's claim is that artifacts are not neutral instruments but mediators that co-shape how people perceive the world and how they act in it, and that philosophy of technology should start from concrete things rather than from Technology as a condition. The book opens by faulting the classical philosophy of technology of Jaspers and Heidegger for treating technology as an alienating whole, moves through Ihde's postphenomenology and Latour's actor-network theory, and ends with Borgmann's device paradigm and a chapter on design. The middle of the book sets out technological mediation on two sides. On the hermeneutic side artifacts transform perception and interpretive frameworks, with a structure of amplification and reduction: some ways of seeing the world are strengthened and others weakened. On the existential or pragmatic side artifacts shape action, with a structure of invitation and inhibition: artifacts translate programs of action, inviting some conduct and discouraging the rest (secondary sources locate this at pp. 155–156). Verbeek's placement of Latour, as Redaelli quotes it, credits him with showing that technologies co-shape interactions (p. 103) but faults him for describing from the standpoint of an outside analyst rather than from a situated human, and offers postphenomenology as the view from inside out (p. 168); humans and things are to be distinguished but not separated (pp. 166–168, per Verbeek 2014). Feenberg's review calls the result an important contribution that keeps the existential concerns of the classical tradition while taking the co-construction thesis from technology studies.

Verbeek (2011), *Moralizing technology: Understanding and designing the morality of things* (University of Chicago Press), extends the mediation thesis to morality: because technologies mediate perception and action, they mediate moral decisions, and people are technologically mediated moral agents; the book's cases run from obstetric ultrasound, which makes the fetus a patient and the womb a site of decision, to persuasive and ambient technologies. The lab holds a snippet-level card for it at `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/verbeek2011.md`, which records page-located passages on force, persuasion and seduction as forms of behaviour-steering mediation (p. 126 there) and on persuasive technology as composite intentionality (p. 148 there). ## Relation to the argument

Arm: `spm2027_lived_arbiter`. Verbeek gives the SPM talk the word it needs, mediation, and the two-sided structure that lets the arm say what the arbiter does. On the hermeneutic side the platform amplifies the guest's rating and the host's response time and reduces everything else the host said; on the pragmatic side it invites the host to write for the filter and inhibits the sentence that would cost a star. Eslami et al.'s reviewers who learn of the filter and begin to write for it are a case of invitation in Verbeek's sense. The talk can present the lived arbiter as mediation of a kind Verbeek's two sides do not jointly describe, because both sides run between one human and a world, while here the mediated object is another person's speech and the mediation is itself an evaluation that binds both speakers.

The methodological point matters for an SPM audience. Verbeek's objection to Latour is that the outside analyst's description misses how access to reality is co-shaped from inside; the arm is on Verbeek's side of that line, because its evidence is how hosts and reviewers report the arbiter, and it should say so. *Moralizing technology* supplies the moral vocabulary if the talk wants it: an arbiter that nobody chose and nobody can see is a mediator of moral decisions whose designers did not accompany it, in Verbeek's sense, into the host's room.
