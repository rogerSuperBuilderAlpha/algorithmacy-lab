## What it argues

A methods paper about an impossible measurement problem. Hargittai and colleagues take up the difficulty that makes algorithm-skill research different from every other skill research: there is no known ground truth. Platform details are proprietary and rarely disclosed, so the researcher studying what users know about algorithms possesses limited know-how herself and cannot score a respondent's belief as correct or incorrect. The paper works through what that implies for instrument design and offers a blueprint for doing the research anyway, alongside a call for more of it to accompany the system-level critique that dominates the field.

The definitional contribution the later literature actually uses is narrower: algorithm awareness as "knowing that a dynamic system is in place that can personalize and customize the information that a user sees or hears."

## Relation to the argument

A resource, and a load-bearing one for §11. This is the field's own admission that the standard measurement strategy — factual knowledge tested against a criterion — has no criterion available. Dogruel, Masur and Joeckel's true/false knowledge items score respondents against a key that the researchers wrote from public reporting about systems they cannot inspect; Hargittai and colleagues say plainly that the key is not knowable. If the manuscript wants to argue that algorithmacy cannot be measured as knowledge, this is the citation from inside the rival camp, not an outsider's objection.

It also supplies the positive argument for measuring conduct rather than report. Hargittai and colleagues conclude that asking users to rate their own literacy will not work and that in-depth accounts of their experience are more likely to surface what they know. That points the same direction as §10's implicit-acquisition property: a capacity acquired through participation in an undisclosed rule shows up in what someone does under the rule, not in what they can state about it. The manuscript should pair this with Swart's verbalisation-gap finding (`swart2021.md`), which says the self-report channel may not reach the thing, and then say what its own instrument does about it.

Nothing here touches the counterpart. Awareness of "a dynamic system … that can personalize … the information that a user sees" is a two-position construct in its definition — one user, one system, one information stream.

## Caution

A methodological essay, not an empirical study; it establishes no findings about how skilled anyone is and cannot be cited for a distribution. The five-author list is complete and in order as given. Read at abstract depth — Taylor & Francis blocks automated retrieval — so the specific design recommendations that constitute the "blueprint" are unverified here, and any claim that the paper endorses a particular method should be checked against the text. The awareness definition quoted above I take from Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum's citation to p. 771, not from the article itself.
