## What it argues

Koltay writes from library and information science, and his question is nominal before it is substantive: the field has produced data literacy, science data literacy, research data literacy and data information literacy, and he wants to know whether these name one construct or several. He examines the terms against the data-related activities that motivate them — data quality, data management, data curation, data citation — and positions each against information literacy, which he treats as the parent construct and the discipline's established asset.

His findings are terminological and institutional. The field needs data literacy and would gain from a unified vocabulary; the existing competence lists overlap heavily with information-literacy competences, which he reads as evidence of close kinship rather than of a distinct construct. He splits the audience in two: researchers who must become "data literate science workers," and a professional track aimed at educating data-management professionals. He closes with a caution against the promises attached to big data, urging "a critical stance on hopes and fears" rather than the enthusiasm the term usually carries.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour at some distance, carded to show the breadth of the literacy field rather than to be beaten. Its value to the manuscript is negative evidence of a specific kind: this is the paper whose entire job is to sort the data-literacy constructs, and it arrives at a taxonomy of *data-handling* competences held by researchers and information professionals. No counterpart, no mediator, no determination. Whatever else the literacy family contains, its own stocktaking exercise does not contain the triad.

The second use is rhetorical and belongs to §4 rather than §9. Koltay demonstrates that when a technical shift creates a new demand, the literacy field responds by proliferating names and then holding a conference about which name to keep. The paper's claim is that co-optation produced no name at all. Koltay is the contrast case: naming pressure exists, the field is not shy of coining, and the coordinated party's competency still went unnamed.

**Competency, not skill.** Koltay's constructs are curricular and taught in library instruction programmes; they are skills in §8's sense.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth only, from Emerald's structured abstract via Crossref. I have not verified which competence lists he compares, how he characterises each of the four named literacies, or what evidence supports the claim of overlap with information literacy. This is a conceptual review with no data, and its disciplinary standpoint — LIS, with academic libraries as the implied institution — shapes what it counts as a competence. Cite it for the terminological state of the field around 2015 and for nothing empirical. If the manuscript wants a second data-literacy definition with more argumentative content, Wolff et al. (2016) is the better one and it was read in full; Koltay is the map, not the territory.
