## What it argues
Detert and Edmondson identify a class of beliefs that operate below deliberation and shut down speaking up before any calculation happens. Across qualitative work and then scale development and experiments, they surface five implicit voice theories, taken-for-granted rules employees hold about what one does and does not say at work: don't bypass the boss upward; don't embarrass the boss in public; don't speak negatively in a meeting; you need solid data or solutions before speaking; don't say anything that could harm your career. The theories are held as self-evident rather than as beliefs open to test, and they are widely shared, appearing across organisations with different climates and among employees who report feeling psychologically safe. The decisive finding is the dissociation: these rules predict withholding *independently* of measured psychological safety and of the immediate supervisor's openness, which means an employee can be in a safe environment, with an open manager and a working channel, and still not speak — because the rule tells her this is not the sort of thing one raises here. The authors argue the rules are learned early, carried across employers, and rarely disconfirmed, since not speaking generates no evidence against them.

## Relation to the argument
This is the sharpest available name for the paper's live interview pattern, and it fills the gap the futility literature leaves. The participant did not judge the channels dangerous and did not judge them absent; she judged them *inappropriate* — the Zoom session and the Discord were not the sort of place one takes this. That is an implicit voice theory operating exactly as Detert and Edmondson describe: a rule about the fit between a concern and a channel, held as obvious, never tested. Their dissociation finding is what makes the citation load-bearing rather than decorative. Because the rules predict silence independently of safety and of channel availability, the paper can say that supplying a channel does not supply contestation, and can say it with evidence rather than by assertion. That is §5's claim about accountability restated at the level of a single decision to speak.

It also arms the §11 protocol. The right probe is not "did you feel able to raise it" but "what would have had to be different for that to be the place you raised it" — which surfaces the rule instead of the affect. And it supplies a discriminating prediction against the rival explanation that low algorithmacy explains non-contestation: an implicit voice theory is orthogonal to skill at reading the system, so a highly competent participant should hold them too.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and citing literature; the five theories above are the standard enumeration, and the scale items and effect estimates need the original before use. The construct was developed inside hierarchical employment, and every one of the five rules references a boss or a meeting — a platform worker has neither, so the specific content will not transfer even where the mechanism does. Treat the transfer as a hypothesis for §11 to test rather than as a finding to apply. The measures are self-report, and the paper's own claim that the rules operate below deliberation sits awkwardly with asking people to endorse them.
