## What it argues

Bishop argues that YouTube's recommender produces a discriminatory visibility hierarchy that favours middle-class creators making highly gendered content aligned with advertisers' needs, using entrepreneurial beauty vlogging as the case. Her secondary hypothesis is the one this cluster needs: creators' own understandings of the algorithm are **learned and embodied within their practice**, shaping self-presentation, tone of voice, choice of content, and even words and sentence structures.

## Relation to the argument

The finest-grained account available of what intent specification costs. Section 7 says something is always lost in the compression and that part of the competency is knowing what can afford to be lost; Bishop's claim that inferred algorithmic preferences reach down into word choice and sentence structure says the compression is not a wrapper around the content but a rewriting of it. If that is right, the facet is more consequential than §7's packaging metaphor suggests, and the section could say so.

The embodiment claim also supports §10's implicit acquisition directly. Understandings that are learned and embodied within practice are precisely what instruction cannot transmit, and Bishop puts the claim in a population where the outcome — visibility, and therefore income — is measurable in principle.

## Caution

A hypothesis-driven interpretive article, and the author frames the second claim as a hypothesis rather than a finding; the abstract does not report a method for establishing that word choice tracks algorithmic belief. One genre, one platform, one national context. The inequality argument is normatively committed and this paper's use should stay descriptive. Read at abstract depth.
