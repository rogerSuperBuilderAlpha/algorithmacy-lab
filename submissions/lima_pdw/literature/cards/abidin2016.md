## What it argues

Abidin names **visibility labour**: "the work enacted to flexibly demonstrate gradients of self-conspicuousness in digital or physical spaces depending on intention or circumstance for favourable ends." More concretely, "the work individuals do when they self-posture and curate their self-presentations so as to be noticeable and positively prominent among prospective employers, clients, the press, or followers and fans, among other audiences." It is also *tacit* labour, in her earlier phrase — "a collective practice of work that is understated and under-visibilized from being so thoroughly rehearsed that it appears as effortless and subconscious."

The setting is Singapore's Instagram influencer economy. Abidin draws on a corpus of over 173 interviews conducted between December 2012 and July 2013 with ethnographic fieldwork running December 2011 to December 2015, and focuses here on a subset: three influencers, twelve followers, and Instagram fieldwork from January 2015. Followers copy influencers' #OOTD posts, tags and reposts, producing unpaid advertising for the brands and free amplification for the influencers. Their strategies fall into three groups, the first of which is a straightforwardly technical literacy: knowing how to use hashtags, @mentions, usertags and geolocation tags, and knowing when to use them singly or in combination — several followers overloading posts with tags in a bid to be noticed.

## Relation to the argument

I went looking for the construct closest to the manuscript's counterpart position — self-presentation directed at another human through a system — and this is it. It is worth carding precisely because of how it fails to be algorithmacy, and the failure is one Abidin states herself.

Two positions are genuinely occupied. The follower self-presents; the influencer is a *specific, named human being* the follower is trying to reach, not a diffuse audience — a person with a feed, an attention budget and a decision about whom to notice. That is closer to a counterpart than anything in DeVito, Cotter, Klawitter or Bishop, where the other end is always an audience or a market.

And then the third position is explicitly not the algorithm. Abidin draws the line herself: "Unlike studies on algorithmic visibility (Bucher, 2012: 1164), visibility labour is concerned with analogue affective labour ordinary users perform to be noticed by prolific elite users." The system in the middle is Instagram's tagging affordances — a *channel* in §9's five-cell sort, and the paper says so. Nothing interprets the follower's intent, nothing interprets the influencer's, and nothing commits a determination binding on either. The follower who tags well is heard; the follower who tags badly is not. That is a delivery problem, not a coordination problem.

The discrimination that survives is therefore sharp and useful: the field's one construct with a real human counterpart is the one construct that deliberately excludes the algorithm, and the field's constructs that centre the algorithm are the ones with no counterpart. Section 9 can state that as a finding about the literature rather than as an assertion — nobody has put both in at once. Tacit labour is also a small gift to §10: Abidin's "so thoroughly rehearsed that it appears effortless" is implicit acquisition described from the outside.

## Caution

Ethnographic and interpretive, no instrument and no distribution; the analytic subset is three influencers and twelve followers, and the 173-interview figure covers the wider project, not this article. Singapore's influencer ecology in 2012–2015, a small and unusually dense market with strong offline ties among participants — the counterpart relation here is quasi-personal in a way an Upwork client relation is not. Visibility labour is an unpaid-labour and exploitation argument first; using it as a competence construct is my framing, not Abidin's, and the manuscript should say so if it cites the card this way.
