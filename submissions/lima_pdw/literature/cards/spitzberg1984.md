## What it argues

The book that consolidated the construct for the communication discipline. Spitzberg and Cupach survey the competing conceptualisations — competence as skill, as impression, as trait, as state, as outcome — and propose the **relational competence model**: competence is not a property of a person but a quality of a relationship, produced jointly, judged by both parties, and specific to the episode and relationship in which it occurs. Its two criteria are **appropriateness** (the interaction respected the situation's norms and the partner's face) and **effectiveness** (goals were achieved). The generative apparatus is knowledge, motivation, and skill: a competent performance requires that the actor know what to do, want to do it, and be able to do it.

They are also explicit that competence resides at multiple levels simultaneously — molecular behaviours, episodes, relationships — and that measurement at one level licenses no inference at another.

## Relation to the argument

The paper's named warrant in §8, and the citation holds. Three things it supplies that the current text does not use.

**Appropriateness and effectiveness are the criteria a scale would need.** §7's three facets describe what a worker does; they do not state what counts as doing it well. Spitzberg and Cupach's pair supplies the missing evaluative axis and would give §11's instrument something to validate against.

**Relational location is the closest prior warrant for §9's structural claim.** If competence is a property of the relation rather than the person, then changing the relation's *shape* — from two positions to three — changes the construct rather than merely its setting. That is exactly what §9 asserts, and Spitzberg and Cupach's own framework implies it. The paper is entitled to say its structural diagnosis follows from its warrant rather than opposing it.

**Multi-level specification is a constraint on §11.** Their insistence that competence lives at several levels at once, with no free inference between them, is the disciplinary form of §8's "the unit of measurement is the person, and the unit of comparison is the form."

## Caution

Relational location is also a problem the paper inherits. If competence belongs to the relation, and the worker's relation is with a system rather than with the counterpart, a strict reading of Spitzberg and Cupach makes algorithmacy a property of the worker–system relation — which is precisely what §9 says the five constructs wrongly measure. The paper needs to say why it takes the person as the bearer while claiming Spitzberg and Cupach as warrant. A 1984 monograph with limited digital availability; the 1989 Springer chapter and the 2015 De Gruyter handbook chapters are the accessible restatements and should be read alongside it.
