## What it argues

Ferguson, Yonge and Myrick take up the specific case the general insider literature skirts: faculty recruiting their own students as research participants. They set out where the coercion lives — consent sought by someone holding grading power, the difficulty of declining without cost, the reputational stakes of what a student says about a course — and they name the practical protections, chief among them separating the person who obtains consent from the person who assesses the student, and timing disclosure so that refusal cannot be observed by anyone with power over the participant.

## Relation to the argument

This is the citation §8 of the protocol needs and does not have. Every element of the consent design — a third party outside the programme holds consent and receives withdrawals, the investigator does not learn who declined until grades are final, no compensation — is a published recommendation rather than an improvisation, and pointing at the source turns the passage from an assurance into a compliance argument. It also supplies the reason the design refuses compensation, which otherwise looks like an oversight.

## Caution

*IJQM* is a low-status venue and the setting is nursing education. It is guidance, not evidence: it will not show that the protections worked, only that they are the recognised ones.
