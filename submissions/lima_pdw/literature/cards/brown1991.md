## What it argues

Brown and Duguid open with an ethnographic finding — Julian Orr's study of Xerox photocopier technicians — and draw an organizational lesson from it. The way people actually work differs fundamentally from the way organizations describe that work in manuals, training programmes, org charts, and job descriptions, and organizations nonetheless manage from the descriptions. Their distinction is between **canonical practice**, what the documentation says the job is, and **non-canonical practice**, what the technicians actually do: narrating breakdowns to each other, building a collective stock of war stories, improvising diagnoses the manual does not contain. The directive documentation is not merely incomplete; it actively obscures where the competence lives. Learning, working, and innovating are then the same activity seen from three angles, and they all happen in the informal community, which is why formal training and formal process design keep failing to capture them.

## Relation to the argument

This is the source that converts §10's claim from an assertion about learning into an argument an organization theorist will recognize, and it is the closest prior statement of the paper's own position. Brown and Duguid already say that what makes a worker effective cannot be conveyed by the manual, and that the gap is structural rather than a documentation failure to be fixed. The paper needs to state clearly what it adds, and the addition is sharp: in Orr's Xerox, the canonical description was inadequate but the machine's behaviour was stable and the community's war stories accumulated into a reliable resource. Under an undisclosed and adaptive rule, the war stories decay, because what worked last month is not evidence about this month. That is a difference in kind, not degree, and it is testable at the Hult site through §11's ledger of rule changes. The war-story mechanism also gives the paper a concrete prediction for its third study question — if noticing travels, it travels as narrative, and the interview protocol should look for stories rather than for rules.

## Caution

A theoretical synthesis built on someone else's ethnography of one occupation in one firm, and the technicians' setting has no algorithmic evaluator. Brown and Duguid's optimism about informal community as a solvent for organizational problems is exactly the reading Contu & Willmott (2003) attack, and the paper's distributive argument in §6 should not inherit it: an informal community that distributes war stories unevenly produces stratified fluency, which is §10's Property B, not a remedy for it. Abstract-level read; the canonical/non-canonical distinction is standard in the reception but verify Brown and Duguid's own wording before quoting.
