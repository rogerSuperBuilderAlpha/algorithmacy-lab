## What it argues
Citron documents what happens when public agencies replace human adjudication with automated decision systems, using cases in which benefits eligibility, terrorist watch-listing and similar determinations were made by software. Her finding is that automation quietly voids the procedural protections the decisions were supposed to carry. Notice fails, because the system produces an outcome without a statement of reasons a person could answer. The opportunity to be heard fails, because there is no decision-maker who exercised judgement and could be addressed. The right to a reasoned explanation fails, because the reasoning is embedded in code written by contractors, is treated as trade secret, and often encodes policy choices no rulemaking process ever considered — the programmer's translation of statute into logic becomes the operative rule without notice-and-comment. She calls the result a due-process vacuum, and argues that the existing doctrine, built around a human official who can be required to explain, does not reach it. Her constructive programme is *technological due process*: procedural and design safeguards suited to automated systems, including audit trails that record why each decision was reached, testing and validation regimes, disclosure of the rules encoded in the software, meaningful hearings before a human with authority to override, and treating the encoding of policy into code as rulemaking subject to public process.

## Relation to the argument
Citron gives the paper the legal-institutional statement of its main result, from a decade before the platform literature caught up. The rights she finds voided are exactly the constituents of a forum: notice, a hearing, a reasoned explanation, and an authority able to override. Her point is that automation removes them *structurally* — not through bad faith, and not in a way a more capable claimant repairs. The people in her cases were often represented and articulate; what they lacked was a party with the standing and the authority to answer. That is §5's claim, evidenced from administrative law.

Her remedies are also the cleanest catalogue of §6's institutional column, and they carry an argument the paper needs: every item is imposed on the system's operator, and the point of due process is that it does not depend on the claimant's capability. That is what makes it a guarantee rather than an opportunity. §6 can say that the institutional route is defined by this property — an arrangement that works for the least capable subject — which is a sharper way of drawing the division than "supplied by institutions."

## Caution
Read at abstract depth only; do not quote, and verify the pagination against the published volume before it enters a reference list, since Crossref has no record. The setting is US administrative law and *state* action, where constitutional due process applies. A platform is not a state actor and its workers hold no due-process right against it, so the transfer is normative and analogical rather than doctrinal — the paper must not imply that platform workers have the protections Citron is defending. The article predates modern machine learning; its systems are rule-based expert systems, and the opacity she describes is trade-secret opacity rather than model opacity. Citron and Pasquale (2014) is the extension to private scoring and is the better cite for that step.
