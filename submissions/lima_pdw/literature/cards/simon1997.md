## What it argues

Simon's foundational treatment of decision-making in formal organizations. Central to the manuscript's use: Simon rechristens Barnard's (1938) "zone of indifference" as the **zone of acceptance** — the range of directives a subordinate will follow without independently weighing their merits, because the subordinate has already, by joining and remaining in the organization, consented to accept authority within some bounded range. Push a directive past that boundary and the subordinate stops complying; the zone marks where formal hierarchical authority actually does its coordinating work, rather than authority being an unlimited command relation. Administrative Behavior situates this inside its larger apparatus of bounded rationality, decision premises, and the organization as a structure that channels individual choice.

## Relation to the argument

Four uses in PAPER.md, always paired with Weber (1978) or in Appendix A's Table A1, to define the Hierarchy coordination form: "An employee operates within an authority-governed zone of acceptance, where managerial direction secures alignment through formal standard operating procedures" (line 37); "institutional zone of acceptance" (line 513, Table A1); "Simon (1997) and Weber (1978) together locate the individual in hierarchy through an administrative zone of acceptance" (line 522); and the bureaucracy definition at line 181. Every use tracks the term precisely — "zone of acceptance" is Simon's own coinage, not a loose gloss — and the manuscript's framing (authority reaches the participant only within a bounded, consented range) matches the concept as documented in secondary treatments. No misattribution risk: this is squarely Simon's term, not Barnard's (the manuscript is right not to cite Barnard for it).

## Caution

Not verified against primary text — Tier 2 targeted check only, resting on convergent secondary summaries rather than the book itself. Two things a primary read would settle and that a reviewer could probe: (1) "formal standard operating procedures" (line 37) is worded closer to Cyert & March (1963, *A Behavioral Theory of the Firm*) than to Simon's own vocabulary of "programs" and "decision premises" — defensible as shorthand since the phrase sits in a joint citation with Weber, whose bureaucratic-rules apparatus does license it, but not verified as Simon's own term; (2) the manuscript's "authority-governed" framing correctly reflects that the zone of acceptance operates *within* hierarchy, but Simon's own argument is that the zone is partly self-imposed by the subordinate (a psychological disposition toward compliance), not purely authority-imposed — a nuance the manuscript's compressed gloss elides but does not misstate.
