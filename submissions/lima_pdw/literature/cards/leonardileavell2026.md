## What it argues

Leonardi and Leavell argue that AI tools which render uncertain futures in fine, realistic detail let laypeople believe the future is known, and that this belief strips authority from the experts whose job is to keep it open. They name the belief **"artificial certainty"** and define it twice. The abstract calls it "the illusion that complex future outcomes are definitively knowable, even though they are inherently uncertain" (p. 516). The introduction defines it on the output side, as "the production of representations that appear authoritative, concrete, and unambiguous despite depicting inherently uncertain phenomena" (p. 517). The two definitions are not the same thing — one is a perception, the other a property of what the tool emits — and the article moves between them without reconciling them.

The certainty belongs to the audience. The abstract's three practices "shape how laypeople perceive the certainty of a representation" (p. 516); artificial certainty creates "dangerous illusions of expertise for laypeople" (p. 517); the Ocean planners had never before met "such high lay certainty in the model" (p. 523). The planners at the enhancing site spoke of the tool with confidence, but the authors never say the planners were themselves deceived about the future. Ocean's planners kept reminding stakeholders that the model was provisional, and the stakeholders stopped listening: they "treated the outputs as binding forecasts and dismissed planners’ interpretive authority" (p. 537).

The experts in question are **process experts**, whom the article glosses as "professionals whose authority rests in their interpretive capacities" (p. 517). Planners are the case: "Planners do not make the plan; as process experts, they facilitate planning" (p. 520). Their expertise is defined as interpretive work: "process expertise can be seen as a form of interpretive work: It involves selecting, sequencing, qualifying, and presenting information so that others can act under uncertainty" (p. 518). It is "a specific kind of expertise rooted in the capacity to manage how uncertainty is represented, circulated, and acted upon across settings" (p. 518), exercised by "strategically calibrating levels of certainty so that depictions are concrete enough to enable action yet provisional enough to guard against overconfidence" (p. 517). The contribution statement in the Discussion repeats it: "process expertise is a distinct form of interpretive work, manifested through representational practices" (p. 539).

The technology is "an AI-powered simulation technology called UrbanSim, designed to predict the effects of policy choices 20 years into the future" (p. 521), whose constituent models were each "trained independently through machine learning" (p. 521). The authors also call it "AI-based urban planning simulation technology" (p. 519). It is not generative AI. One paragraph extends the concept by analogy to "generative AI systems, including large language models (LLMs)" (p. 519), which the authors say create "an illusory confidence in what we can know and how definitely we can know it" (p. 519); that paragraph reports no data.

The evidence is a comparative ethnography of two Metropolitan Planning Organizations, pseudonymised as Ocean and Mountain, "two planning organizations in the western United States" (p. 520), both adopting the same tool while drafting new 20-year regional plans. The authors report: "We conducted 110 interviews" (p. 521), of 45 minutes to 3 hours; "more than 72 hours of observation" (p. 521) of planners and modelers at work, internal meetings and public meetings; and "more than 1,200 pages of documents" (p. 521). They analysed these in four grounded-theory phases (pp. 522–523). The article gives no fieldwork dates. Stakeholders — elected officials, advocates, developers — are treated as laypeople by position: "What unified them analytically was not their power outside the process, but their position as laypeople within it" (p. 521).

Three recursively linked practices carry the findings: controlling the level of detail (pp. 525–528), shaping stakeholder engagement (pp. 528–531), and constructing the model's meaning (pp. 531–534). Ocean ran all three as *enhancement*. It hired modelers in-house, modelled to the parcel, admitted community groups into parameter-building, showed immersive 3D animations and spoke of what the model forecast. Mountain ran them as *modulation*. It contracted the modelling out, kept stakeholders to a visioning phase, showed charts and tables, and called the model a tool. The outcomes diverge sharply. In Table 4, 83% of 132 coded Ocean stakeholder statements about model outcomes were absolute, against 9% of 87 at Mountain (p. 535). The authors found "17 mentions of unique lawsuits" (p. 537) filed against Ocean since the tool's adoption and none at Mountain. At Ocean "authority migrated from planners to the model" (p. 537), and the authors judge that the conflict made "it impossible to develop a widely accepted regional plan" (p. 536); Mountain approved its plan (p. 537).

On cause, the article's settled position is that practice decides, working on what the tool affords: "artificial certainty is not an inherent property of AI tools but emerges from the ways in which process experts position and deploy them and their representations in organizational contexts" (p. 538). The tool supplies the raw material. The authors hold that "AI-powered tools now generate representations with unprecedented levels of detail, coherence, and apparent certainty while simultaneously black-boxing the processes that produce them" (p. 519), that such tools "remove visible markers of uncertainty" (p. 517), and that opacity raised the stakes of framing: "Because the technology’s inner workings remained opaque to most stakeholders" (p. 532), how planners described the tool decided how its outputs were read. Yet "the planners’ practices—not just the technology itself—proved consequential" (p. 538). The same tool produced opposite results at two sites.

The Discussion closes on two moves. It distinguishes representations *of* the future, which present it as known, from representations *for* the future, which serve as "scaffolds for deliberation" (p. 540), and holds that "Whether a representation is taken as of or for is not inherent to its technical design. It is enacted." (p. 540). And it refuses to crown Mountain: "Mountain achieved these outcomes by limiting democratic participation" (p. 539), while Ocean bought unprecedented engagement at the price of expert authority.

## Relation to the argument

**Partly supports the introduction's sentence. It supports the gist — AI output that looks certain while its workings stay hidden — and contradicts the wording on whose certainty it is, what the certainty is about, and how it arises.** The author's introduction cites the article for algorithmic black boxes "paradoxically manufacturing a false sense of epistemological security". The best single warrant is the sentence on p. 519, where opacity and apparent certainty arrive together: "AI-powered tools now generate representations with unprecedented levels of detail, coherence, and apparent certainty while simultaneously black-boxing the processes that produce them" (p. 519). The article also prints "black box" (p. 532), in the authors' own scare quotes, of this tool's algorithms, and describes AI systems "whose operations are often black-boxed yet produce highly polished outputs" (p. 539). So the pairing of black box and unearned certainty is in the text, and the abstract-depth card was wrong to say the article's cause is a practice and not opacity; the body names both.

Four things in the sentence are the author's and not the article's. The phrases "epistemological security" and "false sense" do not occur; the article's nearest equivalents are "an artificial sense of certainty" (p. 538), "an illusory confidence in what we can know and how definitely we can know it" (p. 519), and "an illusion of predictive authority that undermined planner expertise" (p. 523). The words "paradox" and "paradoxically" do not occur either; the article has an "epistemic dilemma" in "This creates an epistemic dilemma" (p. 519) and one irony, "The irony is that the more compelling the representation, the more difficult it becomes to see the expertise necessary to use it well." (p. 539). The certainty is the lay audience's and it concerns *future outcomes*, not the knower's grip on knowledge in general. And "manufacturing" by the black box overstates the tool's share: the Discussion says outright that artificial certainty "is not an inherent property of AI tools" (p. 538). The abstract-depth card had these last two points right and they stand.

What the article supports more directly is a sentence about expert authority and audience. A wording the text would carry: AI-generated representations can give lay audiences an illusion that uncertain futures are knowable, and whether they do depends on how experts stage them (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). That is a claim about staged output and a third party's belief, which is a different claim from the one about reduced visibility that Hinds and von Krogh carry in the same sentence.

**The arrangement has three parties, but the intermediary is the expert, not the AI.** This corrects the abstract-depth card, which described "an AI representation standing between" expert and stakeholder. The article does study three parties and says so twice: its third coding phase examined "the relationships between the planners, lay people, and the model" (p. 523), and the Discussion speaks of "the representational relationship between model, expert, and future" (p. 541). It never calls this a triad; the word does not occur. Its mediation vocabulary attaches to the human: "the mediating role of process experts—professionals whose authority rests in their interpretive capacities" (p. 517); planners as "mediators between what is knowable and unknowable" (p. 517), as "essential intermediaries whose expertise was necessary for translating abstract aspirations into concrete plans" (p. 536), as "interpretive intermediaries" (p. 537) and "epistemic intermediaries" (p. 539). The AI is what threatens to cut the intermediary out — "threatening to bypass such interpretive calibration, presenting information with uniform confidence regardless of underlying uncertainty" (p. 517). The one place AI takes the mediating verb is general: "how process expertise is performed when work is mediated by AI" (p. 539). The algorithmacy triad puts the algorithm in the middle and asks what the two humans need. This article puts a human in the middle and asks how she keeps her place. The two geometries are related and they are not the same.

**The model does come to evaluate and bind, by attribution.** At Ocean, community members saw the technology "being treated as the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533); a stakeholder treated the model "not just as a calculator but as a referee whose outputs carried persuasive authority" (p. 534); stakeholders "treated the outputs as binding forecasts and dismissed planners’ interpretive authority" (p. 537) and acted on them through lawsuits, lobbying and permit applications. This is the article's strongest material for the OT paper: an algorithmic output acquiring adjudicating force between parties. Two limits apply. The model adjudicates between scenarios and visions of a region; it does not score, rank or sanction persons. And its binding force is conferred by the humans who cite it, which is the article's whole point, so it cannot be cited for an algorithm that binds by design.

**The nearest thing to the paper's construct is a competence of the expert in the middle, and the article calls it expertise.** Keeping uncertainty alive requires, in the authors' words, "representational reflexivity": "sustaining process expertise in such environments depends on representational reflexivity" (p. 539). They spell out the content: "Experts must actively curate how models are introduced, how representations are built, framed, and interpreted, and how uncertainty is preserved." (p. 539). Mountain's practice shows "a neglected dimension of expertise: the ability to configure not only what is known but how knowing is made possible and by whom" (p. 539), enacted by "limiting granularity, softening epistemic claims, and foregrounding the model’s contingency" (p. 538) and by making interpretation visible: "Mountain planners worked to make visible their interpretive work—triangulating model outputs against other sources of knowledge, highlighting underlying assumptions, and explicitly addressing limitations." (p. 533). The article ends on the claim that "knowing how to mindfully curate uncertainty may be our most valuable expertise" (p. 541), and that organizations which treat representations as tools for exploration preserve deliberation on one condition: "But they do so only if process experts maintain their authority to keep uncertainty alive." (p. 541). The authors use "expertise", "ability", "capacity", "practices" and "strategies" for this. They do not use "skill", "competence", "literacy" or "sensibility" for it. The competence sits with the professional who stages the output for others. The article says nothing on what the lay party must be able to do, and its lay parties are the ones who lack it. A draft that borrows "keep uncertainty alive" for algorithmacy should say that it transfers an expert's representational competence to the coordinating parties themselves, a move the article does not make.

## Caution

Full-text read; quotes above are verbatim and carry printed pages. Six cautions govern use.

First, the two definitions differ (pp. 516, 517). Quote the one that fits the claim and give its page; do not splice them.

Second, modulation at Mountain is not shown to be a deliberate skill. The authors say Mountain "had intuitively solved the theoretical puzzle" (p. 523); its planners acted "With limited exposure to the technology themselves" (p. 531); and the Discussion traces both trajectories to early structural choices, in-house modellers against outside consultants and early against late stakeholder involvement (pp. 538–539). The text does say "Enabling this separation required restraint by planners" (p. 530). Cite modulation as a practice with a structural origin, and "representational reflexivity" as the authors' normative inference from it.

Third, the article does not endorse Mountain. Modulation worked by restricting participation (p. 539), and the practitioner advice asks for calibration between the two approaches, for instance "pairing high-detail visuals with explicit narration of assumptions and limitations" (p. 541). Do not cite the article as showing that withholding detail is good practice.

Fourth, scope. Two U.S. regional planning agencies, one simulation tool, stakeholders treated as laypeople regardless of their outside power. It is not a study of generative AI, platforms or algorithmic management; "generative" appears in one analogical paragraph (p. 519) and the word "platform" is not used of the technology. The tool does not adapt to or evaluate its users in the sense the OT paper means; it changed because planners and stakeholders added parameters to it.

Fifth, small reporting inconsistencies, none load-bearing. The text reports 110 interviews (p. 521) while Table 1's three "Total interviews" cells sum to 115 (55 Ocean, 55 Mountain, 5 other; p. 522). Table 1's observation totals sum to 72 hours and its document totals to 1,215 pages (p. 522), consistent with the text. The text announces "five common areas of concern" for Table 4 and lists four (p. 535). Site names shift to "OceanPlan" and "MountainPlan" on p. 537. Table 4 reports proportions and Ns only; the article gives no coding reliability for the absolute/provisional split.

Sixth, anonymity. The sites and participants are pseudonymised, but one block quotation on p. 533 prints a real person's name and an agency acronym. This card does not repeat them and no draft should.
