## What it argues

Lave and Wenger reject the picture of learning as the transmission of a body of knowledge from an instructor to a head. Learning is instead a change in a person's relation to a community of practitioners, and its unit is not the individual mind but the *community of practice*. Their name for the process is **legitimate peripheral participation**: a newcomer is admitted to genuine but low-stakes work at the edge of a practice, and competence grows as she moves toward fuller participation. The evidence is a set of apprenticeship cases — Yucatec midwives, Vai and Gola tailors, U.S. Navy quartermasters, meat cutters, and Alcoholics Anonymous — chosen because in most of them there is no teaching at all. The tailors' apprentices learn by making, in reverse order of production difficulty, without a curriculum. What organizes learning is not a *teaching* curriculum but a **learning curriculum**: the field of participation the community makes available to a newcomer. Two further moves matter. Learning is inseparable from **identity**, since becoming competent is becoming a certain kind of person in a community; and the community is not a harmonious whole, since the book explicitly names the tension between continuity and displacement — newcomers eventually replace old-timers, and that conflict is internal to learning, not an accident.

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper's largest uncited debt. §10's implicit-acquisition property — "algorithmacy develops through participation in mediated coordination and not through instruction" — restates the central thesis of *Situated Learning* almost term for term, and a reviewer who knows the book will read §10 as reinventing it. Citing Lave and Wenger costs nothing and buys the paper a forty-year lineage for what is otherwise asserted. The citation must be made as a *departure* rather than an alignment, because the case algorithmacy names breaks the book's central mechanism in a specific and interesting place. Legitimate peripheral participation presupposes that competence exists somewhere in the community and that the newcomer moves toward it; the old-timer is the destination. Under an undisclosed, unilaterally revisable rule there is no old-timer who holds the answer, because the party that holds it is not a member of the community at all. The Hult site sharpens this: the gate's votes are private and the rule changes because twenty-nine peers change it, so there is no stable master practice to approach. That is a genuine theoretical contribution, and it is only visible against Lave and Wenger. §11's third study question — whether noticing travels through the cohort — is a direct test of whether the community-of-practice mechanism operates at all here.

## Caution

An anthropological argument built from five apprenticeship cases, none of them mediated by an opaque evaluative system, so every extension to platform coordination is the paper's own. The book is also the most over-cited text in organizational learning, and citing it loosely reads as ritual; cite it for the specific mechanism (learning curriculum, movement from periphery to full participation) and name the mechanism that fails. Read depth here is metadata plus reception, not the book — verify page-level claims, and especially the continuity/displacement passage, before quoting. Note the widespread misreading that the book coined "community of practice" as a manageable organizational unit; Lave and Wenger use it as an analytic category, and Contu & Willmott (2003) document how the managerial reading displaced the original.
