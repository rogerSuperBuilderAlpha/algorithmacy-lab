## What it argues

Brown and Duguid take up a contradiction in the knowledge literature: knowledge is described as **sticky**, refusing to move where firms want it, and as **leaky**, escaping to competitors who should not have it. Their diagnosis is that the tacit/explicit taxonomy cannot resolve this, because stickiness and leakiness are not properties of knowledge types but of the relation between knowledge and *practice*. Knowledge flows easily along lines of shared practice and sticks at the boundaries between practices, which is why it leaks to a competitor's engineers while refusing to travel between two departments of the same firm. They then argue that the field has attended too much to the idea of *community* and too little to *practice*: practice is what creates epistemic difference between communities, and the firm's advantage over the market lies in dynamically coordinating knowledge across those differences rather than in owning it.

## Relation to the argument

The single most useful card in this cluster for §8's "not a capability" argument, and the paper does not currently have it. Brown and Duguid make a firm-level knowledge argument that is explicitly *not* a possession argument: what the firm has is a coordinating relation across epistemic difference, not a stock. That gives §8 a way to hold its ground against the obvious objection — that "capability" has non-possessive readings — while conceding the strongest version of the other side. Their leakiness thesis also produces a live prediction for the Hult study: knowledge of what the gate rewards should flow easily among participants who share a build practice and stick between those who do not, which is a mechanism for stratified fluency (§10, Property B) that has nothing to do with individual difference. That matters, because §10 as written leaves the source of the stratification unspecified, and a reviewer will ask. The stickiness/leakiness pair also disciplines §8's insistence that "the party owns nothing": on this account nobody owns knowledge anywhere, and ownership is the wrong frame for the contrast the paper wants.

## Caution

A conceptual article, argued from illustrative cases including Xerox and Silicon Valley, with no new data. Its target is the knowledge-management literature of the late 1990s, so its framing of the debate dates; the tacit/explicit critique it advances is now common ground. The move from "community" to "practice" is programmatic and Brown and Duguid do not operationalize epistemic difference, so a study that wants to observe stickiness at a boundary has to build its own measure. Abstract-level read; verify the exact formulation of the coordination-across-difference claim before quoting it into §8.
