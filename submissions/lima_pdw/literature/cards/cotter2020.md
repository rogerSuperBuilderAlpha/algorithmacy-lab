## What it argues

Cotter and Reisdorf make two moves. Conceptually, they separate bare awareness from algorithmic knowledge proper: awareness "provides a foundation on which to build an understanding of the criteria by which algorithms rank content," while "more advanced algorithmic knowledge includes insight about the principles and methods of software development that underlie algorithms and/or the social and political effects of algorithms" (p. 747). Then they argue that algorithms are **experience technologies** — more easily understood through use than through instruction — which makes algorithmic knowledge doubly stratified: socioeconomic position shapes use, and use shapes knowledge.

Empirically, they analyse an online survey of a stratified random probability sample of U.S. internet users, final N = 2,018 (52.6% women; mean age 44.7, SD 17.6). Their dependent variable is improvised rather than purpose-built: a principal components score over six items asking how much influence various factors have on search results — site popularity, the respondent's location, past search history, whether a site has optimised its visibility, relevance to search terms, and advertising or sponsorship fees paid to the engine. One component emerged, explaining 58.3% of shared variance. Education predicted algorithmic knowledge positively (β = .11, SE = .03, p < .001) and age negatively (β = −.12, SE = .002, p < .001); gender and race/ethnicity did not predict it. Socioeconomic factors alone explained only 9.2% of the variance, which the authors flag themselves as leaving most of the variation unaccounted for.

## Relation to the argument

The single most useful empirical card in this sub-cluster for §10, for a reason the authors treat as a limitation. Their own model absorbs 9.2% of the variance in algorithmic knowledge. More than nine-tenths of the spread among 2,018 people sits outside socioeconomic background and search ability. That is the residual §10 needs against the objection raised by `gran2021.md` — that stratified fluency is the digital divide relabelled. Cotter and Reisdorf's data say the divide explains a sliver and something else explains the rest, which is precisely the room a competency acquired through participation would occupy.

The experience-technology claim is a second gift, and it is close to §10's implicit-acquisition property stated in different vocabulary: knowledge of algorithms accrues through use because it cannot accrue any other way. Cite it as prior support, not as a rival — Cotter and Reisdorf offer it as a mechanism for a knowledge gap, not as a property of a competency, and they draw no conclusion about instruction being unavailable in principle.

The counterpart is absent and the setting makes clear how far absent. This is knowledge about how a search engine ranks results — a person, a query, a ranked list. Nothing is committed, nobody else is bound, and there is no second party whose interest the respondent must infer.

## Caution

**Title correction:** the subtitle is "A New **Dimension** of (Digital) Inequality," not "A New Horizon." The running head on the published PDF and the article's title page both read Dimension. Several secondary sources have it wrong; the manuscript should not.

The measure is a repurposed six-item battery from a survey not designed to study algorithms, which the authors say openly; it taps beliefs about search-ranking factors, not knowledge scored against a criterion, and it should never be described as a validated algorithmic-knowledge scale. Search engines only — no social feed, no evaluative gate, no work. Cross-sectional, so the experience-to-knowledge direction is argued rather than shown. IJoC assigns no DOIs and its OJS metadata gives page 21 as a placeholder; the real range 745–765 comes from the running head on the article's first page. One caution on framing: their 9.2% is useful to the manuscript only if the manuscript also concedes that Cotter and Reisdorf's instrument is weak enough that measurement error could account for a good deal of the unexplained variance.
