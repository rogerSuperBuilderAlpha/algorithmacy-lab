## What it argues

Fradkin, Grewal and Holtz report a large-scale field experiment on Airbnb that changed one thing: when a review becomes visible. In the control group a review was revealed as soon as it was submitted, so the second party could read the first party's review before writing its own. In the treatment group feedback was hidden until both parties had submitted a review. The abstract reports three results. The treatment stimulated more reviewing in total, which the authors attribute to curiosity about what the counterparty wrote and/or to the wish to have one's own feedback visible to other users. It reduced retaliation and reciprocation in feedback, and ratings fell as a result. The effects on feedback did not translate into reduced adverse selection on the platform.

The abstract calls the mechanism hiding feedback until both parties have submitted. It does not, in the text I reached, use the phrase "simultaneous reveal," which is the platform's and the trade press's name for the policy; the paper's own title word is "unveiling." The number of listings, hosts or guests in the experiment, the size of the rating decline, and the measure of adverse selection are in the body of the paper and were not read.

## Relation to the argument

For `jms_organizing_truth_arbiter` §5, this is the field evidence that the arbiter's record is produced under rules the arbiter can change and that the parties' reports change with them. When each party can read the other's verdict before writing its own, the record carries reciprocation and retaliation; when the platform withholds both verdicts until both exist, the record carries fewer of either and the ratings go down. The second rule removes a channel through which each party could anticipate the other, which is the "anticipatory report" condition in §3, and the abstract's last sentence is the limit of the counter-measure: the record became less reciprocal without the market becoming better sorted. The paper therefore supports simultaneous reveal as a change to the record, not as a repair of what the record is for.

For `sij_service_triad_ethics` §4, the "reciprocal reveal" mechanism should be named and described from this abstract: the verdict is returned to both parties at the same moment, and the price of that return is a lower average rating and no measured gain in sorting. The arm's claim that this mechanism returns the verdict to both parties at once is consistent with the design; the arm should not add that it makes the verdict more accurate, because the abstract says the opposite about adverse selection.

The triad is exact: host, guest and a platform that holds both reviews and decides when each may be read. The counterpart's review is the only thing in the experiment that the platform changes, and the rule change is made by the platform alone.

## Caution

Abstract only. The design and the three results above are the whole of what was read; every number, including the size of the rating decline, must be taken from the article before it is quoted. The INFORMS record gives the page range as 1013–1029, where the arm's working citation has 1013–1031; check against the PDF. Two traps: the 2015 NBER and EC conference paper "Bias and Reciprocity in Online Reviews" by Fradkin, Grewal, Holtz and Pearson is an earlier four-author study that the authors' site says this paper supersedes, and should not be cited as this one; and an INFORMS press release describes the authors as Airbnb employees at the time of the experiment, which the paper's own disclosure statement should be read for before it is repeated. The lab precursor is Bolton, Greiner and Ockenfels (2013), carded at `bolton2013.md` and, from the 2009 discussion paper, at `dxd_foundation/literature/library/bolton2013engineering.md`.
