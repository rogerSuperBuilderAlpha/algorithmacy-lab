# Wood, A. J., & Lehdonvirta, V. (2021). Antagonism beyond employment: How the "subordinated agency" of labour platforms generates conflict in the remote gig economy. *Socio-Economic Review*, 19(4), 1369–1396.

## What it argues
Wood and Lehdonvirta ask where conflict comes from when there is no employment relation to generate it, and answer with *subordinated agency*: platform workers are formally independent, hold real discretion over how and when they work, and are simultaneously subordinated to a party that sets the terms of exchange and controls access to it. That combination, not employment status, is what produces antagonism. They identify the specific structural antagonisms — over the terms of exchange, over the platform's fees and rules, and over the evaluation and account systems that determine who gets work — and they show these generate grievances that have no institutional destination. Their empirical contribution is the study of what workers do instead: they document online forums and communities where remote gig workers pool information, interpret platform rules for one another, coordinate mutual support, and occasionally organise collective action. The authors trace how a diffuse population with no workplace, no employer and no union nonetheless produces collective identity and collective claims, and they analyse what limits it — the platform's control of the communication infrastructure, the competitive relation among workers, and the absence of any body obliged to receive a collective claim.

## Relation to the argument
This is the best available evidence for §6's institutional route emerging from below in the population the paper is about, and it should sit alongside Turkopticon as the second case of collective construction. Wood and Lehdonvirta show workers building the forum function — interpretation, mutual account-giving, pooled theory — and they show precisely where it stops: no counterpart is obliged to receive what the forum produces. The condition the collective supplies is common understanding, which §5 already places on the supply-able side; the condition it does not supply is accountability, which is what §5 predicts.

Subordinated agency also gives the paper a published construct for the standing problem it inherits from the companion review. Discretion without standing is the shape both papers describe, and Wood and Lehdonvirta name it in a socio-economics journal a reviewer will know.

Their finding that grievances have no destination is the structural statement behind the live interview case: the participant's problem was not that she lacked a place to talk — she had two — but that no place existed where the grievance would be received as a claim.

## Caution
Remote gig work on freelancing platforms, studied through interviews and online-community data; the collective phenomena are documented as occurring, not as effective, and the authors are careful that most of it produces solidarity rather than outcomes. Their frame is socio-economic conflict theory, and individual competency plays no role — the pooling they document is explicitly collective, which cuts against reading interpretive capacity as an individually held competency and is a point §10's stratified fluency must answer. The setting is remote work; place-based platform labour differs, and the paper should not merge them.
