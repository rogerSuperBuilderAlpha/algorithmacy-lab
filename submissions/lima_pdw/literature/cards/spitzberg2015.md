## What it argues

Spitzberg's mature synthesis of what a communication skill actually is, after thirty years of the field producing skill lists that do not agree. He argues the lists are incommensurable because they mix levels — molecular behaviours, molar patterns, and evaluative attributions — and proposes an ordering: skills are repeatable, goal-oriented behaviours or behavioural sequences; competence is the *impression* that those skills were appropriate and effective; and the mediating machinery is motivation, knowledge, and skill interacting with the context. He then surveys the empirical skill taxonomies and shows how many collapse onto four recurrent factors (attentiveness, composure, coordination, expressiveness).

The companion chapter in the same handbook, "Assessing the state of assessment" (doi:10.1515/9783110317459-023), catalogues the measurement problem.

## Relation to the argument

The authoritative modern statement of the skill/competence distinction §8 needs. §8 distinguishes them on trainability and tool-indexing, which is a defensible cut but not the field's cut; Spitzberg's is level plus attribution, and the paper is claiming his lineage. Aligning the two — or explicitly declining his and saying why — is cheap and forecloses the obvious reviewer objection that the paper has borrowed a lineage's name without its distinctions.

The four recurrent factors are also a useful negative check. None of attentiveness, composure, coordination, or expressiveness survives the removal of a co-present partner, which is one more line of evidence for §9's structural claim, and evidence drawn from a synthesis of the whole skills literature rather than from one scale.
