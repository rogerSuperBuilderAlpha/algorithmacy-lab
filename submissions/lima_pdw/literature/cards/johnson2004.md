# Johnson, J. D. (2004). The emergence, maintenance, and dissolution of structural hole brokerage within consortia. *Communication Theory*, 14(3), 212–236.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/j.1468-2885.2004.tb00312.x (Crossref also carries 10.1093/ct/14.3.212 for the same article)  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Oxford Academic
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Johnson brings structural-hole brokerage into communication theory proper and asks what a broker has to *do* communicatively to hold the position. He traces a life cycle — emergence, maintenance, dissolution — and argues that maintenance is the hard part: a broker must actively manage information flow to keep the parties from connecting directly, while remaining useful enough to both that neither routes around him. Dissolution comes when the parties develop a direct tie or when the broker's interpretive work becomes visible as interested.

## Relation to the argument

**The best answer available to the question "has anyone theorised communication competence for a three-party arrangement?" — and it answers on the wrong side.** Johnson theorises the *broker's* communicative competence: what the third party must do to occupy and hold the position. Nobody in this line theorises the competence of the party on the far side of the broker, which is what algorithmacy is. That asymmetry is the paper's opening, and it is much more precise than saying the literature is dyadic, because Johnson's literature plainly is not.

His maintenance analysis also predicts platform behaviour with some accuracy. Disintermediation risk, opacity as a maintenance strategy, the need to remain useful to both sides while preventing their direct connection — this is a communication-theoretic account of why an intermediary would withhold exactly the conditions §5 says are withheld, published in *Communication Theory* in 2004, before platforms.

## Caution

Conceptual, illustrated by research consortia, with no data. Johnson's broker is a person or organisation acting strategically, and the analysis assumes the brokered parties could connect directly if they chose — which is a weaker constraint than the platform imposes. **Watch the duplicate Crossref records and the author-name variants** (J. D. Johnson / J. David Johnson) — both DOIs resolve to the same article.
