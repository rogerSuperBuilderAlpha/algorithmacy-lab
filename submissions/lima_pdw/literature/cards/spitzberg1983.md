# Spitzberg, B. H. (1983). Communication competence as knowledge, skill, and impression. *Communication Education*, 32(3), 323–329.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/03634528309378550  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Taylor & Francis
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Spitzberg's early statement of the position he spends the next forty years elaborating: competence is neither knowledge alone nor skill alone nor the partner's evaluation alone, but a relation among all three. Knowledge is what a person understands about what to do; skill is the repeatable capacity to do it; **impression** is the judgement of quality formed by the other party, and competence is properly an *attribution* rather than a possession. He argues the field's disputes dissolve once the three levels are kept apart — a person can be knowledgeable and unskilled, skilled and judged incompetent, or judged competent on a performance she could not repeat.

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper's warrant one step earlier than Spitzberg & Cupach (1984), and it is more explicit about the awkward part: **competence is an attribution made by someone**. That is the premise that makes §9's triad diagnosis bite. If competence is an impression formed by a counterpart, then removing the counterpart from contact does not merely change the setting — it removes the party whose judgement constitutes the construct, and hands the judgement to a system. The paper's claim that the five constructs "drop the counterpart" is strongest when stated against Spitzberg's own definitional commitment, since he is the source the paper cites for the competence framing.

The knowledge/skill/impression triad also gives §8's table a cleaner defence. The paper distinguishes competency from skill on trainability and tool-indexing; Spitzberg distinguishes them on level, which is more standard in this literature and compatible with §8's version.

## Caution

Six pages in a pedagogy journal, and Spitzberg's own later work (the relational competence model, the 2015 handbook chapters) supersedes it in detail. The impression component was later formalised as the appropriateness/effectiveness pair and should be cited from the later work if that pairing is what the paper needs.
