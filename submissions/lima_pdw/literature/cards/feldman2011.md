## What it argues

Feldman and Orlikowski state the commitments of a practice lens in one place. They distinguish three ways of engaging practice — an **empirical** focus on what people actually do, a **theoretical** concern with the relation between everyday action and larger structures, and a **philosophical** claim that social reality is brought into being through practice — and treat the third as the strongest. The shared premises they name are that situated action is consequential in the production of social life, that the familiar dualisms (structure and agency, mind and body, the social and the material, ostensive and performative) are better recast as mutually constitutive relations, and that relations and enactment rather than pre-formed entities are where organizational phenomena come from. The reflexive half argues that theorizing is itself a practice.

## Relation to the argument

Useful to this cluster for one disciplinary reason: it tells the paper which register it is speaking in when §10 says algorithmacy develops through participation. On the *empirical* reading the claim is that people in fact learn this way and a study could show otherwise. On the *theoretical* reading it is a claim about how individual practice relates to the coordination form. On the *philosophical* reading the competency does not exist apart from its enactment, which would make §8's table row about what the competency is "held by" a category error. §10 currently slides between the first and the third, and the falsifier it offers ("if a cohort taught the system explicitly scores as well…") only makes sense on the empirical reading. ## Caution

A programmatic essay, not an empirical study, and one whose ecumenism lets a reader adopt "practice" as a slogan without taking an ontological position — the fault the essay itself warns against. Its relational, dualism-dissolving stance is contested by critical realists.
