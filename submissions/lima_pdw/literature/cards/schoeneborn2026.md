## What it argues

Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl reduce organizationality to one criterion, collective actorhood, and make that criterion a matter of degree. The abstract defines the construct as "a gradual notion of organization, understood as a quality that social collectives may possess to a higher or lower degree" (p. 1), and the introduction credits the gradual idea to earlier work that "conceptualizes organizationality as an attribute of social phenomena that can vary in degree" (p. 2). The term is not new here: "The term “organizationality” itself was initially introduced by Dobusch and Schoeneborn (2015) as a way of accounting for new and fluid forms of organizing that do not necessarily match traditional notions of formal organization" (p. 3). What the 2026 paper adds is an "axial logic" (p. 2) that replaces the configurational logic of Dobusch and Schoeneborn (2015) and the stage-based logic of Grothe-Hammer (2019), and a mechanism for movement along the axis. The purpose is to solve what the authors call the "openness-specificity dilemma" (p. 13): a concept of organization narrow enough to mean something and open enough to cover fluid forms.

The mechanism is attribution. The authors "define collective actorhood as emerging through the attribution of agency to a collective social address" (p. 3), and they state the framework in two steps: "First, just like individuals, collectives can have a social address, enabling them to become the object of attributed characteristics. Second, one such attributed characteristic is collective actorhood: observers ascribe to the collective the capacity to act on its own volition" (p. 7). "Social address" is borrowed, not coined: "we draw on the sociological notion of the “social address” (e.g., Drepper, 2005; Fuchs, 2005; Luhmann, 1995), a term that has also been picked up in earlier works on organizationality (e.g., Dobusch & Schoeneborn, 2015; Grothe-Hammer, 2019)" (p. 8), and the definition they adopt is Bommes and Tacke's. Having an address does not confer actorhood; "a collective social address could be simply a #hashtag holding together an online community (Albu & Etter, 2016), in which case one would not necessarily assume that actorhood resides with the collective" (p. 8). Attribution practices "charge" the collective address with actorhood, and practices that attribute action to individuals "discharge" it, so that "Organizationality can therefore be understood as varying in degree according to the extent to which a collective is “charged” with actorhood" (p. 12). Three things govern the charge: "(a) who attributes actorhood; (b) the recurrence and history of actorhood attributions; and (c) how consistent or contested these actorhood attributions are" (p. 9).

The paper reports no new data. Figure 1 (p. 7) carries the framework as a sliding bar between an individual and a collective social address, and the authors illustrate each practice from published studies: anthropomorphizing metaphors, legal personhood and accusations of corporate social irresponsibility charge the collective (pp. 10–11); scapegoating and leader heroization discharge it (p. 11), with Volkswagen's Dieselgate serving both sides. There are no numbered propositions and no table. The paper closes on four research questions (pp. 13–14).

## Relation to the argument

The paper gives the algorithmacy argument a graded, attribution-based account of when a *collective* counts as an actor — and so a way to say that a platform's standing as an actor is a matter of degree that observers confer and withdraw — but it does not attribute organizationality to an algorithm or an algorithmic intermediary, and the draft's two citing sentences currently say it does.

**(a) Intro: "As traditional firm boundaries dissolve, these algorithmic intermediaries operate with gradual *organizationality*, functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood."** Not supported as written. Three parts fail separately. First, the bearer: the construct is "a quality that social collectives may possess" (p. 1), and the decisive sentence is "a collective becomes organizational when actorhood is attributed to its social address" (p. 11). The words "algorithm," "algorithmic," "AI" and "intermediary" do not occur in the article. Second, the framing clause: the paper says nothing about firm boundaries dissolving. Boundaries appear as a classic criterion of formal organization that the narrow camp wants reinstated (p. 2) and as an aspect of identity claims (pp. 3, 6); the paper's own closing phrase is "societal phenomena beyond the boundaries of formal organization" (p. 14), which widens the field's scope and makes no claim about firms. Third, "functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood" runs two things together that the authors keep apart — an address is the referent, actorhood is what observers may or may not attribute to it (the #hashtag passage, p. 8).

**(b) Section 2: "these algorithmic intermediaries increasingly operate with "gradual organizationality" (Schoeneborn et al., 2026), functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood."** Not supported, and the quotation marks are wrong. The exact phrase "gradual organizationality" does not occur anywhere in the article, including across line breaks. The nearest wordings are "a gradual notion of organization" (p. 1), "a gradual understanding of organizationality" (p. 11) and "degrees of organizationality" (passim). "Distinct social address" does not occur either. "Increasingly" has no warrant: the authors posit movement in both directions, "temporal dynamics of increasing and decreasing organizationality over time" (p. 3).

**What the paper does say about platforms.** Two mentions, both treating platforms as organizational forms, neither treating an algorithm as an actor. The introduction lists "platform-based, network-based, and community-based forms of organization" (p. 2) among the phenomena that the broad camp says narrow definitions miss. The discussion says attribution practices explain "how new organizational forms, such as digital platforms, rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13). That second passage cuts against the draft's wording: on the authors' account platforms work to *lower* the actorhood attributed to them. A third passage points the same way — "a high degree of digitalization can impede anthropomorphization and thus also attributions of collective actorhood" (p. 10), reporting van Houwelingen and Stoelhorst (2023).

**Could an algorithm qualify under their definition?** Not as the bearer of organizationality. The framework admits non-humans, but on the other side of the axis: actorhood goes "either to a collective social address, thereby “charging” it with actorhood, or to individual (human or other-than-human) social addresses, thereby “discharging” the collective of actorhood" (p. 11). Read strictly, "the algorithm decided" is an attribution to an individual other-than-human address, and it discharges the platform. The following is my inference, not the paper's claim: this reading serves the triad better than the draft's current sentence does, because it makes the opacity of the intermediary an attribution problem — the worker and the customer must decide whether what binds them is the platform (a collective), the algorithm (an other-than-human individual) or the counterpart — and the paper's p. 13 sentence says platforms have a stake in keeping that undecided.

## Caution

Citing this paper for an *algorithmic intermediary* that has organizationality stretches the construct past what the text allows. Organizationality here is a property of a collective's social address; an algorithm is not a collective, and the one place the framework names non-human entities it classes them with individuals whose attributed agency drains the collective's (pp. 8, 11). The paper can be cited for a platform, as a collective, holding actorhood by degree, and for the claim that platforms strategically downplay it (p. 13); it cannot be cited for an algorithm as collective actor, for platforms holding high or rising actorhood, for dissolving firm boundaries, or for the quoted phrase "gradual organizationality." Do not credit these authors with "social address": the term is Luhmannian (Luhmann, 1995; Fuchs, 2005; Drepper, 2005) and the definition quoted is Bommes and Tacke (2005, p. 289). Do not credit the 2026 paper with coining "organizationality" either; that is Dobusch and Schoeneborn (2015), and the 2026 contribution is the single-axis, charge-and-discharge version. Actorhood in this paper is always *attributed* in communication, never a capacity the entity has on its own — "collective actorhood is not something given but something ascribed in communication" (p. 6) — so a sentence in which an intermediary "operates with" organizationality also reverses the direction of the claim. The piece is conceptual: one figure, borrowed illustrations, no data, no propositions. The platform sentence on p. 13 rests on Haase et al. (2025) and Vergne and Wry (2014), which I have not read; cite it as these authors' claim, not as a finding.
