## What it argues

Audrin, Audrin and Salamin identify a gap the manuscript should care about: every major digital-competence framework addresses citizens or learners, and none is built for the workplace. They review the three dominant frameworks — the UK Department for Education's six categories, DigComp 2.1/2.2's five areas, and van Laar and colleagues' six categories, the last of which was validated only on communication and design professionals — and conclude that a workplace instrument does not exist. Their research question is flatly operational: how can we identify and measure digital skills at work?

The development runs through five stages: a literature review that consolidates dimensions across frameworks at the item level, cognitive interviews with five experts, two pilot surveys (n = 22 and n = 106, reduced to 100 after validity screens), and a full validation survey of professionals recruited on Prolific with an approval rate above 95%, located in the USA and professionally active (n = 923, reduced to 916). Items came from existing scales where they existed and were written where they did not, to cover the professional context. The initial pool held 117 items; classical-test-theory item reduction, exploratory factor analysis per dimension and a confirmatory factor analysis cut it to **52 items across 8 dimensions**: Technology use (3 items), Cybersecurity (10), Content management (7), Communication and collaboration (10), Critical inquiry (7), Responsibility (4), Well-being (5), Identity and development (6). All retained loadings were ≥ 0.70. The CFA fit was acceptable — RMSEA = 0.034, SRMR = 0.027, CFI = 0.954, TLI = 0.951 — and external validity was tested against ICT self-regulation, ICT attitudes and perceived ease of use.

Two dimension definitions matter for the comparison. *Communication and collaboration* is "the skills needed to select and use the most relevant digital tools to efficiently interact, exchange and network with various actors while adopting adequate behaviors." *Critical inquiry* is "the skills needed to assess the credibility and reliability of information online, select and use adequate digital tools to solve professional issues and to foster innovation." The introduction names algorithmic management explicitly, citing Duggan and colleagues on gig-economy app-work as one of the transformations motivating the study.

## Relation to the argument

The workplace competence measure the manuscript's §11 will be compared against, and a neighbour rather than a rival — but a neighbour whose introduction announces the paper's own topic and then does not measure it.

**The triad.** Other actors appear in the *Communication and collaboration* definition — "interact, exchange and network with various actors" — which is more than most constructs in this cluster offer. What the dimension measures, though, is the respondent's competence at *selecting and using tools* to do that interacting, with key components interaction, sharing and networking. The algorithm has no position at all: it is neither channel nor partner nor agent nor object, because the framework was built from citizen-literacy frameworks that predate the question. Nothing in 52 items asks about a determination committed by a system on the respondent and a counterpart. So the counterpart is present as a target of outreach and absent as a party to a jointly determined outcome, and the third position is missing outright.

**The most exposed gap in this cluster.** Audrin and colleagues open by citing algorithmic management and platform-transformed employment relations as motivation, then build a scale on which nothing about algorithmic management could be scored. That is precisely §4's asymmetry, restated by a paper that had the topic in view. For a reviewer asking whether the coordinated party's competency has really gone un-named in the *workplace* competence literature, this article is the answer: the best-validated 2024 workplace digital-skills instrument names algorithmic management in its introduction and has no item for it.

**Skill by name and by design.** The construct is "digital skills," measured by self-evaluated tool proficiency, and the paper's stated contribution is to help organisations identify skill gaps and design development programmes. Tool-indexed, instructable, and firmly in §8's top row. Its Technology use dimension survived at three items, which is itself a small warning about how quickly tool-indexed content decays.

**A methodological resource for §11.** The development sequence — expert cognitive interviews, two pilots, a 900-plus validation sample, CTT reduction, per-dimension EFA, then CFA with an external-validity block — is a defensible template for the cohort instrument, and it demonstrates that a workplace competence scale can be built and published without institutional access to a single employer.

## Caution

Self-report throughout, on Prolific, US-located, in a single cross-section; no performance test and no criterion outcome, so the scale measures confidence about skills rather than skills. The samples are professionally active but occupationally uncontrolled, so nothing here speaks to platform-coordinated workers in particular. Fit indices are acceptable rather than strong, and the authors report no discriminant-validity test between the eight factors, which matters for a 52-item instrument with two ten-item dimensions. Crossref gives no issue number: the article number 123279 in volume 202 is the correct locator. Do not cite it as evidence about algorithmic management — the citation to Duggan and colleagues is motivational framing in the introduction and nothing in the instrument follows from it.
