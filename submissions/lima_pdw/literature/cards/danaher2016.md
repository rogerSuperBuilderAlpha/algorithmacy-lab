## What it argues

Danaher takes up "algocracy" as a term for public decision-making — bureaucratic, legislative, legal — increasingly run by algorithms, and asks whether that shift threatens the moral or political legitimacy of those decision-making processes. Modeling the argument on Estlund's "threat of epistocracy," he names this the **threat of algocracy**: algorithmic systems can become so complex and opaque that the humans nominally subject to them, and sometimes the humans nominally running them, lose the capacity to understand, participate in, or contest the decisions being made. Danaher considers two responses — resisting the spread of algocratic systems, or finding ways to accommodate them (for instance through forms of retrospective consent or authentication of the algorithm's outputs) — and argues that neither succeeds without giving up something else of value in social decision-making, landing on a deliberately unresolved, cautionary conclusion about human participation under algorithmic governance.

## Relation to the argument

Danaher's algocracy and the paper's algorithmacy name different things at different scales, and the resemblance in spelling is the whole risk. Algocracy, in Danaher's usage, is a macro/institutional-level diagnosis: a property of a public decision-making *system* — is it opaque, is it participable, does it threaten legitimacy when code replaces deliberation. Algorithmacy is a micro-level competence: a property of an individual person navigating an opaque, adaptive, mutually binding algorithmic intermediary in the course of coordinating with someone else through it.

Because Danaher's paper is the more contemporary, more easily obtained, and more precisely documented elaboration of "algocracy" in a philosophy-of-technology and governance register — cleanly Crossref-verifiable, unlike the page-level detail of Aneesh's 2006 coinage — it is the safer citation to use if the paper needs one clear reference point for readers unfamiliar with the term before drawing the contrast with algorithmacy. A single disambiguating sentence citing Danaher (and, more cautiously, Aneesh) can forestall a reviewer reading "algorithmacy" as a typo or a rebranding of "algocracy" rather than a distinct, individual-level construct.
