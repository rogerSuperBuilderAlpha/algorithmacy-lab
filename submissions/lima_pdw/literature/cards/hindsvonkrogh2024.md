## What it argues

Hinds and von Krogh add one concept to the relational perspective on emerging technology that Bailey et al. (2022) set out. That concept is "progressive encapsulation" — in the abstract's words, it "captures GenAI’s potential ability to increasingly expand the “black box” and reduce visibility into and control over the relations and functions performed" (Abstract, p. 1). The authors take the term from computer science, where "encapsulation refers to legacy code that remains intact (wrapped) and is then connected via interfaces to new layers that allow it to function" (p. 5), and they define their own use in one sentence: "We borrow the term “encapsulation” to capture the way that GenAI can continually expand the “black box” around the relations on which it relies and the functions it performs, thus increasingly reducing the visibility of the relations and functions being enacted and the relational cascades being triggered" (p. 5). What gets encapsulated, then, is organizational functions and relations, and what encapsulates them is the GenAI system: "when GenAI promotes progressive encapsulation, this leads to more and more functions and relations being collapsed and encased" (p. 6). The process is progressive because the system learns: "more and more functions are encapsulated as the GenAI learns and updates its model, thus increasingly doing more without supervision or intervention—but twinned to the effect that how it does so will become increasingly obscure" (p. 6). Its result is a system an observer can study only at its edges: "we can study relations and functions as input and as output, but the process for getting there is opaque (black box)" (p. 6).

The paper is an essay and reports no data. Hinds and von Krogh work the concept through team coordination, which they present as "an illustration as well as a thought experiment in which we speculate about possible (as yet unknown) evolving relations and functions" (p. 6). In that illustration "GenAI might absorb entirely the function of informal coordination and minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7), team boundaries loosen, and resource brokerage between teams "may become increasingly encapsulated as GenAI learns about the utilization and allocation of resources over time" (p. 9). The Discussion turns the concept into five domains of inquiry — power, the division of labor, team creativity, knowledge sharing across teams, and individuals' psychological responses — and asks for process theories of "these forces that accelerate or arrest progressive encapsulation" (p. 11). The authors hold the valence open ("our stance toward GenAI is neutral", p. 2) and say plainly that "The theory of progressive encapsulation needs refinement in future work" (p. 10).

## Relation to the argument

Hinds and von Krogh give the algorithmacy argument a process account, in the target journal, of how a learning system absorbs coordination that people once carried out between themselves and of why those people see less of that coordination as the system learns. The account bears on the two claims the draft cites it for in different degrees.

Claim (a), that organizing depends on relations among humans and algorithmic black boxes "which reduce visibility and control", is supported. The abstract carries the phrase almost word for word: progressive encapsulation "captures GenAI’s potential ability to increasingly expand the “black box” and reduce visibility into and control over the relations and functions performed" (Abstract, p. 1). Three limits apply. The paper's subject is generative AI, not algorithmic systems at large. The claim is hedged as a "potential ability". And "control over" in this sense appears only in the abstract; the body argues reduced visibility throughout (pp. 4–6) and uses "control" otherwise for "control over scarce resources" that "may shift towards AI systems" (p. 10) and for employees' "sense of control" (p. 11).

Claim (b), that "generative AI accelerates the "progressive encapsulation" of these technologies" and so the arbitrator "becomes increasingly opaque and adaptive", is supported in part and misstated in part. The quoted term is the paper's own. Opacity has a passage: "the process for getting there is opaque (black box)" (p. 6), with "increasingly obscure" on the same page. Adaptivity has one too: the concept is introduced "to capture the way that GenAI is able to learn and adapt in ways that are difficult to observe, but nonetheless have discernible and material consequences" (p. 2). Two parts of the draft sentence do not match the source. First, the paper does not say that generative AI accelerates progressive encapsulation. GenAI does the encapsulating ("how GenAI progressively begins to encapsulate functions and relations in organizations", p. 6); the verb "accelerate" appears three times, twice for unnamed "forces in and around organizations that accelerate and arrest such encapsulation" (p. 10; again p. 11) and once for something else: "GenAI is also likely to accelerate the “boundary blurring” between teams" (p. 9). Second, "of these technologies" reverses the object. Technologies are not encapsulated; functions and relations are encapsulated by the technology.

Four passages come closer to the triad than either cited claim and are available to the draft. Managers who use GenAI to evaluate employees set off cascades "between managers, GenAI, and employees", and the system "may ultimately obscure managers’ and employees’ visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions and the behaviors they are autonomously incentivizing" (p. 4). In hiring, "as managers and applicants interact with the system, the evaluation algorithm may become increasingly refined and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). Conventional coordination systems "are rarely designed to actively mediate or manage interdependencies" (p. 7), which GenAI by implication can. And encapsulation may end up "gradually hiding coordination practices from the view of team members" (p. 8).

## Caution

The paper cannot be cited as evidence that anything has happened. Every mechanism is a conjecture in a thought experiment, phrased with "may", "might" and "We conjecture"; the paper has no cases, no propositions, no figure and no table.

It also cannot be cited for the triad. I searched the full text for "triad", "intermediary", "third party", "arbitrat-", "platform", "opacity", "adaptive" and "literacy" and found none of them. "Mediate" appears once, in the p. 7 sentence about conventional systems quoted above. "Triadic arbitrator" is the draft's term and a reader will not find it here. The relational figure the paper does use is a constellation with the system in the middle — "The GenAI sits at the center of these relations" (p. 4) — and its hiring and evaluation examples put a manager and an applicant or employee on either side of one system, but Hinds and von Krogh do not theorize that arrangement as a third position, and they do not say that the system binds both parties. In the hiring example the algorithm becomes inaccessible "to the manager"; the applicant's view goes unexamined.

On the human counterpart the paper runs in the opposite direction from algorithmacy. Its worry is that human-to-human relations thin out as GenAI absorbs coordination: the system may "minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7) at "the cost of weaker social ties and a reduced sense of team identity" (p. 9). Algorithmacy assumes the counterpart remains and must still be coordinated with through the system. The paper says nothing about a skill, sensibility or competence that users need; its remedies are addressed to theorists, not to workers.

The paper also separates generative AI from the earlier platform algorithms of the gig economy. Its one gig-economy reference is to Cameron's ride-sharing drivers, and it draws a contrast: "With GenAI, as compared with previous emerging technologies (including those studied by Cameron), rather than being fixed, these functions and relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4). Citing Hinds and von Krogh for the opacity of rating, matching or dispatch algorithms therefore stretches the source, which treats those systems as the fixed-function baseline. The setting throughout is GenAI inside organizations and teams, not markets or platforms.
