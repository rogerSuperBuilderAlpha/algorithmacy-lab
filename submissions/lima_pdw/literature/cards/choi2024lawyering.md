## What it argues

**"The first randomized controlled trial to study the effect of AI assistance on human legal analysis."** Law students were randomly assigned to complete realistic legal tasks — drafting a complaint, a contract, a section of an employee handbook and a client memo — with or without GPT-4, after training. Time was tracked and the results blind-graded.

The authors' own summary, quoted in their companion article: **"We found that access to GPT-4 only slightly and inconsistently improved the quality of participants' legal analysis but induced large and consistent increases in speed. Moreover, AI assistance improved the quality of output unevenly — where it was useful at all, the lowest-skilled participants saw the largest improvements."**

So: a robust speed effect, a weak and inconsistent quality effect, and a heterogeneity that is again skill-graded and again levelling.

## Relation to the argument

Design-wise this is the strongest of the legal studies — randomised, blind-graded, four tasks — and it is the one to cite when the manuscript needs the levelling result stated from an experiment rather than a within-subject comparison. Substantively it adds a distinction §6 should keep: speed and quality are separable outcomes with separable heterogeneity structures. The quality gains were uneven and skill-graded; the speed gains were **"large and consistent,"** which is to say roughly uniform. Any claim about "outcome variance" therefore has to name which outcome. §1 names earnings and quitting; nothing in this literature measures either.

## Caution

Abstract-level and secondary-quotation evidence only; the sample size (reported elsewhere as sixty law students) is not confirmed from the published article and should not be cited without a full-text check. Law students on simulated tasks, in a US law school, with training supplied by the researchers. Blind grading is a strength; self-selection into a study run by one's own professors is not. Conflating this with Choi & Schwarcz (2024) in the *Journal of Legal Education* is a common citation error — that is a different, non-randomised exam study. Get the full text and re-verify N and any effect sizes before this enters the manuscript with numbers attached.
