## What it argues

Wolff and colleagues take a field with a dozen competing definitions of data literacy and try to give it one foundation. Their method is textual and comparative rather than empirical: they collect existing definitions of data literacy and statistical literacy (Mandinach & Gummer, Deahl, Vahey and others, plus Callingham and Gal on the statistical side), map each definition's components onto the stages of an inquiry process, and then survey the approaches actually used to teach data literacy in schools to see which components practice reaches.

The mapping produces their central diagnosis. Definitions of *statistical* literacy cluster at the conclusions end of an inquiry — assessing whether presented statistics support the argument made with them — while definitions of *data* literacy scatter across the whole cycle without saying which stage they mean. Their argument is that critique presupposes production: to judge a presented statistic you need "at least a working understanding of the process through which such explanations and representations of data are created," including how the choice of analysis and visualisation biases interpretation and how selection skews results. They therefore propose making the inquiry process explicit inside the definition, mapping competences onto a PPDAC-style cycle and arranging them as a hierarchy running from a foundational understanding of inquiry through to specialist hands-on data-handling skills.

The definition they land on: "Data literacy is the ability to ask and answer real-world questions from large and small data sets through an inquiry process, with consideration of ethical use of data. It is based on core practical and creative skills, with the ability to extend knowledge of specialist data handling skills according to goals. These include the abilities to select, clean, analyse, visualise, critique and interpret data, as well as to communicate stories from data and to use data as part of a design process." Their intended user is a citizen or schoolchild in a smart city, working with urban data; the paper's home is the smart-city and community-informatics programme at the Open University, and the sibling article in the same issue reports the classroom work.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour that shares algorithmacy's diagnosis of opacity and answers it in the opposite direction, which makes it worth naming rather than ignoring.

**The triad.** Absent. Data literacy is a relation between a person and a *dataset*, with the inquiry cycle standing where a counterpart would have to be. Even the communicative end of the definition — "communicate stories from data" — casts the other party as an audience for a finding, not as a party whose outcome is jointly determined. So this is a two-position construct in §9's sense, and its second position is not even a system: it is an object of analysis.

**Derivation, and a genuine methodological parallel.** Wolff and colleagues do something structurally similar to what the paper claims for itself. They refuse to collect competences from practice, mapping them instead onto a *process account* — the inquiry cycle — and then reading off which competences the account requires and in what order. §7's derivation from what the coordination form withholds is the same argumentative move with a different generator. The paper could cite this as evidence that derivation-from-a-structure is a recognised way to build a literacy construct, which blunts the objection that its facets are simply asserted.

**Skill.** Their own definition says "core practical and creative skills," the argument is aimed at school curricula, and the paper's contribution is explicitly "a common foundation for teaching and learning." Teachable by design, and therefore on the wrong side of implicit acquisition.

## Caution

A conceptual paper with no instrument, no sample, and no validation — it proposes a definition and a competence space, nothing more, and should never be cited as a measure. The venue is a small open-access journal and the article is a position piece within a smart-cities project, so its authority rests on the argument rather than on cumulative testing. Crossref carries no page range for this DOI; 9–26 comes from the published PDF and the journal's own article page. Its treatment of opacity is about the opacity of *analysis choices*, not the opacity of an adaptive system that acts on the reader — do not let the shared word "critique" imply a shared object.
