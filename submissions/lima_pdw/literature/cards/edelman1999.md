## What it argues
Edelman, Uggen and Erlanger show that organisations do not simply comply with law; they construct what compliance means, and courts then defer to the construction. Their case is the internal grievance procedure. Employers adopted such procedures as visible symbols of attention to civil-rights law, and personnel professionals promoted them as evidence of good faith. The procedures then took on a life inside the organisation that had little to do with vindicating rights: they were staffed and framed as management tools, they translated legal claims into personnel problems, and they resolved complaints in terms of organisational harmony rather than legal entitlement. The decisive step is judicial. Courts, looking for a manageable standard, began to treat the *presence* of a grievance procedure as evidence of compliance, without inquiring into whether it produced remedies. Law thereby became endogenous: the organisational form created to signal compliance became the legal definition of compliance, and the symbol was accepted in place of the substance it symbolised. The authors call the procedures rational myths in the neo-institutional sense — structures adopted for legitimacy, loosely coupled to the outcomes they claim.

## Relation to the argument
This is the single most important corrective to §6, and the paper currently lacks it. §6 divides supply routes into individual and institutional, and treats the institutional side — "forums, statutory review, designed appeal" — as the side that can actually supply accountability. Edelman, Uggen and Erlanger show that a designed appeal can exist, be counted as a forum, and produce nothing, and that its very existence can foreclose the external route by satisfying the body that would otherwise scrutinise. If a platform's in-app appeal queue functions as their grievance procedure does, then the institutional route delivers the *appearance* of the withheld condition and the paper's division needs a grading criterion rather than a binary. Bovens supplies that criterion — demarcated forum, capacity to judge, possibility of consequence — and this article supplies the reason the criterion is needed.

It bears on §5 too, from the other direction. The paper's claim is that accountability cannot be individually supplied. Edelman and colleagues show that it can also fail to be institutionally supplied by an institution that appears to supply it, which means the division in §6 is not individual-fails/institutional-succeeds but individual-cannot/institutional-may. Stating it that way strengthens the result rather than weakening it, because it removes the reading in which the paper is recommending appeal processes.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and citing literature; the argument's stages are well established but pull the article before quoting. The setting is US employment discrimination law and the employers subject to it, and the mechanism runs through courts deferring to organisational structures — a platform's appeal process operates in a much thinner legal environment, so the endogeneity story transfers only in part. Edelman's later work (*Working Law*, 2016) develops the argument and is the better cite for the mature version. The paper should not use this article to argue that institutional routes are futile; its own conclusion is about symbolic structures, and the authors treat effective procedures as possible.
