## What it argues

Hong, Cheng and Liu interview 20 food-delivery workers and ride-hailing drivers and build a three-dimension, nine-indicator framework of **algorithmic skills**. The dimensions are *algorithmic awareness*, *algorithm learning and comprehension*, and *algorithm utilization and mastery*. The nine indicators, in the authors' order, are awareness of algorithm presence, recognition of data dependency, awareness of algorithmic evolution, understanding input–output relationships, reverse engineering, collaborative learning of algorithms, exploiting platform rules, leveraging technical tools, and experience-based decision-making.

The argument is explicitly framed against the control-and-resistance literature. Algorithms constrain autonomy and standardise the labour process, but workers cultivate practical skill through daily exposure, and that skill affords meaningful agency — sometimes enough to challenge platform control. The authors present the algorithmic-skill framework as a more constructive and sustainable pathway for thinking about human–algorithm collaboration at work than control-resistance offers, and they close by insisting that algorithmic skills be contextualised within specific sociotechnical and occupational settings rather than treated as general.

## Relation to the argument

**Rival, and a fresh one — published the same year as Lima, on the same population as Zhou et al., with a nine-part inventory.** It is not a scale (twenty interviews, no psychometrics), but it is a named, structured competence construct for exactly the workers the manuscript's argument is about, and it did not exist when the Lima abstract was accepted. Anyone who searches this literature in October 2026 will find it.

The discriminations hold, and two are sharper here than against Zhou et al. The counterpart is absent, again, and conspicuously: of nine indicators, the only one involving another person is *collaborative learning of algorithms*, which concerns **peers** — other drivers pooling what they have inferred — not the party on the other side of the transaction. The passenger and the customer never appear. So this construct has a social dimension and still has no counterpart position, which is a useful demonstration that the two are not the same thing and that adding sociality to a worker–system construct does not produce a triad. Second, the authors call it a *skill* and the manuscript's §8 line applies with full force: nine indicators including "leveraging technical tools" and "exploiting platform rules" are tool-indexed and date with the dispatch regime they were observed in. Third, the framework is inventoried from interviews, so it can say what workers do and not why the list has nine members.

Their closing insistence on contextualising skills within specific occupational settings is, like Gagrčin et al.'s call for domain-specific approaches, a defence of building a purpose-made instrument rather than importing one.

## Caution

Twenty interviews, qualitative, on-demand delivery and ride-hailing; the three dimensions and nine indicators are analytic categories, not validated factors, and no reliability or item wording exists. Do not describe it as a scale. Published in 2026 and therefore uncited and unreviewed by anyone; treat its claims as unreplicated.

## Verified 2026-08-30 (full text obtained)

Setting confirmed: China (Meituan, Ele.me for food delivery; Didi for ride-hailing), 20 workers (10 food-delivery couriers, 10 ride-hailing drivers), no female participants (acknowledged limitation), 85% full-time, recruited via snowball sampling; interviews 1–2 hours, semi-structured, grounded-theory analysis (Strauss & Corbin) yielding 90 first-level codes and 10 major categories, restructured into the final 3-dimension/9-indicator framework. `PAPER.md` does not attribute this setting to the source, so the earlier caution about checking before attributing one does not apply.

**Confirms the manuscript's characterization exactly.** Of the nine indicators (Figure 1: Awareness of Algorithm Presence, Recognition of Data Dependency, Awareness of Algorithmic Evolution, Understanding Input-Output Relationships, Reverse Engineering, Collaborative Learning of Algorithms, Exploiting Platform Rules, Leveraging Technical Tools, Experience-Based Decision-Making), only **Collaborative Learning of Algorithms** involves another person, and the paper is explicit that this means fellow workers pooling inferences about the algorithm — peer knowledge-sharing (the paper connects this to China's "guanxi" norms and informal networks like WeChat groups), not the customer or rider on the other side of a match. The passenger/customer does not appear as a party to any indicator anywhere in the framework. This is exactly "confined to person-system dyads, treating human counterparts as external peers" as `PAPER.md` line 327 states — verified against the primary text, not just the abstract.
