## What it argues

The field's standing review of third-party mediation research. Wall, Stark and Standifer synthesise the determinants of mediator entry, the taxonomy of mediator techniques (from purely facilitative to strongly directive), and the conditions under which each produces settlement and satisfaction. Their theoretical contribution is a decision-model of the mediator: what a third party does is determined by her goals, her assessment of the dispute, and the constraints of her role — and mediator behaviour is therefore predictable rather than idiosyncratic.

They also distinguish mediation from arbitration on exactly one criterion: **the mediator has no authority to impose an outcome.**

## Relation to the argument

The line this literature draws is the line the paper needs. Mediation research is enormous, thoroughly triadic, and explicitly about a third party positioned between two others — and it is defined by the third party's *lack* of binding authority. Arbitration, where the third party decides, is a separate literature with separate journals.

So the honest report to §9 is this: **communication and conflict research has theorised the triad extensively; what it has not theorised is a third party that decides, that neither disputant selected, and whose criteria are undisclosed.** Mediation is triadic and non-binding. Arbitration is binding but consensual and reasoned. The platform gate is binding, non-consensual, and unreasoned, and no third-party literature has that cell. That is a much stronger and more defensible claim than "the constructs inherit a dyad," and it is the form §9 should take.

Two further points transfer. The facilitative/directive taxonomy is a ready dimension for describing how heavily a platform intervenes. And the review's finding that disputants' satisfaction depends on perceived mediator impartiality bears on why platform neutrality claims matter to workers.

## Caution

Conflict resolution and negotiation, not communication competence; the mediators are human professionals in labour, community, and international disputes. Nothing in it is about competence on the disputants' side. The 1993 Wall & Lynn review is the predecessor and is frequently cited in its place — check which one a citing source means.
