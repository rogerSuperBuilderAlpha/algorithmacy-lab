## What it argues

Peters traces two competing ideas of communication through Western thought: **dialogue**, the Socratic ideal of two souls in reciprocal contact, and **dissemination**, the parable-of-the-sower model in which a message is broadcast without knowing who receives it or how. Modern communication theory, he argues, has been obsessed with dialogue and has treated dissemination as a deficient form, when in fact dissemination is the normal case and reciprocity the exception. His deeper claim is that the fantasy of perfect mutual understanding — minds touching without remainder — is both incoherent and ethically suspect, and that communication's actual condition is asymmetry, delay, and the possibility of never knowing whether one was heard.

## Relation to the argument

A framing resource for §5 and a corrective to a tacit assumption in §7. The paper treats the unmet counterpart as a *deprivation* — a condition ordinarily available that this form withholds. Peters's history says the opposite is closer to normal: speaking without knowing who receives is the ordinary human condition, and the dyadic reciprocity the competence literature builds on is the historical exception.

That does not weaken the paper; it changes what is distinctive about the arrangement. What co-optation withholds is not contact as such — dissemination never had it — but **the possibility of ever finding out**, because a determination arrives, binds, and explains nothing. Peters's speaker into the air at least knows she has spoken into the air. §7's worker receives a verdict shaped like feedback and cannot tell whether anyone was there. Stating the difference that way is sharper than treating the unmet counterpart as a simple lack.
