## What it argues

From a three-year ethnography of British beauty vloggers plus interviews with ten full-time creators and nine intermediaries, Bishop names **algorithmic gossip**: communally and socially informed theories and strategies about recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to produce financial consistency and visibility. Gossip does three things. It is collaborative, circulating through formalised channels such as the Internet Creators Guild's paid Platform Changelog and through closed, status-gated groups where creators take the temperature — "are you having a terrible week too?" — to work out whether a visibility drop is personal or platform-wide. It is a knowledge-request and auditing practice: creators crowdsource evidence from fans, as when one ran an Instagram poll asking followers whether her video appeared in their subscription box, or another asked fans to tweet screenshots at a set time to detect an unannounced change. And it is subversive: creators of colour use it to articulate suspected racial bias they cannot prove against a black box. The talk feeds back into production, pushing creators toward the content they believe the ranking rewards.

## Relation to the argument

The best documented case of **temporal tracking done socially**, and it complicates §10's implicit-acquisition property in a way the section should welcome rather than resist. Taking the temperature and the fan-sourced screenshot audit are change-detection instruments, and they are collective: no individual could distinguish a personal drop from a platform-wide shift, because the signal only appears across cases. Section 10 already allows that a friend telling you what the gate rewards is implicit acquisition operating socially. Bishop shows the stronger version — that for one of the three facets, the individual operation may be *impossible* alone, which is a real constraint on a construct that locates the competency in a person.

The paid changelog is also the sharpest available counter-example to §10's implicit-acquisition claim, and §11 should be told about it. Here is explicit instruction about a specific gate, sold at $75–$500 a year, in exactly the domain where §10 says nothing can be taught because nobody outside the system knows. What is sold is not the rule but a running record of observed drift — which arguably preserves the property, and arguably shows the property needs restating.

## Caution

A qualitative ethnography of a gendered, mostly UK community of full-time beauty and lifestyle vloggers; the gossip's content is by design unverifiable, since the algorithm's criteria are unknown to Bishop as much as to her informants. Its evidential value is that the reorganisation happens and leaves traces, not that any particular theory is correct. The bias claims are informants' articulated experiences that Bishop foregrounds without adjudicating. Creators are not coordinating with an unmet counterpart in §7's sense; the audience is diffuse.
