## What it argues

**"We conducted a study of 1,488 full-time U.S.-based workers (48% male vs 51% female; 58% independent contributors vs 41% leaders) at large companies across industries, roles, and levels. We asked them about patterns and quantity of AI use, work experiences, and cognition and emotions."** A single cross-sectional self-report survey.

The construct: **"AI brain fry,"** defined as **"mental fatigue from excessive use or oversight of AI tools beyond one's cognitive capacity."** **"Fourteen percent of participants in our study using AI for work endorsed the experience of AI brain fry,"** ranging from **"just 6% of those in legal roles"** to **"26% of those in marketing roles."**

Reported associations, all correlational and all self-reported on both sides:
- Oversight intensity: high-oversight workers **"expended 14% more mental effort,"** reported **"12% more mental fatigue"** and **"19% greater information overload."**
- Decision fatigue: brain-fry workers **"experience 33% more decision fatigue than those who did not."**
- Errors: brain-fry workers scored **"11% and 39% higher on the minor and major error frequency measures, respectively."**
- Exit: **"Among workers who did not report AI brain fry, 25% showed active intent to leave. Among those who did report AI brain fry, that rose to 34%. This represents a 39% increase in active worker intent to leave among top users of AI."**
- Offsetting: using AI to cut routine tasks predicted burnout **"15% lower than those who don't use AI in this way."**

And the finding the manuscript's abstract has been leaning on: **"As employees go from using one AI tool to two simultaneously, they experience a significant increase in productivity. As they incorporate a third tool, productivity again increases, but at a lower rate. After three tools, though, productivity scores dipped."**

## Relation to the argument

This is the practitioner source behind §1's "the volume of technology used predicts little of it," and reading it closes that option. Bedard et al. do not find that tool volume predicts little. They find an **inverted-U**: two tools better than one, three better still at a lower rate, four or more worse. Volume predicts something, non-monotonically, in self-reported perceived productivity. The manuscript's sentence is not what the source says.

What the source does supply, and what §1 could legitimately take from it, is that the *mode* of engagement matters more than the *quantity* — oversight intensity is the strongest predictor of cognitive cost, and replacing toil is protective while supervising agents is not. Same tools, opposite outcomes according to how the work is arranged around them. That is a construal-adjacent finding and it points at Zhou et al.

It cannot carry a warrant. Everything here is self-report, cross-sectional and correlational, with no control group, no preregistration, no instrument published, and a consultancy's brand attached to the construct it names.

## Caution

**Grey literature. Do not let it support an empirical premise.** Harvard Business Review is a practitioner magazine with no peer review; the study is proprietary BCG research with no methods appendix, no measurement validation and no way for a reader to check anything. The percentages are ratios of self-report scale scores, not effect sizes, and "39% more" on major errors means a 39% higher score on a self-reported frequency measure, not 39% more errors. The "39% increase in intent to leave" is 25% to 34% — a 9-point difference recast as a relative change, and the sample it describes is not "top users of AI" but self-identified brain-fry sufferers. Every association is equally consistent with reverse causation: workers already struggling report both more fatigue and more AI oversight. Use it, if at all, as an illustration of practitioner concern and as a pointer to a phenomenon worth studying, explicitly labelled as such, and never as the source for a sentence beginning "that variance is well documented."
