## What it argues

Wenger turns the apprenticeship account into a general social theory of learning, built on an ethnography of medical-claims processors at an insurance company. A community of practice is defined by three dimensions: **mutual engagement**, a **joint enterprise** the members negotiate among themselves, and a **shared repertoire** of routines, words, tools, and stories. Learning happens through a duality Wenger calls **participation and reification**: practice runs partly through people doing things together and partly through the artifacts, documents, and forms that congeal meaning into objects, and neither half works without the other. Meaning is never simply read off a reified artifact; it has to be re-negotiated in participation each time. The claims processors are the case in point — the official procedure and the forms tell them one thing, and what the work actually requires is worked out locally, in and around those forms. Wenger adds **boundary objects** and **brokering** as the means by which practices connect, and treats **identity** as the trajectory a person traces through participation and non-participation.

## Relation to the argument

The participation/reification duality is the sharpest tool this cluster offers §10. The paper's claim that instruction can convey the tool and the interface but not what the gate rewards this month is exactly Wenger's point about reification: a rubric, a README, or a build instruction is a reified artifact whose meaning still has to be negotiated in participation, and the negotiation is the learning. The Lima interview shows the duality operating: the participant thought "the instructions for the first build were understood going in" and "made mistakes anyway" — the reification was legible and the practice it pointed to was not. Wenger also supplies the vocabulary for the three-facet derivation in §7, since inbound and outbound common understanding map onto the reading and the producing of shared repertoire. The point of departure is the same as with Lave & Wenger (1991): Wenger's communities negotiate their own joint enterprise, and the defining feature of the arrangement algorithmacy is built for is that the enterprise is set elsewhere and not disclosed.

## Caution

An ethnography of one back-office site, generalized into a theory, and Wenger himself does not treat it as tested. This book is the hinge on which "community of practice" turned from an analytic category into a management concept, a shift Wenger's own later work (2002) completed and Contu & Willmott (2003) criticize; do not cite 1998 and 2002 as one position. Power is thin here relative to the 1991 book, which is precisely the objection the paper's distributive edge in §6 should register rather than inherit.
