## What it argues

**Not established from any source read for this card.** The book is the founding statement of the relational model of interpersonal communication competence, and the model's outcome criteria — appropriateness and effectiveness — carry into Spitzberg's later work, where they are defined as "the perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context" and "the degree to which preferred objectives are optimized." Those definitions are verified from Spitzberg (2006), not from the 1984 book, and attributing them backwards is an inference. The motivation–knowledge–skills triad that organizes the 2006 CMC model is likewise commonly traced to this book, but the 2006 article attributes the triad's lineage to Goffman's dramaturgical perspective by way of Ring and colleagues, which is a different genealogy from the one a casual citation would assume. What the book actually claims, how it defines competence, and whether it locates competence in a person or in an interlocutor's judgement are all open at this depth.

## Relation to the argument

§8 cites Hymes and this book together as the communication-competence lineage that "supplies the warrant for the name," and the clause makes a specific claim: that this tradition distinguishes what a party is able to do in a coordination from what she did on a given occasion. That claim is sound for Hymes and verified there. For Spitzberg and Cupach it is currently an assumption. It matters more than the citation's weight suggests, because the relational model's defining move — competence as a judgement rendered by a partner, not a property residing in the speaker — would either strengthen §8 considerably or complicate it. If competence is an impression formed by the counterpart, then a competency construct for a coordination form whose counterpart is *absent* has a problem the paper has not addressed; if the book locates competence in the person, §8's table row stands as written. Verdict: the one source in Paper 2's competency lineage that is cited but unread, and the one where reading it could change a section.

## Caution

This card records what is not known rather than what is. Do not quote the book, do not attribute the appropriateness/effectiveness pair to it in print, and do not let §8's compound citation imply that Hymes and Spitzberg and Cupach make the same distinction until the second half is checked. The book is a 1984 Sage monograph, so a library copy is the realistic route; a chapter-level check of how competence is located — in the actor or in the perceiver — would settle the question §8 depends on. Until then the safest form of the §8 sentence names Hymes for the competence/performance distinction and cites Spitzberg and Cupach for the tradition's development without asserting what they added.
