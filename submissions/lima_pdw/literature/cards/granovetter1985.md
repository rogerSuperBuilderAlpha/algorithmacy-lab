## What it argues

Granovetter's **embeddedness** thesis argues economic action is neither the atomized, undersocialized calculation neoclassical economics assumes, nor the oversocialized, norm-determined behavior some economic sociology substitutes for it. Economic actors are embedded in ongoing networks of personal relations, and those concrete relations — not abstract institutional arrangements or generalized morality — are what generate trust and discourage malfeasance in economic life. The paper is a general argument about the social structuring of economic action across markets, firms, and organizations; it is not itself a proposal that "network" constitutes a discrete third governance form standing beside hierarchy and market — that specific move belongs more to Powell (1990) and the transaction-cost-adjacent literature Powell engages.

## Relation to the argument

Three uses, always paired with Powell (1990), defining the Network coordination form: "A partner invests in an elective, revocable relationship grounded in informal social reciprocity and mutual reputational capital (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990)" (line 39); Table A1's mechanism cell (line 513); and the Appendix A synthesis, "Powell (1990) and Granovetter (1985) anchor the network participant within elective, revocable partnerships" (line 522). The content attributed — trust, reciprocity, reputation, ongoing personal relations discouraging opportunism — is squarely Granovetter's argument and is accurately drawn.

## Caution

Worth an author judgment call rather than a fix (**FLAG**). Granovetter (1985) is a general embeddedness thesis about all economic action, not a paper proposing network governance as a discrete archetype parallel to hierarchy and market; that specific typological move is Powell's contribution (and Williamson's, from the transaction-cost side), which is presumably why the manuscript always pairs the two names rather than citing Granovetter alone. The pairing is extremely standard practice in organization theory — "Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990" is close to a canonical joint citation for network governance — and every substantive claim attributed to Granovetter (trust, reciprocity, ongoing relations, reputational sanctioning of malfeasance) is accurately his. Not verified against primary text; a primary read would be needed only if the manuscript ever cites Granovetter *alone* for the network-as-governance-form claim, which it currently does not.
