## What it argues

Koch and colleagues do something unusual and worth respecting: before testing MAILS again they write down what the instrument is *for*, derive testable axioms and subaxioms from those stated measurement purposes, and then check the axioms against several datasets, some previously published and some newly collected. Three studies result. The overall verdict supports MAILS's validity, with what the authors call "some unexpected findings that require further research" — a phrasing they do not soften.

The second contribution is a ten-item short form cut from the original 34, selected to represent the MAILS factors and showing good fit under confirmatory factor analysis. The authors are careful that the short scale itself is not yet validated: "further research will be needed to validate the short scale." Markus et al. subsequently used this 10-item version, reporting α = .77, as the subjective-AI-literacy comparison against which AICOS's objective test was benchmarked, which makes it the field's default short measure whether or not it has earned that status.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour; the value for the manuscript lies in the method, not the construct.

The construct is unchanged from Carolus et al. (2023) and so is the verdict: no human counterpart, in any of the five subscales carried into the short version or in the ten retained items. Ten items of "I can…" and "I know…" with AI as object. The abbreviation makes the point starker — when the field compresses AI literacy to its ten most representative items, none of the ten concerns another person, a shared determination, or a party on the other side of a system. If a construct's core survives compression without a counterpart ever surfacing, the counterpart was never load-bearing.

What is genuinely useful here is the derivation-from-purpose move. Koch et al. begin from "what is this supposed to measure and for whom," derive axioms, and test them. That is structurally the same manoeuvre the paper claims for algorithmacy's three facets — derive from a prior account rather than collect from practice — applied at the level of validation rather than construct definition. The paper can cite this as evidence that the field itself recognises derivation as a superior warrant, and then observe that nobody has applied it one level up, to the construct. Nobody has asked what the coordination form makes necessary; they have asked what the instrument is supposed to do.

The "unexpected findings" also give §10 something. A validated instrument that behaves inconsistently across samples is a hint that the thing it measures is not distributed the way its designers assume. That is not proof of stratified fluency, but it is the kind of anomaly stratified fluency would produce.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth: the three studies' samples, the specific axioms, the ten retained items and the nature of the unexpected findings are all outside the abstract and need the full text, which is open access and should be opened before any of this is quoted. The short version is explicitly unvalidated by its own authors, so any downstream use of MAILS-10 — including Markus et al.'s — inherits that caveat. Same population limits as the parent study: German-speaking online panels. Do not treat this paper as independent confirmation of MAILS, since some of the datasets are the ones the original validation used and the author team overlaps almost entirely.
