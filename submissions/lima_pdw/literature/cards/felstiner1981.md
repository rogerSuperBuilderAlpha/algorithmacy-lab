## What it argues
Felstiner, Abel and Sarat reframe disputing by asking not how disputes are resolved but how they come into existence at all, and they find that most never do. A grievance passes through three transformations before it becomes a dispute, and it can die at each. **Naming** is perceiving an experience as injurious — an unnamed injurious experience is the great majority of the pool, and much injury is simply not recognised as injury. **Blaming** is attributing the injury to a party's fault, converting a perceived injury into a grievance. **Claiming** is voicing the grievance to the party held responsible and asking for a remedy; a dispute exists only when the claim is rejected. The authors' central point is that the attrition at each stage is patterned rather than random: it varies with what people believe is available, with how visible the causal chain is, with the costs and social meanings of complaining, and with who the parties are. They insist the transformations are subjective, unstable and reactive — a person's sense of whether an injury is nameable is shaped by the very institutions that would hear the claim — and they argue that studying only the disputes that reach institutions studies the surviving tip and mistakes it for the phenomenon.

## Relation to the argument
This is the canonical citation for the pattern in the paper's first participant interview, and it is more precise than any organisational-behaviour source. The participant named (she identified a review error) and blamed (she attributed it to the gate), then did not claim. The transformation that failed is the third and last, and Felstiner, Abel and Sarat's account of why it fails is exactly the paper's: not ignorance, not fear, but a reading of what the available channel is for and what would come of using it. "That's the way this is" is their language for a grievance that has been assessed against the institutional environment and abandoned — what the literature after them calls *lumping it*.

For §5 the article supplies an argument the paper has not yet made. If claiming rates are patterned by what institutions exist to receive claims, then contestation is not an individual output at all, and measuring how skilfully a worker contests measures the surviving tip. That is an independent reason why competency cannot be the variable that supplies accountability. For §6 it sharpens the institutional route: what a forum does is not only judge claims but make claims *nameable*, and an arrangement without one suppresses grievances before they become contestable. And for §11 it hands the protocol a structure — probe naming, blaming and claiming separately, because a participant who never named will report having had no complaint.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the standard account rather than from a page-verified read; pull the article before quoting the three verbs or the "lumping" formulation, which entered the literature partly through later work. The setting is American civil disputing in the 1970s, and the authors are describing individuals facing courts, insurers and professionals, not workers facing an automated gate — the transfer is the paper's. The framework is deliberately non-quantitative and has been criticised for treating the stages as sequential when they interact.
