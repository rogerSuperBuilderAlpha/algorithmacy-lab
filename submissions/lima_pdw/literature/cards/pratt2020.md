## What it argues

Management journals responding to psychology's replication crisis have imported transparency standards built for experiments, and Pratt, Kaplan and Whittington argue that this spills into qualitative research in unhelpful and potentially dangerous ways. The error is coupling transparency to replication: replication misses the point of what qualitative work does. What advocates actually want is trustworthiness, and trustworthiness has to be established by means appropriate to the method — visible analytic process, the audit trail, negative cases — not by raw-data disclosure that endangers participants and misrepresents interpretation.

## Relation to the argument

The single best defence of what this protocol pre-commits and why. Committing a coding scheme, a saturation rule and a refutation condition before fieldwork is transparency in their sense — it makes the inferential process inspectable — while the audit trail stays with the committee rather than being published, which is what protects participants a third party had to shield from their own instructor. Cite this and the pre-registration-flavoured apparatus reads as trustworthiness rather than as physics envy.

## Caution

It is an ASQ editorial and therefore a position, however authoritative; a reviewer who wants raw transcripts deposited will not be persuaded by it alone. Note also that the paper's own pre-commitments come close to the replication logic these authors warn against — say explicitly that the commitments serve inspectability, not repeatability.
