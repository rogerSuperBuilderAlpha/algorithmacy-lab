## What it argues
Alfrink and colleagues systematically review the design literature and assemble it into a framework for making automated decision systems contestable, which they define as responsive to human intervention throughout the system lifecycle and offer as a guard against harmful automated decision-making. The framework has two halves. Five *system features*: built-in safeguards against harmful behaviour; interactive control over automated decisions; explanations of system behaviour; human review and intervention requests; and tools that let subjects or third parties scrutinise the system. Six *development practices*: ex-ante safeguards, agonistic development that builds disagreement into the process rather than designing it out, quality assurance during and after deployment, risk-mitigation strategies, and third-party oversight. Their positioning argument is the one that matters: contestability is an alternative to transparency rather than an extension of it. Transparency opens a system to view; contestability builds the channel of objection into the system itself. The agonistic strand is distinctive — they take from political theory the idea that legitimacy comes from institutionalised conflict rather than from consensus, and argue that a system is legitimate when it has arrangements through which subjects can contest it, not when it has convinced them.

## Relation to the argument
This is the fullest specification of what §6's institutional route would have to contain, and the paper should use it to make the institutional column concrete instead of a list of three nouns. Every item in the framework is something a designer, an operator or a regulator installs; not one is a capacity of the decision subject. Read as a catalogue, it is an inventory of what a worker cannot supply for herself, which is §5's claim expressed as a design specification.

The agonistic argument earns its own sentence in §6. If legitimacy comes from institutionalised conflict, then a form that offers no arrangement for conflict is illegitimate however well its subjects cope, and coping better does not repair it. That is the strongest available statement of §6's closing point that naming the competency locates the transfer without licensing it.

The framework also gives §11 a coding instrument. Asked what the Hult gate provides, an investigator can walk the five features and the six practices and record which are present — a cleaner empirical question than asking participants whether they felt they could contest.

## Caution
It is a review-derived framework, not an evaluation: the features and practices are assembled from what designers have proposed, and the paper offers no evidence that a system built to the framework produces better outcomes for subjects. "Contestability" here is a designer's property of a system, which is one step short of Bovens's forum — a system can be responsive to intervention requests without any party owing an answer. The literature reviewed skews toward public-sector and high-stakes AI; labour platforms are barely represented. Note the corrected pagination: 33(4), 613–639.
