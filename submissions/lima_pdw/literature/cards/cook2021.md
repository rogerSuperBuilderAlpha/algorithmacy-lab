## What it argues

Labour supply and earnings for **"more than a million rideshare drivers on Uber in the U.S."** The headline: **"We document a roughly 7% gender earnings gap amongst drivers."** The decomposition is the finding: **"this gap can be entirely attributed to three factors: experience on the platform (learning-by-doing), preferences and constraints over where to work (driven largely by where drivers live and, to a lesser extent, safety), and preferences for driving speed."** Rivals are ruled out: **"We do not find that men and women are differentially affected by a taste for specific hours, a return to within-week work intensity, or customer discrimination."** The conclusion is that **"in a 'gig' economy setting with no gender discrimination and highly flexible labour markets, women's relatively high opportunity cost of non-paid-work time and gender-based differences in preferences and constraints can sustain a gender pay gap."**

## Relation to the argument

This is the study of earnings dispersion among platform workers doing identical work, on the exact platform §1's opening scene invokes, at a scale nothing else approaches — and it is the trap in this cluster that has not yet been sprung.

It documents that two drivers on the same platform earn differently. It then explains the gap **entirely**, and the largest single component is **experience on the platform — learning-by-doing**, which is to say accumulated skill. That is the same rival §6 concedes, now attested on Uber earnings rather than on chatbot productivity. A paper opening on "two drivers on the same platform earn differently and the difference does not track hours or tools" is contradicted on its own terrain by the best-powered study of the question, which finds that the difference tracks experience, where you drive, and how fast you drive.

Cite it, and cite it early. Suppressing it would be indefensible in a paper reviewed by organisation theorists, and confronting it strengthens §6: the manuscript's claim has to be that a *residual* survives the Cook et al. decomposition, not that no decomposition has been attempted.

There is a positive reading available. Cook et al. hold the algorithm constant and find the variance in what drivers *do* — where, when and how fast they choose to drive. Those are choices made under an information regime the platform controls, and the paper does not ask whether the ability to make them well is unevenly distributed. That is the unexamined space algorithmacy claims. But it is a space, not a finding.

## Caution

Uber-supplied administrative data with a company co-author (Hall); the framing of "no gender discrimination" is contested in the subsequent literature. The decomposition is of a *gender* gap, not of total earnings dispersion — it does not say the residual variance among all drivers is zero, and it must not be quoted as though it did. US only, 2015–17 window, before much of the current dispatch and pricing apparatus. Quitting is not an outcome; the paper studies earnings and labour supply, not attrition.
