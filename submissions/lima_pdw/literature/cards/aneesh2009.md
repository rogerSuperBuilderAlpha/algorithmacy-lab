## What it argues

Aneesh studies Indian programmers who write code for U.S. corporations without ever crossing a border — labor coordinated transnationally with no direct bureaucratic chain of command spanning the distance. He argues that a third organizing principle governs this arrangement, distinct from the two Weber and the market tradition already name: bureaucracy runs on legal-rational authority, the market runs on price, and this third form — which Aneesh calls **algocracy**, or an **algocratic** mode of organization — runs on the programming scheme and the algorithm itself. The code embedded in the software platform structures what workers can and cannot do, coordinating dispersed labor through the design of the work process rather than through supervisory authority or market signaling.

**Note on the coinage.** Aneesh had introduced "algocracy"/"algocratic" earlier, in his 2006 book *Virtual Migration: The Programming of Globalization* (Duke University Press; ISBN 978-0-8223-3669-3), and multiple secondary sources (the Wikipedia articles on "Algocracy" and on "A. Aneesh") credit that book as the term's point of origin in his work, glossing it there as "the rule of the algorithm, or rule of the code." I was not able to obtain the book's text directly (the accessible online copy returned a 403) and so could not independently confirm the exact page or wording of the 2006 coinage — that attribution rests on secondary description, not a direct read. The 2009 *Sociological Theory* article is the more solidly documented, directly Crossref-verifiable citation for the concept and gives it its fullest published articulation (the three-way typology against bureaucracy and market), so it is the safer primary citation if only one Aneesh source is used.

## Relation to the argument

Algocracy and the paper's own coined term, algorithmacy, sound alike and must not be conflated; they sit at different levels of analysis. Aneesh's algocracy is a macro-institutional concept — a name for a *mode of governance or organization*, comparable in scope to "bureaucracy" or "the market," describing how a system as a whole is ruled when code rather than a legal-rational hierarchy or a price mechanism does the coordinating. The paper's algorithmacy is a micro-level construct — an individual's capacity to interpret, communicate through, and track an opaque, adaptive, mutually binding algorithmic intermediary while coordinating within an arrangement that may itself be algocratically governed.

A disambiguating sentence can be built directly from that contrast: algocracy names the governance form a person is coordinating *within*; algorithmacy names the competence that person exercises *in order to* coordinate within it. One is a property of the system; the other is a property of the person acting inside the system. The two terms are compatible, not competing — a worker in an algocratic arrangement is exactly the kind of person for whom algorithmacy would be observable — but a reader who has seen "algocracy" before should not assume the paper is renaming it.

## Caution

Read at abstract and secondary-summary level; the full article was not directly read, so the typology's exact wording (particularly Aneesh's own phrasing for the "rule of algorithm" formulation) is reconstructed rather than quoted. The 2006 book is widely credited with the term's first appearance in Aneesh's work, but that credit rests on secondary sources (Wikipedia and derivative discussion), not on a page-level primary read; do not cite a specific page number for the 2006 coinage without independently locating and reading the passage. Do not describe Aneesh as having coined the bare word "algocracy" outright — at least one earlier informal, non-scholarly use of the word turned up in a 1998 Usenet post (per wordspy.com), so the safer claim is that Aneesh introduced and developed it as a sociological concept of institutional governance, not that he invented the word from nothing.
