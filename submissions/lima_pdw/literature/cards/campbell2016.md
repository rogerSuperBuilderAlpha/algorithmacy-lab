## What it argues

Campbell's Ely Lecture turns the household-finance evidence into an argument about market design. He argues that "financial ignorance is pervasive and unsurprising given the complexity of modern financial products," and that it "contributes meaningfully to the evolution of wealth inequality." A stylized model then carries the welfare economics of paternalistic intervention, worked through asset allocation in retirement saving, fees for unsecured short-term borrowing, and reverse mortgages.

## Relation to the argument

Two sentences here matter for §4. The first is that ignorance is *unsurprising given complexity* — the demand a market makes on a participant is a property of the market's design, not a fixed feature of markets in general. That is precisely the ordinal reading the seventh row wants: the demand rises as the arrangement departs from the conditions that make a price sufficient, and product complexity is one of the levers that moves it. The second is that the resulting shortfall shows up in the distribution of wealth. The competency demanded by a form is not a curiosity about individuals; it sorts them.

The regulatory framing is also a useful precedent for the paper's conclusion. Where the market's competence problem generated a policy literature about disclosure, defaults and product regulation, the co-optation column has no equivalent yet — which is one thing an unnamed competency costs.
