## What it argues

Van Maanen's argument is that ethnographic authority is a *written* achievement, and that the genre a fieldworker chooses determines what the reader can believe. He distinguishes realist tales, which efface the author and speak in the voice of the culture; confessional tales, which foreground the fieldworker's struggle and stake authority on candour; and impressionist tales, which reconstruct a striking episode and let the reader judge. None is the honest one; each buys credibility with a different currency.

## Relation to the argument

The paper has to choose a tale and should choose knowingly. An insider study written as a realist tale — the author absent, the gate described in the third person — will strike a reviewer as evasive the moment he learns who built the gate. A confessional register, in which the investigator's position is part of the account, matches the design and matches Anteby's decoupling of distance from involvement. Van Maanen is how that choice gets defended in one sentence instead of three paragraphs.

## Caution

A book about writing, not about doing; it will not answer a design objection. Editions matter for page cites — the 1988 first edition, the 2011 edition with a new epilogue. Van Maanen is also, notoriously, easy to cite and hard to follow.
