## What it argues

The stocktaking review of the alliance-capability field. Kale and Singh open on a paradox: firms enter alliances in large numbers to secure and extend advantage, and alliances fail at surprisingly high rates. Their account of the remedy runs at two levels — factors that make any individual alliance work at each phase of its life cycle, and firm-level capabilities, institutionalized rather than improvised, that raise a firm's success across its whole alliance portfolio. The closing agenda names three emerging issues: a new class of alliances between firms and not-for-profits or *individuals*; the value of a portfolio rather than deal-by-deal approach; and the transfer of alliance capability to other interfirm relationships, including acquisitions.

The passing mention of alliances "between firms and not-for-profit organizations or individuals" is worth a footnote. Written in 2009, it gestures at exactly the asymmetric firm-to-individual arrangement that the platform form would industrialize, and the alliance literature never followed it up.

## Caution

Abstract depth only, and this is a review essay in a practitioner-oriented outlet — it synthesizes and advises rather than testing anything, so it carries no independent evidential weight. High failure rates for alliances are reported in this literature with wide variation in definition and measurement; do not quote a specific rate from this source without going to the underlying studies. The whole discussion is firm-level and manager-facing.
