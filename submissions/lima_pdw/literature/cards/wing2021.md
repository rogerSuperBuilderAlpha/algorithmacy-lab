# Wing, L., Martinez, J., Katsh, E., & Rule, C. (2021). Designing ethical online dispute resolution systems: The rise of the fourth party. *Negotiation Journal*, 37(1), 49–64.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/nejo.12350  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Wiley Online Library / MIT Press
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Twenty years on, the fourth party is no longer a metaphor. Wing, Martinez, Katsh and Rule trace the fourth party's evolution from a communication/processing tool to an increasingly capable actor that can manage information as a partner, intervene as a third-party neutral, or render a binding outcome — and argue that as ODR systems incorporate AI and machine learning, the field needs deliberate ethical standards to govern that expanding role. The article does not itself state a four-part list of design requirements as its own thesis. Instead, in its "Envisioning Ethical Standards for Fourth Parties" section, it points to an *external, earlier* framework: Wing's own (2016) "Ethical Principles for Online Dispute Resolution" (also credited to the National Center for Technology and Dispute Resolution, 2016), which the article explicitly describes as "a set of values (**not rules**)." Footnote 3 lists the framework's actual seventeen principles, in full: accessibility, accountability, competence, confidentiality, empowerment, equality, fairness, honesty, impartiality, **informed participation**, innovation, integration, legal obligation, neutrality, protection from harm, security, and transparency. Only "Competence" is quoted in the article's own body text, at length. The article then argues, in its own voice, that "principles alone are arguably insufficient" and that enforceable standards with accountability mechanisms are also needed — a call the 2021 article makes itself, not something it claims already exists.

**"Contestability" does not appear anywhere in this article, in its footnoted 17-item framework, or (as far as this card's search could determine) in Wing's 2016 source article.** "Informed consent" is close to but not identical to the framework's actual "informed participation."

## Relation to the argument

Cited three times in the manuscript. Two uses are sound: characterizing the fourth-party tradition as theorizing the system's obligations rather than the participant's capacity (an accurate reading, corroborated independently by `katsh2017.md`), and describing the article's own frame as normative/ethical rather than legal (its own title is "Designing *Ethical* Online Dispute Resolution Systems").

The third use is not sound as originally written, and this card's own earlier version shares the blame: the manuscript's line 252 attributed a four-item "must guarantee... transparency, accountability, contestability, and informed consent" argument directly to Wing et al. (2021) — but that list does not match what the article actually says. It conflates a 17-item external values framework (Wing's own 2016 work, cited but not authored fresh by this article) with a smaller, invented four-item list, includes a value ("contestability") that appears nowhere in the source, and frames values the article itself calls "not rules" as if they were guaranteed obligations. **This card's own prior "What it argues" section made exactly the same error** ("Wing et al. name transparency, accountability, contestability, and consent as the design requirements for legitimacy") — it was corrected during the 2026-08-30 tiered citation audit after a full-text read (including footnotes) contradicted it. The manuscript's line 252 has been rewritten accordingly, correctly attributing the values framework to Wing (2016) as cited within Wing et al. (2021), dropping "contestability" from the misattributed list, and instead making the absence of any contestation-specific principle in the literature part of the manuscript's own argument.

## Caution

An ethics-and-design article, normative rather than empirical, aimed at ODR practitioners; its systems are court-annexed and commercial dispute platforms, not labour platforms, and its parties have entered a process voluntarily. It does not measure anything and has no competence construct. Four authors, Wing first; Katsh is third here, which matters when tracing the fourth-party lineage from the 2001 book. **This card was previously marked full_text/direct_read but its "What it argues" section had not been checked closely enough against the article's own footnotes** — a caution for how much weight "direct_read" alone should carry without a second, adversarial pass. If any future manuscript use of this source states a specific enumerated list, verify it against footnote 3 of the article directly rather than trusting a paraphrase, including this card's own past ones.
