# Wing, L., Martinez, J., Katsh, E., & Rule, C. (2021). Designing ethical online dispute resolution systems: The rise of the fourth party. *Negotiation Journal*, 37(1), 49–64.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/nejo.12350  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Wiley Online Library / MIT Press
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Twenty years on, the fourth party is no longer a metaphor. Wing, Martinez, Katsh and Rule argue that as ODR systems incorporate machine learning, the technology has moved from supporting the human neutral to displacing her — triaging, recommending, and in some designs deciding — and they set out ethical design requirements for the resulting arrangement: transparency about the system's role, accountability for its determinations, contestability, protection against embedded bias, and informed consent to the process. Their core normative claim is that a fourth party exercising decisional power without those safeguards produces outcomes that are efficient and illegitimate.

## Relation to the argument

**The single most important card in this cluster for §9, and the one that most damages the section as written.** This is a peer-reviewed article whose subject is a non-human party that commits binding determinations in a multi-party arrangement, published in a mainstream negotiation journal, with the position explicitly named and theorised. §9's claim that "what none of the seven supplies is the triad" cannot stand unqualified against it.

The revision that survives is clean and makes the paper better. The fourth-party literature theorises **the system's obligations**; algorithmacy theorises **the party's capacity**. Wing et al. name transparency, accountability, contestability, and consent as the design requirements for legitimacy; §5 names substantially the same list as what the platform *withholds*; §7's facets are what a worker does when the requirements are unmet and nobody is going to meet them. Read that way, the fourth-party literature is the paper's strongest antecedent and its complement — one asks what the system owes, the other what a person does when it does not pay.

That framing also strengthens §6's two routes considerably. Route two (institutional remedy) now has a named, developed, twenty-year design literature behind it, which makes the division between the routes a real one rather than a rhetorical balance.

## Caution

An ethics-and-design article, normative rather than empirical, aimed at ODR practitioners; its systems are court-annexed and commercial dispute platforms, not labour platforms, and its parties have entered a process voluntarily. It does not measure anything and has no competence construct. Four authors, Wing first; Katsh is third here, which matters when tracing the fourth-party lineage from the 2001 book.
