## What it argues
Schou and Bucher study an online community of gig workers over time and document solidarity forming and then coming apart. The community begins as a mutual-aid space in which workers share information about clients, interpret platform changes for one another and support members facing sanctions. It develops a collective identity and a sense of shared grievance against the platform. The breakdown then proceeds through identifiable stages, and the mechanism is competitive rather than external: because the workers are competing for the same finite work, information that helps one member costs another, and the norms of sharing erode as the stakes rise. Status hierarchies form inside the community, successful members distance themselves from struggling ones, and the shared grievance fragments into individual explanations of failure. The authors' analytical point is that the same conditions producing solidarity — shared exposure to an opaque and arbitrary system — also produce the competition that dissolves it, so digital solidarity among gig workers is structurally unstable rather than merely fragile.

## Relation to the argument
This is the necessary counterweight to Turkopticon, Wood and Lehdonvirta, and Kougiannou and Mendonça, and the paper's §6 is weaker without it. Those three show workers building institutional capacity from below; Schou and Bucher show the same process running backwards, and they identify the structural reason. Co-optation's design — individual, competitive, non-portable, dispatched one task at a time — undermines the collective route by the same features that make the individual route insufficient. §6 says the institutional route may be "absent or captured"; this article supplies a third failure mode, *dissolved by competition*, and it is the one most specific to the form.

It bears on §10 as well, in a way the paper should confront. If successful members distance themselves from struggling ones and reinterpret failure as individual deficiency, then stratified fluency is not merely a distribution the researcher observes — it is a narrative the population produces about itself, and interviews will collect it. §11's abductive analysis needs to be alert to that, because a cohort explaining outcome variance by individual capacity is evidence about the cohort's account, not about the construct.

## Caution
One online community, studied through its own archived text; the process is documented over time but the setting is a single case and the authors are careful about generalisation. The frame is solidarity and community, and the study says nothing about competency, appeals or accountability directly — the connection to §6 is the paper's inference. Members who left the community are not represented in the data, which biases what breakdown looks like from inside.
