## What it argues

This is the introduction to a special issue and the piece that named the field. The claim, as the abstract fragments give it, is that expectations about science and technology are not predictions to be scored for accuracy but real-time representations of future situations and capabilities that do work in the present. Such expectations are fundamentally generative: they guide activities, provide structure and legitimation, attract interest and foster investment. The abstract sets out expectations as a medium of coordination among actor communities, and names three coordinating directions — horizontal, across communities; vertical, across micro, meso and macro scales; and temporal, as expectations change in response and adaptation to new conditions. Expectations are most intense early in a technology's life, when the vision is very likely to differ from the future that arrives, and citing literature takes from the paper the cycle of hype and disappointment that follows. One citing source quotes the paper on the direction of hype: it is about the future and the new, rarely about the past, which erases continuities. The article is an editorial review rather than an empirical study, and its purpose is to set an agenda for studying how expectations are produced, circulated and made to perform.

## Relation to the argument

Arm: `io_hype_ai_literacy`. The arm's claim is that hype attaches to remedies as well as to technologies, and that AI literacy is a remedy sold on a promise — understand how AI works and you will be safe, employable and in control. Borup and colleagues supply the theory that makes that a hype-studies claim and not a complaint. The promise of AI literacy is an expectation in their sense: it is generative, because it guides what universities, vendors and certification bodies do; it legitimates, because a mandated curriculum is a visible response to a risk; it attracts investment, because a remedy market needs buyers. The three coordinating directions map onto the arm's four producers. Vertically the expectation runs from a UNESCO or OECD framework down to a course syllabus; horizontally it aligns vendors, universities and regulators who otherwise disagree; temporally it has already begun to adapt, from "learn to code" to "learn to prompt" to "learn how the model works".

The arm's sharper point is one the paper's framework permits but does not make. An expectation about a remedy locates a deficit. If the promise is that the person who understands AI will be in control, then the person who is not in control lacked understanding, and the arbiter that evaluated them is out of the frame. The paper's own observation that hype is about the future and the new and rarely about the past is the handle: the AI-literacy promise is about the worker's future competence and never about the platform's present rule. Van Lente is a guest editor of the target issue, and the arm should cite this paper as the field's founding statement and then extend it, in the arm's words, to the remedy market.
