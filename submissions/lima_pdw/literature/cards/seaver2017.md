## What it argues

Seaver's move is to stop treating algorithms as technical objects that culture then reacts to, and to treat them as culture: heterogeneous, contested, made and remade by the people who build and use them, with no single coherent object at the centre. Access, on this view, is not a problem of getting past a wall — the researcher is already inside whatever he can observe people doing with the system — because the system extends through the practices of everyone touching it. He offers scavenging as the working tactic: assemble whatever traces, documents, talk and observations are available rather than waiting for the definitive view from inside.

## Relation to the argument

The strongest available answer to the objection that the site is not really an opaque algorithmic system because the participants built it. Seaver's premise is that algorithmic systems never have a single author and are always partly opaque to their own makers, which is precisely the §2 claim that a cohort of thirty means twenty-nine sources of unannounced revision. Scavenging also describes the corpus: interviews, observation, a rule-change ledger, and repository records assembled together because no one of them is the view from inside.


**Added for the opacity-folk-theories cluster.** Seaver is the sharpest challenge in this cluster to §7's premise, and the manuscript is better for meeting it. If there is no coherent rule behind the outcomes, then "reconstructing a hidden counterpart's wants from outcomes alone" describes a worker chasing something that does not exist in the form she imagines, and the facet risks reifying the folk theory it studies. Section 7's own wording partly survives: the inference runs backward from an outcome to a rule that was never stated, and is checkable only by being tried again, which is a claim about a practice with a success criterion rather than a claim that the rule exists as a stable object — but §7 should say so explicitly, because Seaver otherwise reads as a defeater. He supports the paper elsewhere: if the black box is a relation rather than a property, then opacity is relative to a position, which is what §5's partition asserts and what §10's stratified fluency requires.

## Caution

A tactics paper written for anthropologists; it does not supply analytic procedure. Its "algorithms as culture" premise is also a strong ontological commitment that Paper 2's construct-oriented framing does not otherwise make — cite it for access tactics, not as the paper's theory of algorithms.
