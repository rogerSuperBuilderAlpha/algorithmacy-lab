## What it argues

An "insider econometrics" study of valve-making plants, where the authors know the technology, the product and the production process well enough to specify what the IT investment actually changes. New IT-enhanced machinery shortens setup and run times, which shifts the plant toward product innovation and smaller customised runs; the plants that capture the gain are those that simultaneously change their human-resource practices and raise worker skill requirements. Same equipment, different plants, different returns, and the difference is in the complementary practices and the skills.

## Relation to the argument

The most tightly controlled demonstration in the productivity-paradox tradition that identical technology produces divergent outcomes — the design deliberately holds the technology and the product constant so that only the organisational and skill variation is left to explain the spread. For a paper whose opening premise is "same system, different outcomes," this is the methodological template: to claim residual variance, hold the technology fixed by design and measure what is left.

It also sets the standard §11's study will be judged against. Bartel, Ichniowski and Shaw could make their claim because they knew the production function. A qualitative study of drivers has no equivalent, which is a limitation worth naming in §12 rather than leaving for a reviewer.

## Caution

No abstract in the Crossref deposit — do not attribute numbers without a full-text read. Manufacturing plants, a narrow industry, pre-2007 IT. The unit is the plant, not the worker; individual-level dispersion is not estimated. The explanation the paper lands on is skill and practice, so like the rest of this tradition it is evidence *for* the rival §6 concedes, not against it.
