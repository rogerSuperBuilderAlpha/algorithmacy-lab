## What it argues

Iyamu and colleagues run an Arksey-and-O'Malley scoping review of how AI and algorithmic literacy are conceptualised and measured **among health workers**, searching seven databases for English-language sources from January 2020 to May 2025. Twelve studies met inclusion. The charting framework is the interesting part: they use Nutbeam's health-literacy model, which splits literacy into three levels — *functional* (basic understanding and use), *critical* (evaluation and ethics), and **communicative** (interacting with AI systems **and explaining AI-mediated information** to others).

The results are a catalogue of absence. Evidence concentrates in health-professions education (10 of 12 studies), mostly medical and nursing students, with **no studies of public health practice at all**. Explicit, theory-grounded definitions of AI literacy are uncommon. Measurement runs almost entirely through borrowed self-report instruments — the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale in three studies, the Meta AI Literacy Scale in two, the Scale for the Assessment of Non-Experts' AI Literacy elsewhere, plus ad hoc tools. **Only one of the twelve studies explicitly defined and measured algorithmic literacy as a distinct construct**; in the rest, algorithmic considerations surfaced indirectly, as recognising that AI is present or evaluating AI-generated content.

The finding that matters here is the distributional one. Across all twelve studies, competencies clustered on the functional and critical dimensions — awareness, use, evaluation, ethics — while **communicative literacies were infrequently assessed**. The authors' own framing places health workers as *intermediaries between algorithmically mediated outputs and patients, communities and decision makers*, and then reports that the intermediary function is the one nobody measures.

## Relation to the argument

**The most valuable single source in this cluster for the novelty claim, because it cuts both ways and the net is favourable.**

The threat first, stated honestly. Nutbeam's communicative level *is* the counterpart position, imported into AI literacy from health literacy: interacting with a system and explaining what it produced to another person. If a reviewer knows this framework, "nobody has named it" needs qualifying, because the position has at least been named as a level of literacy, in a tradition older than any algorithm scale.

The rescue is in the same abstract. An independent team, having read twelve studies of AI and algorithmic literacy among workers, reports that the communicative dimension is *infrequently assessed* and that research "largely overlooks communicative competencies essential to clinical and public health practice." So the position exists as a slot in a borrowed framework and does not exist in any instrument. That is the manuscript's claim with better evidence than the manuscript currently offers, and §9's structural argument can be restated with it: the field's own reviewers say the missing element is the one that involves another person, and adding items to functional and critical measures has not produced it. The manuscript should cite this rather than assert the gap.

Two further uses. The review documents that AI literacy among workers is measured with **non-domain-specific self-report tools** borrowed from education research — the AILS, MAILS and SNAIL applied to health workers — which is the strongest available defence of building a purpose-made instrument for a specific arrangement. And "only one of twelve defined algorithmic literacy as a distinct construct" is direct evidence that the naming in this field is looser than its volume suggests.

## Caution

A scoping review, so it carries no primary data: cite it for the state of measurement, never for a finding about health workers. Twelve studies is a thin base, and ten of them are education studies of students rather than practising workers, so it describes a nascent literature rather than a settled one. The population is health workers under clinical decision support — an arrangement where the algorithm advises and a licensed professional decides — which is materially different from a gate that commits determinations binding on both parties, and the manuscript must not treat the two as the same form. Abstract-depth read: the twelve included studies are not individually named in the abstract, so **do not attribute the AILS/MAILS/SNAIL counts to specific papers without the full text.** Crossref's author list was truncated at retrieval and the given names above were taken from the Frontiers article page rather than from Crossref — verify the full author list and initials before this enters a reference list. Frontiers journals use article numbers rather than page ranges; the article number was not retrieved.
