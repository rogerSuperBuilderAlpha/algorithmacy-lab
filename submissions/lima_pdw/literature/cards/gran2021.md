## What it argues

Gran, Booth and Bucher ask whether algorithm awareness constitutes a new, reinforcing digital divide, and test it on a representative web survey of the Norwegian population (N = 1,624, fielded 15–30 November 2018 as part of a larger study of digital cultural consumption). Awareness is measured by direct self-report — "What kind of awareness do you have that algorithms are used to present recommendations, advertisements and other content on the Internet?" — which Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum single out as the most direct measure in the field.

Two results. Levels of awareness differ sharply by demographic: awareness runs higher among men and among the better educated, and declines with age. And attitudes toward algorithm-driven functions — recommendations on YouTube and Spotify, personalised advertising, personalised news feeds — track both the awareness level and the demographics. A cluster analysis yields a six-group typology of the Norwegian population: the unaware, the uncertain, the affirmative, the neutral, the sceptic and the critical.

## Relation to the argument

A resource for §10, not a rival. Awareness is one place on Dogruel's ladder and DeVito's TCL hierarchy — the bottom one — and Gran and colleagues make no claim to be measuring a competency; they are measuring a distribution. The value to the manuscript is that distribution. Stratified fluency asserts that a capacity is unevenly held across equivalently placed people, and this is the population-scale evidence that even bare awareness is unevenly held in one of the world's most digitised countries. Six clusters in a small, rich, connected society is a strong prior that the spread among gig workers facing an evaluative gate is at least as wide.

Use it carefully, because it also cuts. The stratification Gran and colleagues find is *demographic* — gender, education, age. Section 10's claim is stronger and different: that fluency varies among workers who are demographically and structurally alike, occupying the same role on the same platform. If the divide is education and age all the way down, then algorithmacy is a digital-divide phenomenon wearing a new name, and the manuscript needs an argument, or ideally a residual, showing variance that demographics do not absorb. Gran's finding makes that a live obligation rather than an optional refinement.

The counterpart question does not really arise: this is a one-item self-report about whether algorithms are used on the internet. No second party, no determination, no coordination — the two-position form in its thinnest version.

## Caution

Single-item self-reported awareness, no accuracy criterion, no behaviour observed; the paper explicitly frames itself as exploratory. Norway in 2018 is a high-trust, high-income, high-broadband outlier, so the awareness levels do not transfer to gig populations elsewhere. Read at abstract depth — Taylor & Francis blocks automated retrieval and the repository copies are submitted versions behind landing pages, so the cluster sizes, the regression coefficients and the exact response scale are unverified here. N = 1,624 and the fieldwork dates come from secondary reporting of the paper's own figures and should be checked in the PDF before the number appears in the manuscript. The typology labels are the authors' own and read as attitudinal rather than competence categories; do not press them into a fluency hierarchy.
