## What it argues

Kitchin argues that algorithms are hard to research not only because firms hide them but because they are ontologically unstable: heterogeneous, embedded in larger systems, contingently produced by teams under commercial pressure, and constantly rewritten. He then sets out six ways to study them anyway — examining pseudo-code and published documentation, reflexively producing code, reverse-engineering from inputs and outputs, interviewing designers and doing ethnographies of coding teams, unpacking the wider sociotechnical assemblage, and examining how algorithms do work in the world. Each carries costs, and reverse-engineering in particular can only ever recover a functional approximation from the outside.

## Relation to the argument

Kitchin gives the paper a vocabulary for what asymmetric interpretation is *doing* and what its ceiling is. A worker inferring a rule from outcomes is running the outside-in reverse-engineering method, without instruments, on a moving target, with her livelihood on the result. Kitchin's account of why that method under-determines the system is the strongest statement available of why §5's line holds — a reconstruction is a functional approximation and never becomes the rule, so the worker's private forecast cannot convert into shared understanding no matter how good she gets. Section 5 asserts this in a sentence; Kitchin argues it methodologically.

The instability claim also supports temporal tracking as a separate facet rather than a special case of interpretation. If the object is contingently produced and continually rewritten, then a correct reconstruction has a shelf life, and detecting that the shelf life has expired is a distinct operation from making the reconstruction. Pair with `metaxa2021` and `bandy2021` for what the professional version of the same inference costs in instrumentation.

## Caution

A methodological essay for researchers, not a study of lay or worker inference; Kitchin's subject is how scholars should approach algorithms, and reading his six approaches as a model of what a worker does is this paper's move. He is writing about algorithms broadly, not about evaluative gates that commit binding determinations on two parties, so nothing in him speaks to the counterpart position §9 says the neighbouring constructs drop.
