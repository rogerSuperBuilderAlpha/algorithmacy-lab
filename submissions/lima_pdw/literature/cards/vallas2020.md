## What it argues
Vallas and Schor review the gig-economy literature and sort its competing images of what a platform is: an entrepreneurial incubator, a double agent serving two sides, a chameleon that presents differently to workers, customers and regulators, an unregulated market-maker, and their own preferred image, the *permissive potentate*. The last is the argument. Platforms exercise power not by directing work in the manner of a firm but by setting the terms of a space and then leaving workers formally free inside it — permitting choice over hours, acceptance and routing while controlling pricing, matching, information and continued access. The combination is what makes the arrangement distinctive: autonomy over the small decisions and no standing over any of the large ones. They trace how the arrangement produces high measured autonomy alongside low control, how the algorithmic apparatus substitutes for supervision, and how deactivation functions as the ultimate sanction — exercised without process and without an obligation to explain, and effective precisely because a worker's ratings and history do not travel. They also review the emerging politics: litigation over classification, regulatory intervention, and worker organising outside the union form.

## Relation to the argument
The permissive potentate is the sociological description of what §5 formalises. Choice without standing is the paper's structure stated as an empirical finding about the form, and the sorting of what workers control (small decisions) from what they do not (terms, access, information) is close to the withholding analysis §5 performs condition by condition. The review supplies the paper's warrant that this description is the field's, not one author's.

Deactivation is the specific contribution to §5's accountability claim. A sanction imposed without a stated reason, with no obligation to justify, and with no body to which the decision can be taken is a textbook absence of Bovens's forum, and it is the case where the stakes are highest and the contestation literature thinnest. The review also documents that workers respond to it by managing risk rather than by contesting, which is the behavioural signature §5 predicts.

For §6 the review's politics section is the map: litigation, regulation and organising are the three institutional routes actually being attempted, and it reports on each without the paper having to assemble the evidence itself.

## Caution
It is a review, so cite it for the state of the field and go to the primary studies for any specific finding. Its scope is heavily US and European, and its cases are labour platforms — ride-hail, delivery, freelancing — so a business-school peer-review gate falls well outside it and the analogy must be argued in §11 rather than assumed. The permissive-potentate framing is the authors' interpretive contribution, not a consensus label. Published 2020; the litigation and regulatory landscape it describes has moved substantially since.
