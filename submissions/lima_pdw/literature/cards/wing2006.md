## What it argues

Three pages, no data, and the founding document of a field. Wing's claim is that the concepts computer scientists use to attack problems are general-purpose mental tools and belong in everyone's education: "To reading, writing, and arithmetic, we should add computational thinking to every child's analytical ability." Her working definition is broad — computational thinking "involves solving problems, designing systems, and understanding human behavior, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science" — and she fills it out by ostension rather than by taxonomy, listing abstraction, decomposition, separation of concerns, recursion, parallelism, invariants, heuristic reasoning, trade-offs between time and space, and reformulating a hard problem into a solved one "by reduction, embedding, transformation, or simulation." The everyday illustrations are deliberately domestic: packing a schoolbag is prefetching and caching, retracing steps is backtracking, choosing a supermarket queue is performance modelling for multi-server systems.

The article's most-cited section is the six-point "What it is, and isn't": *conceptualizing, not programming*; *fundamental, not rote skill*; *a way that humans, not computers, think*; *complements and combines mathematical and engineering thinking*; *ideas, not artifacts*; *for everyone, everywhere*. Two of these bear directly on the paper's discriminations. Wing insists computational thinking is a *fundamental skill* — "something every human being must know to function in modern society" — and her closing move is curricular: teach a "Ways to Think Like a Computer Scientist" course to freshmen, reach the pre-college audience, expose school students to computational methods.

One line deserves attention because it is the closest Wing comes to the paper's subject: computational thinking is "having the confidence we can safely use, modify, and influence a large complex system without understanding its every detail."

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour, and the one whose distance from algorithmacy is easiest to state and most useful to state, because computational thinking is the ancestor of most of the literacy constructs §9 sorts.

**The triad.** Wing's relation has two positions and neither of them is a counterpart: a thinker and a problem. Other people appear once, in the "ideas, not artifacts" bullet, where computational concepts help us "communicate and interact with other people" — a subordinate clause, not a structural position. Nothing in the article concerns a party whose outcome is jointly determined, and nothing concerns a system that commits determinations. The line about influencing a complex system without understanding its every detail is tantalising and must not be over-read: Wing means abstraction barriers in systems one is *building*, not opacity imposed on a party by a system that evaluates her.

**Skill, by the author's own word.** "A fundamental skill for everyone." Wing's whole argumentative purpose is to get it taught — to freshmen, to school pupils, to teachers and parents. That places computational thinking in §8's top row, trainable by instruction, and about as far from implicit acquisition as a construct can get. The manuscript can use this cleanly: the field's founding construct is explicitly an instructional target, which is why the family it spawned inherits teachability.

**Uniformity.** Wing assumes universality — "for everyone, everywhere," a reality "when it is so integral to human endeavors it disappears as an explicit philosophy." §10's stratified-fluency property asserts the opposite for algorithmacy: uneven distribution across equivalently placed workers is a defining property, not a curricular failure to be corrected.

## Caution

A Viewpoint column, not a research article: no definition tight enough to operationalise, no evidence, no measure, no sample. Do not cite it for anything empirical, and do not cite it as the *definition* of computational thinking — the definitional work happened afterwards, and Grover and Pea (2013) is the honest citation for what the field made of it. The article's own logic is professional advocacy for computer science as much as it is a theory of cognition; the closing pages argue for the discipline's image and enrolment. Cite it for the founding claim and its curricular framing.
