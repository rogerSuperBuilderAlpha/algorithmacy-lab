## What it argues
Veale, Silberman and Binns examine the algorithmic-management chapter of the EU's proposed Platform Work Directive and argue that its protections, as drafted, will not bind. The Directive would give platform workers rights to information about automated monitoring and decision systems, to human review of significant decisions, and to explanation and to contest — an apparatus modelled on data-protection rights and extended into labour law. The authors' argument is that the individual-rights model imported from the GDPR carries its known weaknesses with it: rights that depend on a worker noticing, asking, and pursuing will be exercised rarely and unevenly, and the obligations are drafted loosely enough that a platform can satisfy them formally while changing nothing operationally. Their proposed fortifications shift weight toward collective and institutional instruments — worker representatives and unions with information and consultation rights over the systems, obligations owed to regulators rather than only to individuals, ex ante requirements on system design and documentation, and enforcement that does not depend on individual complaint.

## Relation to the argument
This is the most direct statement in the cluster of §6's institutional route applied to platform work, written by authors who have thought about individual rights closely enough to distrust them. Their central move is the paper's: a right held by a worker, exercisable by her effort, will not supply the condition, and what supplies it is an arrangement with standing that operates whether or not she acts. For a reviewer asking what §6's institutional column would look like in practice for the actual population the paper is about, this is the citation.

It also gives the paper a live policy reference for the claim that the division is checkable. §6 predicts that forums and statutory review move accountability while training and selection do not, and the Platform Work Directive's algorithmic-management chapter is a natural experiment in progress. Naming it would let §6 say where the prediction will be tested.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth only; do not quote or attribute specific drafting critiques without pulling the article. It analyses a *proposal* — the Directive's text moved substantially after this article, and the adopted instrument differs, so nothing here should be cited as current EU law. Its jurisdiction is the EU, and the paper's fieldwork sites are Trinidad and Tobago and a business-school cohort, where none of this applies. Silberman's name is styled with the nickname in quotation marks in the byline; check the exact form against the published article before it enters a reference list.
