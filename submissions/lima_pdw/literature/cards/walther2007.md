## What it argues

Walther unpacks the sender side of the hyperpersonal model into its technological, linguistic, and cognitive constituents. Technologically, editability and asynchrony give the sender **cognitive resources** otherwise consumed by real-time interaction management, which can be redirected to message construction. Linguistically, senders exploit specific choices — pronoun use, verb tense and aspect, positivity, self-reference — to manage impressions. Cognitively, selective self-presentation is a strategic, effortful process that competes for attention with everything else the sender is doing.

The reallocation claim is the paper's core: mediation does not simply subtract cues, it frees capacity, and what a communicator does with the freed capacity is what produces the effect.

## Relation to the argument

The resource-reallocation argument is a mechanism §10 could use and does not have. §10 says algorithmacy is unevenly distributed across equivalently placed workers and offers no account of *why* beyond differential exposure. Walther supplies a candidate: the cognitive capacity a mediated channel frees is not free for everyone. A worker under time pressure, holding several platform jobs, or composing in a second language has less of it to redirect into packaging her submission — so stratified fluency would partly track the distribution of slack rather than the distribution of learning.

That is a rival explanation with a distinctive prediction, and naming it makes §10's falsifier sharper: if scores track available cognitive slack rather than accumulated participation, the implicit-acquisition property is measuring the wrong thing.

## Caution

A theoretical elaboration with selective empirical support, aimed at online dating and self-presentation contexts. The linguistic markers are English-specific — a real limitation for a study whose cohorts are in Trinidad and Tobago and Peru. The cognitive-resources claim is argued rather than measured.
