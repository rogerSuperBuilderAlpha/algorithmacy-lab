## What it argues

Natale argues that AI's social power rests on **banal deception**: not fraud, but the routine, low-level ways interfaces cue users into treating them as more intelligent, more understanding, and more agentic than they are. The Turing test, on his reading, was never a test of machine intelligence but a test of human interpretation, and the field's history is a history of designing for the user's projective tendencies. His cases are ELIZA, chatbots, and voice assistants, and his consistent finding is that users' sense of a machine's understanding is manufactured by design choices with no relation to its actual capacities.

## Relation to the argument

A caution aimed squarely at §7's first facet. Asymmetric interpretation presumes there is a rule to be inferred and that inference can succeed or fail against it. Natale's argument is that interfaces are built to make users feel their inferences are landing — that the system understood — independent of whether they did. If so, a worker's confidence in her model of the gate is not evidence about the model's accuracy, and **self-report measures of asymmetric interpretation would be measuring the interface's persuasiveness rather than the worker's competence.** That is a serious measurement threat, and §11 should have an answer.

For §9 the book is supporting rather than threatening: Natale's whole argument is that treating the machine as a communicative partner is an artefact of design, which undercuts the HMC premise from a different direction than the paper does.

## Caution

Media history and theory; historical and interpretive argument, no data, no measurement. Natale's cases are conversational interfaces designed to be liked, which is a different design objective from an evaluative gate designed to be efficient — the banal-deception argument may not transfer to systems that make no effort to seem understanding. Crossref lists the record as an edited-book; it is a single-authored monograph.
