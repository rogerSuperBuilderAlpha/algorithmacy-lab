## What it argues

This is a BISE discussion piece — a panel write-up rather than a research article, and its nine authors are the field's current centre of gravity on the topic. Four organisers (Heimbach, Abramova, Baumann, Krasnova) convened five information-systems scholars (Jussupow, Legner, Meythaler, Müller, Pinski), and the article synthesises their positions across three themes: the **conceptualisation**, **measurement**, and **education** of algorithmic literacy. Pinski in particular arrives with his own AI-literacy conceptualisation work behind him.

The working definition on offer is four-part: being aware that algorithms operate in online applications, platforms and services; knowing how algorithms work; being able to evaluate algorithmic decision-making critically; and having the skills to cope with — or even influence — algorithmic operations. That fourth clause is the interesting one, because it moves beyond knowledge into action on the system, and it is the clause that makes this definition broader than the older awareness-and-knowledge formulations.

The panel format matters for what the piece can be used for. It is a statement of where a discipline thinks the construct stands and where it should go, from the people who will review work in this area, published in an IS journal that an organisation-studies roundtable will recognise.

## Relation to the argument

**Rival on standing, resource on diagnosis.** Whatever the manuscript says about the state of this literature, the state of this literature has a 2026 nine-author statement of its own, and a reviewer may reach for it. Being unaware of it would be worse than disagreeing with it.

The definition's four clauses map onto the manuscript's §9 sorting almost exactly, and all four sit on the worker–system axis. Awareness, knowledge and critical evaluation are the algorithm as an object of knowledge. "Coping with or influencing algorithmic operations" is the algorithm as something acted upon. **No clause concerns another person.** The manuscript's structural claim — that the missing element is a position rather than a variable — survives against the field's own most recent consolidated definition, which is a stronger place to make the claim than against any single scale.

The piece is also a resource on measurement. A panel convened partly *because* the construct's measurement is unsettled is evidence for the manuscript's premise, in the same direction as Gagrčin, Naab and Grub's review of 169 studies and Iyamu et al.'s review of twelve worker-focused studies. Three independent stocktakings in two years, all reporting that the field has not settled what to measure, is a better foundation for "nobody has named it" than the assertion currently in the abstract.

## Caution

**Read at metadata depth.** The four-part definition and the three panel themes come from secondary reporting, not from the article; the definition is very likely a quotation the panel adopted from prior literature rather than a new one they coined, and the manuscript must not attribute it to these authors without reading the piece. Verify the full nine-author list and the ordering before citing — Crossref returns all nine, but shortened citations in the wild list only four. This is a discussion or catchword article of seven pages, not empirical work: it has no data, no sample and no instrument, and cannot be cited for any finding. Its disciplinary frame is information systems, which shapes what it counts as literacy; an organisation-studies reader may find its individual-user emphasis narrow, and that is a fair point to make rather than a weakness to hide.
