# Nass, C., & Moon, Y. (2000). Machines and mindlessness: Social responses to computers. *Journal of Social Issues*, 56(1), 81–103.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/0022-4537.00153  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Wiley Online Library
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

The summary statement of the Computers Are Social Actors programme. Nass and Moon review a decade of experiments showing people apply social rules to computers — reciprocity, politeness, gender stereotyping, in-group favouritism, responses to flattery — while explicitly denying that they do. Their explanation is **mindlessness** rather than anthropomorphism: users are not deceived about the machine's ontological status; they are running social scripts triggered by superficial cues, without attending to whether the target warrants them.

## Relation to the argument

Useful chiefly as a corrective to an assumption §7 makes implicitly. The three facets describe a worker who models the system, infers its rule, and packages for it — a deliberate, effortful, cognitively engaged actor. Nass and Moon's evidence is that a great deal of human behaviour toward machines is none of those things. If most workers most of the time are running social scripts at the gate rather than reasoning about it, then algorithmacy describes a minority of behaviour, and §10's stratified fluency might be measuring the distribution of *mindfulness* rather than of accumulated competence.

That is not fatal — it is arguably a description of what the competency consists in, and the paper could adopt it: algorithmacy begins where mindless social response stops working. But the paper should say so, because "some workers treat the gate as a social partner and some as a system to be modelled" is a plausible and testable account of §10's variance that does not require a competency construct at all.

## Caution

A review of the authors' own programme, argued rather than meta-analysed; the CASA findings have faced replication and boundary-condition challenges, and the mindlessness explanation was contested at the time by anthropomorphism accounts. 1990s desktop interfaces. Moon is second author.
