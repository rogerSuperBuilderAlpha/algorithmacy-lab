## What it argues

Klawitter and Hargittai name the construct the manuscript's §8 has to argue against, and they name it as a species of a larger genus: algorithmic skills are "a subset of Internet skills" (p. 3492). **Algorithmic skills** are "users' knowledge about algorithms and their role in making online content visible, as well as users' ability to figure out how particular algorithms work, and then leverage that knowledge when producing and sharing content." Know-how, in their gloss, not knowledge alone — and subordinate, by their own taxonomy, to internet skills generally.

The evidence is 25 semi-structured interviews conducted in 2015 with U.S.-based women aged 55 and under who make and sell handmade pottery, jewellery and textiles. The interviews were the third phase of a longitudinal multimodal study running through 2014–2015 that also collected 43 surveys and, for 39 participants, social media posts and e-commerce sales data. Recruitment ran at ten midwestern arts and crafts fairs and through four selling platforms — Aftcra, Artfire, TheCraftStar and Etsy. Mean participant age 41; most held a bachelor's degree or higher. The authors are explicit that the sample is highly educated and highly motivated, and that this makes the low end of what they found more alarming, not less: if these sellers struggle, the sellers who most need algorithmic skills likely have fewer.

The finding is a gradient. Most participants recognised that algorithms govern who sees their wares; only some knew how to configure listings, tags and posts to improve their odds of reaching buyers. Recognition and capability came apart.

## Relation to the argument

Two jobs for this card. First, it is the source that puts "skill" in the field's vocabulary, and §8's competency/skill/capability distinction has to survive a reader who knows it. The distinction does survive, and Klawitter and Hargittai's own construction shows why: their algorithmic skills are trainable by instruction (the practical remedy is a tagging tutorial), tool-indexed (Etsy's search in 2015, a system that no longer exists in that form), and possessed by an individual acting alone on her own listings. That is skill in the manuscript's sense — it dates with the tool. Algorithmacy's claim is that a competency dates with the *form*, and the form persists across Etsy, Upwork and whatever succeeds them.

Second, this is the sharpest test of the audience/counterpart line, because these workers are earning income and the algorithm decides whether they earn it. A seller who fails at algorithmic skills makes no sales. Say the difference precisely: the seller is reaching a *market* — an anonymous distribution of possible buyers, none of whom is a party to any determination. Nobody on the other end submitted anything the algorithm evaluated; nobody is bound by its output. Etsy's ranking commits a determination about the seller's visibility and about nobody else. Algorithmacy's triad requires that the intermediary interpret two parties and issue an output binding on both — a client's brief and a worker's proposal, matched, rated and priced. Reaching an audience is a one-sided visibility problem; coordinating through a committing gate is a two-sided problem in which the other party's intent is itself the object of inference. The manuscript should use Klawitter and Hargittai as the clearest illustration of the difference, precisely because the stakes here are real money.

Also worth §10: the recognition/capability gap the authors report is stratified fluency in a population of near-identical placement — same country, same trades, similar education, same platforms.

## Caution

Twenty-five interviews, all women, all U.S.-based, all in three handmade craft categories, all in 2015 — the platform algorithms described no longer exist. Self-report about a system whose ground truth the researchers cannot check, a limitation the companion paper (`hargittai2020.md`) makes into its topic.
