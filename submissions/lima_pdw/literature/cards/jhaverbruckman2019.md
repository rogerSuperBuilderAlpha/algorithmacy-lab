## What it argues

Thirty-two million Reddit posts, characterised for what removal explanations users receive and linked to subsequent behaviour. Topic modelling shows explanations often teach the community's social norms. Regression models show explanations play a role in future activity, and the headline result is that **offering explanations for content moderation reduces the odds of future post removals**. Explanations from human moderators held no significant advantage over explanations from bots.

## Relation to the argument

The strongest quantitative evidence available for a claim §6 needs and §7 assumes: that the rule can be learned when it is stated, and that the learning shows up in behaviour rather than only in attitude. Thirty-two million posts is a scale nothing else in this cluster approaches.

Its more interesting service is to §5's accountability argument, where it cuts a fine line. An explanation is not a forum — nobody is answering to the user, and the user cannot demand anything — yet explanations changed subsequent conduct. That is consistent with §5, which says predictability can be supplied and accountability cannot, and it sharpens the distinction usefully: what the explanation supplied was a rule, not standing. The bot-versus-human null makes the point sharper still. If a bot's explanation works as well as a person's, then what is doing the work is information rather than the presence of somebody answerable, which is exactly §5's separation, empirically demonstrated.

## Caution

Observational, at scale but not experimental; the abstract reports regression evidence, so the causal claim about explanations reducing future removals should be treated as associational until the identification strategy is checked. Reddit's volunteer moderation, not a commercial evaluative gate. Read at abstract depth — the bot-versus-human null is the load-bearing item here and must be verified in full text before it carries §5's argument.
