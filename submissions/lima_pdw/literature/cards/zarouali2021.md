## What it argues

Zarouali, Boerman and de Vreese build a standardised measure of algorithmic awareness where none existed. Three phases: deductive item generation from conceptual definitions (28 items on a five-point "not at all aware" to "completely aware" scale), expert content-validity review that cut 11 items and left a pool of 17 across five theorised dimensions, and validation on a Dutch national sample split into three platform subsamples — Netflix (n = 741, exploratory), Facebook (n = 688, confirmatory) and YouTube (n = 677, replication), 2,106 respondents in all, each seeing the items keyed to one platform.

The five theorised dimensions came out as four. EFA on the Netflix subsample returned a four-factor solution explaining 74% of the variance; the three algorithmic-persuasion items failed to load cleanly and were dropped, as was one human–algorithm-interplay item with low communality. The published AMCA-scale is 13 items in four dimensions: **content filtering** (4 items), **automated decision-making** (3), **human–algorithm interplay** (3) and **ethical considerations** (3). CFA on the Facebook subsample fit without re-specification (χ²/df = 3.46, RMSEA = .060, SRMR = .02, CFI = .99, TLI = .98), loadings .69–.92, Cronbach's α of .92, .92, .86 and .89 across the four dimensions, AVE .70–.79. The YouTube replication fit better still (RMSEA = .048, SRMR = .01, CFI = .99, TLI = .99) with αs of .92, .94, .92 and .88, and the scale predicted respondents' algorithmic knowledge (b = 0.83, p < .001, R² = .21). A second-order model held in both subsamples, so the subscales can be used singly or summed.

The items are worth reading as a set, because every one of them has the same grammatical subject and the same object: "Algorithms are used to recommend [media content] **to me** on [platform]"; "Algorithms are used to show someone else see different [media content] than I get to see"; "The [media content] that algorithms recommend to me depend on my online behavior"; "Algorithms use my personal data to recommend certain [media content], and this has consequences for my online privacy."

## Relation to the argument

The best available demonstration of §9's structural claim, and the manuscript should use it that way — quote the items. Thirteen items, professionally developed, expert-reviewed, factor-analysed and replicated across three platforms, and in all thirteen the relation runs *algorithm → me*. Item FIL4 comes closest to a second person and shows the limit exactly: another user exists there only as someone who receives a different feed, never as a party whose submission the same system is also weighing, and never as someone bound by a determination the respondent is also bound by. The missing element is a position, not a variable, and a validated 13-item instrument built by three competent psychometricians makes the point better than an argument does.

Two subsidiary uses. The construct is awareness only — self-rated awareness at that, which Hargittai and colleagues warn against (`hargittai2020.md`) — so it sits at the bottom of every competence hierarchy in the field and cannot be a rival to a competency. And the fate of the algorithmic-persuasion dimension is a small methodological warning for §11: a theoretically clean facet failed to survive contact with respondents. A three-facet derivation is a prediction that can fail the same way.

## Caution

Awareness of algorithms in *media content* recommendation on three consumer entertainment and social platforms. Self-report of awareness, not knowledge tested and not behaviour observed; a respondent who says she is "completely aware" that algorithms tailor content may be wrong about everything else. Platform-specific by design, which the authors treat as robustness and Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum treat as a limitation — the scale must be re-keyed for any new platform and had not been used beyond Facebook, Netflix and YouTube as of their 2025 review. Dutch sample; items were written in English and professionally translated.
