## What it argues
Colquitt and colleagues meta-analyse twenty-five years of justice research across 183 studies and settle several disputes at once. The four justice dimensions are distinguishable and correlated, and each carries incremental prediction beyond the others rather than being redundant. Their pattern of prediction differs systematically: procedural justice is the strongest predictor of system-referenced outcomes — organisational commitment, rule compliance, trust in the organisation — while interpersonal and informational justice predict agent-referenced outcomes, principally evaluation of and trust in the supervisor, and distributive justice predicts outcome satisfaction. The review also tests the interaction the field had claimed, in which procedural fairness matters most when outcomes are unfavourable, and finds support for it. Their broader conclusion is that fairness perceptions are consequential for behaviour independent of outcomes, and that the field's proliferation of dimensions is warranted by the differential prediction rather than being taxonomic excess.

## Relation to the argument
The differential prediction is the finding the paper should use, because it is close to an independent test of §6's division. Justice research has already established that a judgement about the arrangement and a judgement about a party's conduct predict different things, are correlated but separable, and respond to different interventions. §6 predicts that training and selection move the conditions a worker supplies while forums and appeal move accountability — a claim of exactly this shape, in a literature that has confirmed the shape twice over for a different pair of constructs. The paper can point to the precedent and say that its division is the kind of claim that has been vindicated before.

The system/agent distinction also gives §5's hinge a second empirical footing beyond Bovens's conceptual argument, and it comes from the discipline most likely to supply the paper's reviewers.

## Caution
Meta-analysis of studies conducted almost entirely in conventional employment, most of them cross-sectional and self-report, so the correlations are between perceptions measured at one time. It is now two decades old and later meta-analyses have refined several estimates. Everything measured is perceived justice; the review contains no evidence about whether any arrangement actually delivered accountability, and it does not use the concept. The system/agent distinction is a pattern in outcome correlations, not a theory of what accountability is — the alignment with §5 is a useful parallel and not a validation.
