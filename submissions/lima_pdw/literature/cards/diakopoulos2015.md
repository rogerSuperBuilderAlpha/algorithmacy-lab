## What it argues
Diakopoulos treats algorithms as decision-making power that operates without disclosure and asks what journalism can do about it. His framing is that algorithms exercise power through a small number of recurring decisions — prioritisation, classification, association, filtering — and that each carries embedded criteria and thresholds that are the proper object of scrutiny. Because the code is not available, he proposes *reverse engineering* as the investigative method: probing a system's inputs and outputs to characterise the rule without access to its internals, either by observing outputs alone or by supplying constructed inputs and reading what comes back. He works through cases where journalists did this, and he catalogues the obstacles: systems that change under the investigator, legal exposure under computer-misuse and terms-of-service regimes, the difficulty of establishing that a pattern is intentional, and the resource asymmetry between newsroom and platform. The conclusion is that algorithmic accountability is achievable but that it is an *institutional* achievement of the press acting as an external forum, requiring technical capacity, legal cover and organisational commitment.

## Relation to the argument
Diakopoulos is where the phrase "algorithmic accountability" enters the literature the paper is drawing on, and it belongs in §6 rather than §5, for a reason the paper should make explicit. His answer to opacity is a forum — the press — with the standing, resources and legal position to demand and to publish. That is the institutional route, and it arrives through skilled individual effort by a reporter, which is precisely the case §5 anticipates when it concedes that "a skilled worker can construct answerability through third parties... she finds a journalist." Diakopoulos supplies the detail that makes the concession safe: what the reporter brings is not a substitute for the forum but a route to it, and everything that makes the investigation work — the institutional backing, the legal cover, the capacity to publish — the worker does not have.

His reverse-engineering method is also the closest published description of what §7 calls asymmetric interpretation, performed by a professional with a budget. That parallel is worth drawing, because it shows the same operation scaling from a private folk theory to a published account only when an institution stands behind it. And his obstacle list — systems that change under the investigator — is temporal tracking named as a methodological problem.

## Caution
Read at abstract and companion-report depth only; do not quote the article. The audience is journalism and journalism studies, and "accountability" is used loosely here rather than in Bovens's technical sense — Diakopoulos does not analyse whether the press satisfies the forum criteria, and citing him for the paper's definitional claim would be a mismatch. The piece predates the current regulatory apparatus and the modern explainability literature, so its picture of what is legally possible is dated. Reverse engineering is presented as a method for investigators with resources; it is not evidence about what an individual worker can do.
