## What it argues
Where Wachter and colleagues ask whether a right to explanation exists, Edwards and Veale ask whether it would help, and answer mostly no. The right is "a compellingly attractive remedy" that "intuitively promises to open the algorithmic 'black box,'" and they argue it will not deliver the redress for unfairness, discrimination and opacity that motivates it. The case builds in two moves. First, an anatomy of the GDPR machinery — Articles 13 to 15 and 22, the "solely" and "significant effects" limits, the non-binding status of Recital 71 — concluding that the law here is "restrictive, unclear, or even paradoxical," so the individual right is hemmed in before it starts. Second, and this is their distinctive contribution, a taxonomy of what explanation could mean technically: *model-centric* explanations about the system as a whole against *subject-centric* explanations of the region of the model around one input, and *pedagogical* explanations learned from outside the black box against *decompositional* ones that prise the model apart. The pedagogical, subject-centric combination is useful to an individual while dodging the trade-secret objections controllers raise against disclosure.

Their central warning is the **transparency fallacy**: an individual right to explanation looks empowering and delivers little, because most data subjects lack the time, expertise and leverage to act on an explanation, and the explanations that are cheap to give are the least useful. Their positive move goes elsewhere — to erasure and portability at the individual level, and above all to the *systemic, ex ante* instruments of privacy by design, data protection impact assessments, and certification, which they call the seeds for making algorithms more responsible and human-centred.

## Relation to the argument
The transparency fallacy is the closest thing in the legal literature to the paper's own main result, and §6 should engage it directly rather than merely cite it. Edwards and Veale examined an individual right, judged that it transfers a burden to a party without the time, expertise or leverage to discharge it, and concluded that the remedy has to be systemic and ex ante. That is §6's division reached by a different route, in a different discipline, about a different instrument — and the convergence is the paper's best defence against the charge that the division is an artefact of how it framed the question.

The word *leverage* is the one to lift. Edwards and Veale name three things the individual lacks, and the paper's §5 says two of them are supply-able privately: time and expertise are, at the margin, what algorithmacy consists of. Leverage is not, and leverage is the standing to make an answer follow. Their trio is therefore an independent restatement of the paper's split between what a competency reaches and what it cannot.

Their subject-centric/pedagogical taxonomy also describes §7's asymmetric interpretation exactly: learning an approximate model of a system's behaviour from the outside, focused on the region around one's own case, is what a worker does when she reads a rejection back to a rule. The paper can note that the legal literature's best available explanation type is one the worker already constructs for herself, which is why supplying it changes less than expected.

## Caution
It is one position in a three-way dispute with Wachter et al. and Selbst and Powles, and its conclusions about the GDPR predate subsequent guidance, national implementations and CJEU case law — check currency before any claim about the present legal position. The domain is EU data protection and the subject is a data subject; workers appear only in passing. Their preferred systemic instruments (DPIAs, certification) are regulatory compliance mechanisms, not forums in Bovens's sense — they produce documents and audits, not obligations to answer a particular person — so do not let §6's institutional column absorb them without noticing the difference.
