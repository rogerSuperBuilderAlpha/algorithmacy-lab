## What it argues

Beane asks what happens to learning-by-participation when a new technology removes the participation. Two studies — a two-year, five-site comparative ethnography of robotic and traditional surgical practice, and a blinded interview study across thirteen top-tier US teaching hospitals. In open surgery, trainees learn as the situated-learning literature predicts: increasing participation in real work under a supervising surgeon. Robotic surgery destroys that path. The attending sits at a console, the trainee cannot take the instruments, and the graded hands-on role that carried learning is gone. Only a minority of robotic trainees reach competence, and they do it through what Beane calls **shadow learning**: an interconnected set of norm- and policy-challenging practices, enacted extensively, opportunistically, and in relative isolation. The three he documents are **premature specialization** (committing to robotics early, at the cost of general training), **abstract rehearsal** (extensive practice on simulators, recorded procedures, and online video, away from the operating room), and **undersupervised practice** (taking on more of the live procedure than policy permits, with the attending's tacit consent).

## Relation to the argument

The best single analogue in the literature for what §10 claims and for what the Lima interview shows, and it should carry a substantial part of the paper's argument. Beane's setting reproduces the structure algorithmacy is built for: the work is mediated by a system that interposes itself between the learner and the practice, the approved route to competence stops working, and the result is not uniform failure but **stratification** — a minority reach competence by illegitimate means, and the rest do not. That is §10's two properties, demonstrated together in one study, with a named mechanism connecting them. Abstract rehearsal is the direct precedent for the interview wrinkle: Beane's successful trainees learned from recorded procedures and video rather than from the community, exactly as the Lima participant learned from pasting bot responses into an assistant rather than from the cohort. The word *shadow* is doing real work there, and the paper should take the point that comes with it — routes to competence that bypass the community are typically norm-challenging, unevenly available, and unacknowledged by the institution, which is a sharper version of §6's distributive claim than the paper currently makes. Beane also supplies the falsifier §10 needs but does not state: if a legitimate participatory route exists and works, shadow routes should be rare.

## Caution

An ethnography of surgical training in elite US teaching hospitals, an unusually credentialed and regulated setting with an identifiable expert who does hold the knowledge — which is precisely the condition the paper says is absent at an opaque gate. The analogy therefore holds on the blocked-participation axis and breaks on the undisclosed-rule axis, and the paper should say which one it is borrowing. Abstract-level read; the three shadow-learning practices are verified from the abstract, and the comparative findings across the thirteen hospitals are not.
