## What it argues

Burrell separates three kinds of opacity that are routinely run together: opacity as intentional corporate or state secrecy, opacity as technical illiteracy on the part of those affected, and opacity that arises from the mismatch between how machine-learning models operate at scale and how humans reason. Each has a different remedy, and confusing them produces transparency proposals that cannot work.

## Relation to the argument

Useful for precision in the site-fit argument. The gate's opacity is of the first kind — the vote is deliberately private — not the third, and saying so with Burrell's taxonomy keeps the paper from overclaiming a machine-learning problem it does not have. It also sharpens §10's falsification condition: if participants report knowing who voted against them, the withholding condition fails, and the failure is a failure of *secrecy*, which is checkable.


**Added for the opacity-folk-theories cluster.** Burrell supplies the anatomy of the withholding that §5 treats as a partition. Section 5 says common understanding is withheld in both directions and predictability is withheld and inverted; Burrell says *at which layer* a given withholding sits, and therefore whether disclosure could in principle repair it. That bears on §6's division: where the opacity is layer one, an institutional route (statutory disclosure, mandated explanation) is live; where it is layer three, no forum can produce the missing rule. All three facets in §7 are layer-one and layer-two responses — a worker reconstructs a hidden rule because somebody chose not to state it, not because the model is inexplicable in principle. The stacking claim is also §7's defence against the objection that the facets answer a technical limitation rather than a form's design choice: a platform's ability to shelter a governance choice behind a method-intrinsic justification is part of the form. Pairs with `pasquale2015` (firm-level secrecy) and `ananny2018` (why disclosure would not suffice even at layer one).
