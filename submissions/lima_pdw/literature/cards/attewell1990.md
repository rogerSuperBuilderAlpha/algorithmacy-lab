## What it argues

Attewell shows that sociology has been running four incompatible conceptions of skill under one word, and that several of its long-standing impasses follow from the confusion. The **positivist** conception treats skill as an objective property of a job, measurable by complexity, training time, or discretion — the conception behind deskilling debates and job-evaluation schemes. The **ethnomethodological** conception reverses the emphasis: what looks unskilled is only unskilled because it is taken for granted, and everyday competences such as walking across a crowded room or holding a conversation are extraordinarily complex, so skill is inversely related to how visible it is. The **Weberian** conception treats skill as a claim: occupations construct and defend skill designations to protect their market position, so a skill is what an occupational group has persuaded others to call one. The **Marxist** conception ties skill to the labour process and to control over it. Attewell's point is that these traditions talk past each other, and that empirical research needs to declare which conception it is using.

## Relation to the argument

The most serious challenge to §8's word choice after Dreyfus, and it operates on a different axis. §8 rejects "skill" on the ground that skill is trainable by instruction and tool-indexed — which is the **positivist** conception, and only that one. On the **ethnomethodological** conception, skill is precisely the tacit, un-instructable competence at ordinary activity that becomes invisible through familiarity, and algorithmacy as §7 derives it is a textbook case. On the **Weberian** conception, whether algorithmacy is a skill is not a conceptual question at all but a question about whether anyone succeeds in getting it recognized as one — which is a genuinely interesting reading of §6's distributive argument, since a capacity that goes unnamed cannot be bargained over. §8 therefore does not rule skill out; it selects one of four meanings and rules that one out. The fix is a clause: name the positivist conception explicitly, cite Attewell for the plurality, and the sentence becomes defensible instead of stipulative. There is also an opportunity here, because the Weberian reading gives the paper a reason why naming the competency matters politically that §6 currently gestures at without support.

## Caution

A conceptual review in a work-and-occupations journal, arguing about the literature rather than reporting data, and written before both the platform economy and the skills-biased-technical-change literature that now dominates the field. The four-way taxonomy is Attewell's own analytic construction and other reviewers cut the field differently. Abstract-level read; the four traditions are verified from the abstract, but the arguments within each are not, so do not attribute specific claims to specific traditions without checking the article.
