## What it argues

Moynihan, Herd and Harvey give public administration a variable for what the state costs the citizen who deals with it. Administrative burden is "a function of learning, psychological, and compliance costs that citizens experience in their interactions with government" — the effort of finding out what a programme is and whether one qualifies, the stigma and stress of applying, and the documentary work of proving eligibility. Their second contribution is political: the level of burden, and "the distribution of burden between the state and the individual," is often "a function of deliberate political choice rather than simply a product of historical accident or neglect." Because burden is opaque, it serves as "hidden politics," letting significant policy change happen without broad political consideration. The illustration is the evolution of Medicaid policy in Wisconsin across three governorships.

## Relation to the argument

This is the most serious prior art for the seventh row's *logic*, and the paper is stronger for engaging it than for not having found it. Administrative burden is a dimension of an arrangement defined entirely by what it demands of the coordinated party; the learning-cost component is close to what §5 and §7 describe; and the distributive framing — burden as something allocated between the coordinator and the coordinated, by choice — is the same shape as §2's partition, where the platform takes the coordination dividend and the worker takes its inverse.

Two things keep the seventh row distinct, and both should be said aloud. Administrative burden is a *cost* borne, not a *capacity* supplied: it measures what the interaction takes out of the citizen, whereas the seventh row measures what the citizen must be able to do for the interaction to work at all. And burden theory has never been run across coordination forms — it lives inside the citizen-state relation and compares programmes, not market to hierarchy to network to platform. Both differences are real, and both are narrower than a reader would like; the paper should claim novelty for the *cross-form application*, not for the underlying idea that forms make demands.

## Caution

Abstract depth only. The empirical content is a single-state, single-programme historical case used illustratively, so the construct's generality rests on subsequent work rather than on this article. Crossref shows the online date as February 2014 against the 25(1) 2015 issue; cite 2015. Do not present administrative burden as a competence construct — it is not measured as an attribute of persons, and the follow-on literature that does turn to individual capacity is Christensen et al. (2020), carded separately.
