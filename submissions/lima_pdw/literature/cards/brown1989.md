## What it argues

Brown, Collins, and Duguid attack the assumption behind most teaching: that conceptual knowledge can be abstracted from the situations in which it is learned and used, packaged, and delivered. Knowledge, they argue, is **situated** — in part a product of the activity, context, and culture that produced it — and tools are the analogy that carries the argument. You can be handed a tool and a description of it and still not know how to use it; use comes from participating in the community that uses the tool, and it changes your view of the world as you go. Their positive proposal is **cognitive apprenticeship**: instruction that makes the normally invisible reasoning of a practitioner visible, through modelling, coaching, and fading support, and that keeps the activity authentic to the practice rather than to the classroom. Two worked cases in mathematics instruction show what that looks like.

## Relation to the argument

The most useful source in this cluster for the *precise* form of §10's claim, because it is the one that does not overclaim. Brown, Collins, and Duguid do not say that instruction is impossible; they say that instruction which strips activity from knowledge produces inert knowledge, and they propose a form of instruction — cognitive apprenticeship — that works by making a practitioner's tacit reasoning visible. That gives §10 a sharper falsifier than the one it currently states. The paper's stated test is whether a cohort taught explicitly scores as well as a cohort that only used the system; the situated-cognition literature predicts that *decontextualized* instruction fails while apprenticeship-style instruction, in which an experienced participant thinks aloud about a gate outcome, succeeds. If the paper wants implicit acquisition to be a structural claim rather than a claim about bad teaching, it has to say that even cognitive apprenticeship cannot work here — and the reason it can say that is the one §8 already gives: no practitioner, however experienced, has seen the rule. The tool passage also serves §8's "what can be taught is a tool, and the tool is not the competency," which is nearly a paraphrase of their argument and should be attributed.

## Caution

An education-theory position paper, argued from cases rather than from data, and its cognitive-apprenticeship proposal was developed elsewhere (Collins, Brown, & Newman) — do not attribute the full model to this article. The "situated" label covers several incompatible programs, and this paper's version is milder than Lave and Wenger's; Brown, Collins, and Duguid keep a place for designed instruction that the 1991 book largely abandons. Abstract-level read; verify the tool analogy's wording before quoting it into §8.
