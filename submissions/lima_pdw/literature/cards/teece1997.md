## What it argues

Teece, Pisano, and Shuen ask how firms achieve and sustain advantage in environments of rapid technological change, and answer that neither competitive-forces analysis nor a static resource position explains it. **Dynamic capabilities** are the firm's ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments. The framework is organized by **processes** (coordination and integration, learning, reconfiguration), **positions** (the firm's current endowment of technology, complementary assets, reputation, and relationships), and **paths** (where it can go from here, given path dependence and technological opportunity). Capabilities are built rather than bought, embedded in organizational routines, and hard to imitate precisely because they are firm-specific and historically accumulated — which is what makes them a source of advantage rather than a factor of production.

## Relation to the argument

§8 cites this paper to rule out "capability," and the card exists to make sure that citation is fair, because the paper's dismissal is thinner than it needs to be. Two of §8's three grounds hold well. Dynamic capabilities are firm-level and the explanandum here is variance among workers, so the level objection stands; and capabilities are built through the firm's own learning and reconfiguring, which a coordinated party who owns no routine cannot do. The possession objection, which §8 calls decisive, is the weakest of the three, because Teece and colleagues are explicit that capabilities are not assets to be owned but processes to be performed, embedded in routines rather than held as stock. "A capability is held by the party that owns the routine" is a defensible reading of them, but it is a reading, and a strategy reviewer will push on it. The stronger version of the objection is available and closer to the paper's own theory: a dynamic capability is defined by the firm's ability to *reconfigure* what it coordinates through, and the defining feature of the arrangement algorithmacy names is that the coordinated party can reconfigure nothing — she cannot change the gate, the graph, or the rule, only her reading of them. Reconfiguration, not ownership, is the property she lacks.

## Caution

Crossref deposits no abstract for this article, so the card reconstructs it from the canonical reception — verify the processes/positions/paths scheme and the definition of dynamic capabilities against the text before quoting, since the definition is quoted in several variants. A conceptual framework, not an empirical study, and one criticized for tautology (capabilities are inferred from the performance they explain) and for definitional drift across the two decades of literature that followed. Eisenhardt & Martin (2000) and Winter (2003) offer materially different readings; citing "the dynamic capabilities literature" as one position, as §8 does, is a simplification a reviewer from that field will notice.
