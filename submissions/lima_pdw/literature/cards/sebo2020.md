# Sebo, S., Stoll, B., Scassellati, B., & Jung, M. F. (2020). Robots in groups and teams: A literature review. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 4(CSCW2), 1–36.

**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

A systematic review of every study placing a robot in a multi-human group. Sebo and colleagues organise the field by the robot's function — the robot's effect on group dynamics, on individual members' behaviour toward one another, on group performance — and consistently find that a robot in a group changes relations *among the humans*, not merely between each human and the robot. Vulnerable robot disclosures increase human-to-human conversational engagement (Traeger et al., doi:10.1073/pnas.1910402117); robot moderation redistributes speaking turns; robot behaviour shifts group norms.

Their explicit framing: robots in groups are a distinct research problem from robots in dyads, and require different theory.

## Relation to the argument

**The strongest available evidence that a research community has taken the machine-in-a-multi-party-arrangement problem seriously, and the best map of what that community has and has not asked.** For §9, the useful finding is what the review does *not* contain: no study measures a **human participant's capacity** to fare well in the arrangement, and no study features a machine whose output binds the participants. The field studies what the robot does to the group. Nobody studies what a group member must be able to do.

That is precisely the gap algorithmacy claims, and it is a much better-evidenced claim than "the field is dyadic." The revision for §9: the machine-in-the-middle position has been built and studied; **the competence question about the human on the far side of it has not been asked**, and the arrangement studied is never one where the machine's output is binding.

The review also gives §11 a design vocabulary — the manipulations, measures, and group-level outcomes the HRI community uses — if the paper ever wants an experimental complement to a field study.

## Caution

Human-robot interaction: embodied robots, co-present groups, laboratory tasks, mostly small student samples. Nothing about platform work, asynchrony, or evaluation stakes. Sebo publishes as both "Sarah Sebo" and "Sarah Strohkorb Sebo," which fragments the citation record. Four authors; Jung is fourth here and first on the 2015 conflict study.
