**Source-tier:** primary_journal

## What it argues

Goffman dismantles the speaker-hearer dyad that linguistics and communication theory had assumed. **Speaker** is not one role but three: the **animator**, the body that produces the sounds; the **author**, who composed the words; and the **principal**, whose position the words state and who is committed by them. **Hearer** likewise decomposes into addressed recipient, unaddressed ratified participant, bystander, and eavesdropper. "Footing" is the alignment a participant takes to an utterance, and Goffman's claim is that footing shifts constantly within a single conversation and that the participation framework — who occupies which position — is the real unit of analysis.

The three-way split of the speaker role is the analytic core: the person who says a thing, the person who wrote it, and the person it commits are routinely different people.

## Relation to the argument

**The most powerful under-used resource in this cluster, and it complicates §9 productively rather than fatally.** Goffman shows that "two-party communication" was always a simplification and that participation frameworks with many positions were theorised in 1979. §9's implicit premise — that communication theory had two positions and needs a third — is not right; the field has had a positional apparatus for forty-five years, and it is finer-grained than the paper's triad.

But the apparatus makes the paper's case rather than undoing it, if used. Run the paper's arrangement through the production format: when a gate issues a determination, the platform is the **animator**, the rule's designers are the **author**, and the **principal** — the party whose position is stated and who is committed — is ambiguous or absent. That is not a footing Goffman catalogued. It is an utterance that binds parties none of whom authored, animated, or are represented by it, and the worker's interpretive problem is precisely that the principal cannot be identified. **Asymmetric interpretation, in Goffman's terms, is the work of reconstructing a principal from an utterance that has none.**

That is a sharper statement of what the paper is about than "the algorithm is a third position," and it is expressed in the vocabulary of the discipline the paper is borrowing from.

## Caution

Goffman's participants are co-present humans in talk; there is no machine anywhere in the analysis, and importing one is the paper's move, not his. The apparatus is descriptive and analytic, offers nothing measurable, and does not address competence at all.
