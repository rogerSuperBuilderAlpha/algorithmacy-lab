## What it argues

Ayasrah and colleagues build an instrument for what they call **AI-algorithmic competence** in teachers, on the premise that using adaptive learning platforms and intelligent tutoring systems requires a degree of metacognitive malleability — teachers must "align" their own thinking with machine logic. Exploratory factor analysis produced five factors accounting for 64.30% of total variance: *Ethical Alignment in Metacognitive–AI Processes*, *Reflective–Algorithmic Thinking*, *Cognitive Co-authoring with AI*, *Algorithmic Transparency for Teacher Metacognition*, and *Algorithmic–Cognitive Self-Regulation*. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the five-factor structure; the authors report high internal consistency and acceptable test–retest reliability, and position the scale for teacher training, policy and future research.

The construct's distinctive move is metacognitive. Where Zhou et al. measure what a worker does with the system and the AI-literacy scales measure what a user knows about it, MAAS measures how a professional's *own reasoning* accommodates an algorithmic one. "Cognitive co-authoring with AI" is the clearest expression: not use, not knowledge, but a shared production of thought.

## Relation to the argument

**Neighbour, and a useful demonstration that even the most relationally worded rival stays a dyad.** Of the five factors, *Cognitive Co-authoring with AI* comes closest to naming a two-party relation, and both parties are the teacher and the machine. The pupil — the human on the other side of an adaptive learning platform, whose experience the algorithm is actually determining — appears nowhere in the factor names. A construct built for the person in a classroom, where a counterpart is unavoidable and institutionally central, still measures the professional's relation to the software. That is worth saying in §9, because it shows the omission is systematic rather than incidental to gig-work settings.

The paper is also relevant to the manuscript's §8 possession argument in an unexpected way. A teacher using an intelligent tutoring system stands where Waardenburg et al.'s intelligence officers stand: between the algorithm and people affected by it, with professional standing and the ability to override. Her competence is a competence of the *coordinating* party, not the coordinated one. The manuscript's §4 asymmetry — the coordinator's capacity richly theorised, the coordinated party's not — extends to education, and MAAS is an instance of it.

## Caution

**Read at abstract depth, and the abstract was reached through a search summary rather than the publisher's page**, so the five factor names above should be checked against the article before they are quoted. Crossref carries no abstract for this DOI. Critically, the sample is not reported in what was retrieved: **no N, no country, no item count, and no reliability coefficients were confirmed** — the abstract's "high internal consistency and acceptable test–retest reliability" is the authors' claim without numbers attached. Do not cite this as a validated instrument without reading the psychometrics. The first author's group publishes a cluster of closely similar Jordan-based metacognitive-AI scales (an AI-Integrated Metacognitive Learning Resilience Scale, a Metacognitive Diagnostic Disruption in AI Decision-Making Scale, a Metacognitive Stewardship Scale), which raises a question about construct proliferation that a careful reader should weigh before treating MAAS as an established rival.
