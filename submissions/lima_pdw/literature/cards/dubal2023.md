## What it argues
Dubal names and analyses a practice she calls algorithmic wage discrimination: on-demand platforms pay differently priced amounts to different workers for the same work, with the price set individually and dynamically by systems that draw on data about each worker's behaviour, history and predicted willingness to accept. Wages become personalised, variable and unpredictable, and the mechanism is opaque by design, since disclosing it would defeat it. Her legal argument is that this falls outside the frameworks built for wage regulation, which assume a rate attached to a job rather than a price attached to a person, and that it functions as a form of gambling: workers chase bonuses and surge multipliers whose rules they cannot see, and the uncertainty is itself the incentive. She grounds the analysis in extended qualitative work with California drivers, and her most consequential empirical claim for this paper is about opacity's effect on claiming. Drivers cannot tell whether they were paid correctly, cannot compare their pay to anyone else's, and therefore cannot formulate a grievance — the wrong is undetectable to the person suffering it. She traces the racial and distributive consequences and argues for legal prohibition rather than disclosure, on the ground that disclosure regimes leave the burden on the worker.

## Relation to the argument
Dubal supplies the case where §5's accountability claim bites hardest, and the paper should use it because it defeats the strongest optimistic reading. Suppose a worker has maximal algorithmacy: she reads the system expertly, tracks its changes, packages her intent perfectly. Under individually variable pricing she still cannot determine whether she was treated correctly, because the comparison that would establish it is unavailable to her by construction. Competency cannot reach the *first* step of Felstiner, Abel and Sarat's sequence — the injury is not nameable — let alone the last. That is a stronger claim than §5 currently makes and it is evidenced.

Her rejection of disclosure as a remedy is also §6's argument arriving from labour law. She considered the individual route — give the worker the information and let her act — and rejected it because it transfers to workers a burden they cannot discharge, and she reaches for prohibition and regulatory enforcement instead. The paper's institutional column has, in Dubal, a legal scholar who took the same decision on the same reasoning.

## Caution
A law review article: the doctrinal analysis is US-specific and the qualitative material, while substantial, is presented in service of a legal argument rather than as a standalone study. Her normative position is strong and explicitly advocacy — she argues for prohibition — and the paper should cite her mechanism without inheriting her programme. The pricing practice she documents is specific to on-demand transport and delivery platforms in a particular period; it is not a general feature of algorithmic management, and a peer-review gate has no analogue to it.
