## What it argues

Tully, Longoni and Appel asked which consumers are most receptive to AI and got the answer backwards from what they expected. Four surveys establishing lay expectations, cross-country data, and six further studies converge: **people with lower AI literacy are typically more receptive to AI**, not less. The effect survives the obvious explanations. It is not that low-literacy people think AI is more capable, more ethical, or less threatening to humanity — the authors test and rule out perceptions of capability, ethicality and feared impact.

The mechanism they land on is enchantment. Lower AI literacy makes people more likely to perceive AI as **magical**, and to feel **awe** when a system executes tasks that seem to require uniquely human attributes. The effect is mediated by perceived magicalness and moderated by task type: it weakens for tasks not assumed to require distinctly human attributes. The marketing implication the authors draw is uncomfortable and they state it plainly — firms may do better targeting lower-literacy consumers, and "efforts to demystify AI may inadvertently reduce its appeal."

## Relation to the argument

Resource, not rival, and a sharp one. Its use is to break the assumed monotonicity between knowing about algorithms and faring well under them.

The paper's §9 has to explain why adding items to AI literacy will never produce algorithmacy. One version of that argument is structural — the missing element is a position. Tully et al. supply a second, empirical version: AI literacy, as the field measures it, does not even predict the outcomes people assume it predicts. It predicts *lower* receptivity, through a route (disenchantment) that has nothing to do with capacity to act under algorithmic coordination. A construct whose principal validated downstream effect is reduced willingness to adopt is measuring something adjacent to what the coordinated party needs.

There is also a bracing warning for the manuscript's own claims. If algorithmacy is to be more than AI literacy renamed, it must predict something AI literacy does not — and Tully et al. show the field's default outcome variable is receptivity, which is a consumer variable. Algorithmacy's outcomes are coordination outcomes: whether the determination lands where the worker expected, whether she can act before it does, whether she can recover from it. Stating that contrast against this paper is the cheapest way to demonstrate discriminant relevance without new data.

The magicalness mechanism connects to §7's opacity material more directly than its marketing framing suggests. Awe in the face of a system doing something apparently human is the affective response to *unexplained* competence, and it is exactly what a worker cannot afford when the same unexplained competence is issuing determinations that bind her. Enchantment and algorithmacy are opposed dispositions toward the same opacity. That is a genuine finding the manuscript can use, and it should be used with care because Tully et al. did not intend it.

## Caution

Consumer receptivity, not work. Every study is about adoption and willingness to use, with no labour context, no employment relation, and no binding determination — so nothing here speaks directly to platform workers, and the manuscript must not overclaim the transfer. The AI literacy measure is a short self-report; the paper does not use MAILS, AICOS or any of this cluster's validated instruments, which weakens any inference about what those instruments would predict. Read at abstract depth: the number of studies (ten plus cross-country data), the mediation and moderation tests, and the specific literacy measure all need the version of record before quotation, and the *Journal of Marketing* article is paywalled. The PsyArXiv preprint dates from June 2023 and predates two years of revision, so do not treat preprint and article as interchangeable.
