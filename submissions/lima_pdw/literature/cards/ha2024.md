## What it argues

Ha and Kim answer the question the manuscript's §9 might be expected to worry about: does anyone have a *platform* literacy? They argue that no one did, and set out to build one. Their method is a systematic review of 735 papers on digital literacies — digital literacy, media literacy, information literacy and ICT literacy — from which they harvest definitions and frameworks. Consolidating the 141 distinct skills those frameworks name yields 77 core attributes in ten sub-dimensions, which they then arrange into a two-component architecture.

The first component is **flow**, seven sequential steps a user moves through: identify, access, apply, create, participate, evaluate, manage. These are the "seven core constructs" the published abstract advertises. The second is **proficiency**, three foundations that underwrite every step: *understanding context* (media environment, social environment, digital safety), *having an adequate attitude* (pursuing personal growth, displaying a positive attitude, demonstrating cognitive proficiency), and *possessing technical skill* (devices, software, operating systems, online transactions, computational thinking). Proficiency is the base, flow the progression built on it — a structure they take from SCONUL's separation of ability from attitudes and behaviours.

Platform literacy is then this framework specialised. Ha and Kim split it into **fixed attributes** — attitudinal dispositions and general social understanding, constant across contexts — and **variable attributes**, which change with the platform: understanding of the platform environment, platform safety, technical capability, and the platform-specific user flow. Their Table 10 maps digital-platform traits onto framework components: "constructed as a social system" and "multi-sided nature" both map to *understanding of platform environment*; "interaction among different actors" maps to that plus *user flow–participate*; "role of transaction, interaction, and value creation" maps to apply, create and participate. The platform-specific flow they then specify begins "identify own needs for using platform," "identify the scope of platform's function," and proceeds to searching and downloading services, utilising the platform, and transforming and interpreting messages.

## Relation to the argument

The nearest thing in the literacy family to a construct built *for* the platform form, and therefore the rival most worth taking seriously — and it loses on the paper's first discrimination, which is the outcome §9 predicts.

**The triad.** Other actors appear repeatedly, more than in any other construct in this cluster: multi-sidedness is a named platform trait, "interaction among different actors" gets its own row, and *participate* is one of the seven flow steps. But look at what the mapping does with them. Multi-sidedness and social-system character both resolve into *understanding of the platform environment* — that is, into knowledge the user holds about the platform. The counterpart is metabolised into a property of the object of knowledge rather than kept as a position in the relation. The platform-specific flow confirms it: every step names something the user does to or through the platform ("utilize platform," "solve problems," "transform the message"), and none names an outcome jointly determined with another party. So Ha and Kim reach *awareness that a platform has other sides* and stop; algorithmacy begins where a determination binding on the worker and that other side arrives from a system neither controls. §9's structural claim survives its hardest test in this cluster.

**Derivation, inverted.** This is an inventory in the most literal sense available — 735 papers in, 141 skills, consolidated to 77 attributes, grouped into ten sub-dimensions. Nothing in the construct is derived from an account of what the platform form withholds; the platform enters only at the last step, as a context in which a pre-existing digital-literacy framework is specialised. Ha and Kim's fixed/variable split is exactly the move the manuscript refuses: it treats the coordination form as a *setting* for a general competence rather than as the generator of a specific one. The manuscript should name this paper here, because it makes §7's derivation claim concrete by exhibiting the alternative.

**Skill and uniformity.** The framework's technical-skill leg is tool-indexed ("use of operating systems," "download services"), and the flow assumes a user who can be instructed through it. No stratification claim appears.

**Use for the manuscript.** §9 currently sorts five constructs and beats two labels. Platform literacy is a sixth that a reviewer will raise by name — it carries the word "platform" and postdates the manuscript's own framing. Better to name and dispatch it than to be asked about it.

## Caution

I read the ITS 2023 conference working paper, not the published *Telecommunications Policy* article; the abstract matches on every substantive claim (735 papers, seven core constructs, three proficiencies, fixed and variable attributes), but section numbering, table numbering and any figure content may differ, and the working paper's figures 3–5 did not survive text extraction. The framework is conceptual only: no items, no sample, no validation, no empirical test of the fixed/variable distinction. Its evidence base is a review of *other frameworks*, so it inherits whatever those frameworks assumed and does not observe any platform user. Two authors, one institution, and a policy journal rather than an organization-studies venue — cite it as evidence about the state of the literacy field, not as a finding about platform workers.
