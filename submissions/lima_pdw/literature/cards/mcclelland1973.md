## What it argues

The article that founded competency modelling. McClelland attacks the use of intelligence and aptitude tests for selection on the ground that they predict grades better than they predict life outcomes, that their apparent validity is largely an artifact of the credential system, and that they discriminate against minority applicants while adding little over the job performance they claim to forecast. His alternative is to abandon the search for a general trait and test instead for the **competencies** that actually distinguish superior from average performers in a specific job, identified by studying those performers directly. Six methodological commitments follow, of which the load-bearing ones are criterion sampling — test what people actually do in the job rather than a proxy — and the insistence that competencies be learnable and observable in behaviour rather than treated as fixed capacities.

## Relation to the argument

The origin of the word §8 chooses, and the source of the commitments the word still carries. Two of them matter to the paper. First, McClelland's competency is **criterion-referenced**: it is defined by discriminating superior from average performance on a specific job, which means a competency claim is empty until a performance criterion is fixed. §8 says algorithmacy explains "variance under a structural constant," and a McClellandian reviewer will ask what the performance criterion is at the Hult gate, since advancement through a private peer vote is a criterion whose validity is exactly what §11 puts in question. Second, McClelland's competencies are **individual differences measured for selection**, which is the possession-like framing §8 rejects for "capability" but inherits along with the word it chooses. Naming that inheritance and disowning it is a paragraph the paper owes; otherwise "competency" imports a selection apparatus the paper has no use for and a distributive politics — sorting people by measured capacity — that §6 explicitly warns against.

## Caution

A polemical article in a general psychology journal, arguing a methodological position rather than reporting a study, and it launched a testing industry McClelland himself later had reservations about; the empirical claims about test validity have been contested for fifty years and the meta-analytic literature does not support his strongest version. Crossref deposits no abstract, so this card reconstructs the argument from the reception — verify the six commitments and the criterion-sampling formulation against the article before citing them. Do not treat McClelland's "competency" and Sandberg's "competence" as the same concept; they are opposed, and §8 currently draws on both.
