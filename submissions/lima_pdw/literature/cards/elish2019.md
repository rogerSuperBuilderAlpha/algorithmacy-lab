## What it argues

Elish coins the **moral crumple zone**: in a highly automated system, the human operator who retains nominal control absorbs the blame for failures that are properly attributable to the system's design and to the arrangement that placed her there. Her cases are Three Mile Island and the aviation-autopilot accident record, where operators with almost no real control were nonetheless found responsible. The mechanism is structural, not psychological: responsibility attaches to whoever is visibly nearest the failure, and automation puts a human there while removing her capacity to prevent it.

## Relation to the argument

**The best available theoretical statement of what §5 means by accountability being withheld and unsupplyable by any individual.** §7's table has an entry that reads "Nothing. It cannot be supplied individually," and it is asserted rather than explained. Elish explains it: in an arrangement where a system commits determinations and a person is the only visible party, responsibility flows to the person by default, and no individual effort redirects it — because the flow is a property of the arrangement's visibility structure, not of anyone's conduct.

That converts the paper's most under-argued cell into its best-supported one, and it connects directly to §6's second route, since Elish's remedy is institutional attribution of responsibility rather than better operators.

The concept also names a hazard for the construct itself. A competency framing that says variance in outcomes reflects variance in worker capacity is, structurally, a moral-crumple-zone move. §8's closing caution and §10's normative note already sense this; Elish gives it a name and a citation, and using it inoculates the paper against the charge that its own construct is doing the platform's attributional work.

## Caution

Science and technology studies, argued from historical accident cases rather than data, and the concept has been applied more widely than the evidence strictly supports. The cases are safety-critical automation, not evaluative platforms. Cite the 2019 ESTS version, not the SSRN preprint. Hohenstein & Jung (2020) borrow the term for a laboratory effect that is related but not identical.
