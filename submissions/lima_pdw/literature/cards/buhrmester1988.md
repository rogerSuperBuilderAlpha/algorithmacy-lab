## What it argues

The psychology-side counterpart to the communication discipline's competence scales. Buhrmester and colleagues argue interpersonal competence is not unitary and identify five domains — initiating relationships, asserting displeasure with others' actions, self-disclosure, providing emotional support, and managing interpersonal conflict — each with distinct correlates. The Interpersonal Competence Questionnaire operationalises them. Their central empirical finding is **discriminant**: the five domains correlate only moderately, and a person competent in one is not reliably competent in another.

## Relation to the argument

The best available evidence-based precedent for §7's claim that a competency's facets can dissociate. §7 argues that reading a counterpart and making oneself read correctly "are different practices with different occasions and different failures," and rests it on everyday observation. Buhrmester et al. did the analogous demonstration properly and found exactly the pattern: initiating and asserting displeasure, which sound like two faces of one social capacity, dissociate. The paper can point at this as the empirical form of the argument it is making and as the pattern §11 should look for.

The second domain — **asserting displeasure with others' actions** — is also worth notice on its own terms. It is the ability to register a grievance in a form the other party will process, which is precisely the failure §7 uses as its illustrative case: the driver who "cannot get a legitimate complaint recorded in a form the system will process." That the psychology of interpersonal competence already treats grievance-registration as a distinct competence domain strengthens the intent-specification facet.

## Caution

Psychology, not communication; self-report and partner-report on undergraduate and dating samples; a 1988 instrument. The five domains are peer-relationship-specific and do not map onto work coordination. Reis is fourth author and the paper is often cited as Buhrmester alone.
