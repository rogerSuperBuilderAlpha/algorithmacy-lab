## What it argues

Porsfelt, Vestergaard and Hjorth start from a complaint about a fashionable word: the concept of imaginaries has spread until "its relation to neighbouring notions has become so diffuse as to call into question its analytical potential" (p. 1). Their remedy is a genealogy "in the Foucauldian sense of a critical history of the present" (p. 3), built in two halves. The first half (pp. 4–8) walks the philosophy of imagination in three steps — the mental image as a reproduction of sense impressions (Aristotle, Plato, Descartes, Hume), the productive and creative imagination (Kant, Coleridge and the Romantics), and the collective imagination (Taylor, Anderson, Jasanoff and Kim, Castoriadis) — using Ricoeur's presence/absence and belief/critical-distance axes (Figure 1, p. 5) as the organising heuristic. The second half (pp. 8–23) is an "inventory" of how organisation studies took up image, imagination and imaginaries, summarised in Table 1 (p. 9), and its diagnosis is that "inherited conceptual habits continue to constrain the field" (p. 1): scholars reduce images to one function at a time, narrow imagination to creativity, and conflate two lineages of imaginaries.

Each diagnosis carries a proposal. For images, the authors propose the palimpsest and transmodality (pp. 12–14). For imagination, they propose "a situated engagement with the non-present" (p. 1), which the introduction restates as "a situated capacity to enter distal spheres of thought and return" (p. 4) and which rests on Schütz and on Zittoun and Gillespie's three-step loop of trigger, departure and return (p. 18); its mark is "the capacity to shift the stream of thought away from the here-and-now to something absent" (p. 18). For imaginaries, they separate Taylor's inherited cultural horizons from Castoriadis's instituting force (pp. 19–23). The closing section (pp. 23–26) then names two further directions. The first is the inter-subjunctive, collective imagination that "is enacted and coordinated in situ as a shared and situated practice of temporarily stepping outside the here-and-now" (p. 24). The second is the intra-subjunctive, where the authors "build on and extend processual and new materialist scholarship, which understands imagination not as a mental faculty possessed by individuals but as a relational capacity: a power to be affected by potentiality" (p. 4). The paper reports no data; its evidence is other scholars' texts, with short illustrations (a brainstorming session, a rural entrepreneur's daydreaming from Helin et al., 2022).

## Relation to the argument

Porsfelt, Vestergaard and Hjorth give the algorithmacy argument a recent *Organization Theory* warrant for treating imagination as a relational capacity that arises in an encounter rather than a faculty a person owns, and a separate warrant for treating it as a situated engagement with what is not present. The draft's sentence — "Drawing on recent views of imagination as a relational capacity enacted in the encounter between bodies, materials, and circumstances (Porsfelt et al., 2026), algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with the unseen" — is supported in substance, with four points to settle.

First, the wording. The paper does call imagination a "relational capacity", three times (pp. 2, 4, 16). The draft's clause is a near-verbatim lift of the abstract, which offers an account of imagination as "a relational capacity constituted in the encounter between bodies, materials, and circumstances, rather than possessed by a prior subject" (p. 2). The draft changes one word, "constituted" to "enacted", and carries the rest without quotation marks. That string is close enough to the source to need quotation marks and a page number, or a looser paraphrase. The phrase "bodies, materials, and circumstances" recurs in the body, where the processual stream shifts attention to "the concrete coming-together of bodies, materials, and circumstances from which an imagining subject emerges" (p. 16).

Second, "enacted" is not the paper's verb for this account, and the substitution shifts the claim. "Constituted in the encounter" says the capacity and the imagining subject come into being there; "enacted" suggests a person performing a capacity already held, which is the view the passage argues against. The paper uses "enacted" twice (pp. 24, 26), both times for collective imagination as a practice, not for the new materialist account.

Third, whose view it is. The relational account originates in a processual stream of entrepreneurship research (Hjorth, 2014; Hjorth et al., 2018; Perotto, 2023; Yamauchi & Hjorth, 2024) that the inventory surveys in the third person: "This processual stream, with its notion of imagination as a relational capacity constituted in the encounter rather than a faculty possessed by a prior subject, marks a departure in scholarship on entrepreneurial imagination" (p. 16). Hjorth is the third author, and the paper adopts the stream as one of its two forward directions. The adoption is tentative in its phrasing: "this further orientation could be described as the intra-subjunctive" (p. 25). The draft's "recent views" therefore fits. "Porsfelt et al. propose" would overstate it; "Porsfelt et al. extend" would not.

Fourth, the draft joins two proposals the paper keeps apart. "Situated engagement" is the paper's phrase, in the authors' own voice, but it belongs to the phenomenological proposal (Schütz; Zittoun and Gillespie), not the new materialist one. The authors mark the difference themselves: the inter-subjunctive "remains within a broadly phenomenological frame" (p. 25), whereas the processual stream asks whether imagining happens between pre-formed subjects at all. One sentence does bridge them — "As the processual stream suggests, this capacity can also be understood as a receptive sensibility" (p. 18) — so the join is defensible, but the citation should not imply the paper presents a single account.

"The unseen" is the draft's own gloss. The paper's terms are "non-present" (seven times, two of them in Table 1), "absent" and "what is not immediately given"; "unseen" appears once, in "unseen possibilities" (p. 17), where the authors describe the Romantic frame they fault Weick for keeping. The closest sentence to the draft's claim, in the authors' own voice, is the introduction's statement of their contribution: "treating imagination as a dynamic, situated, and socially embedded activity through which organisations engage with what is not immediately given, whether because it is fictional or because it lies in the near or distant past or future" (pp. 3–4).

One passage reaches toward the algorithmacy setting directly. Among the encounters that cue imagination, the authors list "the images and scenarios generated by AI systems that participate in reconfiguring what becomes thinkable in the encounter" (p. 25). It is a single clause, usable as a pointer and no more.

## Caution

The paper's non-present is the fictional, the past and the future (pp. 3–4). An algorithmic intermediary that is operating now and cannot be inspected is present and opaque, which is a different condition; the paper never discusses opacity, algorithms, platforms, evaluation or coordination between two parties through a third. A full-text search finds zero instances of "algorithm" and one of "opaque", the latter describing non-declarative culture (p. 21). Calling algorithmacy an engagement with "the unseen" on this paper's authority therefore extends its concept to a case the authors did not have in view, and the draft should own that extension.

The genre limits what the citation can carry. This is a genealogy and inventory of concepts with a short programmatic close; the relational, new materialist account occupies about two pages of thirty-five (pp. 16, 25) and the authors offer it as an avenue for future work, hedged with "could be described" and "may be better conceived" (p. 25). Citing the paper as the source of a positive, worked-out theory of imagination misreads it. Cite it for the diagnosis, for the three distinctions, and for having proposed the two directions; for the substance of the relational account, the primary sources are Hjorth (2014) and Hjorth et al. (2018), which this card's author has not read. The paper also cannot be cited for an individual sensibility or skill: its relational account dissolves the pre-formed subject, describing imagination as "a body’s capacity to be affected by what is not yet actual and hence only sensed" (p. 16), which sits awkwardly beside a construct defined as something a person needs and can have more or less of. It offers no empirical finding of any kind.
