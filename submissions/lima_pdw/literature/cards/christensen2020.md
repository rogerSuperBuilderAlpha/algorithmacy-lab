## What it argues

The paper that turns administrative burden from a property of programmes into a question about what the coordinated party can supply. Its claim is that "one means by which the state reinforces inequality is by imposing administrative burdens that loom larger for citizens with lower levels of human capital," and it takes one component of human capital — cognitive resources, especially executive functioning — and models how burdens and those resources interact. Three common life factors illustrate the model: scarcity, health problems, and age-related cognitive decline. Each produces a "human capital catch-22," raising the likelihood that a person needs state assistance while simultaneously eroding the cognitive resources needed to negotiate the burdens that stand between her and it. The consequence is reduced access and widened inequality. The article closes by asking behavioral public administration to take human capital seriously.

## Relation to the argument

The closest thing in any adjacent field to the seventh row's own claim, and the sharpest test of its novelty. Christensen and colleagues do exactly what §3 describes: they hold a coordination arrangement fixed, ask what capacity the coordinated party must supply to survive it, note that the capacity varies across parties the arrangement treats identically, and show the variance producing unequal outcomes. The catch-22 is also a structural echo of the co-optation column — the arrangement demands most from precisely the parties least equipped to meet the demand — and §11's cohort design would do well to borrow its logic.

What the paper still has that this does not is the row. Cognitive resources here are a general-purpose endowment, not a competency specific to the form; the analysis compares citizens, not coordination forms; and no one asks what a market or a network demands by comparison. The seventh row's contribution is the cross-form comparison and the form-specific competency, and this card is the reason those two things — not "forms make demands on parties" — must carry the novelty claim.

## Caution

Abstract depth only, and the article is a theory-building essay with illustrative examples rather than an empirical test; the executive-functioning model is proposed, not estimated here. "Human capital" and "cognitive resources" are the vocabulary of a different tradition from competency-as-acquired-practice, and §8's construct work should not blur them.
