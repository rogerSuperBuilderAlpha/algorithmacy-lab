## What it argues

Van Deursen and van Dijk had spent a decade arguing that the digital divide is a divide in skills rather than in access, and this paper turns that programme into an instrument. The earlier framework distinguished four skill types — *operational* ("the skills to operate digital media"), *formal* ("the skills to handle the special structures of digital media such as menus and hyperlinks"), *information* ("the skills to search, select and evaluate information in digital media") and *strategic* ("the skills to employ the information contained in digital media as a means to reach a particular personal or professional goal") — and tested them with performance assignments rather than self-report (van Deursen & van Dijk, 2009, *Government Information Quarterly* 26(2), 333–340; 2011, *New Media & Society* 13(6), 893–911). Those performance studies found operational and formal skills high in the Dutch population and information and strategic skills poor, with education predicting all four levels and age predicting only the first two.

The ISS paper builds a survey instrument that can carry the framework into representative surveys. The development runs in three steps: a literature review to draft items, cognitive interviews in the UK and the Netherlands to fix wording, and pilot surveys in both countries through the Toluna access panel (UK N = 324, NL N = 306; 622 usable overall), analysed by exploratory factor analysis on merged and separate data and then by confirmatory factor analysis on the two independent samples. The factor structure that emerged is five-dimensional, not four: **Operational** (5 items, α = .86), **Mobile** (3 items, α = .94), **Information Navigation** (5 items, α = .90), **Social** (5 items, α = .88), and **Creative** (5 items, α = .89) — 23 items in the reported scale. The authors report the surprise honestly: several items borrowed from information-literacy scales loaded on other factors, the surviving Information Navigation items are all negatively worded, and mobile items loaded heavily on Creative before being separated on theoretical grounds. Factorial invariance held across the two countries.

The Social dimension deserves quotation because it is where a counterpart would have to appear. Its highest-loading items are "I know which information I should and shouldn't share online," "I know when I should and shouldn't share information online," "I am careful to make my comments and behaviours appropriate to the situation I find myself in online," "I know how to change who I share content with," and "I know how to remove friends from my contact lists." Every one is a disclosure-management item. The other person is the audience for a posting, not a party to a coordination.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour, and a usefully instructive one, because the ISS is the field's best-validated example of the thing algorithmacy is not.

**The triad.** No. The counterpart appears only as an audience and only in the Social subscale, and nothing in any of the 23 items concerns another party's outcome, another party's understanding of what the respondent meant, or a system's determination binding on both. The medium in the ISS is a set of affordances a user operates; the paper's mediator commits determinations. §9's channel diagnosis applies, with the additional point that the ISS has no channel *partner* at all for four of its five dimensions.

**Skill, explicitly and by name.** Van Deursen and colleagues call these skills, index them to media whose structures are stated in the item wording ("install apps on a mobile device," "download apps"), and treat them as trainable — the whole policy programme behind the digital-divide literature is about raising them by instruction. §8's table puts skill in the top row and dates it with the tool, and the ISS proves the point against itself: the Mobile subscale exists because the tool changed between 2009 and 2014, and the 2016 item pool now reads as a period document.

**Stratified fluency, though.** Here the ISS is a resource rather than a rival. The whole tradition demonstrates that a capacity to use a technology distributes unevenly across equivalently placed people, and that education rather than exposure predicts the distribution. §10's stratified-fluency property makes the same structural claim about algorithmacy, and van Deursen's performance-test findings are the strongest precedent for expecting uneven scores among workers who all use the same platform. Cite the tradition for the *shape* of the distribution claim, not for the construct.

## Caution

Self-report items with the ceiling effects that implies — Operational means sit at 4.65 on a five-point scale, which leaves little variance to explain. The samples are non-probability access panels in two wealthy European countries in 2014, so population inference is limited; the authors themselves frame the pilot as a step toward the representative Dutch survey rather than as that survey. Do not cite the four-type framework and the ISS interchangeably: the four types (operational, formal, information, strategic) come from the earlier performance-test work, and the five factors the ISS recovered are not the same set — formal skills did not survive as a factor. And van Dijk and van Deursen later added communication and content-creation skills to the framework, so any statement of "the" framework needs a year attached.
