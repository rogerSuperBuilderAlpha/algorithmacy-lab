## What it argues

Bowen's premise is that on-site service encounters "blur the organizational boundary between employees and customers," which makes the customer a resource the organization has to manage without owning. He works through the strategic trade-offs of having customers inside the production process and the human-resource practices that shape their satisfaction and performance. The operative sentence for this cluster names the three things the organization must supply if the customer is to contribute: "role clarity, ability, and motivation."

## Relation to the argument

The direct ancestor of Meuter et al.'s consumer-readiness trio, published the same year as Mills and Morris, and evidence that the triad has been stable in this literature for four decades. Two uses. It dates the idea that a coordination arrangement can be described by what it must supply to a non-employee party before that party can perform — which is close enough to the seventh row to be worth an explicit distinction in §3 or §5. And it states the *supply* side, where the seventh row states the demand side: Bowen's organization gives role clarity, ability and motivation to its customers; §4's platform gives none of the three and lets the worker manufacture them.

That contrast is a compact way to put the paper's inversion. Service organizations of the 1980s understood that enrolling a non-employee into production obliged them to equip her. Co-optation enrolls without equipping.

## Caution

Abstract depth only, and this is a practitioner-facing conceptual article in an HR journal, not an empirical study; it carries no evidential weight beyond framing. The boundary-blurring it describes is physical co-presence in a service encounter, which is not the mediated, non-meeting relation the co-optation column describes. Overlaps heavily with Mills and Morris (1986); if only one is cited, cite that one, which is the more theoretically developed statement.
