## What it argues
Ananny and Crawford attack the assumption that seeing an algorithmic system produces power over it. They enumerate limits of the transparency ideal, and the limits are structural rather than practical. Transparency can be intentionally occluded and can be weaponised, since disclosure invites strategic gaming. It creates false binaries between open and closed. It privileges seeing over understanding, so a disclosure a viewer cannot interpret changes nothing. It has technical limits — a system can be disclosed in full and still be unintelligible, especially where behaviour emerges from scale and interaction rather than from readable rules. It has temporal limits, because a disclosure describes a system that has already changed. And it individualises what is a collective problem: an observer given a view is left alone with it, holding information but no standing. Their conclusion is that transparency is a poor proxy for accountability because looking inside a system is not the same as holding it to account, and that the object of study should be the sociotechnical *system* — the humans, institutions and practices in which the code sits — rather than the model.

## Relation to the argument
This is the article that keeps §5's accountability claim from collapsing into a claim about opacity, and the distinction matters because a reviewer will otherwise ask why explanation does not solve the problem. Ananny and Crawford's answer is that it does not, and their reasoning is the paper's: seeing is a state a party can be in, accountability is a relation, and no amount of the first produces the second. Their individualisation critique is the sharpest fit — a transparency regime hands a subject information and leaves her to act on it alone, which is exactly the transfer §6 describes, in which a withheld condition descends to the party it was withheld from and becomes her private work.

The temporal limit belongs in §7 as well. A disclosure that describes a system that has already moved is why §7's temporal tracking is a facet at all, and Ananny and Crawford supply the general form of the claim rather than the platform-specific version. Their insistence on the sociotechnical system as the unit also supports §9's diagnosis that constructs built for a two-position relation miss what is happening.
