## What it argues

HMC's consolidation volume, sixty-five chapters across theory, methods, contexts, and applications five years after the field was named. It maps HMC's relations to adjacent areas — CSCW, HCI, robotics, journalism studies, media effects — and its recurring theoretical preoccupations remain ontology (what is the machine), agency (what does it do on its own), and the human response to machine communicators.

## Relation to the argument

**The best available evidence for §9's dyadic-inheritance claim, and the best available check on it.** A sixty-five-chapter handbook is the field's own inventory. What the inventory shows is that HMC's organising unit is overwhelmingly the human-machine pair: the chapters partition by machine type, by application domain, and by disciplinary neighbour, and the coordination of two humans *through* a machine that decides between them is not a chapter and not an organising category.

Where it complicates the claim: the CSCW chapter and the human-machine teaming material do bring in multi-party settings, and the handbook is aware that machines enter groups. The distinction the paper must hold is between a machine *present in* a multi-party setting — which HMC and HRI both study — and a machine *positioned between* two parties as the thing that commits determinations binding on both. §9's phrase "commits determinations neither endorsed" is doing the real work, and it should be the phrase the section defends, not the bare count of positions.

## Caution

A very large edited handbook read at table-of-contents depth plus two chapters. The claim above is a claim about how the field organises itself, which a table of contents supports, and not a claim that no chapter anywhere addresses triads, which it does not support. Three editors; Guzman first.
