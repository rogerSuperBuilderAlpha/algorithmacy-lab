## What it argues

Firms facing shorter product cycles, multi-technology products, co-creation with customers and partners, and fast-growing technical knowledge have been experimenting with designs "fundamentally different from existing forms of organizing." Fjeldstad and colleagues name the underlying scheme *actor-oriented* and give it three elements: "(1) actors who have the capabilities and values to self-organize; (2) commons where the actors accumulate and share resources; and (3) protocols, processes, and infrastructures that enable multi-actor collaboration." They demonstrate the scheme across four sectors — global professional services, open source software, computer equipment, national defense — and draw implications for research on organizational forms and for managers designing them.

## Relation to the argument

The most direct competitor for the seventh row's novelty inside the organizational-forms literature itself, and the card the paper cannot afford to miss. Element (1) makes actor capabilities a *component of the architecture*: on this account a form is partly constituted by what its participants can do, which is very nearly the seventh row's premise stated as a design principle. If a reviewer asks whether anyone has already made the coordinated party's capacity a dimension of organizational form, this is the honest answer to give — and then to distinguish.

Three distinctions hold. Fjeldstad et al. describe capability as a *precondition the design assumes*, not as a demand the form imposes and grades. They apply it to one family of forms, the collaborative or actor-oriented ones, and never grade market, hierarchy and network against it — so it fails §3's symmetric-application condition, which is precisely the condition the paper imposes on itself. And their actors self-organize by design, which is the opposite of the co-optation case, where the coordinated party is organized by a counterpart she does not meet. The paper's row is what this becomes when it is applied to all four columns and when the party has no standing.

The card also strengthens §4's network cell: the "capabilities and values to self-organize" is a third independent statement, from a different literature, that the network family demands a great deal of its participants.

## Caution

Abstract depth only; the four sector applications are demonstrations of a scheme's usefulness, not tests of it. This is a design-oriented, prescriptive paper — the actor-oriented scheme is offered to managers as something to build, which colors how "capabilities and values" are described. And the scheme is about firms and professional actors within collaborative communities, so the "actor" is generally an organization or a skilled professional participant, not a coordinated individual with no exit.
