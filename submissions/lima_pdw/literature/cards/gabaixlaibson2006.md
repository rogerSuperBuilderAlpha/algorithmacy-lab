## What it argues

A formal result about what competition does *not* fix. If all consumers were Bayesian they would infer that a hidden add-on price is probably a high one, and firms would not shroud in equilibrium. Add some myopic or unaware consumers and shrouding survives. Firms have an apparent incentive to educate their rivals' customers, but where add-ons have close substitutes a "curse of debiasing" blocks it: unshrouding cannot be made profitable. Two forms of exploitation then coexist in equilibrium — firms exploit myopes through marketing that shrouds high-priced add-ons, and sophisticated consumers exploit the same schemes by buying the base good and avoiding the add-on. Neither driving away the sophisticates' business nor luring anyone to a non-exploitative firm can be made to pay. The authors are explicit that this "flourishes even in highly competitive markets, even in markets with costless advertising, and even when the shrouding generates allocational inefficiencies."

## Relation to the argument

This is the theoretical counterpart to Campbell's cross-subsidy and the hardest single objection to a low market cell, because it does not rely on any market imperfection a defender of §4 can wave away. Competition is assumed, advertising is free, and the demand on the participant's competence still does not go to zero — it is *sustained in equilibrium*, because some firms profit from its not being met. That closes the escape route "well, in a competitive market with public prices the demand vanishes." It vanishes only for the good whose price is public; the shrouded margin is where the competence is charged.

For the manuscript's larger claim the parallel is worth drawing carefully. A platform's opacity is not the same as a shrouded add-on — the platform withholds the *rule*, not a price component — but the equilibrium logic is instructive: opacity persists because it pays somebody, and the party who cannot see through it pays for the party who can. §4's inversion would be stronger stated against this backdrop rather than against a naive picture of markets as transparent.

## Caution

Abstract depth only. This is a theory paper with a stylized model and specific assumptions (base good plus add-on, myopes who do not anticipate the add-on, close substitutes for the debiasing result); do not carry the conclusion beyond that structure. Cite the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* version — the DOI above; the NBER working paper (10.3386/w11755) and the SSRN posting carry the same title with 2005 dates, and the Edward Elgar handbook chapter is a reprint.
