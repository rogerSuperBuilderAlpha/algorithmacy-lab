## What it argues
K. Sabeel Rahman argues that the public-utility tradition — largely abandoned in US law since deregulation — supplies the right framework for governing today's dominant private firms. His analytical move is to identify what made an enterprise a utility in the first place: not public ownership, but a structural position in which a private party controls infrastructure that others must use, so that control over access becomes control over their participation in economic and social life. He shows that the classical utility concept responded to this with a package of obligations — duties to serve on non-discriminatory terms, rate regulation, and public oversight — imposed precisely because market discipline and individual contract could not check the power. He then argues that platforms, financial infrastructure and dominant digital firms occupy the same structural position, and that the appropriate response is structural and institutional: public obligations, oversight bodies, and in some cases structural separation. His argument against individual-rights remedies is explicit: rights that must be asserted one at a time by parties dependent on the firm cannot check a power exercised through control of access.

## Relation to the argument
This is the constitutional-scale version of §6's institutional route, and it names the reason the route has to be institutional rather than individual. Rahman's diagnosis is that when one party controls access to something others must use, the dependent party cannot bargain, cannot exit and cannot litigate her way to parity — which is the situation §5 describes and the reason §5's individual-supply claim holds. His prescription is not a better remedy for the individual but an obligation imposed on the firm and enforced by a body.

He also supplies §6 with a category the paper does not have: an obligation that runs to a *class* rather than to a claimant. A duty to serve on non-discriminatory terms operates without anyone filing anything, which is a different kind of institutional supply from an appeal process and a stronger one on §5's own logic — it does not depend on the worker's capacity at any point. Naming that distinction would sharpen §6's institutional column from a list into a graded set.
