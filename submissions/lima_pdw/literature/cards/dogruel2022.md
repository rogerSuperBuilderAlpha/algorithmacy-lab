## What it argues

Dogruel, Masur and Joeckel build the field's most complete measurement instrument for algorithm literacy and validate it with item response theory. Their conceptual definition has four parts — "being aware of the use of algorithms in online applications, platforms, and services, knowing how algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making as well as having the skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations" — but the scale operationalises only the first two. The published instrument has two interrelated dimensions: algorithm awareness and algorithm knowledge.

Two studies. Study 1 tested 46 items among N = 331 participants and reduced them to a 32-item pool. Study 2 fielded that pool among N = 1,041 German internet users. The final scale retains 11 items per dimension, 22 in total. Awareness items are binary statements about whether particular communication technologies (internet browsers, and so on) run on algorithms; knowledge items are true/false statements about how algorithmic curation operates — "The use of algorithms which deliver personalized content can mean that the content you find is mostly consistent with your pre-existing opinions," "I can influence algorithms with my Internet usage behavior." Both subscales correlated positively with participants' self-reported coding skills, and both predicted how participants handled algorithmic curation across three test scenarios.

The instrument's design choice is the point of interest here. Awareness and knowledge are measured as *factual correctness against a criterion the researchers supply*. That is a defensible move for a knowledge test and it is exactly what forces the construct into two positions: a user, and a system about which the user holds true or false beliefs.

## Relation to the argument

This is the named rival for §9's "object of knowledge" cell, and the manuscript's structural claim survives it cleanly. Nowhere in the definition, the dimensions, or the 22 items does a second human appear. The awareness items ask whether a technology uses algorithms; the knowledge items ask what algorithms do to *the respondent's own* content stream. There is no party on the other side of the system whose wants must be reconstructed, no determination binding on anyone but the respondent, and no coordination problem — only a curation problem. Adding items to this scale cannot produce algorithmacy, because every item takes the same two-place form.

Three further discriminations hold. The scale is an **inventory**, not a derivation: Dogruel's own conceptual work (see `dogruel2021.md`) collects dimensions by analogy from media, digital and privacy literacy research, so the four dimensions are inherited from prior literacy frameworks rather than deduced from what the coordination form withholds. It measures a **knowledge state, not a competency**: correctness against a criterion is precisely what §10's implicit-acquisition property says cannot be the measure, since the rule is undisclosed and the worker's fluency shows in conduct rather than in report. And the validation itself concedes the third point — the subscales correlate with *coding skills*, tying literacy to a trainable, tool-indexed capacity of exactly the kind §8 separates from competency.

Where the scale is genuinely useful: its distributional findings are the closest existing evidence for stratified fluency, and its two-study IRT design is the methodological standard the lab instrument will be measured against. If the manuscript proposes a scale, reviewers will ask why it did not do this.

## Caution

**Which year to cite:** cite it as **2022** — the version of record is 16(2), 115–133, in the April 2022 issue. Most of the field cites "Dogruel et al., 2021" from the online-first date; Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum do. Either is defensible, but the manuscript should pick 2022, give the full page range, and stay consistent, because a reviewer who checks the volume will find 2022 and a reviewer who checks the citing literature will find 2021.

I could not obtain the full text — Taylor & Francis blocks automated retrieval, and the VU repository copy is a submitted version behind a landing page. Sample sizes, item counts and the definition come from the published abstract and from Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum's review, which quotes the definition at p. 4 and two knowledge items from the online supplement. I have not verified the IRT model specification, the discrimination and difficulty parameters, the reliability coefficients, or the three test scenarios; the manuscript must not cite specific psychometrics from this card. German-language sample, general internet population, no work context and no economic stakes. Do not cite this as evidence about workers, and do not claim the scale "fails" on a criterion it never set for itself.
