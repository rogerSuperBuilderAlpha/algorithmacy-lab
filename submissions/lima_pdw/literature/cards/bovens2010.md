## What it argues
Bovens returns to the distinction he flagged in 2007 and makes it the whole subject. Accountability as a *virtue* is a normative quality predicated of actors — an official, an agency or a firm "is accountable" the way it might be honest or responsive — and it functions evaluatively, as a standard of good governance. Accountability as a *mechanism* is a social relation, an institutional arrangement in which an actor is obliged to render account to a forum. The two concepts differ in nearly every analytic respect. The virtue sense is an attribute of one party; the mechanism sense is a property of a relationship between two. The virtue sense is assessed against a normative standard and is inherently contestable; the mechanism sense is assessed by whether the arrangement's constitutive elements are present, and can be operationalised and compared. Bovens argues that much of the confusion in the accountability literature comes from authors sliding between the senses without noticing, and that the mechanism sense is the one supporting comparative empirical research, because an investigator can go and look for a forum. He also notes the asymmetry of direction: a virtuous actor may operate where no mechanism exists, and a mechanism may bind an actor with no disposition to answer at all.

## Relation to the argument
This is the paper's strongest external warrant for the §5 hinge, and the paper does not yet use it. §5 distinguishes conditions that are "states a party can be in" from a condition that is "a relation between parties," and it draws that line as though the line were new. Bovens drew it in 2010 for accountability specifically, and he drew it the same way: virtue is a state a party can be in, mechanism is a relation between parties. Citing him converts the hinge from an assertion into a restatement of a settled distinction in public-accountability scholarship, which is what a hostile reviewer will demand. It also gives the paper a clean way to absorb the commonest objection. A reader who insists that a highly skilled worker "has more accountability" is using the virtue sense; the paper's claim runs on the mechanism sense; and Bovens has already shown the two do not exchange. Private cultivation of answerability leaves the mechanism exactly where it was. The asymmetry-of-direction point earns a place in §6 as well: a well-designed appeal process is a mechanism that may bind a wholly unwilling platform, which is why §6 is right that the institutional route does not depend on any party's disposition.

## Caution
The virtue sense is not an error to be discarded. Bovens treats it as a legitimate concept with its own literature, and the paper should not imply that talk of accountable actors is simply confused; the argument is that the two senses answer different questions and cannot substitute for one another. His preference for the mechanism sense is methodological — it makes comparative research possible — not a metaphysical claim that the virtue sense refers to nothing. The setting is again European public governance, and the platform application belongs to this paper.
