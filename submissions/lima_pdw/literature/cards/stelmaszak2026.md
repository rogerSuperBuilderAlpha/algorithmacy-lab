## What it argues

Stelmaszak, Joshi and Constantiou reject the prevailing treatment of AI as an independent entity sitting inside an organisation, on the ground that entity thinking hides the mechanisms by which AI actually changes organising. Drawing on posthumanism, they propose an ontological shift: AI does not reside in algorithmic actors but **arises from the relations among human and algorithmic actors, as an organizing capability**. Three properties characterise it — connectivity, codependence, and emergence — and it contributes to organisational analysing, learning and acting in pursuit of organisational goals.

The move is deliberately relational. If AI is a capability constituted in relations rather than a thing an organisation acquires, then questions about its effects have to be asked about configurations of humans and algorithms rather than about the algorithm alone, and the authors set an agenda on that basis for human–algorithm collaboration, algorithmic management and organisational intelligence. The article is theoretical throughout: no data, no case, no measure.

## Relation to the argument

**Named in §9 as a construct to be beaten, and the manuscript's disposal of it is correct but told too quickly.** The paper's §8 possession argument does the work: a capability is held by the party that owns the routine, and algorithmacy's subject owns nothing. The platform holds the ratings, the graph and the rule; naming the worker's capacity a capability would attribute to her a possession the form is defined by denying. Stelmaszak et al.'s capability belongs to the organising party — it emerges from relations that the organisation configures, and it is evaluated by what it contributes to *organisational* goals. The manuscript's subject is not inside that "organizational."

The relational framing nonetheless needs a more careful answer than §9 gives it, because the authors would not accept "held by the organisation" as a description of their construct: their whole point is that the capability is held by nobody in particular and arises between parties. That framing, taken seriously, is not obviously incompatible with the manuscript's triad — a capacity arising in the relation among a worker, a counterpart and a system is a relational construct too. The discrimination that survives is about *level and explanandum*, not about ownership alone. Their capability explains organisational analysing, learning and acting; algorithmacy explains variance among individuals facing an identical structural constant. A construct with no bearer cannot answer the question why two drivers on one platform in one week fare differently, because there is nothing it could be measured on.

There is also a warning in the pairing. Agent-side reading of §4: the coordinator's capacity is richly theorised, the coordinated party's is not. Stelmaszak et al. is a 2026 *Journal of Management Studies* article theorising the organising side of human–algorithm relations, which is one more instance of exactly the asymmetry §4 records — and a useful one to cite there rather than only in §9.

## Caution

A purely theoretical article: posthumanist ontology, no empirical material, no operationalisation, no measure. It cannot be cited for any finding, and its three properties (connectivity, codependence, emergence) are conceptual characterisations rather than dimensions. Abstract-depth read; the posthumanist argument's specific commitments and its treatment of agency were not confirmed against the text, and a manuscript that disposes of the article in two sentences should have read it.
