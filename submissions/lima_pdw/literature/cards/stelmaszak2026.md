## What it argues

Stelmaszak, Joshi and Constantiou argue that the relational turn in AI research stopped one step short: scholars adopted relational epistemologies and kept an entity ontology, treating AI as a thing that enters relations with humans. Their correction is ontological. AI "is not an agent or even an entity in itself" (p. 336); it is "a connective, codependent, and emergent capability of a system of relations – among human and algorithmic actors – to organize by analysing, learning, and acting in pursuit of organizational goals" (p. 341). The article is a posthumanist theory piece (Barad, Haraway, Pickering, Hayles) that works three published cases — Uber, Dutch predictive policing, radiology — and collects no data of its own.

**The relations are plural, and the authors say so repeatedly.** AI "resides in a system of multiple, complex, and multifaceted relations among human and algorithmic actors" (p. 337), and these relations form "systems of relations with numerous interdependencies, interrelations, and interconnections" (p. 345). Uber's matching capacity is "collective, hybrid, and relational, distributed among drivers, riders, and hundreds of algorithms" (p. 339), and the Uber system "includes the drivers (human actors), the riders (human actors), and the platform (algorithmic actors)" (p. 346). The policing system "includes police officers, data scientists, intelligence analysts, and predictive algorithms" (p. 345). Neither side is one party: humans and algorithms are "two actor groups, understood here as broad categories that each encompass diverse functions" (p. 344), and "there may be other actors present" (p. 342). Nor is the system local. Human contributions "need not be simultaneous or co-located with the algorithmic activity" (p. 344), contributing actors go "well beyond organizational boundaries" (p. 355), and the system's boundary is functional: it "extends as far and no further than relations among all actors who come to bear on the organizational activities of analysing, learning, and acting that the system of relations enacts" (p. 346). The words *dyad* and *triad* appear nowhere.

**The algorithm is an actor; AI is neither actor nor counterpart.** The authors grant algorithms "a capacity to act" (p. 340) in Bennett's posthumanist sense and call them "algorithmic actors" throughout, restricting the term to learning algorithms — "models that act based on patterns they learn from data rather than following predefined rules" (p. 343). They refuse the next step. AI is not "an agent in its own right" (p. 342), "just learning algorithms without human actors’ involvement are not AI" (p. 344), and the capability "depends on human and algorithmic actors’ participation in relations without privileging either of them" (p. 342). *Counterpart* is Anthony, Bechky and Fayard's term, and the authors list it on the side they reject: the entity view treats "AI as a technology – tool, a medium, or a counterpart – designed and deployed by humans" (p. 351). *Intermediary* and *mediator* do not occur; *mediate* occurs once, inside a Haraway quotation (p. 341).

**Three properties characterise the capability.**
- *Connectivity.* "AI arises when the system of relations among multiple actors is enacted." (p. 347) Table I: "Human and algorithmic actors need to actively enact relations among each other for AI to arise." (p. 351) Where radiologists ignore the tool or intelligence officers substitute their own prediction, no AI is present.
- *Codependence.* "AI is codependent on the participating actors, humans as well as algorithms." (p. 348) Table I: "AI depends on mixed, varied, and specialized contributions from both human and algorithmic actors." (p. 351) The property "does not suggest equidependence" (p. 348). On Uber, "making a decision to match a driver and a rider codepends on numerous humans and algorithms" (p. 349), and "human actors are not capable of deciding which driver to be matched with without depending on algorithms" (p. 349).
- *Emergence.* "AI emerges over time in practice as relations are enacted in a system." (p. 349) Both sides change: "drivers who enact relations with Uber algorithms learn how to change their behaviours and even affect the algorithmic outputs from repeated interactions" (p. 350).

**The level is organizational by stipulation.** The authors "discuss AI strictly in organizational contexts" (p. 346), take intelligence from March as an organizational phenomenon, and hold that "such intelligence is not merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346). Learning is "learning that happens at the level of a system of relations" (p. 353); responsibility is to be conceived "at the level of the system of relations" (p. 357); and "organizational intelligence is not just tied to the humans with the right skills and competencies" (p. 358). The individual enters as a contributor whose role is redefined: Uber drivers "begin to fill the role of the sensor and actuator for AI in the physical world" (p. 352) — "they generate data, report events in interfaces, and rate passengers" (p. 352). The article proposes no individual capacity, skill or literacy.

**The discussion sets three agendas** — human–algorithm collaboration, algorithmic management, organizational intelligence. The algorithmic-management section charges the control literature with an entity view that "obscures important power dynamics within AI" (p. 355), and holds that "while all relations matter for the emergence of AI, not all relations are equal" (p. 356). The conclusion concedes that relations can also degrade organizing — "artificial un-intelligence" (p. 359) — and closes on the widest statement of scope: "AI is not something organizations adopt; it is something we all collectively give rise to." (p. 360)

**On opacity the article is nearly silent.** *Black box* does not occur in the body; the only instance in the PDF is the title of Anthony (2021) in the reference list (p. 361). *Opacity* occurs once, reporting Lebovitz and colleagues: radiologists "must now engage with probabilistic outputs, opacity in model logic, and dynamic learning processes" (p. 355). The authors' own move runs the other way: they fault prior work for studying "an inscribed-in-algorithms and closed-off ‘AI’" (p. 351) and open the box into its human and algorithmic contributors.

## Relation to the argument

**The *Organization Theory* draft cannot use this article as its example of a dyadic account.** The draft's section 2 says the article "inadvertently erases the interdependent human peer on the other side of the screen" and names the result a "dyadic trap." The text does the opposite. Its running case puts driver, rider and platform in one system (p. 346), makes the match codepend "on numerous humans and algorithms" (p. 349), and faults earlier research for the very reduction the draft attributes to it: under the entity view "attention is focused on relations between humans and ‘the AI’ as an entity, often with limited differentiation of relations" (p. 351). A reviewer who knows the article — Stelmaszak sits squarely in the *Organization Theory* readership — will read the draft's sentence as a misreading. The three sentences fare as follows.

- *(a) "organizing now depends on relations among humans and algorithmic black boxes."* Partly supported. The article's own formula is "relations among human and algorithmic actors" (p. 337). "Black boxes" is the draft's gloss; the article never uses the term and argues against treating AI as "closed-off" (p. 351).
- *(b) "arises strictly within 'human–algorithm relations' … elevates the machine to an active counterpart … erases the interdependent human peer."* Contradicted on two of three counts. The phrase is the article's — "human-algorithm relations as a site where AI arises" (p. 337), hyphenated there and in the title, en-dashed in "conceive human–algorithm relations in a new light" (p. 352). "Strictly within" is not; the nearest exclusivity is "AI is only a product of the relations among them" (p. 345), aimed at the entity view and not at other humans. The authors reject "counterpart" by name (p. 351) and decline to privilege the algorithm (p. 342). The peer is present (pp. 346, 349).
- *(c) "an emergent property of a localized human-machine dyad."* Contradicted except for "emergent." The system is neither localized (pp. 344, 355) nor a dyad (p. 345).

**What the text does support is narrower, and it is the better criticism.** Stelmaszak and colleagues count the humans but never theorise the tie between them. Every relation the definition names runs between a human and an algorithm — Table I's "human-algorithmic system of relations" (p. 351) — and human actors enter as "One group of actors" (p. 351) whose members stand in parallel to the algorithms, each contributing data, decisions or code. Driver and rider are co-contributors to a matching capability; the article never asks what the driver must understand about the rider, or the rider about the driver, when an algorithm routes, rates and binds both. Human-to-human links surface only in illustrations: "an intelligence analyst translating this prediction for a police officer" (p. 345), a senior colleague's relation to an algorithm shaped by an anticipated junior colleague (p. 354), drivers who "rate passengers" (p. 352) — and that last, the one place evaluation of one human by another through the system appears, the authors read as data generation for AI. The draft's triad is therefore inside their system of relations as a population and absent from their theory as a structure. The defensible sentence is that the article dissolves the mediated human-to-human tie into a set of human–algorithm relations, so the algorithm never appears as a third standing between two people.

*Carried from the abstract-depth card, confirmed by the full text.* The discrimination that survives is about level and explanandum. The article's capability explains organisational analysing, learning and acting; algorithmacy explains variance among individuals facing an identical structural constant. A construct whose bearer is a system of relations cannot answer why two drivers on one platform in one week fare differently, because there is nothing at the driver's level it could be measured on. The full text hardens the point: the authors place learning and responsibility at the system level by design (pp. 353, 357) and deny that intelligence is "merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346).

**The article also holds material the draft can use in its favour.** The emergence property concedes that people on the worked side develop something: drivers "learn how to change their behaviours" (p. 350) and try to "‘game the system’" (p. 350, quoting Möhlmann et al.), and radiologists build "relational expertise and interrogation practices" (p. 355). The authors treat these as inputs to the system's evolution and leave unnamed the capacity that produces them. That gap — an acknowledged individual accomplishment with no construct attached — is a more accurate opening for algorithmacy than the dyad charge, and it lets the draft cite the article as an ally on plurality and an omission on level.

## Caution

A purely theoretical article: posthumanist ontology, three borrowed cases, no data, no operationalisation, no measure. It cannot be cited for any finding, and its three properties are conceptual characterisations, not dimensions. The Uber material is secondhand from Möhlmann et al. (2021) and from Stelmaszak, Möhlmann and Sørensen (2025, *MIS Quarterly*), which the article itself cites as "Stelmaszak et al., 2025" on eight body pages (pp. 338, 339, 342, 343, 344, 348, 352, 359). The draft's "(Stelmaszak et al., 2025)" therefore points a reader to the wrong paper; cite this one as Stelmaszak, Joshi and Constantiou (2026), with volume, issue and pages (the footer reads © 2025; the issue is March 2026). Table I (p. 351) is the most quotable statement of the properties but is a summary, and its wording differs from the body definitions on pp. 347–349. The article's "organizational" is loose at the edges — the conclusion extends participation to "marginalized human actors, diverse nonhuman actors, and even ecological elements" (p. 360) — so "held by the organisation" misdescribes the construct as badly as "dyadic" does. A fuller card exists in the lab library at `dissertation/research/library/stelmaszakjoshi2026.md`; I did not check it against this read.
