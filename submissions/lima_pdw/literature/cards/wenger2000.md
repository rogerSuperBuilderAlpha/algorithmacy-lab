## What it argues

Wenger compresses the 1998 framework into an argument about organizations as **social learning systems**. Competence, on his account, is socially defined: a community establishes what counts as competent, and an individual's knowing is the interplay between her personal experience and that socially defined competence. When the two align, learning is slow; when experience and competence pull apart, learning happens. He names three **modes of belonging** by which people participate in a learning system — engagement (doing things together), imagination (constructing a picture of the wider system and one's place in it), and alignment (coordinating one's activity with broader processes) — and then reads three elements of such systems through them: communities of practice, the boundary processes between them, and the identities participation shapes.

## Relation to the argument

Two things here bear directly on §8 and §10. First, **imagination** is the mode of belonging that covers what a worker does when she builds a working theory of a system she cannot see, which is exactly the facet §11's first study question probes ("how does a working theory of the gate get built and revised?"). Wenger's term for it predates the algorithm literature's "folk theories" and "algorithmic imaginary" by fifteen years, and citing it places the paper's construct in an organizational rather than a media-studies lineage — useful given that the paper's venue is OS/OT. Second, Wenger's definition of competence as *socially defined* is a direct challenge to §8, which locates competency in the individual in a role. If competence is what a community certifies, then a competency held under a rule set by a non-member is a strange object, and the paper should say why it still counts as competency rather than as a private adaptation. The short journal statement is more citable than the book for an OS/OT audience.

## Caution

A programmatic essay in a journal special issue, not an empirical study; the three modes of belonging are a scheme, not a validated typology. It sits on the managerial side of Wenger's trajectory — the framing is about how organizations can *design* themselves as learning systems — and that design register does not fit an arrangement whose designer withholds the rule. Abstract-level read only; the engagement/imagination/alignment triad is verified from the abstract, but verify Wenger's exact wording on socially defined competence before quoting it against §8.
