## What it argues
Bies and Moag add a third justice type to the distributive/procedural pair and locate it where the other two do not reach: in the *enactment* of a procedure by one person toward another. Studying job candidates' reactions to recruitment, they found that people evaluated the fairness of how they were treated during the process — separately from the outcome and separately from the formal procedure — and that these evaluations turned on communication. Their criteria are communicative: truthfulness, respect, propriety of questions, and justification, meaning an adequate explanation of why a decision was made. Their argument is that fairness is partly constituted in the interaction between a decision-maker and a decision-recipient, and that a formally correct procedure enacted without explanation or with disrespect will be judged unfair. The construct later split, following Greenberg, into interpersonal justice (respect and propriety) and informational justice (truthfulness and justification), and that four-factor structure is what Colquitt validated.

## Relation to the argument
Interactional justice is the piece of the justice literature closest to the paper's accountability condition, and its structure is the answer to a reviewer's question about whether organisational justice already makes §5's distinction. It does, partially and in a different vocabulary: **justification** — an adequate explanation of why a decision was made, owed by the decision-maker to the recipient — is the account-giving stage of Bovens's relation, minus the forum's power to judge and minus consequences. The paper can say that organisational justice named the *obligation to explain* forty years ago and did not develop the machinery around it, because its unit was a supervisor who was manifestly present and could be asked.

The construct is also the one that most clearly requires a second party. Bies and Moag's criteria cannot be satisfied by anything the recipient does: truthfulness, respect and justification are all properties of the other party's conduct. That is the state/relation distinction §5 draws, made in 1986 about a different construct, and it is worth quoting to a reviewer who suspects the distinction is engineered. The corollary matters too: of the four justice types, interactional justice is the one a platform cannot deliver even in principle, because there is no interaction and no party enacting anything.

## Caution
Not read in the original; the construct and criteria are reconstructed from Colquitt's validation and the standard accounts, so quote nothing. The chapter is in a JAI Press volume with no DOI and no digital deposit — verify the volume, editors and pagination from a library copy before it enters a reference list. The construct's status is contested: whether interactional justice is a distinct type or a facet of procedural justice was debated for two decades, and Colquitt's four-factor solution is one resolution among several. Its setting is face-to-face treatment by an identifiable person, which is exactly the condition the paper's arrangement removes — that mismatch is the interesting point and also the reason the construct cannot simply be applied.
