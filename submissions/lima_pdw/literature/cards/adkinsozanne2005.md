## What it argues

Adkins and Ozanne begin from a number that is hard to argue past: almost half of all consumers read below a sixth-grade level, and consumer research had almost nothing to say about how those consumers get their needs met. Their qualitative study examines where literacy skills meet consumption activity and catalogues the coping strategies low-literate consumers use. The informants who fared best were those who could both challenge the stigma of low literacy and deploy a range of coping skills. The conceptual payoff is a redefinition: consumer literacy is "a social practice that includes reading and writing skills but also involves the ability to manage one's identity and leverage personal, situational, and social coping skills."

## Relation to the argument

The most textured counter-evidence to a near-zero market cell, and much the closest in *form* to what §5 and §7 describe for the platform worker. The structure is the same: a party must decode an environment that does not explain itself, cannot ask openly without cost to standing, develops workarounds, and either recruits others into the work or fails. Read beside Rahman on folk theories, the resemblance is close enough that the paper should decide deliberately whether to treat it as a precedent for algorithmacy's shape or as a contrast case — and saying which is better than leaving it unremarked.

The identity-management component is the part worth borrowing. Competence for a form is not only a matter of knowing things; it includes managing what one's incapacity signals to counterparts. That is a facet §7 could recognize in the co-optation case, where a worker's visible confusion is itself costly.

## Caution

Abstract depth only, and the study is interpretive — a small set of informants in one national context, with no claim to generalize a magnitude. The "almost half read below sixth-grade level" figure comes from U.S. adult literacy surveys of the period and should be re-sourced rather than cited from here. Most importantly, low literacy is a condition the participant brings to the market, whereas the seventh row asks what the *form* demands; treating this as a demand-side finding requires an argument, not an assumption.
