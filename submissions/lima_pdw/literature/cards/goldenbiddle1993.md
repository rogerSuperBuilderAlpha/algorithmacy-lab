## What it argues

Golden-Biddle and Locke analyse a sample of ethnographic articles to work out how such texts actually persuade, and they isolate three dimensions. **Authenticity** convinces the reader the writer was there and grasped how members understood their world — achieved by particularizing everyday life, stating the relationship between researcher and members, showing disciplined data analysis, and qualifying personal biases. **Plausibility** convinces the reader the findings matter to a shared concern. **Criticality** provokes the reader to re-examine his own assumptions. A text must have at least the first two.

## Relation to the argument

Their authenticity strategies read as a checklist this paper can meet and mostly already plans to: state the researcher–member relationship (§9), show the analytic discipline (§7's committed scheme, blind coder, discard log), qualify the biases (§10). It is also the citation that makes the case *for* disclosing the instructor position in the text rather than burying it — under their scheme, delineating that relationship is what buys authenticity, not what costs it.

## Caution

A study of how texts convince, which is not the same as how they are true; a hostile reader can turn the whole apparatus around as evidence that rhetoric is doing the work. It analyses 1980s ethnographies, and interview-plus-records designs sit a little outside its sample.
