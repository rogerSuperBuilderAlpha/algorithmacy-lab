## What it argues

Yang and Liechty build a general AI-literacy-and-competency framework for the social work profession, not a child-welfare-specific model. They define AI literacy and AI competency, adapt general AI-literacy frameworks to social work's relational, data, organizational, and equity commitments, and align the resulting constructs with the NASW Code of Ethics and CSWE Educational Policy and Accreditation Standards (EPAS) — proposing a tenth core competency, "Engage AI and Algorithmic Systems Informed Practice." The chapter's competency language centers on "relational integrity" — sustaining trust and supporting client autonomy when digital systems mediate contact — and states that "technology should complement rather than replace authentic relational practice."

**Child welfare appears twice, and one of the two is specific and load-bearing.** Section 6 lists it among domains where algorithmic tools are increasingly embedded ("child welfare, mental health, benefits eligibility, and criminal justice systems"), a one-line mention. But Section 5.3 ("Agency Initiatives and Policy Frameworks for AI Competency") gives a specific, concrete example: "child welfare agencies that use predictive analytics models for risk assessment have organized training sessions to help social workers understand model outputs and interpret them critically rather than blindly trust algorithmic recommendations." This is a genuine algorithmic-risk-scoring example, situated specifically in child welfare, and it frames the AI's output as a "recommendation" the human social worker must critically evaluate rather than a determination that binds — i.e., advisory, not authoritative.
