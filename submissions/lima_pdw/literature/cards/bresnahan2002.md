## What it argues

The empirical core of the skill-biased-technical-change-as-complementarity argument. Using firm-level data linking IT capital, workplace organisation and workforce composition, the authors identify a three-way complementarity: information technology, decentralised work organisation (broader job definitions, team structures, more worker decision authority), and demand for more-skilled labour move together. The clustering is what identifies the argument — firms that adopt IT without the organisational changes do not show the same skill demand, and the pieces are worth more jointly than separately.

The distributional implication is direct. A technology that is complementary to organisational forms which are themselves complementary to skill will not deliver uniform returns; it raises the return to the already-skilled and to the firms already organised to use it.

## Relation to the argument

Together with Brynjolfsson & Hitt (2000) and Bartel, Ichniowski & Shaw (2007), this is the pre-AI evidentiary base for saying that the same technology yields sharply different outcomes. It is also the source of the strongest general prior *against* §1: the historical pattern is that technology-induced dispersion tracks skill, and this paper says so at firm scale with three decades of subsequent citation behind it. The generative-AI heterogeneity results — Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, Choi and Schwarcz, Cui et al. — are, in this frame, a novel *reversal* of a well-established regularity rather than a fresh demonstration of it.

The manuscript is better off acknowledging that prior than writing as though heterogeneity were an unexplained novelty.
