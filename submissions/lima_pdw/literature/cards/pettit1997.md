## What it argues

Pettit's claim is that freedom is non-domination rather than non-interference. A person is free to the extent that no other person or group holds arbitrary, uncontrolled power over them, whether or not that power is used. Secondary sources give the book's definition of domination, at p. 52, in three clauses: someone dominates another to the extent that they have the capacity to interfere, on an arbitrary basis, in certain choices the other is in a position to make. The capacity is what matters. The slave of a kindly master is unfree because the master could interfere at will, and whether he does is, in Lovett's words, neither here nor there; Pettit's own treatment of the benevolent master is reported at pp. 73–74. Consent is no defence: Lindblom quotes Pettit (p. 62) to the effect that a relationship consensual in origin still dominates if it gives one party the effective capacity to interfere more or less arbitrarily in the other's choices. Interference is arbitrary, on the book's account, when it is not forced to track the interests and ideas of the person interfered with; Pettit later preferred to speak of uncontrolled rather than arbitrary interference, and Lovett's procedural reading says power is arbitrary to the extent that it is not reliably constrained by rules, procedures or goals that are common knowledge to those concerned.

The institutional half of the book follows from the definition. If freedom is non-domination, the state's job is to make power non-arbitrary, and Lovett reports Pettit (pp. 186–187) requiring that decision-makers give reasons, that contestation be open to all on equal terms, and that there be impartial courts of appeal. Contestatory democracy, not consent, is what makes public power track the interests of the governed.

## Relation to the argument

Arm: `orgsci_agency_triad`. Standing, in the arm's P2, is a recognised right to contest a verdict before someone obliged to answer. Pettit supplies the normative ground for calling its absence a loss of agency and not merely an inconvenience. A platform that can re-rank, demote or suspend a worker at will, on a rule the worker cannot see and cannot contest, holds the capacity to interfere on an arbitrary basis in choices the worker is in a position to make; by the p. 52 definition that is domination, and by the p. 62 passage the worker's having agreed to the terms does not change it. The kindly-master case is the one the arm needs against the reply that most platforms treat most workers well: a platform that rarely interferes still dominates if nothing forces its interference to track the worker's interests. Standing, on this reading, is non-domination at the scale of a single verdict, and the three requirements at pp. 186–187 — reasons, equal openness to contest, an impartial forum — are a checklist the arm can set against any platform's appeal process. Competence without standing is Pettit's slave with a good map of the master's moods.

The arm must keep the scale right. Pettit's subject is the state, and the lab's `exetasis_sovereignty` arm owns democratic sovereignty; this arm stays at the organizational level and uses non-domination to characterise one relation, the evaluated party's relation to the evaluator.
