## What it argues

Survey plus administrative data giving **"the first comprehensive analysis of the labor market for Uber's driver-partners."** Drivers are drawn by **"the flexibility it offers, the level of compensation, and the fact that earnings per hour do not vary much based on the number of hours worked."** Demographically they resemble the general workforce more than they resemble taxi drivers. **"Most of Uber's driver-partners had full- or part-time employment before joining Uber, and many continue in those positions after starting to drive."**

## Relation to the argument

The load-bearing clause for §1 is buried in the middle: **earnings per hour do not vary much with hours worked.** That is, from an Uber-affiliated economist writing with Uber's own data, the closest thing to a published source for "the difference does not track the hours they work." It is a weak version of the claim — a statement about the returns-to-hours gradient, not about residual dispersion — but it is the only version with administrative backing, and it is on the right platform.

Its second use is as the baseline the manuscript's driver departs from. Hall and Krueger describe a workforce for whom driving is supplementary and exit is cheap. §1's claim that drivers "quit at different rates" needs that context: churn on this platform is extremely high for everyone, and differential quitting has to be established against a very noisy baseline.

## Caution

Co-authored by Uber's own head of economic research using Uber's data, published with a well-known dispute over the treatment of driver expenses; the compensation figures in particular were contested at length. Survey responses with unreported response rates. "Earnings per hour do not vary much based on the number of hours worked" is a claim about the *hours gradient*, not about variance across drivers — do not silently upgrade it into "outcome variance does not track hours," which is a different and unsupported proposition. 2014–15 US data, superseded in most respects by Cook et al. (2021). Grey-adjacent in provenance despite a peer-reviewed venue: flag the source of the data whenever it is cited.
