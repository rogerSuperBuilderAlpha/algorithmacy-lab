## What it argues
Hirsch and colleagues coin *contestability* as a design property, from a machine-learning system built to assess psychotherapy quality by rating clinicians' sessions. Their case has a feature the abstract discourse lacks: the people being rated are experts who disagree with the ratings and have professional standing to say so. From that friction they argue that systems delivering consequential assessments of people must be designed so the assessed party can push back, and they name design strategies to that end — accuracy pursued through visible testing, and *legibility* pursued through explanation, confidence scores and traceability, so that a clinician can see which part of the session drove the rating and challenge that part rather than the verdict. Their broader claim is that contestability is not a bolt-on interface element but a stance toward the relationship between system and subject, and that a system asserting authority over expert judgement without an answer to disagreement will be resisted or ignored.

## Relation to the argument
This is the origin point for the contestability vocabulary the rest of the cluster uses, and it earns a citation for a reason beyond priority. Their subjects are clinicians — people with credentials, professional bodies and a language for disputing an assessment — and the paper's argument is that even *those* subjects need contestability engineered in. That is a hard case for anyone who thinks individual capacity supplies answerability, and the paper should use it as such: if professional standing does not suffice, worker skill will not.

Their legibility strategies also map onto §7 precisely and expose the limit §5 draws. Explanation, confidence scores and traceability are supports for what §7 calls asymmetric interpretation — they make the rule readable. What they do not do is create an obligation to answer. Hirsch and colleagues have to supply legibility *and* a stance toward disagreement, and the paper can point out that the first is on the worker's side of the division and the second is not.

## Caution
A four-page provocation-length DIS paper, read at abstract depth with its content confirmed through Lyons et al.; do not quote it directly without pulling the original. The setting is clinical quality assessment with a small, expert, credentialed population, which is not platform labour. It is a design argument with no evaluation — no evidence is offered that the strategies produce contestation in practice. And "contestability" here is looser than the later frameworks make it; do not read Alfrink's specificity back into this source.
