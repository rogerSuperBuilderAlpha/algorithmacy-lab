## What it argues

A practitioner handbook. Wenger, McDermott, and Snyder take the community of practice and turn it into something a firm can deliberately establish, sponsor, and harvest: seven design principles for cultivating communities, a five-stage development model from potential through coalescing, maturing, stewardship, and transformation, and guidance on sponsorship, measurement, and value. Knowledge is the strategic asset, and the community is the vehicle for managing it.

## Relation to the argument

This card exists to mark a trap rather than to supply support. The paper needs "community of practice" in its 1991 sense — an analytic description of where learning actually happens — and the term now arrives in most management readers' heads in this 2002 sense, as a thing management sets up. §10 would be badly misread if it were taken to say that a cohort community can be cultivated so that algorithmacy spreads; the whole force of the section is that the withheld rule is not in anyone's possession to be cultivated. The book is also the clearest evidence for the reception history Contu & Willmott (2003) document, so citing it in a footnote alongside them lets the paper name which sense of the term it uses and move on. It has one substantive use: its stage model is the closest thing in this literature to a claim about how *fast* participation converts into competence, which is a live question for a sixteen-week cohort.

## Caution

Not a research monograph. Its claims are consultancy-grade, drawn from cases the authors advised on, with no independent evidence and no counterfactual. Cite it as an artifact of the concept's managerialization, never as evidence about how learning works.
