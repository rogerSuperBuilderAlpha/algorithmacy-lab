## What it argues

Ridley and Pawlick-Potts argue that existing digital-literacy and information-literacy regimes do not equip people for AI, and offer algorithmic literacy as the gap-filler, with libraries as the institution to deliver it. Following Bawden, they explain rather than define. Algorithmic literacy is "the skill, expertise, and awareness to" do five things: understand and reason about algorithms and their processes; recognise and interpret their use in systems, embedded or overt; create and apply algorithmic techniques and tools to problems across domains; assess the influence and effect of algorithms in social, cultural, economic and political contexts; and position the individual as a co-constituent in algorithmic decision-making. Two overarching concepts organise the five: creativity (building and using algorithms) and critical analysis (recognising algorithmic decision-making and its implications).

Their sources are pedagogical. Long and Magerko's AI literacy supplies the operational template — "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool" — and its 17 core competencies with 15 associated design considerations. Ridley and Pawlick-Potts add that algorithmic literacy "is not about mastery but levels of competence appropriate" to a person's circumstances, and note that library practitioners identified algorithmic awareness as a new competency missing from the ACRL Framework.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour, and the clearest specimen of the type §8 needs to exclude: a literacy defined *for the purpose of teaching it*. Every element of the definition is a learning objective, the institutional home is the library, and the delivery mechanism is instruction. Set against the manuscript's two properties, this construct fails both by design — it is acquired by teaching rather than through participation, and it is assumed raisable in anyone who takes the workshop rather than unevenly distributed as a function of position. When §8 says a skill is trainable by instruction and dates with the tool, this is the citation for the first half.

The fifth element deserves attention because it is the closest anything in this literature gets to the manuscript's third position, and it is not close. "Position the individual as a co-constituent in algorithmic decision-making" puts a second party into the relation — but the party is the *system's operator*, or the sociotechnical assemblage, not another human subject to the same determination. Co-constitution is a claim about agency being distributed between person and system. Algorithmacy's triad is a claim about a system standing between two people and committing both. The relation still has two positions; the second one has merely been given some agency.

Use this card, too, for the register point. Ridley and Pawlick-Potts write "competence" where the manuscript writes "competency," and they mean levels of attainment on a teachable curriculum. Section 8 has to distinguish its term from this ordinary usage explicitly, or a reader will hear the pedagogical sense.

## Caution

A position paper in a library and information science journal, not empirical research and not a validated framework: no items, no sample, no evidence about how algorithmic literacy is distributed or whether the proposed pedagogy works. The five-element description the authors themselves label provisional. Long and Magerko's AI literacy competencies are cited here at second hand — go to that paper directly if the manuscript needs them.
