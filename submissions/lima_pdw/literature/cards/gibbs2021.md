# Gibbs, J. L., Kirkwood, G. L., Fang, C., & Wilkenfeld, J. N. (2021). Negotiating agency and control: Theorizing human-machine communication from a structurational perspective. *Human-Machine Communication*, 2, 153–171.

**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Gibbs and colleagues argue HMC has been theorising the human-machine encounter as an interaction and should be theorising it as a **structuration**: machines are not just interlocutors but carriers of rules and resources that humans draw on, reproduce, and occasionally subvert, and agency is distributed across the human, the machine, and the institutional arrangement that put them together. They work the argument through organisational cases — algorithmic management among them — and their central claim is that control and agency are negotiated in practice rather than allocated by design.

Their explicit target is HMC's own individualism: a field that studies one person and one machine cannot see the organisational structures that determine what the machine is doing there.

## Relation to the argument

**The closest thing in HMC to the objection §9 raises against HMC, made from inside the field.** If §9 asserts that HMC inherits a dyad, Gibbs et al. is the counterexample a reviewer will produce: an HMC paper whose whole argument is that the dyad is inadequate and that institutional structure has to enter the account. The paper cannot pretend it does not exist.

It also does not defeat the claim, and the reason is precise. Structuration brings in **rules and resources**, an institutional third term, not a **party**. The counterpart §9 needs — a specific human on the other side of the coordination whose reception is the point and who is never met — does not appear in Gibbs et al. either. Their triad is human, machine, structure; the paper's is worker, counterpart, system. That is a defensible distinction and it is exactly the sort of thing §9 should be doing instead of counting positions.

Their distributed-agency argument is separately useful to §5–§6: it gives a communication-theoretic vocabulary for saying the determination is committed by the arrangement rather than by any party in it.

## Caution

Conceptual paper in a young open-access journal, illustrative cases rather than data. Note the author list: Gibbs, Kirkwood, Fang, and Wilkenfeld — **not** Gibbs, Kim & Boyraz, which is a different Gibbs collaboration (the 2017 virtual-teams encyclopedia entry) and a common miscitation.
