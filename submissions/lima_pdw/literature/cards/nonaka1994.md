## What it argues

Nonaka proposes that organizational knowledge is created through a continuous dialogue between tacit and explicit knowledge, and identifies four patterns of interaction between them — the conversions later known as SECI: socialization (tacit to tacit, through shared experience and apprenticeship), externalization (tacit to explicit, through metaphor, analogy, and model), combination (explicit to explicit), and internalization (explicit to tacit, through doing). New knowledge is developed by individuals; the organization's role is to articulate and amplify it, moving it up through a spiral from individual to group to organization. The article develops the analytic framework and then two operational models — middle-up-down management and the hypertext organization — for putting it to work. *The Knowledge-Creating Company* (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, Oxford University Press, ISBN 9780195092691, ISBN-10 0195092694) is the book-length version.

## Relation to the argument

Nonaka is the position §10 must define itself against, and the card's main use is negative. His framework's whole promise is that tacit knowledge can be **externalized** — made explicit through metaphor and model, and then combined and taught — which is precisely what §10 denies for the case at hand. A knowledge-management reader will reach for externalization as the obvious objection: if the gate's expectations are tacit, articulate them. The paper's answer has to be that the SECI spiral presupposes that somebody inside the organization holds the tacit knowledge to be externalized, and here the holder is the system, which is not a participant in any spiral and discloses nothing. Socialization is the more interesting half: it is tacit-to-tacit transfer through shared experience, and it is exactly the mechanism §11's third study question tests when it asks whether noticing travels through the cohort. The Lima interview supplies an early negative for the paper to reckon with — the participant did not know whether other participants had noticed the shift in review focus, and said discussion with others was the missing part.

## Caution

Among the most-cited and most-criticized papers in the field. The standard objection is that Nonaka misreads Polanyi: for Polanyi tacit knowing is structurally non-convertible, while externalization requires it to convert, and Collins's taxonomy suggests only *relational* tacit knowledge could behave that way. The evidence base is Japanese manufacturing firms in a particular period, and the framework is prescriptive as much as descriptive. Cite Nonaka to name the position the paper rejects, and cite Polanyi or Collins for the reason it is rejectable — do not cite Nonaka as authority on tacit knowledge itself. Abstract-level read; the four conversions are verified from the abstract, but the SECI acronym and its diagrams come from the later work.
