## What it argues

The field's own audit, and it is unflattering. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum searched "algorithmic literacy" plus seven neighbouring terms — algorithmic awareness, algorithmic knowledge, algorithmic understanding, algorithmic experience, algorithmic skills, algorithmic divide, and algorithm + belief — across Google Scholar, Communication and Mass Media Complete, PsycInfo and the ACM Digital Library, screened 96 candidates and reviewed 50. The terminological spread is their first finding: the field has two competing definitions (DeVito's capacity-and-opportunity formulation and Dogruel, Masur and Joeckel's four-part one), several partially overlapping sub-constructs, and no agreement on whether literacy is one construct or a collection of others.

Their reading of the measurement situation is the part the manuscript needs. Dogruel and colleagues' scale is "currently the most comprehensive attempt," and it still "breaks into two sub-scales that together do not measure algorithmic literacy as one cohesive construct." Zarouali and colleagues' AMCA-scale is the more developed awareness measure but is keyed to a named platform rather than to algorithms generally, and no published work had used it beyond Facebook, Netflix and YouTube. Awareness elsewhere is measured indirectly, by asking open-ended questions and hoping indicators surface, or by a single self-report item (Gran and colleagues). Underneath all of it sits the problem Hargittai and colleagues raised: the researcher cannot establish how correct a user is, because the algorithm's working is unknown to the researcher too.

Their agenda has four items: balance the expectation that users be literate against developers' responsibility for transparency; develop methods for engaging users in raising their own literacy; build out the affective and behavioural facets, since current measures cluster almost entirely in the cognitive dimension; and address the new algorithmic divide.

## Relation to the argument

The most efficient resource in this sub-cluster, and it should do real work in §9 rather than sit in a footnote. Two uses.

First, it establishes on the field's own authority that the rival constructs measure knowledge and awareness, that they cluster in the cognitive dimension, and that no cohesive construct exists — which means a reviewer cannot answer §9 by pointing at a settled rival. The manuscript can say "algorithmic literacy" is contested territory and cite the review rather than asserting it.

Second, and more valuable, it is the negative result the manuscript's structural claim predicts. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum canvass the whole field, catalogue every definitional variant, tabulate every measure, and propose four directions for future work — and a human counterpart appears in none of it. Not in the definitions, not in the sub-constructs, not in the measures, not in the agenda. The abstract's claim, "co-optation demands the most and nobody has named it," is exactly the kind of claim a systematic review can support or destroy, and this one supports it: 50 reviewed articles, eight search terms, and the third position never surfaces. Cite the search terms and the article count so the negative result reads as evidence rather than as assertion.

One caution about how the review cuts. Their agenda item on "methods for engaging users in increasing their literacy" assumes literacy is raisable by intervention, which is the field's default and the opposite of §10's implicit-acquisition property. The manuscript is arguing against a research programme with momentum, not against an unexamined assumption, and it should acknowledge that.

## Caution

**Which year to cite:** version of record 27(2), 681–701, February 2025; online first July 2023. Cite 2025 with the page range.

A narrative review, not a systematic one in the PRISMA sense — the search is documented but the screening criteria are stated loosely ("we determined if the article empirically assessed or theorized Internet users' understanding of or interaction with algorithms"), inclusion moved from title to abstract to full text without reported inter-rater agreement, and additional articles were snowballed from the initial set. Social media is the declared focus, so work in employment, credit, welfare and platform labour is largely outside the frame; the absence of a counterpart in the reviewed corpus is therefore partly a consequence of where they looked, and the manuscript should say so rather than overclaim. The cut-off is early 2023; anything published since is not covered. Page numbers cited above refer to the published pagination in the Duisburg-Essen deposit, which matches the version of record.
