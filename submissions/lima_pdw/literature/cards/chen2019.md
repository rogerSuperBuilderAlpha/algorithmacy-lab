## What it argues

Using Uber administrative data and experimental variation in driver pay, the authors estimate the labour-supply elasticity of rideshare drivers and the surplus they derive from schedule flexibility. The central finding is that drivers' reservation wages vary enormously moment to moment and driver to driver, and that the flexibility to work exactly when one chooses is worth a substantial fraction of earnings — surplus that a fixed schedule would destroy. Heterogeneity in when and how much drivers work is treated as preference heterogeneity, and it is large.

## Relation to the argument

The best available evidence that dispersion in platform outcomes is generated in part by dispersion in *supply behaviour* — when a driver chooses to be online — rather than by anything the platform does differently to different drivers. That is a third rival explanation for §1, distinct from prior skill and from task mix, and §6's list does not currently name it. If earnings differ because reservation wages and schedule preferences differ, no competency is required to explain the spread.

Naming it costs the manuscript a sentence and buys credibility. It is also the rival that a qualitative design can partly address, since when-and-why-I-drive is exactly the kind of thing an interview reaches.

## Caution

No abstract in the Crossref deposit; do not attribute numbers without a full-text read. Uber administrative data, with the access-and-independence questions that carry. The paper's project is welfare estimation of flexibility, not outcome dispersion, so the heterogeneity is a modelling input rather than a documented finding about who earns what. US, pre-2019. Nothing about quitting, about algorithmic management as such, or about the information environment.
