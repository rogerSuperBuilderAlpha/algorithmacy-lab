## What it argues
Hirschman asks what a member does when an organisation's quality declines, and finds two recuperative mechanisms rather than one. *Exit* is the economist's answer: the customer stops buying, the member leaves, and the aggregate loss of custom signals decline. *Voice* is the political scientist's: the member stays and complains, petitions, protests, or appeals to a higher authority — "any attempt at all to change, rather than to escape from, an objectionable state of affairs." Voice is costly, graduated and requires effort and skill; exit is cheap and binary. The two interact in ways that are not symmetric. Easy exit atrophies voice, because the dissatisfied leave rather than argue, and the organisation loses its most quality-sensitive members precisely when it most needs their objections. *Loyalty* is the third term, and it functions as the mechanism that holds exit at bay long enough for voice to be exercised: a loyal member with somewhere else to go stays and complains, which is the combination that repairs organisations. Hirschman's darkest case is the "lazy monopoly," which prefers to shed its most demanding customers rather than satisfy them, and which is therefore *strengthened* by an available exit that removes its critics. His closing chapters argue that the design question is institutional — whether an organisation has built channels through which voice can be raised and received — and not merely a question of members' willingness to speak.

## Relation to the argument
Hirschman gives the paper the vocabulary its §5/§6 division needs and, read carefully, supports the division rather than complicating it. Voice is an *attempt*, and Hirschman is explicit that the attempt's efficacy depends on whether the organisation has arrangements to receive it — the member supplies the effort, the institution supplies the reception. That is §5's asymmetry stated thirty-five years earlier in a different vocabulary: a worker can become better at raising voice and cannot, by becoming better at anything, cause voice to be received. The platform case is Hirschman's lazy monopoly with the sign flipped. Exit is cheap for a gig worker (deactivate, switch app), which by his own argument should atrophy voice; and the platform, holding a large and replaceable supply, has little reason to build reception. The result his theory predicts is the observed one: high exit, weak voice, no repair.

The book also supplies §6's institutional-route argument in its original form. Hirschman treats voice channels as things that are *built* — grievance procedures, consumer bodies, political representation — and treats their absence as an explanation of why organisations decline without correction. And it names the paper's live interview pattern in part: the participant who identified a review error, judged the two available channels inappropriate, and never contested has exercised neither exit nor voice, which is a residual category Hirschman's scheme leaves thin and which the silence literature (Pinder & Harlos; Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin) later fills.

## Caution
Hirschman writes about members of firms, organisations and states who hold membership in the first place, which is exactly the standing co-optation withholds; the transfer to a party enrolled without standing is the paper's extension. He is a theorist of *decline* and recuperation, not of injustice or accountability, and voice in his sense is aimed at improving the organisation's performance rather than at obtaining redress for the individual — do not silently convert voice into appeal. His treatment of loyalty is the book's most criticised part, and later work disputes both its status as a third option and its measurement. Finally, exit for a platform worker is not the clean exit of Hirschman's consumer: reputation, ratings and earnings history are non-portable, so leaving forfeits an asset, which is a case his framework does not model.
