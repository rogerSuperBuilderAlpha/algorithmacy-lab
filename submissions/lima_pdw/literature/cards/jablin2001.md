## What it argues

Jablin and Sias import the competence construct into organisational communication and argue it has to be respecified once it enters an organisation, because organisations distribute competence across levels. They propose an **ecological model** with four: the individual, the relationship/dyad, the group, and the organisation — and argue that competence at one level does not aggregate to competence at the next, that organisations hold competence in routines and structures as well as in people, and that competence requirements are role-specific and change as an incumbent is assimilated.

The assimilation argument is the chapter's most useful move: newcomers acquire communication competence through participation in an organisation's practices, not through instruction, and what counts as competent is learned by inference from how others respond.

## Relation to the argument

**Two of the paper's three claims about competency already exist here.** §8's "Competency | the individual in a role | participation | the form | performance in a role" row is close to Jablin and Sias's role-specific, assimilation-acquired competence, and §10's implicit-acquisition property is close to their assimilation account. That is not a defeat — their organisation is a bounded, disclosing, human-populated one, and what the worker infers from is colleagues rather than an undisclosed adaptive rule — but it means the paper's claim to be doing something new with "acquired through participation" needs qualification. This is the closest antecedent in the organisational-communication literature to §8's table.

Their multi-level ecology also anticipates §8's possession argument from the other side. They say competence lives at the organisational level too, in routines; the dynamic-capabilities literature §8 argues against says the same thing in another vocabulary. §8's answer — that this party owns nothing — is the right one, and it is stronger stated against Jablin and Sias, who are inside the communication lineage the paper claims, than against Teece alone.

## Caution

A 2001 handbook chapter; the organisational-communication literature has moved on and the platform case is entirely outside its frame. The multi-level model is descriptive rather than measured. Sias is second author; the chapter is often cited as Jablin alone.
