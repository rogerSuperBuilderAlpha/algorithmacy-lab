## What it argues

The founding statement of trustworthiness as the naturalistic parallel to conventional rigour, with four criteria and their techniques: **credibility** (prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative case analysis, member checking), **transferability** (thick description sufficient for a reader to judge fit), **dependability** and **confirmability** (both served by the audit trail and an external audit).

## Relation to the argument

Every term §7 uses — member checking, audit trail, negative case analysis — comes from here, and citing the source is what turns that paragraph from a list of good intentions into a claim against a published standard. Transferability is also the right frame for §10's concession: the study does not claim that a competency found in a technical, self-selected cohort generalises, it claims to describe the setting thickly enough for a reader to judge where else it might hold.

## Caution

Forty years old, book-length, and so heavily cited that it can read as ritual. The four criteria have been substantially criticised since — Morse (2015) and others argue they certify process rather than quality — so pairing Lincoln and Guba with Pratt, Kaplan & Whittington (2020) is the stronger move in a management venue.
