## What it argues

Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn and Nelson identify **gig literacies**: critical literacies emerging among online freelancers, comprising the skills and work strategies workers adapt in order to use platforms creatively and productively as part of their "personal holding environment." The construct has two halves, and the second is what distinguishes it — using the resources the platform provides effectively, *and* negotiating or working around its imposed structures and control mechanisms. Working around the platform is part of being literate in it, not a deviation from literacy.

The evidence base is what makes this card urgent. Twenty interviews with online freelancers, **nineteen interviews with the corresponding clients**, and a first-hand walkthrough of Upwork. The framing problem is precarity: platforms appear to solve parts of it by making work findable and securable, and introduce demands and constraints of their own in the process.

## Relation to the argument — FLAG

The manuscript already claims this as its nearest precedent, and the claim is right, but the card has to record something the manuscript should not discover from a reviewer: **this is the only source in the sub-cluster whose empirical design contains the human counterpart.** Sutherland and colleagues interviewed nineteen clients alongside twenty freelancers, on the same platform, about the same transactions. Upwork's ranking, matching and Job Success Score bear on both sides. The triad is present in the research setting.

What it is not present in is the construct. Gig literacies are defined as the freelancer's skills and strategies for using and working around the platform — a two-position relation between worker and system, with the client supplying context and corroboration rather than occupying a theorised position. Nothing in the definition requires the worker to reconstruct a specific client's intent, and nothing turns on the platform issuing a determination binding on both parties. The clients were interviewed to understand the platform's operation, not because the construct is defined on them.

**And it is narrower still than the client interviews alone suggest.** Converging secondary summaries of the article name five aspects of gig literacy — reputation and ratings management, self-presentation, productivity management, risk management, and **relationship building**. If "relationship building" is the authors' own heading, then the counterpart is not merely in the room; it is inside the construct, as one fifth of it. The discrimination then has to run on *where* the relation sits rather than on whether it exists. Gig literacies put the counterpart **beside** the platform: the freelancer manages the platform and separately builds a relation with the client, frequently by moving that relation off-platform, which is a strategy for escaping mediation. Algorithmacy is defined on coordination that runs **through** an intermediary neither party can bypass, and on an intermediary that commits determinations binding on both. Upwork matches and ranks; it does not adjudicate between freelancer and client in the way the manuscript's fourth form requires. That distinction survives the five-aspect list, and it is the only one that does. **Verify the five names against the article's section headings before Lima** — the manuscript's §9 depends on the answer.

So the discrimination survives, but narrowly, and the manuscript must state it in exactly this form: the nearest precedent had the third position in the room and did not put it in the construct. That is a far better sentence than a vaguer claim of descent, and it is also a warning — a reviewer who knows this paper will ask what algorithmacy adds beyond gig literacies, and the answer has to be the positional one, not "we studied clients too."

Two further discriminations. Gig literacies are **literacies, plural and inductive** — assembled from what freelancers reported doing, which is the inventory side of §6's derivation line, and the authors say as much by presenting them as "emerging" from the interviews. And "skills and work strategies" is the vocabulary §8 sorts as skill: adaptable, teachable, indexed to Upwork's particular structures. Algorithmacy's claim that the competency dates with the form and not the platform is the load-bearing difference in §8, and this is the paper to test it against.

## Caution

Twenty freelancers and nineteen clients on one platform, Upwork, at one time; a qualitative construct with no instrument and no distributional claim, so it cannot support anything about stratified fluency.
