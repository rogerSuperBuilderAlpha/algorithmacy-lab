## What it argues

The field-defining review of financial literacy, published in economics' own review journal. Lusardi and Mitchell assess a fast-growing body of work that models financial knowledge as a form of human capital in which people invest, and they draw the welfare and policy consequences of endogenizing it. Their survey evidence establishes two facts that matter here: people know strikingly little about the instruments they transact in, and the shortfall is not evenly spread — the review identifies "the least financially savvy population subgroups." They then trace the effect of literacy on economic decision-making in the United States and elsewhere, and take stock of what remedies work.

## Relation to the argument

This is the single strongest piece of ammunition against §4's market cell, and the paper is better off meeting it head-on than hoping an economist reader misses it. Financial literacy is a competency demanded of market participants, validated in short standardized item batteries (the "Big Three"), measured in nationally representative surveys across dozens of countries, unevenly distributed across education, gender, age and income, and predictive of outcomes — retirement planning, portfolio diversification, borrowing costs. Structurally it is the market column's answer to network competence: named, scaled, replicated, consequential. If the seventh row's test is "does some capacity of the coordinated party vary with outcomes under this form," the market cell passes it loudly in this domain.

The defensible version of §4's claim therefore has to be narrower than "the market demands almost nothing." Two narrowings are available and both are in the paper's own text. The first is Hayek's: in a *spot market with a public price*, the price is a sufficient statistic and the demand on the participant is small; the demand rises as the market departs from those conditions, and consumer finance — multi-period, contingent, compounding, with the payoff separated from the purchase by decades — departs about as far as a market can. The second is the paper's own ordinal reading: the cell already says *almost* nothing and already concedes Akerlof. Financial literacy should be added to that concession explicitly, with the boundary stated, or a reviewer will state it for you.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth; the specific figures, country comparisons and effect sizes must be taken from the article before any of them enters a draft. The literature has a live internal dispute the review cannot settle: measured literacy correlates with outcomes, but educational interventions produce small and fast-decaying effects, and part of the correlation plausibly reflects cognitive ability, numeracy and unobserved preferences rather than knowledge as such. Lusardi is a principal architect of the standard instrument, so the review is not a disinterested audit of it.
