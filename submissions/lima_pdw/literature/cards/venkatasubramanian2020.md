## What it argues
Venkatasubramanian and Alfano ask why recourse should be provided at all, and answer from moral and political philosophy rather than from machine learning. Their grounding is temporally extended agency and the conditions of autonomy: a person's capacity to act as an agent depends on being able to form plans that connect present action to future outcomes, and that capacity requires a world in which the rules connecting the two are stable enough to plan against and open enough to act on. A decision system that classifies a person without leaving any action open to her does not merely inconvenience her; it removes the conditions under which she can be an agent with respect to that domain. They connect this to the philosophical literature on autonomy and to the political requirement that people subject to a power be able to do something about it, and they argue that recourse is therefore a normative requirement on decision systems rather than a courtesy, with the burden falling on the system's operator. They also distinguish recourse from explanation: knowing why a decision went against you is not the same as having something to do about it, and only the latter restores agency.

## Relation to the argument
This is the normative argument §5 and §6 are implicitly relying on, and the paper never states it. §6 says naming the competency "does not license" the transfer of an institutional obligation onto workers, and that sentence needs a ground. Venkatasubramanian and Alfano supply one: the obligation sits with the operator because the operator is what removed the conditions of agency, and no amount of the subject's effort restores what the operator withdrew. That converts §6's closing move from an ethical aside into a claim with philosophical backing.

Their explanation/recourse distinction is also the cleanest available statement of §5's split. Understanding why is a state the subject can reach; having something to do about it is a property of the arrangement. The paper's two supplies-privately conditions are on the first side and accountability is on the second, and this article draws the line at the same place from a different discipline. Their emphasis on *temporally extended* agency also underwrites §7's temporal tracking as more than a coping skill: a rule that changes without announcement destroys the planning horizon on which agency depends.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth; do not quote or attribute specific philosophical positions without pulling the paper. It is a normative argument and supplies no empirical evidence about systems or subjects. Its recourse remains recourse in the technical sense — an available action that changes an outcome — and is not accountability in Bovens's sense, so the paper should cite it for why the obligation sits with the operator rather than for what a forum requires. The authors' philosophical sources are lightly handled in a nine-page conference paper, and a philosopher reviewer may find the autonomy argument thin; do not lean the whole normative case on it.
