## What it argues

Qualitative interviews with 30 German internet users about awareness of **algorithmic decision-making** specifically, and about what users think it does to their autonomy. Dogruel, Facciorusso and Stark treat algorithms as socio-technical systems with human as well as technical components, and ask across areas of internet use rather than about one platform.

Three findings. Users are broadly aware that algorithms operate across a wide range of applications and hold a basic working understanding of how they function — a higher baseline than the earlier awareness literature reported. A third-person effect appears clearly: users judge algorithms to shape *other people's* internet use more than their own. And awareness is bound up with perceived autonomy in a direction worth noting — when users feel in control of their online interactions, they are *less* aware of the algorithms governing those interactions. The authors close on implications for transparency measures in algorithm regulation.

## Relation to the argument

Card this one because of the phrase "algorithmic decision-making," which is the nearest the literacy literature comes to §3's committing intermediary, and because of what the study nevertheless turns out to be about. The decisions in question are decisions about what a user sees. Nothing here adjudicates between two parties; nothing issues an output binding on a second person. The construct is awareness of ADM as it bears on the respondent's own information environment — two positions again, with the vocabulary of adjudication attached to a curation relation. If the manuscript wants to show that even the most decision-flavoured construct in the field has no counterpart in it, this is the citation.

The two psychological findings are more useful than the awareness finding. The third-person effect is a direct threat to self-report instruments: respondents systematically discount the algorithm's grip on themselves, which biases any scale asking how much algorithms affect *you*, and §11 should say what it does about that. The autonomy–awareness inversion is stranger and more interesting — a felt sense of control suppresses awareness. Read against §10, that predicts fluency will be *lowest* where the worker feels most in command, which is a testable and counterintuitive implication the manuscript could claim rather than a mere hazard.

## Caution

Thirty interviews, German internet users, general online activity rather than work; qualitative and exploratory, so the third-person effect and the autonomy relation are interpretive findings from interview talk, not measured effects, and neither should be reported with the confidence a survey result would carry. Read at abstract depth — Taylor & Francis blocks automated retrieval — so the interview protocol, the sampling and the coding are unverified here. Cite as 2022 (issue 25(9)); Crossref's issued date of December 2020 is the online-first date, and note that this is a *different* Dogruel 2022 from the algorithm literacy scale (`dogruel2022.md`) — the reference list needs a disambiguator.
