## What it argues

Mikalef and Gupta build an **AI capability** construct on the resource-based theory of the firm. Their three moves are sequential: identify the AI-specific resources that jointly constitute the capability and define it; develop an instrument to capture a firm's AI capability; and test whether that capability raises organisational creativity and firm performance. The empirical study supports the framework and the instrument, and finds AI capability associated with higher creativity and performance.

The construct is firm-level throughout, by design and by theoretical commitment. Resource-based theory asks what a firm has that rivals cannot easily copy, and AI capability is answered in those terms — a bundle of tangible, human and intangible resources the firm assembles. The instrument is a survey of firms, and the outcomes are organisational. The individual appears only as a component of what the firm has.

## Relation to the argument

**Resource for §8, and the cleanest available illustration of what "capability" commits you to.** The manuscript argues that algorithmacy cannot be a capability because a capability is held by the party that owns the routine, and this party owns nothing. Mikalef and Gupta show what a genuine AI capability looks like when someone builds one properly: firm-level resources, a firm-level instrument, firm-level outcomes, and a resource-based logic whose whole point is heterogeneity among *firms*. Nothing in that apparatus can address why two workers under one gate diverge.

It is also the discipline case for §8's warning. The manuscript says a study that measured algorithmacy on firms would be measuring something else; here is a well-executed study measuring the AI-adjacent thing on firms, published in a good IS journal, with an instrument that has its own PsycTESTS record and a substantial citing literature. When the manuscript declines the word capability, this is what it is declining — not a vague usage but an established construct with a validated measure and a theory behind it. Cite it in §8 alongside Teece, Pisano and Shuen and the manuscript's terminological argument stops being a stipulation and becomes a choice between two well-specified options.

The pairing with Ritter and Gemünden matters and should be handled together. Agent-side reading of that card records that network competence is also a firm-level construct, which exposes §4's precedent claim. Mikalef and Gupta is the same problem in the AI literature: every well-validated capability instrument in this space measures organisations, and the individual-level competency the manuscript proposes has no instrumented precedent at all. That is a harder position than §4 currently admits, and also a better statement of the contribution.

## Caution

Firm-level survey research: cite it for what AI capability is and how it is measured, never for anything about individual workers. Abstract-depth read — **the resource categories, the item count, the sample size and country, and the fit statistics were not confirmed**, so do not attribute a dimension structure to it without the article. Its theoretical frame is resource-based, which an OS/OT roundtable may regard as dated; use it as a specimen of a construct type rather than as authority. *Information & Management* uses article numbers; the reference needs 103434 in the page position. Note that the PsycTESTS entry (doi:10.1037/t85936-000) indexes the instrument separately and is not a second publication.
