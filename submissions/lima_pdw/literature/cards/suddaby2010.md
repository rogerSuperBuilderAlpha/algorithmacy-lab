## What it argues

A single-authored "Editor's Comments" essay by Roy Suddaby, then AMR Associate Editor, prompted by "construct clarity" being one of the most common stated reasons for AMR desk rejections despite editors and reviewers struggling to define the term precisely. Suddaby states two goals up front: a pragmatic one (clarify how AMR handles the complaint) and a broader one (open a discipline-wide dialogue on the role of constructs in theory). He is explicit that construct *validity* — a related but distinct topic already well covered elsewhere (Bagozzi & Edwards, 1998; Cook & Campbell, 1979; Schwab, 1980) — is outside the essay's scope.

Construct clarity, he argues, comprises exactly four elements (p. 347): **definitions** (precise, non-tautological, parsimonious — narrow enough for accuracy, broad enough to stay relevant), **scope conditions** (the contextual boundary limits — space, time, and values — under which a construct will or will not apply; omitting these "exposes one's theoretical argument to almost certain rejection"), **relations between constructs** (a construct's semantic position in the field's "nomological network," not created de novo but built on and against prior constructs), and **coherence** (whether the construct's definition, scope, lineage, and relations to other constructs "hang together" logically with the theory built on it). A closing section argues these standards vary across epistemological traditions (positivist vs. constructivist; Eisenhardt's 1989 "constructs as lenses" vs. Dyer & Wilkins's 1991 "good stories, not better constructs"; Hirsch & Levin's 1999 "umbrella advocates" vs. "validity police") but that the underlying need for stated clarity holds across all of them.

## Relation to the argument

This is the article's own organizing rubric, borrowed directly and correctly. PAPER.md line 173 restates Suddaby's four elements verbatim in his own order — "a precise definition, explicit scope conditions, stated semantic relations to neighboring constructs, and demonstrated theoretical coherence" — and the subsections that follow are genuinely structured around that sequence (construct boundary, scope conditions, relations to neighboring measures, then the paper's own additional content on operations and ontological category). This is accurate and appropriately load-bearing: Suddaby's four elements really are what he says they are, checked directly against pp. 346–347 of the source.

The lighter citation at line 31 ("Algorithmacy is a bounded behavioral construct... leaving institutional justice to the governance literatures equipped to judge it (Suddaby, 2010)") is a defensible extension of Suddaby's scope-conditions argument (pp. 347–348: a construct must specify what it does and does not cover) but risks reading, because of citation placement at the sentence's end, as if Suddaby himself discusses "institutional justice" or "governance literatures" — he does not. Moving the citation earlier, immediately after the scoping claim it actually supports, removes the ambiguity without changing the argument.

## Caution

This is an editorial essay, not an empirical study or a peer-reviewed theoretical contribution subjected to its own review process in the ordinary sense — Suddaby frames it himself as "a review and synthesis of prior writing on the subject" intended to "renew the conversation," not as a validated methodological instrument. It is legitimately citable for the four-element rubric (that is simply what the essay says), but should not be over-leaned-on as though it settles contested questions about construct clarity across epistemological traditions — the essay's own second half argues that standards genuinely vary by tradition (positivist vs. constructivist, "umbrella advocates" vs. "validity police"). It also explicitly disclaims covering construct *validity*, a term sometimes conflated with construct *clarity* in citing literature; PAPER.md does not make that conflation, but future edits should watch for it.
