## What it argues

Mills and Morris propose that clients of service organizations be treated analytically as "partial" employees. Clients have real roles to perform in creating the service, yet the field had paid little attention to their participation in "complex and demanding client performance domains." The paper offers a model of client involvement stages and takes up role definition and control for clients in complex service creation — how an organization specifies what a non-employee must do, and how it secures compliance without the employment relation to lean on.

## Relation to the argument

Prior art of an uncomfortably close kind, and the paper should meet it rather than hope it stays buried. Twenty years before the platforms, organization theory had a construct for a party who does organizationally necessary work without holding employment standing, and it asked what that party must be able to do and how the organization gets it done. That is a version of the seventh-row question, asked of one form, in 1986.

What keeps the seventh row novel against it is worth stating precisely. Mills and Morris treat the client as a *role* the organization designs, defines and controls — the analysis stays on the arrangement's side of §3's firewall. The client is told what to do; the difficulty is compliance and role clarity, not reconstruction. Co-optation's worker is not given a role definition to comply with; she must infer one from outcomes. The distinction is between a form that specifies badly and a form that does not specify.

## Caution

Abstract depth only, and this is a 1986 conceptual paper — a model, not evidence. "Partial employee" is a metaphor with legal freight it cannot carry, and it should not be transported into the paper's discussion of employment status. The client here is a customer receiving a service, whose interests the organization is at least nominally serving, which is a materially different position from a provider whose output the platform sells.
