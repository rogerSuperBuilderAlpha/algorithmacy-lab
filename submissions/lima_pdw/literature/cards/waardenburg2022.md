## What it argues

Waardenburg, Huysman and Sergeeva spent 31 months inside the Dutch police as a machine-learning system for predicting crime incidents was implemented. Their subject is a small group of intelligence officers assigned to stand between the machine-learning community that built the model and the user community — police management — that had to act on it. The officers' job was translation: making opaque algorithmic predictions usable by people who could not read them.

What the ethnography records is escalation. The brokers moved through three increasingly influential roles over time — **messenger**, then **interpreter**, then **curator**. As messengers they relayed outputs; as interpreters they explained what outputs meant; as curators they selected which outputs travelled at all. The knowledge boundary that the black-boxed model created proved impassable, and at the end of that escalation the brokers were substituting their own judgment for the algorithm's predictions while the organisation continued to treat the outputs as algorithmic. Nobody on either side could check them. Hence the title: in the land of the blind, the one-eyed man is king.

## Relation to the argument

**The closest thing in the organisation-studies literature to a competence exercised inside a triad, and it must be distinguished rather than ignored.** Everything else in this cluster is worker-and-system. Waardenburg et al. have three positions: a machine-learning community, a user community, and a person in between whose capacity determines what passes. That is structurally a triad, published in *Organization Science*, and an OS/OT roundtable will know it well.

The distinction is clean, and it runs on who occupies which position. The manuscript's triad places the *worker* at one vertex, a counterpart at another, and the system in the middle committing determinations binding on both. Waardenburg et al. place the *system* at one vertex and a human broker in the middle. Their broker's competence is a capacity to translate an opaque output to another human — a knowledge-brokerage problem — whereas algorithmacy is a capacity to reach a counterpart *through* an opaque intermediary that neither party can go around. Put concretely: the intelligence officer can read the model's output and choose what to say; the platform worker cannot read the gate and cannot address her counterpart except through it. Brokerage presupposes the very access algorithmacy is defined by the absence of.

The paper is also a resource for §5 and §6. It is a documented case of an opaque system displacing an integrating condition onto a party, and of that party supplying the condition privately — the curator role is exactly private supply of common understanding — with the organisation none the wiser. The manuscript's claim that a competency can be made to do the work an institution used to do has an ethnographic instance here, in a public-sector setting, with the accountability cost visible.

## Caution

A single-organisation ethnography in Dutch policing, with a handful of focal informants; the three roles are a processual finding from one case, not a general typology, and nothing supports a distributional claim. No competency is named, no construct proposed, no measure offered — this is practice research, and calling its brokers "competent" is the manuscript's gloss, not the authors'. The setting is an employed, hierarchical organisation, which is the manuscript's *second* column, not its fourth; the officers have supervisors, colleagues and standing that a platform worker does not, so the analogy must be drawn at the level of opacity rather than of form. Abstract-depth read: the three roles are named in the abstract and can be cited, but the practices constituting each were not confirmed.
