## What it argues

Law students at the University of Minnesota re-sat versions of their final exams with GPT-4 access after an hour of training, and their scores were compared against their own real, AI-banned exams. Participation: **"Sixteen out of fifty-two students taking Insurance Law (roughly 31%) completed the study, while thirty-two out of 128 students taking Introduction to American Law (roughly 23%) completed the study"** — 48 participants in all.

Averages split by task type. On multiple choice, AI assistance produced **"a twenty-nine percentile-point improvement relative to these students' performance on the real exam."** On essays, **"AI assistance had no effect on student grades in the essay components of the two exams."** Speed rose: for Introduction to American Law, 74.5 minutes without AI against 62.9 with (66.9 excluding three very fast finishers), a difference the bootstrap shows as statistically significant.

The heterogeneity is stated flatly in the results section: **"Beyond these averages, GPT-4 substantially improved the scores of students at the bottom of the class and negatively impacted the scores of students at the top of the class."**

## Relation to the argument

A fifth confirmation of the skill moderator, on a fourth kind of work, and the most explicit statement anywhere in the cluster that the effect *reverses sign* at the top of the distribution. Same instrument, same training, same exam, opposite outcomes according to where the taker started. §6's "novice-levelling" concession is at its strongest here and this is the compact citation for it.

It also gives the manuscript a clean instance of task-level jaggedness inside one worker's day — large gains on structured questions, nothing on open-ended reasoning — which reinforces Dell'Acqua et al. from an unrelated design.

## Caution

Small: 48 self-selected volunteers across two classes, no random assignment (each student is their own control across two different exams taken under different stakes and at different times), and the authors devote a section to these limitations. Students, not workers; grades, not earnings. The 29-percentile-point figure is multiple choice only and is routinely quoted without that qualifier. Distinguish carefully from the companion randomised trial, Choi, Monahan and Schwarcz (2024) in *Minnesota Law Review* — the two are separate studies with different designs and are frequently conflated.
