## What it argues

Kapania and colleagues study a population the human–AI interaction literature had mostly ignored: users for whom AI is aspirational rather than suspect. Thirty-two interviews and 459 survey respondents in India. Acceptance is near-universal — 79.2% of survey respondents indicate they accept AI decisions — and the authors name the pattern **AI authority**: AI holds a legitimised power to influence human action without requiring adequate evidence about the system's actual capabilities.

Authority manifests as four user attitudes, all of them attitudes of vulnerability: **faith, forgiveness, self-blame, and gratitude**. Each raises tolerance for system misfires; together they create room for irreversible harm at individual and societal scale. The authors call for calibrating AI authority, reconsidering success metrics and responsible-AI approaches, and offer methodological suggestions for research and deployment in India.

## Relation to the argument

Not a competence construct and not a rival, but a hard test of a premise the manuscript makes tacitly. Algorithmacy assumes the worker treats the intermediary as opaque and adaptive and works to infer its rule — the whole apparatus of asymmetric interpretation presupposes a subject who does not simply believe what the system says. Kapania and colleagues document a large population that does. Self-blame in particular is the exact opposite of the inferential stance: when the system misfires, the user concludes she was at fault, and no folk theorisation follows because nothing needs explaining.

That has two consequences the manuscript should absorb. First, it bounds the construct's scope: algorithmacy may be a competency that develops only where the worker holds the system accountable enough to model it, and a stance of faith or gratitude forecloses the whole facet structure. Second, it sharpens stratified fluency. If the variance in fluency is partly variance in *whether the system is treated as an object of inference at all*, then §10's distribution is not only a distribution of skill but of epistemic stance — and stance in this evidence tracks cultural and institutional context, not individual aptitude. A Lima audience working on Latin American platform labour will find that immediately relevant.

The counterpart is absent, as elsewhere. The relation runs user → AI system → decision about the user.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth — ACM blocks automated retrieval — so the 32 interviews, the 459 respondents and the 79.2% acceptance figure come from the published abstract; the sampling frame, recruitment and question wording behind that percentage are unverified, and 79.2% acceptance of "AI decisions" is a summary statistic whose item wording matters enormously. The domains studied are consumer AI, not employment adjudication. Four attitudes of vulnerability are an inductive typology, not a measure. The author list includes one name recorded in Crossref with "SP" as family name and "Azhagu Meena" as given name, which is how the proceedings print it; check the author's preferred form before citing. Do not generalise the findings past India, which the authors themselves foreground as the point.
