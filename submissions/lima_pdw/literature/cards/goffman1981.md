## What it argues

Five essays on talk as social organisation. "Replies and responses" attacks the adjacency-pair model of conversation as too thin, arguing that what makes a response fitting is a participant's read of the whole interactional frame. "Footing" (reprinted from *Semiotica* 1979) decomposes the speaker and hearer roles. "The lecture" and "Radio talk" examine institutional and broadcast speech — one-to-many arrangements where the audience cannot reply — and Goffman's treatment of radio announcers' error-corrections is a study of how a speaker manages a relation with an audience she cannot see or hear.

## Relation to the argument

"Radio talk" is the surprising item. Goffman analyses a communicator who must construct an audience she has no feedback from, cannot see, and cannot ask — and who must nonetheless calibrate her talk to it and repair when she misjudges. That is structurally the worker's problem in §7: producing for a reception she cannot observe, correcting from thin and delayed evidence. It is the closest thing in Goffman to **intent specification under an unmet counterpart**, and it predates every construct §9 sorts.

The book as a whole also supports the caution §9 needs. Goffman's argument throughout is that participation is organised into positions and that the positions are what the analysis must track; his own catalogue of positions has no machine in it, but the *method* — enumerate the positions, ask who occupies which, ask who is committed by what is said — is exactly the method §9 uses. Citing Goffman lets the paper present its structural move as a standard analytic procedure rather than a novel demand.

## Caution

Essays rather than a systematic theory, and the interactional-sociolinguistics tradition Goffman founded has since done the systematic work he did not. Radio talk of the 1970s is a broadcast arrangement, one-to-many and non-binding, and the analogy to a platform gate should be offered as illustrative rather than argued. Multiple imprints (Penn, Blackwell) with distinct ISBNs — cite Penn, 1981.
