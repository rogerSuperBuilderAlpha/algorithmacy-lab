## What it argues

McCroskey argues the discipline has been sloppy with the word and proposes a hard line: **competence is knowledge and the ability to perform; it is not the performance.** He insists the two be measured separately, because a person may know exactly what an effective message would be and be unable or unwilling to produce it — communication apprehension being his standing example. Conflating the two, he argues, produces pedagogy that trains behaviour without building understanding, and assessment that penalises the anxious rather than the incompetent.

He also warns against the drift by which "competence" becomes an evaluative label for people we like talking to, and argues the discipline should either keep the Chomskyan distinction honestly or stop using the word.

## Relation to the argument

Short, sharp, and directly quotable for §8's opening move. McCroskey supplies the disciplinary precedent for insisting the word choice is "forced rather than stylistic": the communication field had this exact argument in 1982 and settled it in the direction the paper takes.

His willingness/ability distinction is also a gap in §10. Algorithmacy's two properties — implicit acquisition and stratified fluency — treat the distribution of scores as a distribution of capacity. McCroskey's case is that low performance often reflects an unwillingness or an inhibition rather than a missing capacity, and on a platform there is an obvious analogue: a worker who has correctly inferred what the gate rewards and declines to supply it. The construct as defined would score her low. Naming that as an out-of-scope case is cheaper than being handed it by a reviewer.

## Caution

A brief pedagogical position paper, not an empirical study; it carries authority by consensus rather than by evidence. McCroskey's own programme was built around communication apprehension, which colours the framing. Use it for the competence/performance discipline claim and the willingness caveat, nothing more.
