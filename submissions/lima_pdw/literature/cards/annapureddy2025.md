## What it argues

Annapureddy, Fornaroli and Gatica-Perez propose a competency model for generative AI literacy: twelve competencies covering the skills and knowledge someone needs to interact with generative systems. The range runs from foundational AI literacy through prompt engineering and programming to ethical and legal considerations, and the authors order them as a logical progression — a roadmap, in their word, rather than a factor structure. The intended users are individuals, educators, policymakers and government officials, and the stated purpose is to seed assessments, curricula, guidelines and regulation.

Nothing here is validated. The twelve competencies are a conceptual framework built from the literature and from the authors' reading of what generative AI demands, published in an ACM digital-government venue with a policy audience in view. Its influence is nonetheless real — it is among the most-cited generative-AI literacy frameworks of its cohort — and it is the framework most likely to be reached for when someone asks what a worker in an AI-mediated setting needs to know.

## Relation to the argument

**Neighbour, and a clean illustration of §9's "object of knowledge" category.** The twelve competencies make the algorithm something to understand, use and use responsibly. Prompt engineering is the closest any of them comes to the manuscript's intent-specification facet, and the gap is instructive: prompt engineering is a technique for getting a *machine* to produce what you meant, where intent specification concerns getting a *person* to receive what you meant through a machine that reformulates it. One is human–machine; the other is human–machine–human. The manuscript's structural point holds exactly here — adding a thirteenth competency to this list does not produce algorithmacy, because the missing element is a position rather than a variable.

Two further discriminations, both from §8. These are explicitly teachable: the article's whole purpose is to seed educational programmes and professional training, which places the construct on the skill side of the manuscript's line and puts it in direct tension with algorithmacy's implicit-acquisition property. And they are tool-indexed — prompt engineering and programming skills date with the generation of models they were written for, which is the manuscript's own argument for why a competency a coordination form demands must survive its mediator changing.

## Caution

A conceptual framework, not an instrument: no items, no samples, no reliabilities, no factor analysis. Cite it for the existence and shape of a generative-AI competency inventory, never as a measure or as evidence about workers. Abstract-depth read — the twelve competencies are not individually named in the abstract, so **the manuscript must not list them without reading the article.** Dating is inconsistent across indexes: Crossref places it in volume 6, issue 1 of 2025 while Semantic Scholar records 2024 for the online-first version; cite 2025 with the volume and issue, and expect to be corrected either way. The venue is a digital-government policy journal, which carries little weight with an OS/OT audience — use it as an example of the field's output, not as authority.
