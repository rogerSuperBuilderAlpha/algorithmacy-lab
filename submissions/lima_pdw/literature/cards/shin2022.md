## What it argues

Shin, Rasul and Fotiadis treat algorithm literacy (AL) as an antecedent in a user-acceptance model rather than as a capacity to be measured for its own sake. Their setting is over-the-top streaming platforms and their question is what AL does to users' information processing about algorithmic recommendation. Method: multiple-group equivalence testing across two groups, comparing invariance and the differential effects of AL.

The findings are framed through dual-process theory. AL functions heuristically: users high in AL process recommendations differently, and that processing runs through trust to attitude and behaviour. Credibility of recommendations, and users' sense of how accurate and how personalised the results are, both depend strongly on the heuristic route. The authors' more interesting claim is that AL should not be understood only as an instrument for evaluating whether a service is trustworthy, but as something *performatively* bound up in how algorithmic personalisation gets modelled in the first place.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour, and the one that shows what happens when algorithm literacy gets absorbed into technology-acceptance research. The construct here does no work as a competency at all: it is a moderator in a trust-attitude-behaviour chain, and the outcome of interest is whether users accept an OTT platform's recommendations. Section 9's five-cell sort has no trouble with it — the algorithm is an object of knowledge, the user is the knower, and the second position is the streaming service.

Two specific uses. First, the paper is evidence that "algorithm literacy" travels well beyond the communication literature's own definitional debates and is already being used as an off-the-shelf independent variable with no agreed operationalisation, which strengthens the point Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum make about terminological drift (`oeldorfhirsch2025.md`). Second, the trust finding is a warning about what literacy is measured *for*: an instrument validated by its power to predict acceptance is optimised to detect a disposition toward the platform, not a capacity to coordinate through it. If the lab instrument's validation criterion is worker outcomes rather than platform acceptance, §11 should say why that difference matters.

The counterpart is absent, unsurprisingly. A viewer, a recommender, and a queue of films.
