## What it argues

Orlikowski and Scott argue that digitalization changes industries twice: once in the visible "waves" aimed at products, services and business models, and again in an unseen "digital undertow" that unseats the mechanisms an industry uses to coordinate and regulate itself. The abstract states the claim: digitalization is "producing less visible transformations in core institutional values, norms, and rules, which are indirectly, yet more profoundly, reconfiguring how organizations and industries perform" (p. 1), and these corollary effects are "displacing institutional apparatuses from their positions of primacy and authority within industries" (p. 1). The Introduction then defines institutional displacement as "processes of digitalization that are unwittingly undermining core institutional values, norms, and rules, which over time are reconfiguring how organizations and industries perform" (p. 2). The thing displaced is an institutional apparatus, which the authors gloss as "the established industry-wide structuring mechanisms (e.g., rules, standards, protocols, norms, values, and measures) that coordinate and regulate the performance of activities, flows, and relations across an industry or sector" (p. 7).

Two cases from the authors' earlier fieldwork carry the argument, and the paper reports no new data. In book publishing, the e-book left the ISBN standard without a physical object to identify. In hospitality, anonymous online reviews and ratings, "aggregated into rankings via the platforms’ proprietary algorithms" (p. 3), eroded inspection-based accreditation such as the Michelin Guides and the AA star system; the UK government withdrew its official star-rating scheme in 2011 in favour of consumer-led systems (p. 4). In neither case was the standard the target. The wave that transformed travel services "was not directed at or intended to change hospitality quality criteria" (p. 4), and displacement arises "not as a direct result of intentional, strategic initiatives of digitalization, but as the corollary effects of digital transformations that were aimed elsewhere" (p. 11).

The mechanism is a loss of fit, which the authors theorize with Barad's agential realism under the label strong sociomateriality. An apparatus is built around how its core phenomenon materializes — the bound paper book, the in-person inspection. Once that phenomenon is digitized, apparatus and phenomenon are "no longer in correspondence" (p. 8), the digitized phenomenon is constitutively excluded from the apparatus, and "tactical tensions" (p. 9) build. Figure 1, the "Process model of institutional displacement" (p. 10), draws the fork that follows: the industry revises the apparatus and reaffirms it, or revision fails, "existential tensions" (p. 10) persist, and the apparatus is "sidelined or replaced" (p. 10). The accreditation schemes "constitutively exclude online user reviews" (p. 9) and so "lose capacity to effectively coordinate and regulate hotel and restaurant establishments now dealing with a daily onslaught of online reviews and algorithmic rankings" (p. 9). A methods passage (pp. 11–12) recounts the genealogical procedure of the book study, and the authors extend the argument by conjecture to cryptocurrency and financial regulation (p. 13).

## Relation to the argument

The paper gives the algorithmacy argument an industry-level precedent in the target journal: digitalization displaces, as an unintended side effect, the established apparatuses that coordinate and evaluate a sector, and in the hospitality case a platform's algorithmic ranking takes the place of the accredited inspector. That hospitality case is the usable half. It shows the evaluating authority in an exchange between guest and hotel passing from an institutional scheme with published criteria to a platform whose rankings come from "proprietary algorithms" (p. 3). The paper stops there, at the level of the industry, and algorithmacy starts where it stops — with what a person on either side of that exchange must then know how to do.

**Claim (a)**, the intro sentence that "quieter, (sociomaterial?) displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are what actually reconfigure how organizations perform": supported in substance, overstated in two words. The triplet "values, norms, and rules" is the paper's own, in that order, in the abstract (p. 1) and in the definition of institutional displacement (p. 2), and "reconfiguring how organizations and industries perform" (p. 1) is nearly the draft's verb phrase. Three points need the author's decision. First, "what actually" claims exclusivity the paper does not: the authors write that the less visible changes operate "in addition to these direct and visible changes" (p. 1), call the direct displacements "critically important" (p. 2), and close by calling the changes beneath the surface "just as consequential" (p. 14); the strongest wording available is the abstract's "more profoundly" (p. 1). Second, "quieter" is the draft's word; the paper's are "less visible" (p. 1) and "indirect" (p. 2). Third, the parenthetical "(sociomaterial?)" should go: in this paper sociomateriality names the research approach, and the displacement is "institutional"; the phrase "sociomaterial displacement" does not occur. The paper also says "organizations and industries," and its cases and its definition of apparatus are industry-wide.

**Claim (b)**, the section 2 sentence that the paper "powerfully articulated how algorithmic systems quietly displace established institutional norms": not supported as worded. The agent throughout is "waves of digitalization" and "processes of digitalization," never algorithmic systems. One of the two cases, the e-book and the ISBN, involves no algorithm at all, and in the other the displacing force is user reviews and ratings together with algorithmic rankings. Algorithms otherwise appear twice in passing: in a sentence on ethics that mentions "opaque and distributed platforms with inscrutable, mutable, and executable algorithms" (p. 7), cited to Zuboff, and in a forward-looking list of "generative AI, inscrutable machine learning algorithms, cloud-based platforms, and distributed ledgers" (p. 13). The object is also narrower than the draft has it. What gets displaced is an institutional apparatus — the ISBN, an accreditation scheme — of which norms are one listed component (p. 7). A version the paper supports: Orlikowski and Scott articulate how digitalization unwittingly displaces the established institutional apparatuses that coordinate and regulate an industry. The venue claim ("recent work in *Organization Theory*") is correct.

## Caution

Do not cite this paper for anything at the level of the person. It has no worker, user or manager who needs a skill, a literacy or a sensibility; its units are industries, standards and accreditation schemes, and "coordinate" in this paper is what an apparatus does to a sector (p. 7), not what two people do with each other. It says nothing about a triad, a third party or a human counterpart. The words "triad," "third party," "intermediary," "counterpart," "literacy" and "sensibility" appear nowhere in the article. "Skills" occurs once, in a sentence about the "conspicuous displacement of labor, jobs, and skills" (p. 2) that the authors set aside as the direct kind of displacement they are not studying; "workers" occurs once, for the temporary office staff who handled book metadata (p. 11); and "mediate" occurs once, inside a Barad quotation that denies apparatuses are "machines that mediate" (p. 6). The hospitality case does contain a platform standing between travellers and establishments, but the authors never analyse it as a three-party relation, and reading it that way is the algorithmacy paper's own move, to be stated as such.

Do not cite it for adaptivity or for an intermediary that binds both parties. The single mention of "mutable" algorithms (p. 7) is a characterization borrowed from Zuboff in a paragraph about the ethics of exclusion, not part of the undertow mechanism. Do not cite it as evidence, either. The paper is a theory article that restates findings from the authors' earlier studies; the empirical record for the ISBN case is Scott and Orlikowski (2022, *Information Systems Research*), where the term "digital undertow" was introduced, and for TripAdvisor it is Orlikowski and Scott (2014) and Scott and Orlikowski (2012). Displacement here is also unwitting by definition, so the paper cannot support a claim that platforms set out to supplant institutional norms. One bibliographic trap: the reference list prints Barad (2007) as "Meeting the university halfway" (p. 14), a typo for *universe*; do not copy the entry from this paper.
