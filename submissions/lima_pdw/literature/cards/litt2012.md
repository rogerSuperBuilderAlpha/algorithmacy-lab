## What it argues

Litt names and theorizes the **imagined audience**: the mental conceptualization a person builds of who they are communicating with when the actual audience for an act of online disclosure cannot be directly observed. The article's contribution is conceptual rather than empirical at this stage of the line — it defines the construct, argues for its significance given how much social media communication happens without visible feedback about who is actually watching, and identifies a structural tension between the audience a poster imagines and the audience a post actually reaches. Litt draws on Giddens's structuration theory to theorize what shapes variation in imagined-audience composition — why one person pictures a narrow, known set of readers and another pictures a broad, diffuse public — and treats that imagined composition as the thing self-presentation is calibrated against.

## Relation to the argument

Imagined-audience research is the closest existing construct to the paper's individual-level focus, and it is worth carding for how precisely it stops short. Litt's audience is a set of *people* the poster cannot see and must guess at; the guessing runs entirely inside the poster's head, calibrated against social knowledge of who is likely to be reading, not against any system that itself reads, interprets or judges the post. There is no third party in Litt's account that intercepts the disclosure, forms its own verdict on it, and returns a binding consequence to both poster and audience — the audience simply does or does not see the post, and does or does not react.

That absence is exactly the gap the paper's construct occupies. The individual capacity the paper names is exercised toward an intermediary that is opaque, adaptive, and mutually binding — it interprets the act, and its interpretation settles something for both parties, not just for the person doing the imagining. Imagined-audience work supplies the closest available model of *anticipatory* self-presentation under uncertainty about an unseen party, but it should not be cited as already covering the mediated case; the paper's contribution is precisely to add the algorithmic intermediary that Litt's account has no place for.
