## What it argues

Thirty interviews, four focus groups with 22 users, and "rich pictures" drawn by participants to represent their understanding of Spotify's recommendations, all in Costa Rica. Two folk theories dominate. One **personifies** Spotify as a social being that recommends because it watches you; the other treats it as **a system full of resources**, a computational machine that yields an individualised musical experience if given the right kind of **training**. The authors argue the first draws on local conceptions of social relations and the second on a global imaginary of technology, and that which theory a person holds supplies different resources for exercising power and resistance.

## Relation to the argument

The training theory is a documented instance of intent specification in a consumer setting, and its contrast with the personification theory is analytically useful to §7. A user who thinks the system is a social being that watches her has an inbound problem only — she can be read, but there is nothing to specify to. A user who thinks it is a machine to be trained has an outbound practice available. Section 7 asserts that the two directions of common understanding are separate facets; Siles et al. show a population in which holding one theory rather than another *determines which direction is even available*, which is a mechanism for the dissociation §7 predicts.

The global-South framing also flags a scope condition §10 should absorb. If folk theories draw on local conceptions of social relations, then the competency's content is culturally indexed even if its structure is not, and a cohort study in one setting buys internal comparability at the cost the section already names.

## Caution

Consumer music recommendation, no stakes, no evaluative gate, no counterpart. The two theories are inductive categories from one country's sample; the global-South framing is the authors' contribution and carries commitments this paper need not adopt. "Training" is the users' metaphor, not a measured practice — nobody shows that training behaviour changed recommendations. Read at abstract depth.
