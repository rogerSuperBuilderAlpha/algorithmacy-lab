## What it argues

Sandberg attacks the **rationalistic** approach to competence that dominates human-resource practice: the picture in which competence consists of a set of attributes — knowledge, skills, abilities, personal traits — that a worker possesses and applies to a job, and which a competency model can enumerate independently of the work. His alternative is **interpretative**, and his study is phenomenographic: twenty engine optimizers at Volvo Car Corporation, doing what is on paper the same job, whose competence sorts into three qualitatively different **ways of conceiving the work**. Conception 1 treats optimizing as optimizing separate qualities; conception 2 as optimizing interacting qualities; conception 3 as optimizing from the customer's perspective. The reception reports ten optimizers in conception 2 and four in conception 3, with an independent coder agreeing on 90% of a ten-transcript check. Attributes do not distinguish these groups; the conception of the work does, and the attributes a worker develops and uses follow from it — "the particular way of conceiving of work delimits certain attributes as essential and organizes them into a distinctive structure of competence." Peers and superiors rated the conception-3 optimizers most competent, which is the finding that makes the hierarchy more than a taxonomy. His conclusion is that competence is constituted by the worker's way of conceiving her work, so a competency model built from a list of attributes describes the shadow rather than the thing.

## Relation to the argument

§8 already names Sandberg as one of two anchors, and reading him carefully turns up a contradiction that a reviewer will find. The section defines competency as "the combination of knowledge, skills, abilities, behaviours and attributes enabling effective performance in a role or context" — which is, sentence for sentence, the **rationalistic** definition Sandberg's article exists to refute — and then immediately cites Sandberg's conception-of-work account as the anchor for it. The two cannot both stand. The repair strengthens the paper rather than costing it anything, because Sandberg's actual position is the better fit: if competence *is* a way of conceiving the work, then algorithmacy is the worker's conception of what the system is doing and what a coordination through it requires, which is exactly what §8's sentence about asymmetric interpretation says, and it explains why the competency travels across mediators (the conception persists) and fails across forms (a different form is a different work to conceive). Drop the KSAO clause, define competency Sandberg's way, and the definition and the anchor line up. Sandberg also supplies the strongest methodological argument for §11's abductive, sensitizing-concept design: a prior coding frame built from attributes would find attributes.

## Caution

Crossref deposits no abstract for this article, so the card reconstructs it from the reception rather than from the text — verify the three conceptions and the sample details before citing them, and read the article before rewriting §8 around it. **An earlier version of this card gave the sample as fifty optimizers; two independent secondary accounts put the AMJ study at twenty, and I could not confirm fifty anywhere.** Use twenty, or drop the number until someone reads the article. Phenomenography is a specific and contested method: the findings are conceptions elicited through interview and analysed for qualitative variation, not measured constructs, and the sample is twenty engineers in one Swedish firm, selected by convenience. The interpretative position implies that competence cannot be captured by a standardized scale, which is in tension with the companion three-wave instrument §11 reports as built and approved; the paper should decide whether Sandberg is an anchor for the construct or an objection to its measurement, and it currently uses him as the first while leaving the second unaddressed.
