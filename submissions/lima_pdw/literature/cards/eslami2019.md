## What it argues

Two studies of Yelp's review-filtering algorithm — an analysis of 242 users' online discussions and an interview study with 15 Yelp users to whom the algorithm's existence was disclosed through a tool. Users question or defend the filter and its opacity depending on their engagement with it and on what they personally gain from it; the same opacity reads as protection against fake reviews to one user and as arbitrary suppression to another. The result that matters most here is what disclosure did. Once told the filter existed, users reported one of two intentions: **write for the algorithm in future reviews**, or leave the platform.

## Relation to the argument

This is the single most direct empirical warrant in the cluster for **intent specification** as a named practice, and the paper should lean on it. "Write for the algorithm" is the facet in the informants' own words: a reviewer who has something to say about a restaurant now composes it so that the filter will carry it, accepting whatever is lost in the compression. Two features make it more valuable than the influencer literature for §7's purposes. The compression is verbal rather than behavioural, so what is lost is legible; and the counterpart is real and specific — a reader the reviewer is trying to reach — which is the third position §9 says the neighbouring constructs drop. Most outbound-signalling evidence in this cluster is about visibility to an anonymous audience; here somebody is trying to say something to somebody through a gate.

The bifurcation is also §11's design in miniature. Disclosure produced either intent specification or exit, and nothing in between, which is the exit/voice structure §6 needs and a checkable prediction: workers who cannot or will not learn to write for the gate should leave rather than persist.

## Caution

Consumer reviewing, not paid work, and the intentions are self-reported immediately after a disclosure intervention rather than observed as behaviour — a well-known gap. Fifteen interviewees plus a discussion corpus from one platform. The abstract does not report whether users who intended to write for the algorithm actually did, or whether it worked, so the source establishes the practice's existence and not its efficacy. Read at abstract depth: this is a card that should be upgraded to full text before the claim carries weight in §7, because it is currently the best single citation for the paper's least-evidenced facet.
