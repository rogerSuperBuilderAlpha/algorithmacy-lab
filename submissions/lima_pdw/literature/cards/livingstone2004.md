## What it argues

Livingstone takes the four-part definition of media literacy — access, analyse, evaluate, create — and asks what survives the move from broadcast to networked media. Her answer is that the components hold but their relations change: access becomes continuous rather than episodic, creation becomes routine rather than exceptional, and evaluation becomes far harder because the institutional cues that signalled provenance in broadcast media are absent online. She also makes a political argument the AI-literacy literature has largely dropped: literacy framings **individualise a problem of institutional design**, and a policy that responds to bad media environments by improving citizens' skills has chosen the cheaper remedy.

## Relation to the argument

The second argument is the one that matters, and it is the strongest external statement of the worry §8 raises about itself. Livingstone's point — that raising a population's literacy without changing the institutions is a transfer of burden dressed as empowerment — is exactly §8's "a rise in average algorithmacy is not a good outcome on its own" and §6's division between routes. She made it about media policy in 2004, and having a citation from outside the paper's own reasoning makes the caution look like an established critique rather than a rhetorical hedge.

She also supplies the genealogy §9's "object of knowledge" row implies but does not state: AI literacy is the latest member of a literacy family (media, digital, information, data), and the family has a documented tendency to expand its component list without a derivation rule and to individualise structural problems. That is one sentence in §9 and it would land harder than the current treatment.

## Caution

A short position piece on media-literacy policy, mainly European and UK regulatory context, 2004. It predates platforms, algorithmic curation, and AI entirely. Cite for the individualisation critique and for the literacy-family genealogy, not for anything about AI.
