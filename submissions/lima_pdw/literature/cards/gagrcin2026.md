## What it argues

Gagrčin, Naab and Grub conducted a systematic integrative review of algorithm literacy across the social sciences and humanities, covering **169 studies** (PRISMA-documented: 91 from an initial Web of Science search across Communication, Political Science, Anthropology, Human Geography, Information Systems, Sociology and Educational Science, 1 January 2000 – 6 September 2023, plus 78 more from forward/backward citation searches). Of the initial 91, 86 are peer-reviewed journal articles and 5 are doctoral dissertations. They organize the field along three research questions: how algorithm literacy is conceptualized and measured, how users acquire it, and what outcomes they can hope to have. Their diagnosis is blunt: research on how individuals understand algorithms and their effects is expanding rapidly and **lacks a cohesive framework** — a multitude of overlapping constructs (algorithmic awareness, knowledge, skill, understanding) and measures that don't integrate with one another.

Their constructive proposal is that the existing work can be reframed as **experiential learning cycles**: people acquire algorithm literacy by encountering algorithmic outcomes, forming accounts of them, testing those accounts against further outcomes, and revising. The future agenda they set out has four items: define the core competencies relevant to algorithm literacy, standardize the measures, integrate the subjective and factual aspects (what people believe they understand versus what they can demonstrate), and develop task- and domain-specific approaches rather than general ones.

## Caution

A review, so it carries no primary evidence about users or workers directly — cite it for the state of the field. Its scope is *algorithmic media use* (recommendation, curation, news feeds, dating apps, e-commerce) rather than algorithmic management of work specifically, so it should not be read as establishing that no work-specific competence construct exists; the manuscript's own citation doesn't make that claim.
