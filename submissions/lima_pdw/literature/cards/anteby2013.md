## What it argues

Anteby argues that the discipline's discouragement of scholars writing about fields in which they are personally involved rests on an epistemological error: the assumption that distance and involvement are opposite ends of one continuum. Learning from a field requires *both*. Involvement — the set of mental activities that connect a scholar to a field — creates opportunities for theoretical insight rather than eroding rigour, and the taboo, having become too extreme, now suppresses insight the field could have had.

## Relation to the argument

The affirmative case, where Brannick and Coghlan give the defensive one. Anteby lets the paper say that the author's authorship of the programme is a source of the construct's content validity (which §11 of Paper 2 already claims for GauntleTT) without that claim collapsing into "I know because I was there." His distance/involvement decoupling is precisely what the protocol enacts: maximal involvement in the site, engineered distance in the analysis. Published in *Organization Science*, so it is the right currency.

## Caution

Anteby's cases are scholars writing about fields they belong to, not instructors studying enrolled students who depend on them. The power asymmetry that makes this design hard is not his subject, and citing him for it would over-read the paper. He relaxes the taboo on *telling*; the consent and coercion problem needs Ferguson et al. (2004) or Mercer (2007) instead.
