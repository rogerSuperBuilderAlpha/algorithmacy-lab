## What it argues

This is the conceptual companion to the scale, and it is where the four-dimension structure comes from. Dogruel reviews media literacy research (Baacke's four-part scheme, Pfaff-Rüdiger and colleagues' three dimensions), then digital and code literacy, new media literacy (Chen and colleagues' four dimensions, from functional consuming upward), and Trepte's and Masur's online privacy literacy, and derives algorithm literacy by analogy from all of them. The result is two cognitive dimensions — awareness and knowledge, and (critical) evaluation of algorithms — plus two behaviour-related dimensions: coping behaviours, and creation and design.

The chapter's most interesting move is a caveat inside the coping dimension. Dogruel writes that this dimension "is not about the competence of being able to use algorithms but about the ability to use algorithms competently," and that coping strategies are downstream of knowledge and evaluation: a user must first be aware of algorithmic decision-making, then evaluate its effects, then implement adequate coping behaviour. Three types of coping strategy get distinguished, running as far as refusing particular services altogether. The creation-and-design dimension — users developing or changing algorithms themselves, largely a programming competence — Dogruel concedes is the least developed of the four.

On measurement, the chapter is candid. Knowledge is assessible through factual questions, true/false queries in particular. Evaluation "may recur on users' self-reports using Likert scales." Sub-dimensions were developed from qualitative interviews with internet users, and Dogruel reports that older users in that sample were markedly less competent at coping.

## Relation to the argument

Cite this, not the scale, when the manuscript needs to show *how* the rival construct was built, because the chapter states the method openly: existing literacy concepts supply the dimensions, and interviews supply the sub-dimensions. That is inventory, not derivation, and Dogruel does not pretend otherwise. Section 6's discrimination — facets deduced from what the coordination form withholds versus facets collected from adjacent literacies and from what users report doing — has its cleanest textual warrant here.

The chapter also hands the manuscript a small but real concession. Dogruel's phrase "the ability to use algorithms competently" is a competency claim, not a skill claim, and her observation that coping ability differs sharply across users of equivalent standing anticipates §10's stratified-fluency property in a general-population setting. The manuscript should acknowledge that the competency/skill distinction is not wholly unanticipated in this literature, and then hold the line on the two remaining discriminations.

Neither survives contact with the human counterpart, which is the point. Dogruel's user faces "algorithmically-curated online environments": news provision, search, shopping, music and film platforms, targeted advertising. Every dimension is a relation between a person and a curation system operating on that person's own information stream. The word for what algorithmacy adds — a second party whose determination the system commits alongside yours — has no place to sit in this framework, and no amount of adding dimensions creates one.

## Caution

Conceptual chapter with illustrative interview material, not an empirical test; the German-language interview base is small and Dogruel presents the operationalisation as a proposal for follow-up work, which became the 2022 scale. Published in a German association's open-access book series rather than a journal, so it has not been through journal peer review — flag it as a book chapter when cited. The SSOAR PDF has ligature-encoding damage in places; I read around it, but any verbatim quotation beyond the two phrases used here should be re-checked against a clean copy.
