## What it argues

Three lectures built on one sentence: **we can know more than we can tell.** Polanyi's evidence is ordinary — we recognize a face among a thousand without being able to say how, and we ride a bicycle by obeying a rule of balance we cannot state and would not be helped by hearing. His analysis makes tacit knowing a *structure* rather than a residue. Knowing has a **proximal** term, the particulars we attend *from*, and a **distal** term, the whole we attend *to*, and we are aware of the particulars only subsidiarily, through their bearing on the whole. The blind man's stick is the standing example: attend to the stick and you feel pressure in the palm; attend from it and you feel the pavement. **Indwelling** names the relation — we dwell in the particulars, so making them explicit destroys the knowing they support. From this Polanyi derives a claim about the limits of formalization: an articulated system always relies on an unarticulated background that cannot in principle be exhausted, so no set of explicit rules is ever self-sufficient.

## Relation to the argument

The philosophical floor under §10, and the source that explains *why* a rule can be understood without being statable. But the card exists mainly to mark a distinction the paper must draw explicitly, because a reviewer will otherwise collapse the two. Polanyi's tacitness is a property of the **knower**: the rider of the bicycle possesses the knowledge and cannot articulate it. The opacity §8 rests on is a property of the **arrangement**: nobody outside the system possesses the rule, so the question of articulation does not arise. Algorithmacy sits at the intersection — the worker's working theory of the gate is tacit in Polanyi's sense, and it is a theory *of* something withheld in a sense Polanyi never treats. Making that two-source account explicit is the cleanest way for §10 to show it is not simply relabelling tacit knowledge. The bicycle example is also a problem for §8's word choice: Polanyi's paradigm cases of tacit knowing are *skills*, which undercuts the claim that skill is what instruction can supply.

## Caution

A short, non-technical lecture series in the philosophy of science, not an empirical or organizational work; every organizational use of "tacit knowledge" is a downstream construction, and the most influential of them (Nonaka) is widely held to have misread Polanyi by treating tacit knowledge as convertible into explicit knowledge, which Polanyi's structure denies. Cite Polanyi for the from/to structure and for the limits of formalization, never for a tacit-to-explicit conversion. Editions and paginations vary sharply between the Routledge and Doubleday printings and the 2009 University of Chicago reissue, so fix an edition before citing a page.
