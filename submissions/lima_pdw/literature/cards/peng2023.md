## What it argues

A controlled experiment in which **"recruited software developers were asked to implement an HTTP server in JavaScript as quickly as possible."** The result is a single headline number: **"The treatment group, with access to the AI pair programmer, completed the task 55.8% faster than the control group."** On heterogeneity the abstract says only this: **"Observed heterogenous effects show promise for AI pair programmers to help people transition into software development careers"** — a gesture at larger gains for the less experienced, consistent with the novice-levelling direction, but with no coefficient, no moderator test and no sample size in the abstract.

Three of the four authors were at GitHub or Microsoft at the time; the study evaluates their employer's product.

## Relation to the argument

Cite only as corroboration of direction, and only alongside the peer-reviewed studies. Its evidential weight in this cluster is small: it is a one-task benchmark, the heterogeneity claim is qualitative, and the paper never reached peer review despite three years of availability and heavy citation. Its real function here is cautionary — it is the single most-cited number in practitioner discourse about AI and worker productivity, and it rests on a working paper about writing one HTTP server. That contrast is itself worth a sentence if §1 ever needs to characterise the state of the evidence.

## Caution

Not peer reviewed; no version of record; author affiliation with the vendor is a live conflict. The 55.8% figure is task completion time on a single artificial task, not productivity in any organisational sense, and it has been laundered into general claims about developer productivity throughout the grey literature. Do not use it to support a heterogeneity claim in either direction — the abstract's one sentence on heterogeneity will not carry a warrant. Where a peer-reviewed developer study is needed, use Cui et al. (2026, *Management Science*) instead.
