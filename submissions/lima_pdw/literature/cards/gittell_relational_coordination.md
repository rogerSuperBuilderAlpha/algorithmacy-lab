## What it argues

Relational coordination is the construct the manuscript's Extant Constructs opening names as the counterpart-facing shelf. The 2002 *Management Science* abstract calls it "a communication- and relationship-intensive form of coordination" and shows that the performance effects of boundary spanners, team meetings, and — against organization design theory — routines all run through it: routines "work by enhancing interactions among participants," and all three mechanisms gain force under input uncertainty. The formal definition most of the literature quotes, "a mutually reinforcing process of interaction between communication and relationships carried out for the purpose of task integration," carries the page cite (Gittell, 2002, p. 301), which places it in her second 2002 paper, *Journal of Service Research*, 4(4), 299–311 (10.1177/1094670502004004007) — see Caution.

The construct's seven dimensions, stable from the airline studies through the current theory statements: three relationship dimensions (shared goals, shared knowledge, mutual respect) reinforcing and reinforced by four communication dimensions (frequent, timely, accurate, problem-solving). The validated Relational Coordination Survey (Gittell et al., 2000; Valentine, Nembhard & Edmondson, 2015) measures it as a network of ties between *work roles*: each role rates its communicating and relating with every other role in the work process. Gittell's own overview states the mechanism the manuscript leans on: relational coordination "is carried out via direct contact among workers at the front-line, through networks that cut across functional boundaries at the point of contact with the customer."

## Relation to the argument

Anchors the Extant Constructs opening sentence, and the anchor holds. The construct includes the counterpart by design — parties doing interdependent work rate their communication with each other, and the 2016 extension (relational coproduction, *Transforming Relationships for High Performance*, Stanford UP) folds clients and families directly into the ties. What it presupposes is exactly what the manuscript says it presupposes: identifiable counterpart roles a respondent can name, reach, and answer. The survey is unadministrable where the counterpart's judgement arrives anonymized through a platform — Rahman's TalentFinder freelancer cannot rate the timeliness of communication from a client whose feedback the platform withholds. Bolton, Logan & Gittell's systematic review of every empirical study 1991–2019 contains no platform, gig, or algorithmic-management application; and the one finding that touches systems standing in for contact runs the manuscript's way — in Gittell (2000), shared information systems that *replaced* direct contact were negatively associated with relational coordination.

## Caution

The in-text "(Gittell, 2002)" is ambiguous between two 2002 papers, and the reference list must pick: *Management Science* 48(11) is the standard organizational anchor and fits the manuscript's paraphrase ("communicating and relating among parties doing interdependent work"); the verbatim definition sentence sits at p. 301 of the *JSR* paper, a placement verified only through citing papers, not against the primary PDF. Gittell's own 2021 review compounds the tangle — it credits the definition to "(Gittell, 2002a: 300)" while its 2002a is the *Management Science* paper, whose pagination (1408–1426) contains no p. 300. Do not quote the definition with a page number without the *JSR* citation attached. One AI-era application exists and should not be mistaken for a breach: Hall, Gardner & Wright (2025, *Production and Operations Management*, 10.1177/10591478251407777) find AI *stimulating* relational coordination — but their AI is a shared advisory tool inside a three-person lab team whose members talk to each other directly, a delegate on the team's side, not an intermediary reading both parties.
