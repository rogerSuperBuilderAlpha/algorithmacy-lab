## What it argues

Cotter and Reisdorf argue that knowledge about algorithms is unevenly distributed along the familiar axes of digital inequality — education above all — and that the unevenness constitutes a new dimension of the knowledge gap, with consequences for who can act effectively in algorithmically mediated environments.

## Relation to the argument

The clearest existing statement of §10's stratified-fluency property, made about algorithmic knowledge rather than about a coordination competency, and made as a *demographic* claim. That difference is the point of citing it. Section 10 asserts that algorithmacy is unevenly distributed across **equivalently placed** workers, and holds the arrangement fixed to buy internal comparability. Cotter and Reisdorf show that the nearest measured construct stratifies by education and prior digital skill — which is the rival explanation §6 names and §11 must separate. The paper needs this source not as support but as the thing to be ruled out: if the observed variance in a cohort tracks education, the account has explained nothing the knowledge-gap literature had not already explained.
