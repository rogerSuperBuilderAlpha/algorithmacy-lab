## What it argues
Thibaut and Walker created procedural justice as a research object by asking a question adjudication scholarship had not put to psychology: independent of who wins, what makes a dispute-resolution procedure acceptable to the parties? Their experimental programme compared adversarial and inquisitorial procedures and found that people evaluated the *process* separately from the outcome, and that they preferred procedures giving them a voice — an opportunity to present evidence and argument — even when this did not improve their chances. The theoretical apparatus splits control into two kinds. *Process control* is the disputant's control over the development and presentation of evidence. *Decision control* is control over the verdict. Their central finding is that people will trade decision control away and still judge the procedure fair if they retain process control, which is the origin of what the field later called the fair-process effect or the voice effect. Their explanation was instrumental: process control is valued because, over repeated disputes, it improves the chance of a favourable outcome.

## Relation to the argument
This is where the paper's §5 distinction meets its most serious complication, and the paper has to face it because a reviewer from organisational behaviour will. Thibaut and Walker's finding is that voice matters even without decision control, which sounds like an argument that a channel without standing supplies something after all. Two things keep §5 intact and both need stating. First, what process control supplies on their account is *perceived* fairness, a state of the party, not answerability, a relation — the two-concept distinction Bovens draws is the right frame for reading their result. Second, process control is itself an allocation made by the procedure's designer: a disputant has it because the procedure grants it, not because she is skilled. Their whole experimental method varies the *procedure* and holds the person constant, which is the paper's structural claim expressed as a research design.

The split also gives §6 a more precise vocabulary than "forum." What §6 calls the institutional route supplies decision control and process control together; what an individual worker can bring is neither, and what a badly designed appeal supplies is the appearance of process control without its substance — which is a testable difference and the right way to grade the channels in the live interview case.

## Caution
Not read in the original; the account above is reconstructed from Colquitt's derivation and from published reviews, and no phrasing should be quoted. The programme is laboratory experiments on simulated legal disputes with student participants in the early 1970s, and its generalisation to workplaces came later and through other hands. Their instrumental explanation of why voice matters was subsequently displaced — Lind and Tyler's group-value model argues voice signals standing and respect rather than serving outcome control — so do not present the instrumental account as the field's settled view. Procedural justice in this book is about *dispute resolution procedures*, not about accountability, and the two literatures use "voice" for different things.
