## What it argues

Grover and Pea take stock of the six years of work that Wing's 2006 column set off and ask what the field now agrees on. Their answer is that the definitional fight is largely over. Abstraction has settled as computational thinking's keystone — "defining patterns, generalizing from specific instances," which they take from Wing's later 2011 statement — and nine elements are, in their words, "now widely accepted as comprising CT" and form the basis of curricula that teach and assess it: abstractions and pattern generalizations (including models and simulations); systematic processing of information; symbol systems and representations; algorithmic notions of flow of control; structured problem decomposition (modularizing); iterative, recursive, and parallel thinking; conditional logic; efficiency and performance constraints; and debugging and systematic error detection. They pair this with the College Board / NSF CS Principles "big ideas" and with the Royal Society-era policy movement in the UK, Israel, Russia, South Africa, New Zealand and Australia.

Having declared the definitions settled, they redirect the field to two practical problems. The first is environments and tools — Scratch, Alice, Kodu, Agentsheets, computational-craft kits, the "low floor, high ceiling" design principle, and their worry that CS Unplugged-style activities may keep learners "from the crucial computational experiences involved in CT's common practice." The second, which they treat as the binding constraint, is assessment: "Without attention to assessment, CT can have little hope of making its way successfully into any K–12 curriculum," and existing work relies mostly on student-created or predesigned artefacts, deconstruction, reverse engineering and debugging tasks. They also flag equity and identity questions — the gender gap, the rationale for requiring computational competencies of children with no CS ambitions — and the zero-sum problem of finding curricular room.

The review's own scope caveat is worth carrying: they restrict themselves to recent K–12 work with 21st-century tools, setting aside the LOGO- and BASIC-era literature they acknowledge is relevant.

## Relation to the argument

A neighbour, and the card that pins the computational-thinking family in place for §9.

**The triad, definitively absent.** Run the paper's decisive question over the nine elements: not one of them mentions another person. The relation throughout is a learner and a *problem* — decompose it, represent it, control its flow, debug it. Even the assessment work reaches for artefacts the learner produced, never for a counterpart's receipt of what the learner meant. Computational thinking is the purest case in the whole literacy family of a two-position construct where the second position is an inert object.

**Explicitly teachable, explicitly assessable — the opposite of implicit acquisition.** Grover and Pea's entire agenda is instructional: which environments teach it, which curricula carry it, how to assess whether it developed. §8's competency row acquires through participation because the rule is undisclosed; computational thinking's rules are the point of the curriculum. The contrast is not a criticism of the field, and the paper should not present it as one — it is the cleanest available demonstration that a construct built for instruction cannot be the construct a form withholds the instructions for.

**A warning the paper should heed.** Grover and Pea show a field that agreed on a nine-item list and then spent a decade discovering it could not measure it. §11 licenses a three-wave instrument. Their assessment section is the record of what happens when a construct's elements are enumerated before an assessment logic exists, and it strengthens rather than weakens the manuscript's derivation-first strategy: facets derived from what the form withholds carry their own criterion for what a valid item must ask about.

## Caution

A six-page review essay in an education journal, not an empirical study — no sample, no measure, no effect. Its claim that the definitions are settled is a claim about consensus in 2013, and the definitional argument in fact continued (Shute, Sun & Asbell-Clarke's 2017 review re-opened it). The nine elements come from the field's convergence as Grover and Pea read it, not from an original analysis of their own, so attribute them as "the elements Grover and Pea report as widely accepted" rather than as their framework. The review deliberately excludes the LOGO-era literature, which matters if the manuscript wants to say anything about how long the field has been at this.
