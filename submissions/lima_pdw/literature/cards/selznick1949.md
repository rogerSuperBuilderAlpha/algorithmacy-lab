## What it argues

Selznick's TVA case study introduces **cooptation**: "cooptation is the process of absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence" (p. 13, verified verbatim against the raw OCR). He splits the concept in two: **formal cooptation** publicly absorbs outside groups through "openly avowed and formally ordered relationships" — "Appointments to official posts are made, contracts are signed, new organizations are established — all signifying participation in the process of decision and administration" (p. 13) — sharing responsibility for power without sharing power itself. **Informal cooptation** is an unpublicized response to actual power centers, drawing organized interests into the real leadership without public acknowledgment. The TVA case shows the Authority's "grass-roots" doctrine routing its agricultural program through local land-grant colleges and the Farm Bureau, building an administrative constituency that constricted its later choices and displaced its broader conservation goals — the founding case of goal displacement through absorption.

## Caution

Selznick explicitly distinguishes formal from informal cooptation, and the manuscript's contrast (classical cooptation confers a seat; the platform confers none) draws specifically on the formal branch. That's a defensible choice — it's the branch that literally involves a seat, which is the exact axis of contrast the paper needs — but it simplifies away a distinction Selznick treats as load-bearing; the *informal* variant (unpublicized capitulation to actual power centers, no seat conferred) is arguably closer in spirit to platform enrollment than the formal one being contrasted against. This is a judgment call for the author, not an inaccuracy: worth a clause if a Selznick-literate reviewer is expected, not otherwise necessary.
