## What it argues

Large-scale adoption surveys of Danish workers linked to administrative labour-market records, in a difference-in-differences design around the launch of ChatGPT. Adoption is broad — most employers in exposed occupations ran chatbot initiatives and workers self-report time savings — and the labour-market consequence is nothing: **precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours** at both worker and workplace level within two years, with the confidence intervals ruling out effects larger than about 2%. The revision's positive finding is reorganisational rather than distributional: new task categories appear (content generation, AI oversight, integration work) and some workers move into better-paid AI-relevant roles, but not in numbers sufficient to move average earnings.

## Relation to the argument

The single best citation for the negative half of §1 — that outcomes do not track the volume of technology used. Humlum and Vestergaard have administrative earnings and hours, which no other study in this cluster has, and they find that self-reported productivity gains from a widely adopted tool do not show up in either. If measured tool use does not move earnings across an entire national labour market, then the earnings dispersion §1 opens on is not a function of tool volume, and the manuscript can say so with a source instead of an assertion.

What it does not do is supply the positive half. A precise null on the average is not a documented variance; the paper reports no dispersion result and no evidence of divergence among equivalently positioned workers. It closes off one explanation and leaves the residual as unexplained as §1 found it.

Note also that this is the AI-oversight-as-new-work finding that Bedard et al.'s grey-literature "brain fry" piece is groping toward, arriving through administrative data rather than a self-report survey.

## Caution

Working paper, not peer reviewed, and **retitled between versions** — check which version any secondary source is quoting before repeating a number from it. Denmark: a high-union-density, compressed-wage labour market where earnings dispersion is structurally suppressed, which is exactly the wrong setting from which to generalise about earnings dispersion. Employed workers with recorded hours, not platform workers — Danish administrative records do not capture the London driver. Chatbot adoption, not algorithmic management. The widely repeated "3% of time saved" and "25,000 workers / 7,000 workplaces / 11 occupations" figures come from the 2025 version and secondary summaries; verify against the March 2026 full text before either enters a draft.
