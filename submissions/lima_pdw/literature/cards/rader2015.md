## What it argues

Rader and Gray analyse open-ended survey responses to a single question — do you believe your News Feed shows you every post your friends create? — and find a wide spread of beliefs and causal inferences, with different behavioural consequences following from each. Their framing observation is the loop: user behaviour is both input to the algorithm and constrained by it, so patterns of belief have consequences for the system as a whole, not only for the individual.

## Relation to the argument

The companion to `eslami2016` at survey scale, and useful to §7 mainly for the *variance* it documents. Beliefs about the same system, held by people in the same position, ranged from accurate to absent. That is the distributional fact §10's stratified-fluency property asserts, observed in the least demanding setting imaginable — no income at stake, no evaluative gate, no counterpart. If the spread is that wide where the stakes are nil, §10's assumption that the spread reflects a competency rather than mere attention has to be argued, not assumed.

The input-and-constraint loop is the same point `bucher2017` makes affectively, stated in causal terms.

## Caution

A single survey question with open-ended coding; no behaviour observed, no accuracy criterion applied, one platform in 2014. The beliefs are elicited about existence and completeness of curation, not about what a gate rewards, so this is one step further from §7's inference problem than the Eslami studies. Read at abstract depth.
