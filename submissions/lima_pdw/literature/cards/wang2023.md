## What it argues

Wang, Rau and Yuan produced the first quantitative AI literacy scale and titled it around competence in a way none of the later ones do: measuring *user competence in using* artificial intelligence. Four core constructs organise it — **awareness, use, evaluation, ethics** — a scheme drawn largely from the digital literacy tradition rather than from AI education, which is why it differs from Ng et al.'s. They wrote 65 items, cut to 31 through three rounds of content validation, then collected two samples: the first to reduce items, the second to test reliability and validity. The final instrument is **12 items**, and the four-construct model held.

The nomological net is the part worth keeping. AI literacy correlated significantly with digital literacy, with attitude towards robots, and with daily usage of AI. The authors frame the payoff in two directions: helping researchers understand "how user competence in using AI technology affects human–AI interactions," and helping designers build applications "aligned with the AI literacy levels of the target users." Both directions terminate at the interface.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour. Its interest for the manuscript is the word "competence" in the title paired with a construct that is unambiguously skill.

No counterpart appears. Awareness is awareness of AI. Use is use of AI. Evaluation is evaluation of AI outputs. Ethics is ethical concern about AI. Twelve items, four objects, one object — the system. The stated purpose closes the point: the scale exists so designers can match interfaces to user levels, which presupposes a dyad of user and product. Nobody stands on the other side.

The paper should use this card for the *skill* discrimination specifically, because Wang et al. make the tool-dependence explicit in a way the later scales obscure. The strong correlation with digital literacy and with daily AI usage says the construct is largely a function of exposure to consumer technology. Exposure-driven capacity is trainable by instruction and dates with the tool; it is not held by an individual in a role, and it is not acquired through participation in a coordination arrangement. A scale whose best correlates are digital literacy and how often you use Siri is measuring familiarity, and familiarity is not competency in the paper's sense.

There is a second, subtler use. Wang et al. call the construct "user competence," and "user" is the position algorithmacy denies. A user faces a tool she has chosen and can put down. A worker under an opaque intermediary faces determinations she did not choose and cannot exit, made with reference to a counterparty she never meets. Naming the role differently — user versus coordinated party — is a compact way to stage §9's structural argument, and this title supplies the contrast in three words.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth. The abstract does not report sample sizes, sample composition (the authors are at Tsinghua; the samples are probably Chinese, but I could not verify that), the twelve item wordings, or reliability coefficients — all of which need the version of record before quotation. The 12-item count is corroborated by Carolus et al.'s independent description of the instrument, so it is safe; nothing else about the psychometrics is. Note the citation year: Crossref records online publication 10 May 2022 and the print issue as 42(9), July 2023, so both years appear in the literature. Cite as 2023 with the volume and page range, and expect to see it cited as 2022 elsewhere. Do not cite it for workplace AI competence; the framing is consumer users and interface design throughout.
