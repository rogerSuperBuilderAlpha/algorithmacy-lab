## What it argues
Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin take the silence construct into the field with interviews, asking employees directly what they have not said upward and why. Most respondents report withholding something, and the withheld topics cluster: problems with a supervisor, concerns about pay and fairness, and objections to organisational processes — the subjects on which upward information is most needed. The reasons cluster too. The dominant one is fear of being labelled or viewed negatively, especially as a troublemaker, and it is described as a fear about the *relationship* with the boss rather than about formal retaliation. Running alongside it is the belief that speaking would be futile because the person who would hear it lacks the will or the power to act. Respondents also report an inference from observation: they have watched what happened to others who spoke, and they generalise. The authors conclude that silence is not confined to poorly managed firms and is not chiefly about the courage of the individual — the same person speaks freely on some subjects and not others, sorted by what they expect the listener to do.

## Relation to the argument
This is the empirical companion to the conceptual silence papers and the one to cite when a reviewer asks whether the interview pattern in §11 is anything more than one participant's reticence. It is not: a substantial interview literature finds the pattern, finds it concentrated on fairness and process complaints, and finds it explained by expectations about reception rather than by disposition. Two findings bear directly on the paper. First, respondents distinguish having a channel from having a hearing, which is the distinction the paper needs to make about the Zoom session and the Discord. Second, the same employee speaks on some issues and not others, which shows the behaviour is a judgement about the forum rather than a trait of the person — and a judgement about the forum is not something individual competency alters.

The finding also constrains the paper's own protocol. If silence sorts by topic, §11's accountability question ("what does a participant do when a gate outcome strikes her as wrong") must be asked about specific incidents rather than in general, or it will collect a self-report about willingness instead of an account of what happened.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and citing literature; it is explicitly exploratory, with a modest interview sample and no claim to representativeness, so use it for the existence and shape of the pattern rather than for rates. The setting is employment with an identifiable supervisor, which is the structural feature co-optation removes — for a platform worker there is no boss to fear labelling from, so the fear mechanism may not transfer while the futility mechanism does. That asymmetry is useful to the paper and should be stated rather than glossed. Do not quote without pulling the original.
