## What it argues

Spitzberg's compact restatement of the model, written for an audience outside communication. Competence is an **impression** of appropriateness and effectiveness, attributed by participants and observers to an interaction; it is not a set of behaviours, because no behaviour is competent independent of context, and the search for a universal skill list is therefore misconceived. The model's generative components are motivation, knowledge, and skill, operating at the level of the actor, the co-actor, and the episode, and competence is a function of all three levels jointly.

The polemical target is the health professions' habit of importing communication-skills checklists — behaviours certified as competent in the abstract, taught as if context-free.

## Relation to the argument

The best single-source citation for the framework, and the most quotable statement of the attribution claim. For a paper being read by organisation theorists rather than communication scholars, this is also a model of the export the paper is attempting: taking the competence lineage into a field that has been treating the object as a skill list.

The polemic is directly usable in §8. Spitzberg's argument against context-free skill checklists in medicine is the argument §8 makes against calling algorithmacy a skill — "what can be taught is a tool, and the tool is not the competency" — and Spitzberg is the lineage's own authority for it. Currently §8 asserts the point; here it can cite it.

## Caution

Brief, framing-oriented, aimed at a health audience; it summarises rather than advances. Open-access and easily reached, which makes it convenient rather than authoritative. For the mature technical statement use the 2015 De Gruyter chapters; for the paper's warrant use Spitzberg & Cupach (1984), as it already does.
