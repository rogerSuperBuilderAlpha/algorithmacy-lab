## What it argues

The methodological reference for the **algorithm audit**: repeatedly querying an algorithm and observing its output in order to draw conclusions about its opaque inner workings and possible external impact. The monograph traces the method's descent from social-science audit studies of discrimination, summarises two decades of audits across health, politics and discrimination, sets out best practices, and closes on the social, ethical and political dimensions of auditing, proposing normative standards for its use.

## Relation to the argument

The professional benchmark against which §7's asymmetric interpretation should be measured, and the comparison is unflattering in a way the paper can use. An audit needs repeated controlled queries, sock puppets or scrapers, a comparison condition, and statistical machinery, and it still yields only a behavioural characterisation from outside. A worker has one account, no control condition, a handful of observations, and a livelihood riding on the inference. Section 7 says the inference is "checkable only by being tried again"; Metaxa et al. describe what checking properly requires, and the gap between the two is the measure of how hard the facet is. Stating that gap would strengthen §7's claim that the facet names real, unevenly distributed work rather than a universal cognitive reflex.
