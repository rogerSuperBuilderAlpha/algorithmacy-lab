## What it argues
Okhuysen and Bechky recast a fragmented literature by shifting attention from the catalogue of coordination mechanisms to what mechanisms accomplish. Their answer is three *integrating conditions*, arrived at by iterative comparison across the reviewed articles and then winnowed to three: accountability, predictability, common understanding. These are "the means by which people collectively accomplish their interdependent tasks in the workplace," and each addresses some demand that integrating specialised work imposes and resolves some uncertainty that interdependence creates. Five mechanism families — plans and rules, objects and representations, roles, routines, proximity — each produce one or more of the conditions, and Table 10.2 maps which mechanism yields which condition by what route.

The condition definitions matter, and they are more particular than their reputation. **Accountability** "addresses the question of who is responsible for specific elements of the task"; by creating it, "organization members make clear where the responsibilities of interdependent parties lie." The authors deliberately widen it past the traditional association with formal hierarchy: making responsibilities visible renders each party accountable for its own contribution *and* renders the others accountable for theirs, and this can be achieved "through informal and emergent action as well as by formal means" — lateral interaction in meetings, public status reports, display practices that post work-in-process, spreadsheets, ships' logs. Their own contrast is the hot-plate assembly line, where the technology channels accountability automatically, against semiconductor final assembly, where accountability "is accomplished through emergent dialogue, rather than a formal assembly structure." They also flag that accountability as an integrating condition rests on trust: where parties can count on consistent performance by others, establishing the scaffolding suffices. **Predictability** "enables interdependent parties to anticipate subsequent task related activity by knowing what the elements of the task are and when they happen" — a sense of what subtasks compose the larger task and in what sequence, quoting Simon on forming "accurate expectations as to what the others are going to do." **Common understanding** is a shared conception of the activities being performed and of how each party's part fits the whole, developed top-down through plans handed down a hierarchy or bottom-up through members building their own schedules and maps. A later section argues the three interact: sometimes additively, sometimes by substitution, so a strict separation is "somewhat artificial."

## Relation to the argument
The triad is the paper's inherited target, and §5 turns on it. Everything the paper says about what a coordination form owes its parties runs through this article, and every claim about what co-optation withholds is a claim about these three conditions. It also supplies the framing move the whole dissertation borrows: read a form by the conditions its mechanism produces rather than by the mechanism's surface, which is what lets hierarchy, market, network and co-optation sit on one template.

The card must also register where §5 departs from the source, because that departure is the paper's largest citation risk. §5 asserts that accountability is "a relation between parties" requiring "a forum with standing to demand justification," and attributes the triad to Okhuysen and Bechky in the same breath. The forum definition is Bovens's, not theirs. Their accountability is responsibility-allocation: who is answerable for which piece of the task, made visible so interdependent parties can see where the work sits. The paper is entitled to import Bovens's structure — the companion review already does, and §5 says so — but it must not let a reader infer that Okhuysen and Bechky defined accountability as a forum relation. The gap is not cosmetic. Their accountability is explicitly achievable "through informal and emergent action," including by the coordinated parties themselves, which is exactly what §5 says accountability cannot be. A reviewer who reads the source will find, on the same page, an example of assemblers *constructing* accountability through dialogue, and will ask why a platform worker cannot do the same. The answer available to the paper is that emergent accountability in their cases runs among parties who can see and reach each other and who hold reciprocal standing, and that co-optation removes precisely those preconditions — but the paper has to make that argument rather than assume the definition settles it. Their point that the conditions can *substitute* for one another is a second live thread: if predictability and common understanding can partly stand in for accountability, §6's clean division needs a sentence on why the substitution does not run in this case.

## Caution
The triad and the "integrating conditions" label are the authors' own, and this is verified against the text. The p. 463 pinpoint carries the definition of coordination, not the conditions; the conditions are defined around pp. 483–490, and a pinpoint attached to them should not read 463. Do not present the three conditions as a typology of coordination *forms* — they are outcomes that mechanisms produce, and the authors synthesised coordination research generally rather than the hierarchy/market/network triad, so laying the conditions across the four columns is the dissertation's move and is correctly flagged as such. Above all, do not attribute the answerability or forum framing of accountability to this source.
