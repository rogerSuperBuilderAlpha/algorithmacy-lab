## What it argues

A grounded theory of how online labour platforms organise work, from 36 observed Uber rides, 34 driver interviews, 8 interviews with Uber managers and engineers, and 1,092 coded forum posts across 2015–2019. Algorithmic management splits into **matching** (algorithmically mediated coordination of demand and supply, a market view) and **control** (algorithms monitoring behaviour and aligning it with the platform's goals, an organisational view). The control side runs through the algorithm as boss, process data, and behavioural nudging, and it operates without an employment relation — an Uber executive states it plainly: because drivers are not employees, "we can't tell them what to do... The only thing we can do is use the algorithms to incentivize drivers." Three tensions follow, in work execution, compensation and belonging, with the algorithm's logic described as a black box; responses split into market-like (bypassing, switching platforms mid-shift) and organisation-like (striking, unionising, embracing).

## Relation to the argument

Distinct from `mohlmann2023`, and the two should not be conflated in the bibliography — same first author, same platform, different construct. This one supplies the *structure* the sensemaking paper later studies workers making sense of, and its value here is the executive quotation. "We can't tell them what to do... the only thing we can do is use the algorithms" is co-optation's mechanism stated by the party that built it, and it is the cleanest available evidence for why the coordination form withholds what §5 says it withholds. The withholding is not incidental to the business model; commanding would make the platform an employer.

## Caution

One extreme case chosen for high scores on both dimensions, two metropolitan markets, a pre-regulation window; the authors flag social-desirability and recall bias and note they have no data on how prevalent any response is. The paper frames matching-plus-control as a two-dimensional plane, not a single form.
