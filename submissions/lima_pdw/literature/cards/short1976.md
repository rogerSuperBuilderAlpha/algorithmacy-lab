## What it argues

The origin of **social presence**: the degree to which a medium conveys the salience of the other person in the interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal relationship. Short, Williams and Christie treat it as a subjective quality of the medium, measured by semantic-differential ratings, and rank media on it — face-to-face highest, audio lower, text lower still. Their applied claim, made for the British Post Office, is that media low in social presence are unsuited to tasks involving conflict, negotiation, or getting to know someone, and suited to information exchange.

## Relation to the argument

The concept the paper's arrangement negates, and naming the negation is worth a sentence. Social presence is the salience of the *other person*. In the arrangement §7 describes, the counterpart's salience is not merely low, as it is in a lean channel: the counterpart is **absent from the interaction entirely**, and what is salient instead is the system. That is not a point on the social-presence continuum; it is a different variable, and saying so is a compact way of making §9's structural claim in a vocabulary the CMC literature owns.

There is also a measurement suggestion. If social presence is measurable by semantic differential, then so is whatever occupies its place here — the salience of the *system* relative to the salience of the counterpart. A worker for whom the system is wholly salient and the counterpart wholly absent is in a different phenomenological position from one who keeps the rider or the reviewer in view, and §11 could measure the difference. That would be an original contribution rather than a borrowed one.

## Caution

1976, telephone-and-videoconference era, semantic-differential methodology, and the medium-determinist ranking Walther (1992) demolished. Social presence has been redefined repeatedly since (Biocca, Harms & Burgoon 2003; Lee 2004) and the original operationalisation is not usable. Cite for the concept's origin and for the negation argument.
