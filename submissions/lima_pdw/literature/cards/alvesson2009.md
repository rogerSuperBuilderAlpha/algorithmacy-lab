## What it argues

The organizational-ethnography statement of the same argument, and the place where "at-home ethnography" gets its name: research in a setting to which the researcher already belongs, using the membership itself as the instrument. Alvesson treats the everyday material a member has anyway — meetings attended, decisions witnessed, one's own reactions — as data of a kind an outsider cannot generate, on condition that the researcher works continuously against the closure that membership produces.

## Relation to the argument

This is the label the paper should claim if it claims one. The design is not autoethnography (the author is not the subject) and not classical insider action research (he is not intervening to change the programme); at-home ethnography is the closest fit, and it comes with an OS-legible citation and an edited volume behind it. It also legitimates the repository records and the rule-change ledger as the ordinary organizational traces a member has access to.

## Caution

Book chapter, so it carries less weight than a journal article with a sceptical reviewer, and the 2003 article covers most of the same ground. Note also that at-home ethnography assumes membership, not authorship — the author *built* the site, which is a stronger position than Alvesson describes, and the paper should say so rather than let the label smooth it over.
