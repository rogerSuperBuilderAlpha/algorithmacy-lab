## What it argues

Gherardi's editorial introduction to the *Organization* special issue that consolidated practice-based studies of learning. Her move is to replace the noun *knowledge* with the verb **knowing** and to make practice, not the individual or the organization, the unit of analysis. Learning is not the acquisition of a possession but participation in a situated practice, and knowing is an activity carried in and by that practice — distributed across people, artifacts, texts, and bodies rather than held in heads. She sets practice-based theorizing against both the cognitive tradition and the knowledge-management tradition, which she reads as sharing a mistaken picture of knowledge as a transferable object, and she foregrounds the aesthetic, sensory, and embodied dimensions of knowing that neither reaches. Her later book-length statement, *Organizational Knowledge: The Texture of Workplace Learning* (Blackwell, 2006; ISBN 9781405125598, OpenLibrary records the edition as 2005), develops the same programme through empirical studies of building-site safety and of practice as a texture rather than a bounded unit.

## Relation to the argument

Gherardi's shift from knowledge to knowing is what allows §8's table row to hold together. The row claims a competency is acquired through participation and dates with the form, which only makes sense if the thing acquired is a way of doing rather than a stock held — and Gherardi's programme is the cleanest statement of that ontology in organization studies. Her insistence that knowing is distributed across artifacts is also the bridge to the interview wrinkle: if knowing lives in a texture that includes tools and texts, a participant who works out the gate through an AI assistant is not doing something outside the practice but is enrolling a new artifact into it, and the analytic question becomes what changes when the artifact is a language model rather than a manual. Her critique of knowledge management doubles as support for §8's "not a capability" argument, since a possession-based reading of what a worker knows is the target she names.

## Caution

An editorial introduction, short and programmatic, framing a special issue rather than presenting evidence or a full theory; the substantive development is in Gherardi's books. The practice-based literature it introduces is internally divided, and Gherardi's aesthetic and sensory emphasis is a minority position within it — do not cite her as a spokesperson for practice theory generally.
