## What it argues
Cropanzano, Byrne, Bobocel and Rupp survey the theoretical accounts of *why* people care about fairness and sort them into families rather than adjudicating among them. The families answer different questions and are not rivals. Instrumental models hold that fairness is valued because it predicts favourable outcomes over time. Relational or group-value models hold that fair treatment signals standing and inclusion in a valued group, and that this is why voice matters even without outcome control. Moral or deontic models hold that people react to injustice because a moral principle has been violated, including injustice done to others from which they gain nothing. Fairness-heuristic theory holds that people form an early fairness judgement under uncertainty about whether an authority can be trusted, and then use it as a heuristic for interpreting later events. The article's other organising contribution is the *social entity* question: justice judgements are directed at particular targets — the supervisor, the organisation, a work group — and the same event can be fair with respect to one entity and unfair with respect to another, which the authors argue the field must model rather than average away.

## Relation to the argument
The social-entity strand is the closest organisational justice comes to §5's state/relation distinction, and it is where a reviewer will point. Multifoci justice says fairness judgements have a *target*, and the target has to be a party capable of being fair or unfair — which is the same requirement §5 places on accountability. The paper can use this constructively and should: the co-optation case is one in which the natural entity is missing. A worker asked whether her supervisor treated her fairly has no supervisor; asked whether the organisation did, she has no organisation she is a member of. The multifoci framework asks a question the arrangement cannot answer, and that failure is diagnostic rather than a measurement problem.

The relational family also gives the paper the strongest version of the counterargument it must beat. If fair treatment matters because it signals standing, and standing is what co-optation withholds, then the relational account predicts exactly what §5 claims — that no procedural courtesy substitutes, because the signal is about the relation and not about the process. And fairness-heuristic theory names the mechanism behind the live interview case: an early judgement formed under uncertainty about whether the authority can be trusted, then used to interpret everything after, which is what "that's the way this is" reports.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth; the four families and the social-entity argument are the standard rendering, and nothing should be quoted or attributed to specific pages without pulling the article. It is a theory review, not evidence — it maps positions rather than testing them, and the families have fared differently since. The multifoci literature it launched is large and post-dates it; later work (Rupp and colleagues) is the better cite for the developed version. Its subject remains perceived justice inside employment, and none of the theories are about accountability as a mechanism.
