## What it argues

Flanagan names and codifies a method built out of ten years of work in the Aviation Psychology Program of the USAAF (pilot-elimination-board studies, critical-requirements-for-combat-leadership studies) and its postwar extension at the American Institute for Research. The critical incident technique is "a set of procedures for collecting direct observations of human behavior" that facilitate inferences about what makes an activity effective or ineffective. An **incident** is "any observable human activity that is sufficiently complete in itself to permit inferences and predictions to be made about the person performing the act"; to be **critical**, the incident's purpose must be clear to the observer and its consequences definite enough to leave little doubt about their effect. The technique rests on two basic principles: (a) reporting facts about behavior is preferable to collecting "interpretations, ratings, and opinions based on general impressions," and (b) reporting should be limited to behaviors that, per competent observers, make a significant contribution to the activity. Flanagan's own opening illustration is diagnostic of this stance: pilot-elimination boards that reported causes as clichés ("lack of inherent flying ability," "poor judgment") were less useful than boards that reported specific observed acts.

Flanagan lays out **five steps** for the procedure as a research design, not as a structure for any single incident narrative: (1) determine the general aim of the activity; (2) develop plans/specifications for collecting factual incidents, with instructions to observers as specific as possible; (3) collect the data, by interview, group interview, or questionnaire — individual interviews should avoid leading questions, let the observer talk, and use restatement of the observer's own remarks to draw out further detail rather than redirecting; (4) analyze the data to build a functional description of the activity; (5) interpret and report the requirements, with explicit statement of the biases and limitations of the prior four steps. He reports the technique's use across nine application domains (typical/proficiency performance measures, training, selection, job design, operating procedures, equipment design, motivation/leadership, counseling/psychotherapy) and stresses that critical incidents "represent only raw data" — the technique does not itself supply solutions.

## Relation to the argument

Cited once (PAPER.md line 579) as the warrant for the harness's per-module elicitation design: prompting for "a complete behavioral episode," redirecting participants who answer with a high-level generalization back toward a specific incident, and omitting theoretical terminology so the participant reports in native categories. All three map onto Flanagan's own stated principles (specific observed acts over general impressions/interpretations; the incident-completeness criterion). The manuscript's four-part per-incident structure (baseline objective, system determination, tactical adaptation, resolution) is correctly marked "operationalized here as" — it is the study's own scaffold for eliciting one incident's narrative arc, not a structure Flanagan proposes; his own four/five-part structure is a *research-procedure* sequence (aim → plan → collect → analyze → interpret), not an incident-narrative template. Keeping the "operationalized here" framing is the correct move and should not be reverted toward implying the four parts come from Flanagan.

## Caution

Flanagan's technique is agnostic about interview versus questionnaire administration and about how to handle a "negative" initial response — his interview guidance (avoid leading questions, restate the observer's remarks to prompt elaboration) is the closest analogue to the manuscript's "reframes from an alternative angle," but that specific move is not verbatim in Flanagan and should stay attributed to the harness's own protocol design, not folded into the Flanagan citation as if it were his prescription. The article is a 1954 procedural monograph out of aviation/industrial psychology, not a qualitative-methods paper in the contemporary sense — it predates and is methodologically distinct from grounded theory/abductive-analysis literature (Timmermans & Tavory) cited elsewhere in the same section; the two are compatible (CIT structures elicitation, abductive analysis structures coding) but should not be blended rhetorically.
