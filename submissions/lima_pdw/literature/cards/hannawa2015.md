## What it argues

The field's stocktaking volume: twenty-two chapters covering the theoretical models, the domains (interpersonal, intercultural, health, organisational, instructional, computer-mediated), and the assessment problem. The editors' opening chapter (doi:10.1515/9783110317459-002) states the organising claim — competence is a judgement of appropriateness and effectiveness, made in context, generated by motivation, knowledge, and skill — and concedes that after four decades the field has neither a common measure nor an agreed componential structure.

## Relation to the argument

**The most important thing this volume establishes for the paper is negative.** It is the discipline's own map of where competence had been theorised as of 2015, chapter by chapter, and there is no chapter on competence in a three-party arrangement with a non-human adjudicator. The domain chapters partition by *setting* — health, organisation, classroom, screen — and every one of them retains a human counterpart whose judgement constitutes the competence. That is direct evidence for §9's structural claim and better evidence than the five-construct sort currently offered, because it is a comprehensive survey rather than a selection.

Chapter 7 (Bunz & Montez, computer-mediated communication competence) is the volume's answer to the mediated case, and it treats the medium as a channel exactly as §9 says.

## Caution

An edited handbook: the coverage claim above rests on the table of contents and four chapters read in full, not on all twenty-two. Do not assert "no one has theorised X" on the strength of a handbook's silence — assert that the field's own stocktaking volume partitions by setting and retains the human counterpart throughout, which is what can be shown. Hannawa is first editor.
