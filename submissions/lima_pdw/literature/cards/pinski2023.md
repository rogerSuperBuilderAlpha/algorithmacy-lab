## What it argues

Pinski and Benlian build the information-systems answer to AI literacy, and unlike the education-derived scales they route it through IS competence theory — Bassellier et al. on business-IT competence, Schuetz and Venkatesh's revised IS assumptions for an AI world. Their construct is deliberately **socio-technical**: items must cover competence in AI technology *and* competence in human factors. They also split explicit from tacit literacy, treating experience as tacit.

Method runs in four steps: systematic literature review, five expert interviews, two rounds of card sorting with six then five Prolific judges, and a pre-test with 50 Prolific participants pre-screened for daily technology use and programming skill. Seven dimensions entered card sorting — AI technology knowledge, Human actors in AI knowledge, AI input, AI processing, AI output, AI usage experience, AI design experience — grouped into three categories: AI actor knowledge and AI steps knowledge (both explicit literacy) and AI experience (tacit). Round one produced a hit-ratio of .52 and κ = .27; after dropping and rewording, round two reached .86 with κ = .74, range .70–1.00 by dimension.

The pre-test broke the seven-dimension model: the three step dimensions suffered multicollinearity (VIF > 5.00), so input, processing and output collapsed into a single AI steps knowledge dimension with one item retained from each. The adjusted model has **five dimensions and 13 items**, R² = .79, all Cronbach's alphas above .84, composite reliabilities above .91, all loadings above .70 at p < .001, Fornell-Larcker satisfied throughout. Only AI technology knowledge carried a strong significant path to the focal construct (β = .34); the authors retained the other four anyway on content grounds.

## Relation to the argument

**This is the most dangerous card in the sub-cluster, and it still fails — but the manuscript must handle it explicitly rather than sweeping it into a general claim, because a reviewer who reads it superficially will think it has a human position in it.**

Pinski and Benlian have a whole dimension called **Human actors in AI knowledge**, defined as "Knowledge of the role of human actors in human-AI collaboration and interaction." Its items, verbatim from Appendix A ("I have knowledge…"):

- HK1 "…of which human actors beyond programmers are involved to enable human-AI collaboration"
- HK2 "…of the aspects human actors handle worse than AI"
- HK3 "…of the aspects human actors handle better than AI"
- HK4 "…of the human actors involved to set up and manage human-AI collaborations"
- HK5 "…of the tasks that human actors can assume in human-AI collaboration"

HK1, HK2 and HK3 are the three retained in the 13-item model. Read them carefully and the near-miss resolves. Every human actor named is a **builder, operator, or generic member of the species**: who enables the collaboration, who sets it up and manages it, what tasks humans can take, what humans do better or worse than machines. Not one item concerns a *specific other party to whom the respondent is bound by a determination she did not make*. The dimension is a knowledge dimension — "I have knowledge of…" — about the division of labour between humans-in-general and AI-in-general. The counterpart in algorithmacy is not a category of actor to be known about; she is a position in a live relation whose interests are jointly settled by a third thing. HK gives you an ecology, not a triad.

The overall construct item confirms it: AIL1 reads "In general, I know the unique facets of AI and humans and their potential roles in human-AI collaboration." *Human-AI collaboration* is the whole relation, and it has two terms.

Two further discriminations run through this card cleanly. On **derivation**, Pinski and Benlian derive from IS competence theory rather than collecting from practice, which is the most respectable warrant in the cluster — but they derive from a theory of *competence*, not from an account of what a coordination form withholds. The difference is the paper's, and it is worth stating: deriving items from a prior competence literature reproduces that literature's assumption that competence is a property of a knower facing a technology. On **capability versus competency**, the authors sell the instrument to firms: it lets companies "analyze and define AI literacy requirements of different roles (product manager, top manager, developer, etc.)" and "identify AI literacy deficits and set up targeted training programs." Targeted training programmes are the definition of skill by instruction, and the roles named are all roles that own something.

The authors also do the manuscript a favour by saying the quiet part: their instrument works "without focusing on a specific instance of AI or human role." Generality is their selling point. Algorithmacy's claim is that competency dates with the *form*, which requires precisely the specificity Pinski and Benlian have engineered out.

## Caution

A pre-test with 50 participants, pre-screened for programming skill, on Prolific — the authors concede the sample sits "at the lower recommended end" and pre-selects English-speaking people with panel access. No cross-validation. Only one of five dimensions showed a significant path coefficient to the focal construct, and the other four were retained on judgement rather than evidence, which means the five-dimension structure is asserted more than demonstrated. Crossref carries no page range for HICSS proceedings; I took pp. 165–174 from the running page numbers in the ScholarSpace PDF itself (handle 10125/102649), which is reliable but did not come from the Crossref record. The item wordings quoted above are verbatim from Appendix A of the ScholarSpace PDF and are safe to quote; the Δ markers there identify the 13 retained items, and I have reported them correctly. Pinski and Benlian have since published organisational-level work (Pinski, Hofmann & Benlian, 2024, *Electronic Markets* 34(1), doi:10.1007/s12525-024-00707-1; Benlian & Pinski, 2025, *Business Horizons*, doi:10.1016/j.bushor.2025.10.001) that moves the construct up to the firm — relevant to the capability discrimination and worth a separate look.
