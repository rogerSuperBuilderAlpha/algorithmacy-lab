## What it argues
Myers West surveys several hundred users whose content or accounts platforms had removed, and asks what they made of it. The finding is that people build *folk theories* of moderation — lay explanations of why the system acted, assembled from the fragment of information the platform provided, from what happened to others, and from prior beliefs about the company. The theories cluster into recognisable types: the platform is enforcing a rule she inadvertently broke; a group of other users organised a mass report; an automated system misclassified; the platform is politically biased against people like her. She shows that platforms' notices are too thin to adjudicate between these, so the theories persist and shape subsequent behaviour — users self-censor in ways the actual rules do not require, or conclude that appeals are futile and stop trying. Her argument about appeals is the pointed one: formal appeal processes exist on the major platforms and users largely find them opaque and unresponsive, receiving templated replies that restate policy without addressing the case, which reinforces rather than corrects the folk theory.

## Relation to the argument
This is the paper's evidence that folk-theorising and forbearance are the *same population's* response to the same arrangement, which §5 needs and does not currently have. Rahman documents the folk theories; Vaccaro documents the failed appeals experimentally; Myers West documents both in one study and shows the causal link between them — the appeal returns a templated non-answer, and the non-answer becomes evidence for the theory. That sequence is precisely what §5 claims: the worker's interpretive capacity keeps operating and produces a private account, while the relational condition stays exactly where it was.

For the live interview case it supplies a mechanism that fits better than either fear or ignorance. Her respondents knew the appeal existed and had often used it; what they had learned was what it returns. A participant who judged the Zoom and the Discord inappropriate had made the same judgement, either from experience or from what she had seen happen to others — and Myers West shows that vicarious learning is a major source of these theories.


**Added for the opacity-folk-theories cluster.** This is the only source in the folk-theory cluster that documents theorising **and** redress-seeking in the same population, which makes it the natural test bed for §5's asymmetry claim. Section 5 says a worker can get better at predicting how an appeal will be received but cannot cause it to be received, and that accountability admits no private supply. Myers West's respondents took steps to seek redress; whether those steps produced answers is precisely what §5 stakes the paper's hinge on, so this card should be upgraded to full text to see what the redress-seeking achieved — a finding that skilled redress-seeking worked would be the disconfirming case §5 invites. Note also that folk theories of *moderation* are a different object from folk theories of *ranking*: moderation issues a determination about a specific act, which is much closer to §7's "opaque, adaptive intermediary that commits determinations binding on both parties" than a feed ranker is.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth; do not quote or report sample figures without pulling the article. It is a survey of users who self-selected into reporting a moderation experience, so the population is skewed toward the aggrieved. The domain is social-media content moderation, where the stake is expression and account access rather than income, and the user is not in an economic relationship with the platform — a substantial difference from platform labour. Folk theories here are theories about *moderation policy*, which is a published rule set, whereas the paper's workers theorise an undisclosed evaluation rule; the two are not the same epistemic problem.
