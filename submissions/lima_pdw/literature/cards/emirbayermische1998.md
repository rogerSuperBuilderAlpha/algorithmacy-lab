## What it argues

Emirbayer and Mische open by saying the word agency has an elusive, resonant vagueness in social theory, and they set out to fix it by treating agency as a process in time rather than a property of persons. Their definition, as citing literature reproduces it, calls agency a temporally embedded process of social engagement, informed by the past in its habitual aspect, oriented toward the future as a capacity to imagine alternatives, and oriented toward the present as a capacity to contextualise past habits and future projects within the contingencies of the moment. Secondary sources place a fuller version at p. 970: human agency is the temporally constructed engagement by actors of different structural environments — the temporal-relational contexts of action — which, through the interplay of habit, imagination and judgment, both reproduces and transforms those structures in interactive response to the problems posed by changing historical situations. I have not checked that wording or that page against the article.

The three orientations are named the iterational, the projective and the practical-evaluative elements, and the authors call the whole the chordal triad of agency. The iterational element is the selective reactivation of past patterns of thought and action, which gives stability to social settings. The projective element is the imaginative generation of possible future trajectories, in which received structures may be reconfigured in relation to hopes and fears. The practical-evaluative element is the capacity to make practical and normative judgments among alternative possible actions in response to emerging demands and ambiguities. The authors insist these are analytical distinctions: all three are present in varying degrees within any concrete instance of action, and the degree to which each contributes varies with the actor's situation. One citing reading objects that the article nonetheless ties each temporal orientation to a type of agency in practice. The article's sources, as citing literature lists them, are the pragmatists (Mead's philosophy of the act is in the reference list) and Schutz; the authors describe their own method as a relational pragmatics.

## Relation to the argument

Arm: `orgsci_agency_triad`. The arm's P2 says a co-opted person's agency has two parts that vary independently, competence and standing, and its conclusion says organization theory should locate agency in the triad as the joint product of the two. Emirbayer and Mische supply the vocabulary that makes competence a part of agency rather than a substitute for it. Competence, as the arm defines it — interpreting the system's verdicts, addressing the other party through it, noticing when it changes — is the practical-evaluative element at work under an opaque, adaptive intermediary: judgment among possible actions in response to emerging demands and ambiguities. The iterational element names what the worker brings from before the platform, and the projective element names the capacity to imagine a different verdict or a different counterpart. None of the three, however, names standing. The chordal triad is a theory of the actor's orientations toward a structural environment; it has no term for a recognised right to contest a verdict before someone obliged to answer. That gap is the arm's opening: the triad describes competence exhaustively and standing not at all, which is why the arm pairs this paper with Bovens and Pettit rather than reading standing out of it.

A second use is defensive. Reviewers at *Organization Science* will ask whether "agency" in the paper means anything beyond doing things, and this paper is the field's standard answer. The arm should take the temporal definition and say that the platform's adaptivity attacks the iterational element directly, because a rule that changes without notice makes past patterns unreliable guides.

## Caution

This is a reconstruction. The article was not opened, and the quotations and page numbers above are reported by citing literature whose accuracy I could not test; the p. 970 definition in particular circulates in several slightly different wordings. The term "chordal triad" is confirmed by several secondary sources but I did not see it in the article. The paper is sixty pages of theory with no empirical case and no treatment of technology or intermediaries, so any claim that it speaks to platforms is the arm's extension. Do not cite a page from this card until someone opens the article; the open PDF at web.augsburg.edu/sabo/EmibayerandMisheWhatIsAgency.pdf should be the first thing a session with network access reads.
