## What it argues

Chen and Starosta separate intercultural competence into three: intercultural **awareness** (the cognitive component, understanding of cultural difference), intercultural **sensitivity** (the affective component, readiness to engage difference positively), and intercultural **adroitness/competence** (the behavioural component). The paper builds and validates a 24-item scale for the affective component alone, on five factors — interaction engagement, respect for cultural differences, interaction confidence, interaction enjoyment, interaction attentiveness.

The architectural claim is that the three components are separable and that most measures conflate them, treating knowledge about difference as if it were the capacity to act well across it.

## Relation to the argument

The cognitive/affective/behavioural separation is directly transferable to §9's fifth construct. AI literacy, as Long and Magerko (2020) build it, is the cognitive component of something whose behavioural component nobody has specified — which is a sharper way to say what §9 says with "knowing how a recommender works predicts nothing about getting a counterpart to receive what you meant." Chen and Starosta's argument is that this gap is a general failure mode of competence measurement, not a peculiarity of AI literacy, and that framing makes §9's objection principled rather than pointed.

The affective component is also a gap in the paper worth acknowledging. Algorithmacy's three facets are cognitive and behavioural; nothing in §7 addresses whether a worker is *disposed* to engage the system's opacity rather than to disengage from it, and Spitzberg's knowledge/motivation/skill triad and Chen and Starosta's sensitivity both put that disposition inside the construct.
