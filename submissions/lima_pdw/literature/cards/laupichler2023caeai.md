## What it argues

Laupichler, Aster and Raupach make the field's most explicit bid for content validity, and they do it by asking experts. Fifty-three subject-matter experts worked through three iterative questionnaire rounds in a Delphi design: generating candidate AI literacy items, rating their relevance, proposing wording improvements, and evaluating each other's wording suggestions. Of 47 candidate items, **38** were judged relevant enough to include in a final AI literacy questionnaire. The authors present this as one of the first freely available AI literacy item sets, and they are candid about the state of the field they are entering — "there are very few psychometrically sound and thoroughly evaluated instruments that attempt to assess AI literacy in a valid way."

The design choice is the substance. Content validity by expert consensus means the construct's boundary is drawn by whoever counts as an expert, which in this literature means people who study or teach AI. What fifty-three such people converge on is what an educated non-expert ought to know about AI systems.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour, and the card that best exposes *whose* judgement fixes the boundary of algorithmic competence in this literature.

No counterpart appears. Three rounds, fifty-three experts, forty-seven candidate items and not one of them puts a second human being in the relation — a fact worth stating as a fact rather than an inference, because a Delphi is precisely the procedure that would surface an omitted dimension if the expert panel felt one. Fifty-three people whose job is thinking about AI literacy generated items for a year and produced a construct with two positions. That is strong evidence that the missing position is not an oversight in any single instrument but a boundary condition on how the field conceives the object.

This is the point where the paper's derivation discrimination earns its keep. A Delphi collects; it aggregates expert intuition about what belongs. It cannot produce a category that no expert already holds, which is exactly why algorithmacy could not have emerged from one. The paper's three facets are derived from what the coordination form withholds — accountability, predictability, common understanding — and a derivation from structure can generate a category that consensus would never nominate. The manuscript should say so here, and should resist the temptation to treat the omission as negligence. Nobody missed the counterpart. The counterpart was outside the frame the field had built.

Worth noting who the experts were not: no platform workers, no drivers, no couriers, no gig workers of any kind, and no workers coordinated by an algorithm they do not control. The paper's claim of descent from gig literacies (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn & Nelson 2020) gets a contrast here — that tradition builds its categories from what workers report doing, this one from what experts say workers should know, and algorithmacy from what the form withholds. Three different warrants, and the manuscript can lay all three out in a sentence.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth. The abstract gives the expert count, the round count and the item counts, but nothing about the experts' disciplines, countries or selection criteria, nor the relevance thresholds used — all of which determine what the consensus means and all of which need the open-access version of record. Delphi produces content validity only; there is no factor structure, no reliability estimate and no sample of actual respondents in this paper. The 38 items are an item *set*, not an instrument; the instrument is the 31-item SNAIL that followed (Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp & Raupach, 2023, *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* 12, 100338). Keep the two straight and keep their author lists straight — Haverkamp appears on the SNAIL paper and not on this one.
