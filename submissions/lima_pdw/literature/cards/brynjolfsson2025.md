## What it argues

The staggered rollout of a generative-AI conversational assistant across **5,172 customer-support agents** raises productivity, measured as issues resolved per hour, **"by 15% on average, with substantial heterogeneity across workers."** The heterogeneity is the paper's substantive finding, and it is fully characterised by position: **"Less experienced and lower-skilled workers improve both the speed and quality of their output, while the most experienced and highest-skilled workers see small gains in speed and small declines in quality."** Two further mechanisms are reported. AI assistance **"facilitates worker learning and improves English fluency, particularly among international agents,"** which the authors read as the model diffusing the tacit practice of high performers to everyone else. And the gains **"are largest for moderately rare problems, where human agents have less baseline experience but the system still has adequate training data"** — heterogeneity by task difficulty layered on top of heterogeneity by worker skill. The paper closes with an experience-of-work result: **"customers are more polite and less likely to ask to speak to a manager."**

The design is a staggered difference-in-differences on administrative conversation data from a single software firm, not a randomised experiment, and the outcome is a within-firm productivity metric rather than earnings or exit.

## Relation to the argument

This is the study §1 is closest to reaching for and the one it must be most careful with. It does document large outcome differences among workers on the same system at the same intensity — but it explains them. The variance is **divergence by position**: prior skill and tenure are the moderator, and the direction is novice-levelling, so the dispersion of outcomes *narrows*. Cited loosely, it appears to support the premise; read properly it supplies the leading rival explanation that §6 already concedes is "directly supported." Its correct use in the manuscript is as the rival, named and stated in its own terms, not as evidence for the residual. It is also the source of the "novice-levelling effects" phrase in §6 and should carry that sentence.

A second, subtler use: the moderately-rare-problems result shows that even within one worker, gains are not uniform across the task mix. That gives §6's "task mix" rival its own citation rather than leaving it as an unsupported list item.

## Caution

Do not cite for "the difference does not track the hours they work or the tools they use." The study measures neither hours nor tool volume, and its heterogeneity result is the opposite of unexplained. One firm, one occupation, one country's outsourced workforce; the effects are on resolutions per hour, not earnings or quit rates, so it cannot speak to §1's two named outcomes at all. Quality declines among top performers are described as **"small"** — do not upgrade them to a harm claim. Finally, the paper is not about platform work: these are employed agents inside a firm with supervisors and a rulebook, which is precisely the condition §1's London driver lacks.
