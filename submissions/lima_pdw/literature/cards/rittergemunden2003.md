## What it argues

This is the paper that gives the network form a named, measured competency, and it is the precedent §4 invokes. Ritter and Gemünden open on a deliberate analogy: the 1980s and 1990s produced scales for *market orientation* (Kohli & Jaworski, Narver & Slater) to test claims about marketing's effect on performance, but "business models have moved even further" and firms are now "embedded in networks of cooperative and competitive relations with other organizations" (p. 745). Market orientation measures the firm's own operation; what the network era needs is a construct for "a firm's ability to initiate, handle, use, and terminate interorganizational relationships" (p. 745). They call it network competence, and they build it because the ability is unevenly distributed: quoting Håkansson, "there are substantial differences between companies in their ability to handle networks. Some become highly talented and stable practitioners while others are quite simple 'amateurs'" (p. 746).

The construct is explicitly two-sided, and both sides matter for the seventh row. Competence "is used by some to describe resources and preconditions, i.e., qualifications, skills, or knowledge, necessary to perform certain tasks without considering the actual execution," and by others "as a process of activities"; the authors "incorporate both aspects" (p. 747). Network competence is therefore defined as "(a) the degree of network management task execution and (b) the extent of network management qualifications possessed by the people handling a company's relationships" (p. 748). Tasks split into relationship-specific ones (initiation, exchange — technology-related, person-related, organization-related — and coordination) and cross-relational ones (planning, organizing, staffing, controlling). Qualifications split into *specialist* qualifications (technical and economic knowledge, the capacity to "anticipate and evaluate critical situations and to select appropriate action") and *social* qualifications — "the extent to which a person is able to exhibit independent, prudent, and useful behavior in social settings," covering communication ability, extraversion, conflict management, empathy, emotional stability, self-reflectiveness, sense of justice, cooperativeness (p. 748).

The empirical test uses 308 German mechanical and electrical engineering companies and a LISREL model. Network competence raises the degree of technological interweavement and raises product and process innovation success directly, not only through interweavement. Four organizational antecedents raise network competence: access to resources, network orientation of human resource management, integration of intraorganizational communication, and openness of corporate culture (Abstract, p. 745).

## Relation to the argument

This is the load-bearing citation for the network cell of the seventh row and for the paper's precedent claim that "this paper does for co-optation what the network literature did for networks." Three things in it do work. First, it establishes that asking what a coordination form demands of a party inside it is a *tractable, publishable, measurable* question — the row is answerable, not merely askable. Second, its own framing is the seventh row in miniature for one column: the network form's coordinating media (reciprocity, trust, relational exchange) arrive with no price and no rulebook, so the party must supply the judgment itself, and Ritter and Gemünden name that supply. Third, the market-orientation lineage in the opening paragraph is useful ammunition in the other direction: the pre-network construct was an *orientation of the firm toward its customers*, not a competence the market demanded of participants — which is exactly the asymmetry §4 records in the market cell.

The paper should also take the four antecedents seriously as a template. They say network competence is not a fixed endowment but an organizationally produced one, which prefigures §10's question of how the competency is acquired and how it is distributed.

## Caution

**The unit of analysis is the firm, and §8 has already ruled on what that means.** Network competence is a property of a company: the definition makes it "the degree of network management task execution and the extent of network management qualifications possessed by the people handling a company's relationships," the survey items ask about the company's handling of its relationship portfolio, the antecedents are organizational (resource access, HRM orientation, communication integration, culture), and the N is 308 firms. Individuals appear only inside clause (b), and there only as the aggregate of qualifications a firm's relationship managers hold — the bearer of the competence is the company, which is why the paper can regress innovation success on it.

§8 of the manuscript then commits, in its own words: "The unit of measurement is the person, and the unit of comparison is the form. A study that measured it on firms would be measuring something else." Ritter and Gemünden is a study that measured it on firms. So the manuscript's precedent in §4 is, by the manuscript's own §8 test, measuring something else — and §8's table puts firm-held constructs in the *capability* row, which is the row §9 uses to dismiss Stelmaszak, Joshi and Constantiou's AI-as-organizing-capability ("it is a capability of the organizing party, and this paper's subject owns nothing"). The same argument disposes of the paper's central precedent. **A reviewer will find this, and the manuscript must handle it explicitly rather than let §4's parallel run unqualified.**

The repair is available and costs little. What transfers from Ritter and Gemünden is the *argumentative move* — a coordination form makes a demand, someone names the capacity that meets it, someone else measures it and shows it predicts outcomes — not the construct and not the level. §4 currently writes as though the precedent were about the same kind of thing; it should say that the network literature answered the seventh question at the firm level and that the co-optation column requires the same question answered at the individual level, which is a harder problem because the party there owns no routine to hold the competence in. Stated that way, the level difference becomes part of the paper's contribution instead of a hole in its warrant.

Two smaller points on the same sentence. §4 says the network literature named the capacity "three decades ago"; Ritter and Gemünden is 2003, which is two decades from a 2026 submission, not three. Either move the anchor back — Ritter's 1999 *Industrial Marketing Management* work and Håkansson's 1980s network research are genuinely older — or change the number. And §4's "somebody has named it" versus §1's "nobody has named it" is the paper's central rhetorical contrast; it will only carry weight if the two constructs being contrasted are the same kind of thing, which the level difference is precisely what threatens.

Two smaller cautions. The measures are self-reported by managers and the design is cross-sectional, so the causal arrow from competence to innovation success is a model assumption, not an identified effect. And Crossref stores the title in truncated form ("Network competence"); the full published title is as given above, confirmed against the typeset article and Semantic Scholar.
