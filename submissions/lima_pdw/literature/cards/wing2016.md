## What it argues

Wing's 2016 article proposes the "Ethical Principles for Online Dispute Resolution," also credited to the National Center for Technology and Dispute Resolution (2016) — a field-endorsed framework of seventeen named values, explicitly offered as "a set of values (not rules)" rather than enforceable standards: accessibility, accountability, competence, confidentiality, empowerment, equality, fairness, honesty, impartiality, informed participation, innovation, integration, legal obligation, neutrality, protection from harm, security, and transparency. Each principle is framed at a high level of generality to remain flexible across technology, sector, jurisdiction, and culture. Wing, Martinez, Katsh & Rule (2021) quote the Competence principle in full as an illustration: "ODR systems, processes, and practitioners will be competent in or provide access to relevant technological or human competency required for the effective implementation of the dispute resolution process that they undertake to assist with. This includes but is not limited to relevant dispute resolution, legal, and technical knowledge; languages; and culture."

## Relation to the argument

Cited once in the manuscript, at the point (line 252 in the current draft) where the paper names the closest available benchmark of values an institutional intermediary owes its participants, before arguing that algorithmic coordination regimes withhold the closest analogues of transparency, accountability, and informed participation, and that no principle in this framework (or elsewhere in the fourth-party literature) names a distinct value for contestation — a gap the manuscript's own argument turns to its advantage. This is a values framework, not a set of legal or contractual guarantees, and the manuscript's revised wording (2026-08-30) reflects that: it describes the framework as "offered explicitly as guidance rather than enforceable rules," matching Wing's own "values (not rules)" framing exactly.
