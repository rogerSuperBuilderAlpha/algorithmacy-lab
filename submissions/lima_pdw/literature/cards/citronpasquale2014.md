## What it argues
Citron and Pasquale carry the due-process argument from public agencies into private scoring. Their subject is the proliferation of predictive scores — credit, insurance, employment, fraud risk — that sort people into consequential categories using proprietary models and data the scored person cannot see. Their diagnosis has three parts. Scores are presented as objective while embedding contestable judgements about which variables count and how much. Scores are self-fulfilling: a low score produces worse terms, which produce worse outcomes, which confirm the score. And scored people have no way in — no notice of what was used, no explanation, no route to correct an error in the underlying data, and no forum in which to argue that the inference is wrong. Their proposal is procedural rather than substantive: not a right to a particular score but a right to *process*, including notice that scoring occurred, access to and correction of the data, disclosure of the scoring system's logic, audit trails, and interactive modelling that lets a person see how changes would move the score. They propose regulatory oversight — an agency with licensed auditors — because they do not believe individuals can police the system one complaint at a time.

## Relation to the argument
The self-fulfilling-score mechanism belongs in §5 and the paper does not yet have it. It supplies a reason beyond opacity for why individual competency cannot reach accountability in a scored arrangement: the record the worker answers for is not merely authored by someone else, it is *causally productive of the outcomes it purports to measure*. A worker who becomes skilled at performing well under the score improves the score without ever gaining standing over it, and the improvement confirms the schema. §5's sentence about becoming "expert at absorbing its absence" is this mechanism.

Their remedies are §6's institutional column stated for private actors, which is the version the paper needs — Citron (2008) covers state action, and this article is what extends the argument to a firm scoring a person with no state involvement. And their explicit judgement that individual complaint cannot do the work, so a regulator must, is the closest thing in the legal literature to §6's division: they considered the individual route and rejected it on capacity grounds.

The remedies are proposals, not law: the "scored society" apparatus described here has not been enacted in the US, so citing it for what protections exist would be wrong. Their subject is consumers and citizens being scored, not workers being managed; the platform-labour application is the paper's, and the difference matters because a worker's relation to a platform is continuous and productive rather than a one-off assessment.
