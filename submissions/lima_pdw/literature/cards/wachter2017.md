## What it argues
Wachter, Mittelstadt and Floridi dismantle the claim that the GDPR confers a right to an explanation of automated decisions. Working through the text, the recitals and the legislative history, they show that the binding articles require information about the *system* — the existence of automated decision-making, meaningful information about the logic involved, the significance and envisaged consequences — and that this information is owed at or before the time of processing. What the articles do not establish is a right to an explanation of a *specific decision after it has been made*. The only textual support for the latter sits in Recital 71, which is not legally binding and which, they show, was moved out of the binding text during the legislative process. They introduce the distinction the field has used since: *ex ante* explanation of system functionality versus *ex post* explanation of a particular decision, and they argue the Regulation clearly provides the first and, at best, ambiguously gestures at the second. Their conclusion is not that explanation is worthless but that the legal instrument that was supposed to supply it does not, and that the gap should be closed by legislation rather than by optimistic reading.

## Relation to the argument
This is the source that keeps §5's institutional column honest about what exists. §6 names statutory review as a supply route for accountability, and a reviewer will reasonably ask whether the GDPR already supplies it. Wachter and colleagues' answer is that the most-cited candidate right does not exist in binding form, which means the statutory route is a route that would have to be built rather than one already in place. That strengthens the paper's structure — the division in §6 is between what individuals cannot supply and what institutions *could* — while removing a false comfort.

Their ex ante / ex post distinction also maps directly onto §5's split, and the mapping is exact enough to be worth stating. Ex ante information about system functionality is an input to what §7 calls asymmetric interpretation and temporal tracking: it helps a worker form expectations, which is the predictability side. Ex post explanation of a particular decision is the first step of a forum relation — an account rendered about this case, to this person — and it is the thing the Regulation does not require. The legal literature's central controversy therefore falls on precisely the line §5 draws.

## Caution
Contested. Selbst and Powles (2017) replied directly and argued that Article 15(1)(h)'s "meaningful information about the logic involved" is capable of supporting an explanation right, and Edwards and Veale reach a third position; the paper must present this as a live dispute, not as the settled reading. The analysis is of the GDPR as adopted and predates subsequent guidance, national implementations and case law, including CJEU developments on Article 22 — check currency before making any claim about the present legal position. The domain is EU data protection; nothing here concerns employment or platform work, and a peer-review gate in a business-school cohort is outside its scope entirely.
