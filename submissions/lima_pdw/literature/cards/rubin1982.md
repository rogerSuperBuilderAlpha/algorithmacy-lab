## What it argues

Rubin builds the Communication Competency Assessment Instrument, the field's first serious attempt at criterion-referenced performance assessment of communication competence. The design starts from a competency statement — nineteen speaking and listening competencies drawn from the Speech Communication Association's guidelines — and constructs observable tasks for each, scored by trained raters against behavioural anchors rather than by self-report. The instrument deliberately samples performance in structured tasks and infers competence, and Rubin is explicit about the inferential gap that creates.

The follow-up (Rubin, 1985, *Communication Monographs* 52(2), 173–185, doi:10.1080/03637758509376103) reports the validity evidence: convergence with instructor ratings, discrimination from verbal ability, and the limits of both.

## Relation to the argument

The methodological ancestor of what §11 will have to build, and a warning about the cost. Rubin's route — competency statement, task design, trained raters, behavioural anchors — is expensive, and it is the route that avoids the self-report trap. Zhou et al. (2025), the rival scale the paper owes a discriminant argument, is self-report; algorithmacy could be measured Rubin's way, on gate submissions and their revisions, and §11's cohort design makes that unusually feasible because the gate produces a behavioural record.

The instrument's structure is also an argument for the paper's facet count. Rubin's nineteen competencies were criticised for proliferation without a derivation rule; §7's rule ("a facet is what a worker does in the absence of a withheld condition") is exactly the constraint Rubin lacked. That contrast is worth making, because it shows the derivation rule doing work rather than decorating a list.

## Caution

College-level speaking and listening, 1982, face-to-face tasks, trained-rater scoring. Nothing about the content transfers. Cite it for the assessment architecture and the criterion-referenced logic. Two Rubin papers are in play — the 1982 instrument paper and the 1985 validity paper — and they are routinely conflated.
