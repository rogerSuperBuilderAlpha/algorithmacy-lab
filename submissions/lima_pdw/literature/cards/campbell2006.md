## What it argues

Campbell's American Finance Association presidential address founds household finance as a field, and it founds it on a competence claim. Evidence on participation, diversification and mortgage refinancing shows that "many households invest effectively, but a minority make significant mistakes," and that this minority "appears to be poorer and less well educated than the majority of more successful investors." Two further findings sharpen it. Households show some awareness of their own limits and avoid strategies for which they feel unqualified — competence shapes not just outcomes but the choice set people enter. And some financial products "involve a cross-subsidy from naive to sophisticated households," which can inhibit welfare-improving innovation: the incompetent party pays, and the payment flows to the competent one.

## Relation to the argument

The cross-subsidy result is the most useful sentence in this cluster for the manuscript's overall argument, because it is the market's version of §4's inversion. A form that advertises cheap participation charges the less capable party privately, and the charge lands as a transfer to the capable. §4 says co-optation "advertises the market's cheap integrating debt and charges the worker privately for what the market supplies free"; Campbell shows a real market doing a structurally similar thing. Used well, that strengthens the paper — the seventh row is measuring a real quantity with real distributive consequences — while forcing the market cell to be honest about magnitude.

Cite it alongside Lusardi and Mitchell when conceding the market cell, and alongside Grossman and Stiglitz (already in the library) for the theoretical version: prices cannot be fully revealing if acquiring information is costly, so somebody must always be paying to know.

## Caution

Abstract depth only; every number and every asset-class result must be checked against the article. This is a presidential address — programmatic, wide-ranging, and selective in its evidence by design. Note also that "mistake" is a normative judgment against a benchmark model, and that benchmark is contested; households with different constraints, tax positions or beliefs may be doing something other than erring.
