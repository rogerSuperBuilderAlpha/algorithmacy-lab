## What it argues

Le Deist and Winterton show that "competence" means different things in different national systems and that the resulting literature is not one conversation. The **US** tradition, descending from McClelland and Boyatzis, is behavioural and person-centred: a competency is an underlying characteristic of a superior performer. The **UK** tradition, built into NVQs and occupational standards, is functional and job-centred: a competence is an outcome a worker can demonstrate against a defined standard, which makes it assessable and, by construction, trainable. The **French** tradition distinguishes savoir, savoir-faire, and savoir-être — knowledge, skill, and behavioural disposition. The **German** tradition organizes Kompetenz into Fachkompetenz, Sozialkompetenz, and Personalkompetenz within an action-competence frame. Their proposal is a multi-dimensional typology holding cognitive, functional, social, and meta-competence together, and their diagnostic point is that arguments about whether something is a competence usually turn out to be arguments between traditions.

## Relation to the argument

The card that tells §8 what its chosen word actually commits it to, and it is not a single thing. The section's own definition mixes the US behavioural tradition (attributes, characteristics) with the UK functional tradition (effective performance in a role) and cites Sandberg, who rejects both. More sharply: the UK functional conception makes competence explicitly assessable against a published standard and therefore trainable, which is the property §8 assigns to *skill* in order to rule skill out. Under that conception the skill/competency contrast the section rests on does not exist. Le Deist and Winterton also supply the constructive route out, since their meta-competence category — the capacity to acquire the other competences, to learn how to learn in a domain — is arguably the best existing home for algorithmacy, which is at bottom a capacity to work out what an unstated standard is. That reading would let §8 keep the word, place the construct precisely within a recognized typology, and stop resting the argument on a skill/competency stipulation.
