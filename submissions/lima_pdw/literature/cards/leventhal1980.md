## What it argues
Leventhal argues that equity theory had over-invested in allocation outcomes and under-studied the procedures that produce them, and he supplies the procedural criteria the field had lacked. His six rules specify when an allocative procedure will be judged fair. *Consistency*: the procedure applies the same way across people and across time. *Bias suppression*: the decision-maker's self-interest and prior commitments are kept out. *Accuracy*: decisions rest on good information. *Correctability*: opportunities exist to modify or reverse a decision. *Representativeness*: the concerns of all affected parties are reflected in the process. *Ethicality*: the procedure comports with prevailing moral standards. He also argues that procedural fairness judgements are made instrumentally and situationally — people weight the rules differently depending on what is at stake — and that procedures carry justice significance independent of outcomes, which is the move that opened organisational procedural-justice research.

## Relation to the argument
Leventhal's six rules are the closest thing organisational justice has to Bovens's constitutive elements, and §5 should use them for triangulation. Two of the six are the ones a platform arrangement fails structurally rather than incidentally: **correctability**, which requires an opportunity to modify or reverse a decision, and **representativeness**, which requires the affected party's concerns to be in the process. Both are properties of the procedure, established by whoever designs it. The other four — consistency, bias suppression, accuracy, ethicality — are also procedural properties no participant supplies. The whole apparatus of the field's founding statement of procedural fairness therefore locates every criterion on the arrangement's side, which is the paper's §5 claim arriving from a literature that never set out to make it.

Correctability is worth pulling out for §6. It names precisely what §6's institutional route delivers and what the individual route cannot: the existence of a mechanism through which a decision can be changed. And it gives the paper a much older citation than the contestability literature for the same idea, which matters for an organisation-theory audience.

## Caution
Not read in the original; the six rules are reconstructed from Colquitt's derivation and the standard account, so do not quote and do not attribute exact wording. The chapter is a theoretical proposal, and the six rules were not empirically derived — later work found the rules load onto fewer factors than six. Leventhal's frame is allocation in social relationships, not accountability, and "correctability" is not answerability: a procedure can be correctable through automated review with no party owing an explanation. The chapter predates the interactional-justice literature and does not address the relational dimension at all.
