## What it argues
Suzor argues that platforms govern — they make rules, adjudicate disputes and impose sanctions on hundreds of millions of people — and that they do so without any of the constraints that make governance legitimate. His organising concept is the rule of law, taken not as a body of doctrine but as a set of conditions: rules that are knowable in advance, applied consistently, administered by a process the governed can understand and challenge, and constrained by something outside the governor's will. He shows platforms failing each condition. Terms of service are unilateral, vague and revisable at will. Enforcement is opaque and inconsistent, with decisions made at scale by systems and outsourced reviewers. There is no appeal that constitutes review by an independent body, because the platform is rule-maker, enforcer and adjudicator at once. And contract law, the instrument that formally governs the relation, gives the user nothing, since she agreed in advance to whatever the platform decides. His constructive programme is a digital constitutionalism: importing rule-of-law values into platform governance through external oversight, procedural obligations and, where necessary, regulation.

## Relation to the argument
Suzor gives §5 the concentrated-power argument in its cleanest form: the platform occupies all three governmental roles simultaneously, so an appeal routed inside it is an appeal to the party complained of. That is a structural reason for the paper's claim that is independent of opacity, of scale and of technology — it would hold for a wholly transparent, wholly manual platform — and the paper should have it, because it forecloses the objection that better explanation or better interfaces would repair the deficit.

For §6 he supplies the criterion that separates the two supply routes. What makes a route institutional, on his account, is that it is *constituted independently of the governor*, which is exactly the phrase §6 uses. Suzor is the citation for it, and he is the source for why an in-platform appeal fails the test by construction.

His treatment of contract also matters for §2's standing argument. The companion review says employment, contract and membership each stipulate standing in advance, and Suzor's contribution is to show that the platform's contract does the opposite: it stipulates the *absence* of standing, and is the instrument by which the withholding is accomplished.

## Caution
Full text not read; use `suzor2018` for claims that need a page-verified source, and pull the book before quoting. It is written for a general as well as an academic audience, and the argument is normative and programmatic. Its subject is users of social platforms — speech, accounts, content — not workers and not pay; the transfer to platform labour is the paper's, and the differences are material, since a worker's relation is economic and continuous. The digital-constitutionalism programme is a proposal.
