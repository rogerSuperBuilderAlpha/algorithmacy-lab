## What it argues

The paper's claim is that newer technologies differ from earlier tools because they can hold intentionality over parts of an organizational routine, and that organization theory therefore needs a vocabulary for agency shared between humans and technology. Murray, Rhymer and Sirmon call that shared agency conjoined agency and, as a citing paper quotes them, define it as the shared capacity between humans and nonhumans to exercise intentionality (reported at p. 555; not checked). The locus of agency is split along two components of a routine: protocol development, which sets the guidelines for how the routine is to be carried out, and action selection, which is the choosing of what to do within it. Each component can rest with the human or with the technology, and the two-by-two yields four forms. With assisting technologies the human holds both protocol development and action selection and the technology is a tool. With arresting technologies the technology holds action selection, so it constrains what the human can do in the moment. With augmenting technologies the technology holds protocol development, so it shapes the rules before the human acts. With automating technologies the technology holds both. The authors acknowledge a Deloitte Insights report for the terms assisting, augmenting and automating, and say they coined arresting themselves.

The dependent variable is the routine. The paper theorises how each form affects a routine's change at a particular moment and the routine's responsiveness to feedback over time, and citing literature reports that automating technologies in particular let routines adapt and evolve from the data they process with little human intervention. Later work finds the arresting category hard to locate in practice for AI.

On accountability the record is empty. Three targeted searches for responsibility, accountability, blame or attribution in connection with the paper returned nothing from the paper itself; the one hit was a service-design paper's own observation that hybrid human–AI systems leave no single actor in full control. Either the paper does not treat accountability, or it treats it in a passage no citing source has picked up. I cannot tell which from here.

## Relation to the argument

Arm: `orgsci_agency_triad`. Murray is a guest editor of the special issue, and the paper is the AMR statement of the human–technology dyad the arm wants to open into a triad. Its two-by-two has exactly one human and one technology, and the routine it modulates belongs to the organization that deploys the technology. The arm's case has three parties and the technology evaluates two of them, neither of whom owns the routine. Read against the four forms, an evaluating platform is arresting for the worker and the counterpart at once — it selects which actions are allowed to count — and augmenting for the operator, whose protocol it writes. That one technology sits in two cells for different parties is the arm's point, and it is a point the paper's own grid can be used to make: conjoined agency is defined per human–technology pair, so a triad produces two conjoined agencies that are not the same and may not be reconcilable.

The accountability silence, if it is real, is the second use. The arm says that when the operator acts through the agency of the parties it evaluates, accountability is distributed, deflected and denied. A conjoined-agency framework that assigns intentionality to the technology without assigning anyone the obligation to answer for it is the theoretical form of that denial. The arm should say so, carefully, and only after the article has been read.

## Caution

Reconstruction, not a reading. The p. 555 locator and the definition's wording come from a citing paper; the four-form descriptions come from several secondary summaries that agree with one another but may all be paraphrasing the abstract. The claim that the paper is silent on accountability is an absence of evidence from search, not a finding about the text, and must not appear in the manuscript until the article is read; the UCL accepted manuscript is the copy to open. Publication timing: the article went online in 2020 and appeared in the July 2021 issue, so some listings date it 2020. Fisher and Fleming (2025, Organization Studies) call the framework's "dark side" unexamined, which is adjacent to the arm's claim and should be carded before the arm cites either.
