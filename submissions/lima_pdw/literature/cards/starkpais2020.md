## What it argues

A conceptual essay naming the platform's social form: not market, hierarchy, or network but a **Möbius organizational form** that turns the classical triad inside out — markets *contract*, hierarchies *command*, networks *collaborate*, and platforms *co-opt* assets and activities "not part of the firm." The managerial problem this poses is answered through a **triangular geometry** of owner, provider, and user-customer: platform owners co-opt provider/user behavior by "enrolling them in algorithmic management 'without managerial authority having been delegated to them.'" The paper is explicit that the platform's core operation is *matching*: "the managerial challenge for platforms is 'making matches'" and turns on "matching protocols." Ratings by counter-parties are "algorithmically translated into rankings," circulated through feedback loops the paper calls twisted rather than circular: "at each fold of the feedback loop accountability can be deflected and denied." A historical table contrasts mid-century Scientific management (hierarchical accountability), late-century collaborative production (heterarchical), and platformization (deflected accountability): "accountings are plentiful, but no one is accountable."

## Relation to the argument (Lima PDW manuscript)

Cited three times (lines 44, 513, 524), always paired with Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024) for the same claim: automated enrollment/matching/evaluation/termination as coordinative co-optation's defining mechanism. This is the correct source to carry the "matching" vocabulary specifically — "match"/"matching" appears repeatedly in this article (the platform's "making matches," "matching protocols") and nowhere in Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024). One place in the manuscript (line 296) attributes the identical "matches unchosen parties" claim to Vanden Broeck alone, dropping this citation — that line should restore the pairing used everywhere else.

## Caution

A conceptual essay building on others' fieldwork (eBay via Curchod et al., Uber via Rosenblat, Spotify via Prey), not new data of its own. Its strongest claim — deflected accountability / "no accountability in algorithmic accounting" — is explicitly contested by other scholars in its own literature (Curchod et al. read eBay as a bureaucratic panopticon, which this paper rejects as a misreading), which the manuscript need not relitigate but should not present as an uncontested consensus finding if it ever cites the accountability claim on its own. The "unilaterally terminate accounts" claim the manuscript pairs with this source (lines 44, 524) rests, in the source itself, on a single footnote crediting the deactivation finding to Rosenblat (2018) rather than to Stark and Pais's own analysis — minor looseness, previously flagged and judged not to require a fix since the citation covers the general mechanism rather than a specific termination-rate finding.
