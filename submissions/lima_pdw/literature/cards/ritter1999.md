## What it argues

The origin point of the network-competence construct, four years before the *Journal of Business Research* paper that made it citable. Ritter sets out the two-dimensional structure that the 2003 paper then tests: a company's network competence consists of network management *task execution* and network management *qualifications*, with qualifications divided into specialist and social skills. The paper's own contribution is the antecedent side — what makes a company able to cope with relationships and networks effectively rather than badly — and it names the four organizational conditions later carried into the 2003 model: availability of (or access to) resources, network orientation of human resource management, integration of intraorganizational communication, and openness of corporate culture.

## Relation to the argument

Useful mainly for dating the construct. §4 says the network literature "named that capacity three decades ago," and 1999 is the date that claim rests on; 2003 is when it was measured at scale. Citing both makes the precedent claim precise instead of approximate. The antecedent framing also matters for §10: from its first statement, network competence was theorized as *produced* by organizational conditions rather than possessed as a trait, which is the position the paper takes on algorithmacy's acquisition.

## Caution

I have not read the article itself; the content above comes from the 2003 paper's explicit attribution (including a reproduced figure with a page citation) plus publisher metadata. Anything quoted from Ritter (1999) in the manuscript should be checked against the article before it enters a draft. As with the 2003 paper, the unit is the firm, and the "networking company" is an actor choosing and managing its own partners — not a party being coordinated by someone else.
