## What it argues

Coins the "second-level digital divide": having Internet access (the "first-level" divide, the literature's prior focus) does not mean people can use the medium effectively, and that gap in effective use is this paper's subject. Skill is defined narrowly and explicitly: "the ability to efficiently and effectively find information on the Web." Hargittai measured it by giving five search tasks (local cultural events, music, a site comparing presidential candidates' positions on abortion, tax forms, kids' art) to a random, in-person-observed sample of 54 adult Internet users from suburban Mercer County, New Jersey (summer/fall 2001), scoring binary success/failure and time to completion for each.

Findings: half the sample (27/54) completed all five tasks successfully, but even among the fully successful, completion time varied up to twelvefold (2.5 to 30.3 minutes). Age is negatively associated with skill (a clear generational gradient — teens and 20s fastest, 60s/70s slowest). Experience (years online, hours of weekly use) is positively associated with skill. Gender differences are not statistically significant. Education's relationship is confounded with age in the raw data (the least-educated subgroup scores best, but skews notably older); controlling for age by excluding current students, graduate-degree holders do best. Conclusion: mere access does not resolve digital inequality — training and support investment are needed alongside wiring, because a second-level divide in effective use persists among the formally connected.

## Relation to the argument

PAPER.md cites this once (the proposition near "second-level digital divides") only for its *structural logic*, not for any empirical finding: formally equal access does not guarantee equal effective use, and the resulting unmeasured skill gap generates a hidden inequality that looks, from the outside, like uniform opportunity. The manuscript transposes that two-level-divide frame from general Web-search skill to triadic algorithmic coordination — arguing that within formally undifferentiated labor pools, latent algorithmacy differences generate earnings, safety, and evaluation disparities platforms attribute to merit rather than informational asymmetry. The manuscript does not put any platform-labor claim, earnings claim, or safety claim in Hargittai's mouth, and states nothing about her design or findings that isn't there. This is a narrow, correctly-scoped use: borrow the typology, attribute it, build past it.

## Caution

A single-county, higher-income, more-educated-than-the-general-population sample (N=54, 2001) — Hargittai's own paper flags this as likely making her education effects conservative estimates. The five search tasks operationalize "skill" narrowly as static-content search-and-navigation competence on the pre-algorithmic-feed Web of 2001; there is no adaptive, opaque, or binding intermediary anywhere in this study, and no counterpart — do not cite this paper for anything about algorithmic evaluation, coordination with a human party, or mutual bindingness, only for the access/effective-use distinction. Education's effect is explicitly confounded with age in the paper's own analysis (Table 5A vs. 5B); do not cite "less-educated users perform best" as a clean, unqualified finding — the paper itself withdraws that reading once age is controlled.
