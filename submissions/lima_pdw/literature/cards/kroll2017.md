## What it argues
Kroll and colleagues argue that the policy debate has misdiagnosed the problem. Transparency — publishing source code or disclosing a model — is neither necessary nor sufficient for accountability in automated decisions: source code does not reveal how a trained model behaves, disclosure invites gaming, and much of what matters is in the data and the deployment rather than in the program. Their alternative is *procedural regularity*, the assurance that the same rule was applied to every subject and that the decision actually made was the decision the rule specifies. They then show that computer science already has tools to deliver such assurance without disclosure: cryptographic commitments that bind a decision-maker to a rule in advance, zero-knowledge proofs that a decision followed the committed rule, verifiable random selection for lotteries and audits, and software verification and audit logs. The argument's shape is that technical design and legal governance must be built together, because the guarantees a court would want are ones that have to be engineered into the system before it runs, not extracted from it afterwards. They are careful that procedural regularity is not fairness — a consistently applied unjust rule remains unjust — and they treat their tools as necessary infrastructure for accountability rather than as accountability itself.

## Relation to the argument
Kroll and colleagues sit on the institutional side of §6's division, and they sharpen what that side has to build. Their point that accountability guarantees must be *designed in advance* is the technical counterpart of §5's claim: what makes a decision contestable is a commitment made by the decision-maker before the decision, and no capacity of the subject can retroactively create one. A worker facing an uncommitted, revisable rule has nothing to hold the platform to, however skilled her reconstruction of it — and their argument explains why in engineering terms. This is worth having because it answers the objection from the other direction: a reader who suspects §5 overstates the impossibility can be shown what would actually be required, and it is a cryptographic commitment scheme and an audit infrastructure, not a more capable worker.

The paper should also take their transparency argument. §5 and §7 lean on opacity, and Kroll and colleagues show that lifting opacity would not by itself supply accountability — which protects the paper from a reviewer who proposes disclosure as the fix and expects the argument to fold.
