## What it argues

The paper that named **algorithmic management**: software algorithms that assume managerial functions, plus the institutional devices that support them. Twenty-one driver interviews, twelve passenger interviews, 128 forum posts and 132 company communications across Uber and Lyft, examining three features against Mintzberg's managerial roles — work assignment (decisional), surge information (informational), and rating plus acceptance rate (evaluative). Two findings matter most here. The knowledge asymmetry is explicit and consequential: drivers who understood more of the assignment algorithm built workarounds — toggling the app at traffic lights to shrink the pickup radius, parking among other cars to avoid pings, arranging repeat riders off-app — while drivers who understood less misattributed bad assignments to system error and rejected them, lowering their own ratings. Lee et al. state the asymmetry as **"more knowledge more advantage,"** and note that companies may desire a degree of user ignorance to prevent gaming, so the opacity is strategic. The second is that with little direct company contact, drivers turned to forums for **fragmented social sensemaking** — effective for rating tips, where experience and no single right answer prevailed, and useless for assignment and surge questions, where only an authority figure had the right information, so those threads produced emotional support and jokes instead of answers.

## Relation to the argument

"More knowledge more advantage" is §10's stratified-fluency property stated as a field finding, with the mechanism attached: the better-informed drivers acted differently and got better outcomes, and the worse-informed made errors that damaged their own scores. This is the closest thing in the cluster to the variance §1 opens with, in a population where the arrangement is held fixed. Section 10 should cite it as the property's empirical warrant, and §11 should note that Lee et al. observed the gradient without designing for it.

The fragmented-sensemaking finding is more valuable still, because it draws a line **inside** the interpretation facet that §7 does not have. Collective folk theorising worked where the answer was distributed across experience (what raises a rating) and failed where the answer was held by an authority (how assignment works). If that generalises, asymmetric interpretation is tractable only for rules whose evidence is spread across many workers' outcomes, and simply unavailable for rules that are held centrally and never leak. Section 7 currently treats the facet as uniformly possible; Lee et al. suggest a scope condition.

## Caution

Twenty-one drivers with average tenure under five months, thirteen US cities, 2014–2015; the authors could not interview company representatives or developers. An HCI design study whose conclusion is design recommendations. Interview quotes are illustrative, not measured frequencies, so "more knowledge more advantage" is an inductive claim rather than an estimated effect — which is precisely why §11 is needed and should say so.
