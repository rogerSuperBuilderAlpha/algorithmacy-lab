## What it argues
Pinder and Harlos take silence out of the residual category and give it internal structure. Their subject is what an employee does after perceiving injustice, and their first move is to separate silence from voice not as absence from presence but as two forms of *communicative choice*, each with its own antecedents. They then split silence itself. **Quiescent silence** is withholding out of fear: the employee recognises the injustice, believes conditions could change, and stays quiet because speaking is dangerous. **Acquiescent silence** is withholding out of resignation: the employee has disengaged from the possibility that anything will change, does not consider alternatives, and stays quiet because contesting is pointless rather than risky. The two look identical from outside and differ entirely inside. Quiescence is active suppression under threat and can flip to voice when the threat lifts; acquiescence is a settled belief about the arrangement, and lifting the threat does nothing, because the employee is not silent out of fear. They locate the sources in perceived injustice, in the organisation's history of responses to complaints, and in the credibility of the channels the employee has available.

## Relation to the argument
This is the closest published name for the pattern in the paper's first participant interview. A participant who identified a review error, had a Zoom session and a Discord open to her, judged neither appropriate, concluded "that's the way this is," and never contested is not exhibiting fear and is not exhibiting ignorance of the channels. She is exhibiting **acquiescent silence**: the channels were available and she had already settled the question of whether using them would produce anything. Pinder and Harlos supply both the label and the diagnostic that separates it from the fear case, which matters for the study in §11, because the two require different interview probes and support different conclusions. Quiescence would suggest the platform suppresses voice; acquiescence suggests the platform has made contestation legible as futile, which is the stronger version of §5's claim — the worker has read the absence of a forum correctly.

The chapter also strengthens §5 against its own objection. If acquiescence is a reasonable inference from the arrangement rather than a deficit in the worker, then a more skilled worker will reach it *faster*, not slower, and higher competency will predict *less* contestation rather than more. That is a checkable prediction the paper does not currently make, and it would be a sharp one: it separates the paper's account from any reading in which algorithmacy is a route to redress.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and the citing literature; the quiescence/acquiescence definitions above are the standard rendering, and pinpoint quotation requires pulling the chapter. It is a conceptual chapter in an annual series, not an empirical study, and the distinction has proved harder to measure than to state — later scales (notably Van Dyne and colleagues) add a third, prosocial form of silence and disagree about boundaries. Its setting is employment; a platform worker's acquiescence has no supervisor at its far end, which changes what the construct is about even where the behaviour matches.
