## What it argues
Contestability appears in AI ethics frameworks — it is one of the eight core principles in Australia's proposed framework, the first to include it — with almost no specification of what it requires. Lyons, Velloso and Miller supply one empirically, through a thematic analysis of 65 submissions responding to Australia's AI Ethics Framework discussion paper, drawn from industry, government, civil society, academia and individuals, including many of the entities that would have to build contestation processes. Two agreements emerge about contestability's nature. It is fundamentally about protecting individuals against the power differential between decision-maker and decision-subject. And respondents treat contesting an algorithmic decision as requiring the same processes as contesting a human one, reaching for the review mechanisms that already exist — internal review by the original decision-maker, external review by an independent body — with heavy emphasis on human review, while flagging its costs in decision fatigue, complacency and automation bias. Several submissions argue AI should not be distinguished from human decision-making at all, and that accountability and liability should apply consistently across both. To operationalise contestability the authors distil three interacting requirements: policy decisions that set the scope, above all *what* can be contested — the decision, the input data, the process, the outcome; a contestation process with concrete steps, centrally an explanation of the decision and an avenue for the subject to respond and have the response considered; and alignment of that process with relevant legislation.

## Relation to the argument
This is the paper's best evidence that contestability is constitutively institutional, gathered from the parties who would have to supply it. Every element the respondents converge on — a scope set by policy, a process with steps, a reviewer, a statutory alignment — is something the decision-maker or a regulator establishes. Nothing in the corpus locates contestability in the subject's capacity. For §6 that is close to direct support for the division: asked what contestability requires, sixty-five stakeholders answered with institutions.

The three requirements also give §6 a grading instrument it currently lacks. A channel that offers no explanation, or that leaves undefined what can be contested, or that has no obligation to consider a response, fails on their scheme even though it exists — which is exactly the diagnosis the paper needs for the live interview case, where two channels were open and neither was a contestation process on this definition. Their observation that respondents model algorithmic contestation on *human* contestation is worth §5's attention too: the intuition that a decision should be answerable to someone is not a technology-specific demand, which supports reading accountability as a general relational condition rather than an artefact of automation.

## Caution
The corpus is submissions to a single national consultation, which is a specific and self-selected population — organisations with the resources to respond, arguing positions that serve them. It records what stakeholders *say* contestability requires, not what works; there is no evaluation of any implemented process. The setting is Australian AI policy, and legislative alignment as a requirement is jurisdiction-bound. The paper draws on it for structure, not for evidence about outcomes.
