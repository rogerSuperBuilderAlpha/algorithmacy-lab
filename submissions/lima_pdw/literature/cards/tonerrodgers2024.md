## What it argues

Claimed that AI-assisted materials discovery at a large corporate R&D lab sharply raised discovery and patenting, with gains concentrated among the most able scientists — a widely circulated result on heterogeneity by skill that ran in the same direction as Otis et al. and against the novice-levelling studies. It was covered by the *Wall Street Journal*, *Nature* and *The Atlantic* and entered a great many 2025 literature reviews.

**The findings are not usable.** After an internal review, MIT stated on 16 May 2025 that it had **"no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data"** and **"no confidence in the veracity of the research contained in the paper."** The author is no longer affiliated with MIT. Because only authors may withdraw an arXiv preprint and the author did not do so when directed, MIT took the unusual step of requesting the withdrawal directly; the paper was marked withdrawn on 20 May 2025.

## Relation to the argument

A negative entry, carded so that it does not enter the manuscript by accident. It circulates in secondary summaries of "AI heterogeneity" evidence — including AI-assisted literature searches, which frequently surface the December 2024 preprint without the May 2025 withdrawal — and it is precisely the kind of striking, convenient finding a paper arguing for skill-graded divergence would want.

It also carries a methodological warning worth one line somewhere in §12: the most-cited single result on AI and worker heterogeneity in 2025 was fabricated, which is a reason for this manuscript to be conservative about the whole cluster rather than expansive.
