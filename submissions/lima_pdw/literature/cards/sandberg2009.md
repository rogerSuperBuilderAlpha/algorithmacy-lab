## What it argues

Sandberg and Pinnington survey the existing accounts of professional competence — scientific knowledge, tacit knowledge, knowing-in-action, understanding of work — and argue that each gives a fragmented picture, because none explains how knowledge and understanding are *integrated* into a specific competence in actual performance. They propose an existential ontological alternative, drawn from Heidegger: competence is a **way of being** in the work. Their study of corporate lawyers finds that different ways of practising corporate law each distinguish and integrate a particular understanding of the work, a particular self-understanding, a particular relation to other people, and a particular relation to tools, into distinct forms of competence. Competence, on this account, is not held and applied; it is the shape a person's involvement in a practice takes.

## Relation to the argument

The 2009 statement is more useful to §8 than the 2000 one in one specific respect: it names **tools and other people** as constituents of a competence rather than as its environment. That is the sentence the Lima interview needs. When the participant says she came to realize it was not possible to understand the requirements alone, or to complete really successful builds, without the assistant, she is describing a competence in which the tool is internal to the way of practising, not an aid bolted onto a competence she holds independently. On the attribute view that is a confound to be controlled for; on Sandberg and Pinnington's view it is the competence. The paper has to decide which, because §8's table says algorithmacy is held by "the coordinated party" and the interview suggests it may be held by a party-plus-assistant. Their inclusion of **self-understanding** also bears on §10's stratification: two participants facing the same gate with different self-understandings are, on this account, differently competent by constitution rather than by degree.

## Caution

An interpretive study of corporate lawyers in one professional setting, with a Heideggerian apparatus that many organization-theory readers will find heavy; adopting "ways of being" commits the paper to an ontological position it may not want alongside a psychometric instrument. The account is explicitly anti-attribute, so it compounds rather than resolves the tension between Sandberg as anchor and the three-wave scale in §11. Abstract-level read; the four integrated aspects are verified from the abstract, and the corporate-lawyer findings are not.
