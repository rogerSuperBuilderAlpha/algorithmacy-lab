## What it argues

Krakowski, Luger, and Raisch use chess as a controlled setting for a resource-based question: what does adopting AI do to competitive capabilities? Comparing the same players across conventional, centaur (human plus engine), and engine tournaments, they find that AI adoption triggers **substitution** and **complementation** at once. Traditional competitive capabilities — the ones that made a player strong at conventional chess — are made obsolete by substitution, while working with an engine creates **new sources of persistent heterogeneity**. The striking result is that these novel human-machine capabilities are unrelated to traditional capabilities, and in places negatively related: being good at chess does not make you good at centaur chess. Their contribution is an integrated view in which AI drives both dynamics and thereby shifts where advantage comes from.

## Relation to the argument

The best available empirical support for the paper's founding puzzle, and it comes from the strategy literature §8 argues against, which makes it doubly useful. §1 needs evidence that equivalently placed people diverge under a mediating system for reasons that are not prior skill, and the existing citations in `REFERENCES.md` were flagged for pointing the wrong way — Brynjolfsson and colleagues find heterogeneity tracking skill and tenure, and Dell'Acqua and colleagues find divergence by task. Krakowski and colleagues find divergence that is **orthogonal to, and sometimes inverse to, prior domain capability**, in a setting where the structural conditions are identical for every player and prior ability is measured precisely by Elo. That is very close to the variance §1 wants to isolate, and no other study in the paper's current reference set delivers it. The finding that human-machine capability is a distinct and persistent source of heterogeneity is also the strongest external corroboration available for §10's second property. The paper should also register the awkwardness: the construct here is called a *capability*, sits inside a resource-based frame, and is measured on individuals — which is a counterexample to §8's tidy division of labour between the words.

## Caution

Chess tournaments are the study's strength and its limit: a closed, fully observable game with a precise ability measure and an engine whose objective is known, none of which holds at an opaque evaluative gate on unendorsed objectives. The identification rests on comparing the same players across tournament formats rather than on assignment, and "capability" is operationalized as performance rating, which is a proxy. Abstract-level read; the substitution/complementation finding and the orthogonality result are verified from the abstract, and the empirical detail is not.
