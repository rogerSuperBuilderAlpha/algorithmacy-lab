## What it argues

Tour and Zadorozhnyy define **prompt literacy** as "a combination of multiple capabilities required to engage in dynamic and iterative interaction with GenAI," and argue it matters for English language learners because prompt quality directly determines the relevance, accuracy and creativity of what the model returns. Their contribution is pedagogical rather than psychometric: they take Freebody and Luke's Four Resources Model — the reading-literacy framework of code breaker, meaning maker, text user, text analyst — and show how each resource maps onto teaching learners to prompt, with worked classroom examples.

The choice of the Four Resources Model is telling. It is a *reading* framework, and importing it means treating the generative model as a text to be decoded, interpreted, used and critiqued. Prompt literacy on this account is a species of textual practice, and its object is output.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour, and the clearest case in the cluster of a construct that is unambiguously **skill** by the paper's criterion.

No counterpart appears. Prompting is a two-position practice by definition — a person types, a model responds, the person revises. Tour and Zadorozhnyy's whole apparatus is built to make that loop tighter. There is no third party, no determination binding on anyone, and no coordination with a person the prompter never meets. The Four Resources import makes this structural: reading frameworks presume a reader and a text, and a text does not have a counterparty.

The skill discrimination lands hardest here and the paper should use this card for it, because prompt literacy is the construct a sceptical reader is most likely to reach for as evidence that algorithm-facing competence is already named and already teachable. It is teachable — the article's entire purpose is to tell teachers how to scaffold it. It is tool-indexed — prompt literacy as described is a competence with respect to instruction-following language models circa 2023–2025, and it will date when the interaction paradigm changes, as prompt-engineering advice from 2022 already has. It is acquired by instruction rather than by participation under an undisclosed rule; indeed the model's behaviour is being *disclosed* to the learner, item by item, as the pedagogy proceeds. And nothing about it is stratified: the article's premise is that scaffolding closes the gap.

Contrast this with algorithmacy on the two properties and the difference is not a matter of degree. A prompter learns to speak more effectively to a system that is trying to help her. A coordinated party learns to anticipate a system that is not addressing her at all, that is simultaneously reading someone else, and that will issue a ruling neither of them wrote. Prompt literacy is a skill for a cooperative interlocutor; algorithmacy is a competency for an adjudicating third party.

Use it in §9 as the "skill, not competency" exhibit, and note that the field has produced a considerable prompt-literacy and prompt-engineering literature — Lo's CLEAR framework (doi:10.1016/j.acalib.2023.102720) and his "art and science of prompt engineering" piece (doi:10.1080/10875301.2023.2227621) among them — without any of it acquiring a relational position.

## Caution

A discussion article, not an empirical study: no instrument, no sample, no validation, and the authors describe it as addressing a gap in how teachers can scaffold prompt literacy. There is no "prompt literacy scale" here and I found none elsewhere that meets the verification bar — the searches for a validated prompt-literacy instrument returned book chapters and non-indexed venues, so the manuscript should not claim one exists or that one does not without a fuller search. Read at abstract depth; the Four Resources mapping and the classroom examples need the version of record, which is paywalled at Wiley. Scope is English language education and adolescent/adult literacy, so do not transport claims about learners to claims about workers. Note the related but distinct empirical work by Hwang, Lee and Shin on prompt iteration in EFL vocabulary learning (doi:10.1515/iral-2025-0089, *International Review of Applied Linguistics in Language Teaching*, 2025) — a different paper from this one, and the two are easily conflated.
