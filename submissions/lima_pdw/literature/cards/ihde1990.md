## What it argues

The book's first program, "A Phenomenology of Technics", sorts human–technology–world relations into a small set of types, and that typology became postphenomenology's standard vocabulary. In embodiment relations the technology is taken into the body and the world is experienced through it, as with eyeglasses or a telephone; Verbeek writes the form as (human – technology) → world. In hermeneutic relations the technology represents the world and the human reads the representation, as with a map, a thermometer or an MRI; the form is human → (technology – world). In alterity relations the technology is faced as a quasi-other, something stronger than an object and weaker than an animal or a person — computers, automata, a film seen from a certain angle (citing sources give pp. 98 and 101–102); the form is human → technology (world). Background relations are the technologies that shape experience without being attended to, heating and refrigeration, which one citing source quotes Ihde calling a present absence (p. 109); the form is human (technology / world). Ihde describes the first three as points on a spectrum from which the fourth stands apart. Each relation carries a structure of amplification and reduction: the technology magnifies some aspects of the world and diminishes others. The second program, cultural hermeneutics, adds multistability, the claim that one artifact stabilises into different uses and meanings in different contexts, and the book as a whole asks for a double-sided analysis pairing microperception with cultural hermeneutics (p. 30, per one citing source).

## Relation to the argument

Arm: `spm2027_lived_arbiter`. An SPM audience will expect the talk to say which of Ihde's relations the platform's evaluating algorithm is. The answer the arm should give is that it is none of the four, and the reason is structural. Every Ihde relation has one human and a world, and the technology sits between them in one of four positions. The lived arbiter has two humans. The host writes to the guest and the algorithm reads both; the relation the host stands in is not to a world through a technology but to another person through a judge. The hermeneutic relation is the nearest, because the host does read the platform's representation (a rank, a badge, a visibility drop) to learn how the world stands, but in Ihde's hermeneutic relation the instrument reads the world and the world does not read back, whereas the platform reads the host while the host reads it. The alterity relation is the tempting wrong answer. Jhaver et al.'s hosts speak of the algorithm as a presence they negotiate with, which sounds like a quasi-other, but the hosts are not coordinating with the algorithm; they are coordinating with the guest and being heard by the algorithm, and a quasi-other is faced, not written for. The background relation fits the algorithm's invisibility but not its judgment: a present absence that heats the room does not rank you. Embodiment does not apply.

What the arm can take from Ihde is the method. The doubled address the arm describes — speaking to the other person while being heard by the arbiter — is a microperceptual structure of the kind Program One isolates, and the talk can present it as a fifth relation that the four cannot compose: human → (technology → other human), where the technology's reading of the first human is part of what the second human receives. Amplification and reduction carry over: the arbiter amplifies whatever its rule rewards and reduces the rest of what the host says to the guest. Multistability carries over in a form Ihde did not consider, because the same evaluating technology stabilises differently for the two parties it evaluates at the same moment.
