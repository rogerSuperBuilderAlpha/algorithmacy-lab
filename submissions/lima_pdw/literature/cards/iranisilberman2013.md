## What it argues
Irani and Silberman analyse Amazon Mechanical Turk as an infrastructure that renders workers invisible — as "human computation," an API call, a resource requesters draw on without seeing. The asymmetry they name is the one the paper cares about: requesters evaluate workers, reject work without stating a reason, and withhold payment, while workers have no way to evaluate requesters, no route to dispute a rejection, and no interface through which Amazon owes them anything. The authors' response is not a critique but a system. Turkopticon is a browser extension and server they built and ran, which lets workers review requesters on communicativity, generosity, fairness and promptness, and which surfaces those reviews inside the Mechanical Turk interface at the moment a worker is deciding whether to accept a task. The paper reports the system's design, its uptake, and what happened when it ran — workers used it heavily, and the authors frame it as an intervention that makes an invisible party visible and creates a record the platform did not provide. Their reflection is that the tool works by building a *parallel* infrastructure alongside a platform that would not build one, and that this is activism through design rather than a feature request.

## Relation to the argument
Turkopticon is the most serious empirical challenge in this cluster to any strong reading of §5, and the paper should meet it head-on because a reviewer in HCI or platform studies will raise it. Here are workers who, facing an arrangement with no forum, *built one*. If accountability can be constructed from below, §6's institutional/individual division looks less clean than the paper claims.

The answer, and it is the right one, is that Turkopticon confirms the division rather than breaking it. What the workers built was an institution — a persistent system with its own governance, moderation, funding and maintenance labour, staffed over years, eventually with organisational form. It is §6's institutional route arriving through individual effort, which is exactly the case §5 already concedes when it says a skilled worker who finds a journalist or a regulator "located the route; it did not substitute for what the route reached." Turkopticon is the strongest version of that concession, and stating it strengthens the paper: what individual competency produced was not private answerability but a second institution. Two further details matter. Turkopticon holds *requesters* accountable to workers deciding whether to accept work; it does not obtain reversal of a rejection already made, so it supplies predictive information rather than redress. And its maintenance burden — documented in the authors' later reflections — is the cost of an institution built by parties who hold no standing.

## Caution
Full text not read; the reconstruction rests on the system's well-documented design and the secondary literature, so do not quote the paper or report specific uptake figures without pulling it. It is a design-and-deployment paper with a reflective evaluation, not a study of outcomes for workers. The setting is microtask crowdwork in 2013, and both Mechanical Turk and Turkopticon have changed substantially since; Irani and Silberman's own later writing on the sustainability of the system is the better source for what happened next. Do not describe Turkopticon as an appeal mechanism — it does not adjudicate anything.
