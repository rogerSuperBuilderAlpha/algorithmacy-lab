## What it argues

The Dreyfus brothers describe how a person becomes expert at a skilled activity and argue that the process runs *away* from rules rather than toward them. Five stages: the **novice** follows context-free rules on recognized features; the **advanced beginner** starts to recognize situational aspects from experience; the **competent** performer adopts a plan or perspective to manage the flood of relevant information and becomes emotionally invested in outcomes; the **proficient** performer sees situations holistically and intuitively but still deliberates about action; and the **expert** no longer solves problems at all, responding fluidly and intuitively out of a vast store of discriminated situations. Chess, driving, aircraft piloting, and nursing (through Benner's uptake) are the standing cases. The polemical target is artificial intelligence: because expertise is not rule-following, a rule-based machine cannot reproduce it, and the attempt to elicit an expert's rules yields the novice's rules rather than the expert's competence.

## Relation to the argument

This is the strongest single challenge to §8's word choice, and the paper should meet it head on. §8 asserts that "skill is trainable by instruction," and the most-cited theory of *skill acquisition* in the literature says the opposite: instruction carries a learner through the first two stages and no further, and everything above advanced beginner comes from accumulated concrete experience that cannot be given as rules. On the Dreyfus account, "acquired through participation rather than instruction" is the definition of skill above the novice level, not a contrast with it. The paper's premise therefore does not survive contact with the skill literature, and a reviewer who teaches this model will say so. The repair is available and does not cost the argument anything: keep "competency" for the reasons that hold — the unit is the person in a role, the thing is a composite rather than a technique, and it is indexed to a coordination form rather than to a tool — and drop the instruction premise, which is doing no work that tool-indexing does not do better. The model also supplies §11 with a developmental scale for the sixteen-week cohort, and a prediction worth testing: if the gate's rule changes under the participant, she is repeatedly returned to advanced-beginner conditions, which is a mechanism for stratified fluency that does not require individual difference.

## Caution

A trade book advancing a philosophical argument against symbolic AI, with the five-stage model presented as phenomenological description rather than as a validated developmental sequence; empirical support comes largely from later adopters, principally Benner in nursing, and the model has been criticized for the vagueness of its stage boundaries and for resting on retrospective self-report. The precursor is the 1980 University of California, Berkeley, Operations Research Center report for the Air Force Office of Scientific Research, which has no DOI or ISBN and circulates in several scanned versions — cite the 1986 book. Metadata-level read; verify the stage descriptions against a copy before quoting, since the 1980 report and the 1986 book word them differently.
