## What it argues

The AILit Framework is the most institutionally consequential document in this cluster, and the reason is buried on its welcome page: it "contributes to the PISA 2029 Media & Artificial Intelligence Literacy assessment." Whatever definition survives here will be the one thirty-odd countries measure their fifteen-year-olds against. It also sits downstream of the EU AI Act, whose **Article 4 requires providers and deployers of AI systems to ensure that their staff, and anyone using the systems on their behalf, have an adequate level of AI literacy** — a legal obligation with no agreed operationalisation, which the framework is partly written to supply.

The definition: "AI literacy represents the technical knowledge, durable skills, and future-ready attitudes required to thrive in a world influenced by AI. It enables learners to engage, create with, manage, and design AI, while critically evaluating its benefits, risks, and ethical implications." Four domains — **Engaging with AI**, **Creating with AI**, **Managing AI**, **Designing AI** — encompass **22 competences**, each specified through knowledge, skills and attitudes and each accompanied by primary and secondary learning scenarios. (The project website renders the fourth domain as "Shape AI"; the review draft says Designing AI.) The framework builds on the EU AI Act, OECD, UNESCO and DigComp 2.2, and an international expert group informed the draft. Over 2,000 educators and policymakers responded to the consultation; a final version followed in 2026.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour, and the most important one for the manuscript's *stakes* rather than its logic. If PISA measures this construct in 2029, then "AI literacy" is what the word will mean for a generation, and the paper's claim that nobody has named the coordinated party's competency needs to be made now, against this text.

The four domains are four relations between a person and a system, and the draft says so in its own descriptions. Engaging with AI is "using AI as a tool to access new content, information, or recommendations." Creating with AI is "collaborating with an AI system in a creative or problem-solving process." Managing AI is "intentionally choosing how AI can support and enhance human work" — delegating tasks, guiding outputs, assessing "whether AI's role aligns with their goals and values," so that "AI works for them." Designing AI is understanding how systems work in order to shape them. Every verb takes AI as its object. No competence concerns another person whom the same system is reading, and none concerns a determination the system commits that binds two parties.

The **Managing AI** domain is where the paper should press, because its name promises the most and it delivers a picture that inverts algorithmacy exactly. The learner delegates; the AI executes; the learner retains judgement, empathy and creativity; agency is built by ensuring "AI works for them." That is the algorithm as an **agent serving the worker** — the third row in §9's sorting, drawn straight from AI-mediated communication — and it is what algorithmacy is not. The coordinated party does not delegate to the intermediary. The intermediary was there first, it serves the platform, and its determinations arrive whether or not she has assessed their alignment with her values.

Where "others" enter, they enter as an impact category. The attitudes section asks for "an awareness of AI's impact on themselves and others," which is moral consideration of third parties, not a position in a relation. That distinction is worth stating explicitly in §9, because it is the form most objections will take: the frameworks do mention other people, and they mention them as objects of concern rather than as parties to a determination.

One thing the manuscript should take from AILit and use: **EU AI Act Article 4 has created a legal duty of AI literacy for deployers and their staff, with no construct adequate to it.** The duty falls on organisations that deploy systems, which is exactly the setting where a worker is coordinated with counterparties through an intermediary. A construct built for fifteen-year-olds engaging, creating, managing and designing does not measure whether a deployer's staff can operate under a system that binds them and someone else. That gap is a practical argument for algorithmacy and it is stronger than any theoretical one.

## Caution

**Review draft, May 2025, explicitly provisional** — the document says it is "intended to elicit feedback from educators and stakeholders," and a final version followed after consultation with more than 2,000 respondents, so the 22 competences and the domain names may have changed. I read the draft, not the final. No DOI exists for the draft; it carries no ISBN in the copy I read and is verified only against its own title and copyright pages, so treat the citation as unverified against any registry. Grey literature throughout: no peer review, no empirical basis, no instrument, no validation. Scope is primary and secondary education; the framework says nothing about workers, and the Article 4 connection is the framework's own citation of the AI Act, not evidence that anyone has operationalised the duty. The claim that "Shape AI" and "Designing AI" name the same domain rests on comparing the project website against the draft PDF and should be checked against the final version before either name is quoted.
