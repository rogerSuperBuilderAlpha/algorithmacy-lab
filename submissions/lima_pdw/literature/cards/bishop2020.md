## What it argues

The market that grows on top of algorithmic gossip. Bishop examines self-styled algorithmic experts who sell advice about YouTube's recommender — courses, consultancies, channels — and shows that the authority they claim rests on lore rather than access, since none of them can see the system either. The expertise is performed and monetised, and it circulates advice whose correctness nobody can check.

## Relation to the argument

The commercial counterpart to §10's implicit-acquisition property and its most awkward case. Section 10 says algorithmacy develops through participation and not through instruction, with the structural reason that the rule is undisclosed so there is nothing to teach. Bishop documents a functioning industry that teaches it anyway. The property survives — what is sold is lore, not the rule, so the market's existence does not show the rule is teachable — but the survival needs saying, because the falsifier §10 names (a cohort taught explicitly scoring as well as a cohort that only used the system) has a ready supply of subjects in this market and a reviewer will notice.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth here and at the lab card. Content-creator economy, not paid platform work with a counterpart. No measurement of whether the advice sold improves outcomes, which is exactly what §10's falsifier would need; cite for the existence of the market, not for its efficacy.
