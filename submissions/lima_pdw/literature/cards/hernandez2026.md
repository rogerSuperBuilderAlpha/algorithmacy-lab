## What it argues

Hernandez and colleagues study what gig drivers know that the platform does not. Twenty-five semi-structured interviews with rideshare and delivery drivers yield three knowledge practices. Drivers **adapt** platform systems to local conditions, closing the gap between a globally standardised interface and a particular city's streets, traffic and demand. They develop **algorithmic-local knowledge** by fusing what they infer about platform behaviour with what they know about local market dynamics — a hybrid the authors treat as irreducible to either component. And they **strategically regulate knowledge exchange with peers**, deciding what to share and what to withhold in driver networks.

The framing is infrastructural rather than psychological. The smooth functioning of the gig economy depends on local execution of globally assigned tasks, and the authors argue that drivers' knowledge practices are what bridges global standards and local conditions — the labour of localisation that platforms rely on and do not compensate. The third practice is the one that distinguishes the paper from earlier folk-theory work: knowledge here is a social good with circulation rules, not a private cognitive achievement.

## Relation to the argument

**Neighbour, and the sharpest available evidence for stratified fluency.** The peer-regulation finding is directly on the manuscript's §10 property: if drivers deliberately choose what algorithmic knowledge to circulate, then equivalently placed workers end up with unequal knowledge by a documented mechanism, and the distribution is not an artifact of tenure alone. The manuscript already concedes that a friend telling a worker what the gate rewards is implicit acquisition operating socially rather than a refutation of it; Hernandez et al. supply the fieldwork for that concession, and it should be cited there rather than left as an aside.

The construct is not a rival. "Knowledge practices" is a practice-theoretic description, not a named competency, and the authors do not propose a measure or claim to have named what the arrangement demands. The counterpart is absent in the usual way — the passenger appears as a destination and a rating, never as a party the driver coordinates with through the system. Algorithmic-local knowledge is knowledge *about* the algorithm crossed with knowledge *about* the city; neither term is knowledge about how to reach another person through the algorithm.

## Caution

Twenty-five interviews, US rideshare and delivery, qualitative; the three practices are analytic categories from coding, not dimensions of an instrument. Abstract-depth read only — the recruitment, platforms, cities and coding procedure were not confirmed against the text, so cite the three practices and the localisation argument, not the method. Crossref lists volume 35, issue 1 with **no page range** as of retrieval; the article is online-first and the locators may change. The 2026 date makes this too recent to have accumulated critique, and a CSCW venue means an OS/OT roundtable may not know it.
