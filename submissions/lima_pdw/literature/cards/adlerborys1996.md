## What it argues

Adler and Borys set out to reconcile the two verdicts organization theory had reached about bureaucracy — that it alienates employees, and that it helps them do their work. Their move is to read workflow formalization as an organizational *technology* and to import the equipment-design distinction between systems that deskill users and systems that support them. Formalization then comes in two types. **Enabling** formalization gives employees the means to master their tasks better; **coercive** formalization is a device by which management extracts effort and compliance. Whether formalization's effect on employee attitudes is positive or negative depends on which type is in force. They name forces that push organizations toward the coercive orientation and countertendencies that sustain the enabling one, and suggest the typology extends beyond workflow rules to internal labor markets, hierarchy, and staff functions.

## Relation to the argument

The best available support for §4's hierarchy cell, and a check on how it is worded. Adler and Borys make explicit what Simon's decision premises imply: a well-designed hierarchy equips the subordinate rather than merely constraining her, and rules that do this are rules a competent employee can read, understand, and use to diagnose her own situation. §4 says hierarchy "economizes on what it demands"; the enabling type is the mechanism of that economy — the organization does the reconstruction work and hands over the result as a rule.

The typology also gives the fourth column a sharper contrast than "hierarchy versus platform." Coercive formalization at least tells the worker the rule while withholding the reasons; algorithmic direction withholds both. On this scale co-optation is not the coercive end of a bureaucratic continuum but off the scale entirely, since there is no formalization for the worker to read at all. That distinction is worth a sentence in §5.
