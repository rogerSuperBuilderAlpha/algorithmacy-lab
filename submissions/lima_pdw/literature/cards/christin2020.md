## What it argues

Christin's methodological reply to the black-box framing. Rather than treating opacity as an obstacle to be defeated, she argues algorithms can be explicitly enrolled in ethnographic research, which then illuminates unexpected aspects of algorithmic systems including their opacity. She sets out three strategies: **algorithmic refraction**, examining the reconfigurations that occur when computational software, people and institutions interact; **algorithmic comparison**, using similarity and difference across sites to identify an instrument's distinctive features; and **algorithmic triangulation**, enrolling algorithms to help gather rich qualitative data.

## Relation to the argument

**Refraction** is the concept §7 most needs and does not have. It says the algorithmic system and the social context it enters reconfigure each other, so what a worker confronts is never the model but the model as refracted through her institution, her client base, her local market. That has a direct consequence for the derivation: the "rule" asymmetric interpretation reconstructs is the refracted rule, not the code, which is why two workers on the same platform in different cities can both be right and disagree. Section 7's checkability clause and §10's same-withholdings assumption both need this qualification, and refraction supplies the vocabulary.

**Comparison** is also §11's method stated abstractly. Section 10 buys internal comparability by holding one cohort under one gate; Christin's second strategy is the argument that a single site cannot isolate the instrument's features, so §11's design choice has a known cost and Christin names it.
