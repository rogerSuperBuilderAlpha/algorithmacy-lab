## What it argues
Romzek and Dubnick read the Challenger disaster as an accountability failure and build a typology to explain it. Two dimensions generate four systems: whether the source of control sits inside or outside the agency, and whether the degree of control over agency action is high or low. Bureaucratic accountability runs on internal, high control — supervision and rules. Legal accountability runs on external, high control — an outside party with a mandate, enforced through contracts, audits and courts. Professional accountability runs on internal, low control — deference to expertise, the engineer's judgement. Political accountability runs on external, low control — responsiveness to constituencies. Their reading of NASA is that the agency's dominant system shifted away from professional toward political and bureaucratic accountability under pressure to deliver a routine launch schedule, and that the shift is what stripped engineering objections of standing. The general claim is that organisations operate under several accountability systems at once, that those systems make incompatible demands, and that failure often traces to which system prevailed rather than to an absence of accountability altogether.

## Relation to the argument
The typology names the *kinds* of forum §6's institutional route could reach, and shows they are not interchangeable. Statutory review is legal accountability. A designed in-platform appeal is bureaucratic. A worker forum or union is closer to political. Professional accountability — the type running on internal deference to expertise — is the one a highly skilled worker might be thought to supply herself, and Romzek and Dubnick's own case is why that hope is thin. The Challenger engineers held the expertise and exercised the professional judgement; what they lacked was a system in which their objection carried standing against the schedule. That is §5's claim evidenced from a canonical public-administration case rather than from platform work, and it is worth having, because it shows the claim is not an artefact of studying gig labour. Even the accountability type that most resembles individual competence turns out to be a *system*, and it fails when the system around it changes.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and citing literature; the four-cell rendering above is the standard summary, and pinpoint claims about the Challenger analysis should be checked against the original before quotation. The typology is contested — later work argues the two dimensions blur, and that "internal" professional accountability is not a forum relation in Bovens's sense at all. That criticism runs in the paper's favour and should be acknowledged rather than suppressed. Public agencies are the unit of analysis; platforms are not.
