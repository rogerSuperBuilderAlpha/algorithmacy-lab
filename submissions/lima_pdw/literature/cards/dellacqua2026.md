## What it argues

The paper introduces the **"jagged technology frontier"** — **"the uneven impact of artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities, where AI assistance improves performance for some tasks but worsens it for others, even within the same knowledge workflow and with a seemingly similar level of difficulty."** The preregistered experiment ran with Boston Consulting Group and **758 knowledge workers**, randomised after a baseline to no AI, GPT-4, or GPT-4 plus a prompt-engineering overview.

Two results, in opposite directions, on either side of the frontier. Inside it, across **18 realistic knowledge tasks** "ranging from creative to analytical," subjects using AI **"completed 12.2% more tasks and completed them 25.1% more quickly on average while also delivering solutions of significantly improved quality."** Outside it, on **"a complex managerial task selected to be outside the frontier, subjects using AI were 19% less likely to produce correct solutions compared with those without AI."**

The unit of the heterogeneity is the task, not the worker. Whether AI helps or hurts is a property of where the work sits relative to a capability boundary that is invisible and irregular from the worker's side.

## Relation to the argument

This is the second rival explanation §6 names — **divergence by task** — and it is the strongest available statement of it, in the same journal family the manuscript is writing for and with a co-author (Kellogg) whose algorithmic-control work the paper already cites. Two drivers on the same platform in the same week are not doing the same task mix; a jagged frontier means that alone could produce the observed spread without any competency difference. §1 cannot assert unexplained variance without disposing of this, and §6's "task mix" clause is where it belongs.

It also does positive work for the manuscript's own construct. The frontier is described as invisible and irregular from inside the workflow — the worker cannot tell in advance which side of it a task falls on. That is a structural withholding of *predictability* in exactly §5's sense, and it is measured rather than asserted. Used that way, the paper supports the demand side of algorithmacy even while it undercuts the residual-variance premise.

## Caution

A laboratory-in-the-field experiment with consultants at one elite firm on artificial tasks; external validity to platform drivers is a stretch and should be flagged if invoked. The 19% figure applies to a **single** deliberately chosen out-of-frontier task, not to a class of tasks — do not generalise it into "AI harms complex work." The paper reports no earnings or attrition outcomes. The oft-repeated "centaurs and cyborgs" typology comes from the 2023 HBS working paper (24-013) and its framing shifted on the way to print; cite the *Organization Science* version of record, 2026, not the working paper year. Do not cite this for worker-level heterogeneity: the abstract's heterogeneity claim is about tasks.
