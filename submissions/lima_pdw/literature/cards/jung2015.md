## What it argues

Jung, Martelaro and Hinds put a robot in a *human-human* conflict and ask whether it can repair it. Pairs worked on a task designed to produce interpersonal violations; a robot intervened with conflict-repair utterances directed at the violation between the two people. The intervention changed the interpersonal dynamic — participants' perceptions of each other and of the interaction shifted — and the effect depended on how the robot's intervention was framed.

The design is the point: the machine's target is not the user's relationship *with the machine* but the two users' relationship *with each other*.

## Relation to the argument

**A machine occupying the mediator position between two humans, experimentally, in 2015 — and it is the cleanest empirical counterexample to §9 as written.** Whatever the five constructs do, the human-robot interaction community has built and tested a machine third party whose function is to act on a relation between two other parties. §9's claim that a construct with two positions "will always be describing a projection" is fine as a methodological point but cannot be paired with the implication that nobody has built the three-position case.

The boundary the paper keeps is authority. Jung's robot **intervenes**; it does not **determine**. Its utterances are proposals the humans may take or ignore, its role is disclosed, and nothing it does binds either party. The platform gate's output is not a proposal. Once §9 states its criterion as *binding determination* rather than *third position*, this study becomes a supporting citation — evidence that the mediator seat can be machine-occupied, with the paper's contribution being what happens when the occupant decides.

Jung's later work with Sebo and colleagues (doi:10.1145/3415247, doi:10.1073/pnas.1910402117) extends the programme to robots in groups.
