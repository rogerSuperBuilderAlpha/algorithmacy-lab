## What it argues

The AI-MC programme's largest study, across two experiments. Using AI-generated smart replies made conversations faster, more positive, and improved partners' evaluations of each other — but when a participant was *suspected* of using AI, the partner evaluated them more negatively and the relationship suffered. The authors' summary: AI assistance helps until it is detected, and the penalty falls on the person suspected, not on the system.

The asymmetry is the finding. The benefit accrues to the interaction; the cost accrues to an individual, on the basis of a suspicion that may be wrong.

## Relation to the argument

The strongest evidence available that **a system's involvement in a coordination gets attributed to a party**, and that the attribution is consequential for that party's standing with the other. That is a mechanism the paper needs and does not have. §5 argues co-optation withholds accountability; Hohenstein et al. show empirically what fills the vacuum — the counterpart attributes the system's contribution to the worker and penalises her for it, with no route for her to contest the attribution because the suspicion is never stated.

It also sharpens the **asymmetric interpretation** facet. §7 has the worker inferring backward from an outcome to an unstated rule. Here the outcome is a counterpart's evaluation, and part of what she must infer is what the counterpart believed about the system's role in her work — a second-order inference the facet's current definition does not quite reach.

Nine authors, so a substantial and well-resourced study; the correction is metadata only.

## Caution

Laboratory and online conversation tasks with strangers, smart replies rather than the generative systems now in use, and self-report evaluations. The relationship outcomes are short-horizon. Do not extend the suspicion penalty to work settings without saying it is an extrapolation. Cite all nine authors or use "Hohenstein et al." — never "Hohenstein & Jung 2023," which is the 2020 paper.
