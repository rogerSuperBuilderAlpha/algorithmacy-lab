## What it argues

The measurement paper. Ritter, Wilkinson and Johnston argue that a firm's ability "to develop and manage relations with key suppliers, customers and other organizations and to deal effectively with the interactions among these relations is a core competence of a firm — one that has a direct bearing on a firm's competitive strength and performance." The first half describes the German scale-development and calibration work; the second reports preliminary studies testing the scale's validity in an English-speaking context. Two findings carry: the scale travels — the same relations between network competence and performance found in the German data hold — and network competence is "empirically and conceptually distinct" from the market orientation scale.

## Relation to the argument

This is the card that closes the precedent argument in §4. A reviewer can grant that Ritter and Gemünden named a capacity and still doubt that naming it produced anything more than a label; discriminant validity against market orientation, replicated across two language communities, is the evidence that the naming produced a construct. It also supplies the exact template the co-optation column would have to follow — conceptualize, scale, calibrate, then show discriminant validity against the nearest existing construct — which is worth stating in §12 as what this paper has not yet done.

The market-orientation contrast is doing double duty. The one thing the network-competence programme had to distinguish itself from was the market's own firm-level construct, and it did so by showing the two are not the same thing. That is a small piece of evidence for §4's claim that the market column has no participant-competence construct of the same kind.

## Caution

Read at abstract depth only, so treat the psychometrics as unverified: I have not seen the item pool, the factor structure, or the fit statistics. "International evidence" here means Germany plus an English-speaking sample described as preliminary, which is a thin base for a generality claim. Same level-of-analysis caveat as the rest of this cluster — the competence measured belongs to a firm, not to a coordinated individual.
