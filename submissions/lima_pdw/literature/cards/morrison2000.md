## What it argues
Morrison and Milliken argue that silence in organisations is a collective, systemic phenomenon rather than an aggregation of individual reticence. Their construct — organisational silence — names a shared condition in which employees withhold information about problems, and they locate its causes in management beliefs and structures rather than in employee character: the belief that employees are self-interested, that management knows best, and that dissent signals disunity. Those beliefs produce structures and practices — centralised decision-making, absent formal upward-feedback channels, hostile responses to negative information — which employees read accurately, and from which they infer that speaking up is dangerous and futile. The paper's decisive move is the pair of employee beliefs it puts at the centre: silence follows when employees conclude that speaking up carries risk *and* that it will not change anything. Both are inferences about the organisation, not dispositions of the person. The authors argue this produces a climate of silence that is self-reinforcing, blocks error correction and organisational learning, and leaves management systematically uninformed about the problems it most needs to hear about.

## Relation to the argument
This is the organisational-behaviour statement of §5's asymmetry, and it is written from inside the literature a reviewer is most likely to hold the paper to. The pair of beliefs — risk and futility — are inferences about whether a forum will receive what is brought to it, and Morrison and Milliken argue that no amount of individual willingness overcomes them, because they are read off structures the individual does not control. That is a second discipline arriving at §5's conclusion by a different route. The futility belief in particular is the mechanism that converts a *present* channel into an unused one, which is precisely what the paper's first participant interview records: two channels open, neither judged appropriate, no contest attempted.

For §6 the article cuts both ways and should be used carefully. Its remedy is managerial — change the beliefs, build the channels, reward dissent — which is an institutional route, consistent with the paper's division. But it is a route the platform has no counterpart for, since there is no management holding a relationship with the worker in which trust could be rebuilt. The paper can use that gap: silence research assumes an employment relation in which a forum could be constructed and is not, whereas co-optation forecloses the relation itself.

## Caution
Reconstructed from the abstract and citing literature; the article is conceptual, not empirical, and its propositions have been tested unevenly since. Its unit is the organisation and its subject is employees inside an employment relation, so transfer to platform work needs argument. "Silence" in this literature means withholding information about *problems the organisation could fix*, which overlaps but does not coincide with declining to contest a decision that has already gone against you — for that, Pinder and Harlos and the disputing literature are closer.
