## What it argues

Qualitative thematic analysis of open-ended answers from a 2019 representative survey of Norwegians, yielding five folk theories: algorithms are **confining, practical, reductive, intangible, and exploitative**. Ytre-Arne and Moe read the affective residue as **digital irritation** rather than the digital resignation the surveillance literature expects, and argue that irritation carries a small but real potential to motivate political action against datafication.

## Relation to the argument

The population-level counterweight to the small-N folk-theory studies, and the source that keeps §10's stratified-fluency claim honest. A nationally representative survey shows the theories are widespread rather than the property of engaged or professional users, which supports the claim that this is a general capacity. But the five theories are *evaluative stances* — confining, practical, exploitative — not accounts of how the system decides. Nobody in this corpus is reconstructing a rule. That is a useful boundary for §7: holding a folk theory of algorithms in Ytre-Arne and Moe's sense is not the same as doing asymmetric interpretation, and a scale that measured the first would not measure the second.

The irritation-not-resignation finding also bears on §6's second route. If opacity generates irritation with political potential rather than passive acceptance, the institutional route has a constituency, which is what §6's claim about accountability substitutes requires.

## Caution

Open-ended survey answers analysed thematically; no behaviour, no accuracy criterion, no stakes. One country with unusually high digitisation and unusually high institutional trust, which plausibly shapes both the theories and the irritation. The five theories are the authors' inductive categories from one instrument and have not been replicated. Read at abstract depth.
