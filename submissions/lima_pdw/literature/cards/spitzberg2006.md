**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/j.1083-6101.2006.00030.x  ·  **Read depth:** full_text  ·  **Source read:** Oxford Academic (JCMC archive)
**Source-tier:** primary_journal
**Evidence basis:** direct_read
**Cluster:** communication-competence

## What it argues

Spitzberg extends the relational competence model to mediated interaction and builds the first CMC competence measure. The model keeps its architecture — motivation, knowledge, skills, context, outcomes — and adds media-specific parameters: **efficiency**, **appropriateness**, **effectiveness**, **satisfaction**, and coordination across the medium's constraints. Knowledge is respecified as media knowledge (what this channel affords, what its conventions are) alongside content and relational knowledge. Skills gain a media-management component. The measure is developed on student samples and reported explicitly as preliminary.

The theoretical claim is that mediation changes the *parameters* of competence — what a channel affords, what it costs to use, what conventions govern it — without changing the structure, because the parties on either end are still the coordinators.

## Relation to the argument

§9's "channel" row, and the paper's characterisation is accurate rather than a caricature. Spitzberg's medium genuinely is a conduit with properties: it constrains, it filters, it imposes conventions, and competence is the ability to work well through it. Nothing in the model gives the medium a stake, a judgement, or an output of its own.

§8 also calls this "the nearest prior case of a competence construct built for a mediated channel," and that is right — it is the only member of the competence lineage proper that addresses mediation at all, which makes it the paper's closest genuine predecessor and the one whose difference has to be stated most carefully. The difference is exactly the one §9 names: Spitzberg's medium carries; the paper's mediator **commits determinations binding on both parties**. A channel that decides is no longer a channel, and Spitzberg's parameters — efficiency, appropriateness, satisfaction — are all measured by the human partner, who in the paper's arrangement is unreachable.

## Caution

Preliminary by the author's own description: student samples, self-report, exploratory factor structure, and the CMC of 2006 is email, IM, and discussion boards. Do not cite it as a validated instrument. This card carries no item count, no N, and no reliabilities, and I could not re-verify them — Oxford Academic and the Wiley legacy page both refused retrieval on re-check, and the item counts circulating in the citing literature (a 23-item five-point scale covering knowledge, attentiveness, expressiveness, composure, effectiveness and clarity) describe *downstream adaptations* of Spitzberg's measure rather than the 2006 article's own reported instrument. Anyone citing the measure's properties in §8 or §11 must read the article first. Bunz & Montez's 2015 handbook chapter (doi:10.1515/9783110317459-008) is the later review of the area and should be checked for anything Spitzberg's 2006 model has since acquired.
