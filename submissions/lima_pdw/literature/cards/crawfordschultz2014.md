## What it argues
Crawford and Schultz argue that big-data analytics produces a class of harms existing privacy law cannot reach. The harm is *predictive*: an inference drawn about a person from data she never disclosed, often from data about other people, which then determines how she is treated. Notice-and-consent fails against it, because the person consented to nothing relevant and the inference did not exist when the data was collected; and the harms are not the disclosure harms privacy tort recognises. Their proposal borrows from procedural due process rather than from privacy: a right to *procedural data due process*, which would give a person subject to a consequential predictive determination notice that a prediction is being used, a statement of the type of prediction and the data categories behind it, an opportunity to be heard before a neutral arbiter, and a written record of the determination adequate for review. They are explicit that the right is procedural rather than substantive — it does not prohibit the prediction or dictate its content, it establishes an arrangement through which the determination can be challenged, and they extend it to private actors on the ground that the harm does not depend on whether the state is involved.

## Relation to the argument
This article is the paper's best legal source for the claim that *procedure*, not information, is the missing element, and it draws the same line §5 draws. Crawford and Schultz surveyed the available remedies for opaque predictive determinations, found notice and consent insufficient, and concluded that what has to be added is a hearing before a neutral arbiter — a second party with standing. Their explicit refusal to make the right substantive is the point to lift: they are not asking for better predictions or more disclosure, they are asking for a forum, because they judge that nothing given to the individual alone repairs the harm.

Their neutral-arbiter requirement also sharpens §6 against the reading in which a platform's own appeal counts. An arbiter routed through the party that made the determination is not neutral, and by their standard supplies nothing. That is the criterion the paper needs to distinguish the two channels in the live interview case from a forum.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth only; do not quote, and verify pagination against the published volume, since no DOI exists. This is a legal proposal, not law — data due process has not been adopted, and citing it as an existing protection would misstate the position. The setting is US law, and the extension to private actors is the authors' argument, not settled doctrine. Their subject is predictive determinations about individuals, principally in consumer and government contexts; algorithmic management of workers is not their case, and a rating that continuously governs a worker's earnings differs from a one-off predictive determination in ways the framework does not address. Note that this Crawford is Kate Crawford; the dissertation library also holds a Margaret Crawford under `crawford1995`.
