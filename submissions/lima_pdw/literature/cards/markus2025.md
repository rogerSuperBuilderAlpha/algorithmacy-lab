## What it argues

Markus and colleagues attack the self-report problem head-on. Every instrument before AICOS — MAILS, SNAIL, Wang et al., Pinski and Benlian — asks people how competent they think they are, and MAILS's own validation had found AI literacy scores correlating suspiciously highly with self-efficacy. AICOS replaces agreement ratings with a knowledge test: multiple-choice items with one correct answer, administered with instructions not to use aids, so the score reflects what the respondent knows rather than what she believes about herself.

The construction is laborious. The authors screened existing instruments and online learning platforms for items, assembled an initial pool of 282, translated where necessary into German, and applied coding rules for content redundancy and psychometric weakness across two rating rounds (Cohen's κ good in round one, consensus in round two). They then wrote theory-based items to fill underrepresented sub-competencies — four for Create AI, one for Detect AI, five for AI Ethics, eighteen for Generative AI — giving a 107-item test pool sorted into six sub-competencies: Know & Understand AI, Use & Apply AI, Detect AI, Create AI, AI Ethics, and Generative AI literacy as a distinct dimension. Testing used N = 514 Prolific participants (273 male, 235 female, 6 diverse), mean completion time 62.78 minutes, paid £7.20/hour. Item response theory analysis retained **51 items** in the final pool; the 3PL model fit best (χ² = 1287.14, df = 1224, p = .102, RMSEA = .010, SRMR = .068, CFI = .980, TLI = .979). An **18-item short version** follows. Concurrent validity comes from participants with a computer science background outscoring those without (F(1, 512) = 17.45, p < .001), and convergent validity from correlation with MAILS-10.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour, and the most sophisticated instrument in the cluster — which is why its failure to contain a counterpart is the most decisive.

AICOS's six sub-competencies are the Ng et al. four with Detect AI and Generative AI added. Every item is a knowledge question about AI systems: what they are, how they process, what generative models can do, where the ethical risks lie. There is no item about another person, no item in which a determination binds two parties, and no item that could be answered differently depending on who the respondent is coordinated with. A knowledge test *cannot* contain a counterpart, structurally — a multiple-choice item with one correct answer requires that the right response be the same for every respondent, and coordinating with a specific counterparty through a specific system produces no such invariant. That is a stronger version of §9's structural claim than anything about item lists: objectifying algorithmic competence into testable knowledge is precisely what forecloses the relational position.

The competency/skill discrimination lands cleanly. AICOS calls itself a competency scale and measures skill — declarative knowledge, teachable by instruction, dating with the tool. The eighteen items written for Generative AI make the tool-indexing visible: a construct whose contents required eighteen new items in 2025 to cover a technology from 2022 will require another tranche in 2028. Algorithmacy's claim is that competency dates with the *form*, not the tool. AICOS is the counterexample that shows what tool-dating looks like in practice.

Implicit acquisition also fails, and here the failure is diagnostic. AICOS assumes the capacity it measures is articulable and thus testable; algorithmacy's implicit-acquisition property says the capacity develops through participation under an undisclosed rule, which means the holder cannot state what she knows. A driver who reliably anticipates a dispatch algorithm's behaviour would score no better on AICOS than one who does not, because AICOS asks how gradient descent works. That gap is the manuscript's opening — and it is worth stating as a testable prediction rather than an assertion.

One thing AICOS gives §10 for free: the authors report systematic score differences by gender, age, occupational background and educational qualification, and the companion short-version paper (Markus, Carolus & Wienrich, 2026, *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans* 8, 100298, doi:10.1016/j.chbah.2026.100298) provides population norms for exactly that reason. Unevenness is real and the field measures it. But it is unevenness across *differently* placed people, which is not stratified fluency. Stratified fluency is unevenness across **equivalently placed** workers, and no instrument in this literature is designed to detect it.

## Caution

I read the arXiv preprint (2503.12921, March 2025), not the *CAEAI* version of record published in December 2025; the 51-item and 18-item counts, the fit statistics and the sample figures are the preprint's and may have changed in review. Verify before quoting a number. N = 514 German-speaking Prolific participants only, with 38% currently studying — a sample the authors themselves flag as limited in representativeness. The scale is German-language; no English validation. Concurrent validity rests on a computer-science-background contrast, which is a weak criterion (it may index education generally). Nothing links AICOS scores to any work outcome, so do not cite it for what AI competence does at work. Sixty-three minutes of testing time makes it impractical for field research on platform workers, which the manuscript should note if it ever proposes a discriminant-validity study.
