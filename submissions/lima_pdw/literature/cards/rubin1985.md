# Rubin, R. B. (1985). The validity of the communication competency assessment instrument. *Communication Monographs*, 52(2), 173–185.

## What it argues

The validation study for the CCAI. Rubin assembles construct-validity evidence across samples: the instrument converges with instructor and peer ratings of competence, discriminates from general verbal ability and from academic achievement, and shows the expected developmental pattern across course exposure. She is candid about the weak points — rater training costs, the dependence of scores on task selection, and the fact that criterion-referenced mastery scoring makes conventional reliability coefficients awkward to interpret.

## Relation to the argument

The template for the discriminant work §9 owes and §11 licenses. The paper's problem with Zhou et al. (2025) is not that the rival scale is bad but that no evidence yet separates the two constructs. Rubin's design shows what separation looks like when done properly: convergence with an independent judgement of the same thing, discrimination from the adjacent thing it is most likely to be confused with, and a predicted pattern of variation that a confound would not produce.

Applied to algorithmacy, that maps onto three specific studies §11 could name: convergence with an independent rating of coordination outcomes under the gate; discrimination from general AI literacy and from platform tenure; and the §10 falsifier — a taught cohort against a participation-only cohort — as the predicted pattern. Naming Rubin makes those look like standard practice rather than a defensive scramble.

## Caution

Mid-1980s validation standards; MacKenzie, Podsakoff & Podsakoff (2011) and Lambert & Newman (2023), already in the paper's reference set, are the current authorities and should carry the methodological citations. Rubin is useful as the communication discipline's own precedent for taking validation seriously in a competence construct, which is a rhetorical rather than a technical use.
