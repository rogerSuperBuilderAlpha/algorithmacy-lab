## What it argues

Chopra and Haaland introduce an AI-assisted method for conducting semi-structured qualitative interviews at scale and demonstrate it on the stock-market participation puzzle with **395 interviews**. Their claim is that the approach keeps the depth of qualitative interviewing while producing a corpus large enough for quantitative analysis, and that it yields richer and more robust conclusions than either a conventionally sized qualitative study or a single open-ended survey question. They also report high interviewee satisfaction — a majority of respondents stated a strict preference for the AI-assisted interview over a human-led one.

## Relation to the argument

The preference finding is the one that matters here, and it cuts against the intuition a reviewer will bring. If participants prefer an AI interviewer, the instrument may *reduce* rather than increase one of this study's specific risks: participants managing the expectations of an instructor who is in the room. An agent interviewer, with consent held by a third party and the transcript reviewed by the participant before submission, puts more distance between the student and her teacher than a face-to-face interview would. That is a defensible design argument and this is the paper that supports it.

## Caution

**Unpublished.** As of 18 August 2026 it exists only as a working paper (SSRN/CESifo/CEBI) with no journal DOI, and an OS/OT reviewer may discount it accordingly — cite it as a working paper and say so. It is also economics: 395 short interviews about stock-market participation, analysed quantitatively, which is nearly the opposite of a 45–60 minute interpretive interview about a contested gate outcome. The satisfaction result is self-reported and its participants were not talking to an instrument built by someone with power over them.
