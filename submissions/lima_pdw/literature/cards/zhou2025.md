## What it argues

**"A two-wave survey of 213 online platform workers in China"** models four dimensions of algorithmic management — **"algorithmic recommending, restricting, evaluating, and rewarding"** — against two opposed outcomes, perceived work overload and customer-oriented service behaviour. The mediator is attribution, and it is the whole argument.

**"Algorithmic recommending and rewarding improve customer-oriented service behavior and reduce work overload through AM commitment attributions. However, AM control attributions link algorithmic restricting, recommending, and evaluating (the latter two at low algorithmic transparency) to increased work overload. Algorithmic transparency moderates these effects, reducing the negative impacts of AM through AM control attributions."**

Read the recommending path twice. The *same* algorithmic operation raises service behaviour and cuts overload when the worker attributes it to commitment, and raises overload when she attributes it to control. One apparatus, one operation, opposite signs, and what switches the sign is the worker's construal — with transparency determining which construal forms.

## Relation to the argument

This is the wedge §1 needs and the only peer-reviewed study in this cluster that supplies it. Every other source here explains outcome divergence by something the worker *is* or the task *is*: prior skill, tenure, baseline performance, occupational exposure, position on a capability frontier. Zhou and colleagues explain it by something the worker *does* — how she reads what the system is doing to her. That is divergence by **construal** rather than by position, measured, mediated and moderated, on platform workers rather than consultants or students.

It also lands squarely on §5 and §6. Transparency is the moderator, which is to say the withheld condition is doing the work: where the platform supplies less understanding, the control attribution forms and the same instrument turns harmful. That is the displacement principle of §6 with a coefficient attached, and it is the empirical anchor for treating common understanding as individually suppliable while accountability is not.

Reference-list hygiene: never cite this as a bare "Zhou et al. 2025." An overlapping team published "Algorithmic competency of on-demand labor platform workers" in *Asia Pacific Journal of Human Resources* 63(2) the same year (10.1111/1744-7941.70004), and that paper is a rival scale, not a supporting one.

## Caution

Self-report on both sides at both waves — algorithmic management dimensions, attributions, overload and service behaviour all come from the worker, so common-method variance is a live threat and the "opposite outcomes" are perceptual, not administrative. N=213 in one country, two waves, cross-sectional in structure despite the temporal separation. Attribution is measured, not manipulated, so the causal direction between construal and outcome is not identified: workers who are already overloaded may read the algorithm as controlling. Outcomes are overload and service behaviour, **not earnings and not attrition** — so this cannot carry §1's specific sentence about earning differently and quitting at different rates. And construal is not competency: nothing here shows that the commitment attribution is a skill, is learnable, or is unevenly distributed by anything the manuscript would call algorithmacy.
