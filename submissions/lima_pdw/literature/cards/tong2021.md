## What it argues

The paper separates two effects of giving employees performance feedback generated by an AI system. The deployment effect is the gain: better data analytics may improve the feedback and so raise productivity. The disclosure effect is the loss: an employee who learns that the feedback came from an AI may view it negatively, and productivity may fall. The two effects are confounded whenever a firm introduces AI feedback and says so, and the paper's design unconfounds them in a field experiment that randomises, separately, whether the feedback an employee receives is generated by an AI system or by a human manager and whether the employee is told who the provider is. The research summary reports that both effects coexist and that the adverse disclosure effect is mitigated by the employee's tenure in the firm. As suggestive evidence from mediation analyses, disclosure lowers the employee's trust in the quality of the feedback and raises concern about being displaced by the technology, and both harm learning and performance. The managerial summary, as relayed, proposes a tiered use: AI feedback for veterans, human managers for novices.

## Relation to the argument

For `jitcar_post_ai_enterprise`, this is the empirical anchor for AI performance feedback inside the firm. The design is the point: the firm can deploy the evaluating system and separately decide whether the evaluated person is told, and the two decisions have opposite signs. That is a dyad, worker and system, with the firm holding the disclosure rule, and it shows that what the worker knows about the evaluator changes what the evaluation does. The tenure moderation is the arm's bridge to algorithmacy. A longer-tenured employee has more to read the feedback against, more experience of the job's own standards and of the firm's prior feedback, and the disclosure effect weakens with that experience. The arm can state this as the paper's finding and can say, as its own claim, that experience here is doing the work that a competence for reading an opaque evaluator would do. It should not say the paper measures such a competence; it measures tenure.

The limit for the triad is the one the OT hostile review already named: the study is a dyad. There is no counterpart whom the employee must coordinate with through the system, so the paper shows the evaluator's opacity mattering to one person and shows nothing about the second person. The arm should cite it for the firm-internal case and say where it stops.
