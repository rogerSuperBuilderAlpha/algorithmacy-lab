## What it argues

Gagrčin, Naab and Grub conducted a systematic integrative review of algorithm literacy across the social sciences and humanities, covering **169 studies**. They organise the field along two axes: how algorithm literacy has been conceptualised, and which endogenous, exogenous and personal factors shape it. Their diagnosis is blunt. Research on how individuals understand algorithms and their effects is expanding rapidly and **lacks a cohesive framework** — the constructs proliferate faster than they converge, and the measures do not talk to each other.

Their constructive proposal is that the existing work can be reframed as **experiential learning cycles**: people acquire algorithm literacy by encountering algorithmic outcomes, forming accounts of them, testing those accounts against further outcomes, and revising. The future agenda they set out has four items, and each is a confession about the present state of the field — define the core competencies relevant to algorithm literacy, standardise the measures, integrate the subjective and factual aspects (what people believe they understand versus what they can demonstrate), and develop task- and domain-specific approaches rather than general ones.

## Relation to the argument

**The single most useful resource in this cluster, and the closest thing the manuscript has to independent corroboration of its own premise.** The abstract's claim — "nobody has named it" — is strongest when it does not have to be defended by the person making it. Here three researchers, having read 169 studies, report that the field has no cohesive framework and that defining core competencies is *future* work. That is a citation the manuscript can put in §9 to convert a bold claim into a documented one, and it costs nothing.

Two further uses. Their experiential-learning framing is direct support for algorithmacy's **implicit acquisition** property: an entire review concludes that this literacy is acquired through encounter and revision rather than instruction, which is what the manuscript's §10 falsifier stakes itself on. And their call for **task- and domain-specific approaches** rather than general measures is a defence of the manuscript's decision to build an instrument for a specific arrangement instead of adapting a general literacy scale — a reviewer who asks "why not use Dogruel?" can be answered with the field's own review.

The review is also a warning. It maps a field that generates constructs prolifically, which means the manuscript's novelty claim is exposed not to any one rival but to the rate of production. Between the abstract's acceptance and Lima, at least three new named constructs appeared (Scolari et al.'s platform work literacies, Hong et al.'s contextual algorithmic skills, Ayasrah et al.'s metacognitive-algorithmic alignment). The claim should be stated as a claim about a *position* — nobody has built a competence construct with the counterpart in it — and not as a claim about a name, because names in this field are cheap and getting cheaper.

## Caution

A review, so it carries no primary evidence: cite it for the state of the field, not for any finding about users or workers. Its scope is *algorithmic media use* — recommendation, curation, news feeds — rather than algorithmic management of work, so its 169 studies underrepresent the labour literature where the manuscript's rivals sit, and it should not be cited as establishing that no *work*-specific competence construct exists. Abstract-depth read; the conceptualisation taxonomy and the endogenous/exogenous/personal factor scheme are named in the abstract but not detailed there, so do not reproduce the categories without the full text. Dating is awkward: Crossref records 2024 for the article with the 28(1) issue paginated 423–447 in 2026 — cite as 2024 with the volume, issue and pages, and expect variation across reference lists.
