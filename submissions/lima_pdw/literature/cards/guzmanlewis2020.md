## What it argues

People's interactions with AI — through virtual agents, socialbots and language-generation software — do not fit paradigms of communication theory built around human–human communication, and the article treats that misfit as the problem to solve. It sets out to articulate what distinguishes communicative AI from earlier technologies and offers human–machine communication (HMC) as the theoretical basis for studying it. The research agenda has three parts: the *functional* dimensions through which people make sense of these devices and applications as communicators; the *relational* dynamics through which people associate with the technologies and, through them, relate to themselves and others; and the *metaphysical* implications raised by blurring the ontological boundaries around what counts as human, machine and communication.

## Relation to the argument

§9 files this as the construct that makes the algorithm **a partner** — an interlocutor in its own right, studied as a human–machine dyad. The abstract carries that reading without strain, and the word "dyad" is close to the authors' own framing, since the relational dimension is about how people associate with the technologies. The paper is therefore a clean instance of §9's first diagnosis: the machine is moved onto one axis of a two-party relation, and the third position never appears. There is a sharper version of the point available. Guzman and Lewis's *functional* dimension asks how people make sense of these systems as communicators, which is a question about interpretation rather than about coordination — so HMC and algorithm sensemaking, two of §9's five, converge on interpretation from opposite directions and neither reaches a counterpart. Verdict: correctly placed and lightly cited; the paper is being used for its framing rather than its findings, which is the right weight for a research-agenda piece.

## Caution

Abstract-grounded. The article is an agenda-setting essay rather than an empirical study, so it carries no findings to misreport but also no evidence to lean on. One risk in §9's summary: HMC as Guzman and Lewis define it is a *field* proposal, broader than the machine-as-partner reading, and its metaphysical strand explicitly questions the boundaries the dyad diagnosis assumes are stable. A reviewer from that community may object that §9 characterizes HMC by its most tractable strand. The objection does not defeat the diagnosis — a research programme that questions ontological boundaries still positions the machine as the second party — but the sentence would be safer if it named HMC's dyadic *design* rather than implying the field has not noticed the question.
