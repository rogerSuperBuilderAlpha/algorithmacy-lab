## What it argues

An early experimental test of whether people evaluate a machine communicator differently from a human one. Participants viewed a Twitter feed attributed either to a person or to a bot and rated source credibility, communication competence, interactional intentions, and attraction. Differences were small: the bot was rated slightly lower on credibility and attraction, but participants judged the *communication quality* comparably and reported similar intentions to interact.

The finding is a specific instance of the Nass-and-Reeves pattern — that the source label matters less than the interaction — and it is one of the studies HMC cites to justify treating machines as communicators rather than as tools.

## Relation to the argument

Two uses, one of them awkward for the paper. **Competence is one of the dependent variables**: the study measures perceived communication competence of a machine communicator, which means HMC has in fact applied the competence construct to machines. §9 says the five constructs make the algorithm into things; here the algorithm is made into a *bearer* of competence, which is a sixth thing and one the section does not name. The distinction the paper needs is that this measures the machine's competence, not a human's competence at coordinating through a machine — a different explanandum, easily stated, and better stated than left for a reviewer.

Second, the near-parity finding is evidence for a rival mechanism behind §10's stratified fluency. If workers do not reliably distinguish machine from human sources, then part of what looks like differential competency may be differential ontological classification of the gate, which HMC would predict and algorithmacy does not currently address.

## Caution

Small experimental study, 2013 Twitter, student participants, single-message stimulus, tiny effects. It is a citation-classic in HMC because of timing rather than power. Four authors, two of them Edwards — Chad first, Autumn second.
