## What it argues

A doctoral student writing autoethnographically cannot be anonymous — the viva sees to that — and Doloriert and Sambrook build a fictionalised but evidenced account of the resulting bind: the pull toward disclosing a vulnerable self, and the institutional machinery that removes the protection anonymity would give. They develop relational ethics around self-disclosure and extend Medford's notion of "mindful slippage" as a strategy for removing personal material that could harm the writer or others.

## Relation to the argument

Directly relevant to the doctoral situation here. The investigator is identifiable by construction — one named programme, one named school, one instructor — so anything he writes about himself is attributable, and so is anything a participant says about him. Their relational-ethics frame is the right lens on `ANONYMIZATION.md` and on the protocol's rule that repository records are never used to contradict a participant to her face.

## Caution

Emerald venue, low citation weight, and the account is explicitly fictionalised. The paper's ethical exposure is mostly the *participants'*, not the author's, which is the reverse of this article's emphasis — read it for the mechanism, not for the priority.
