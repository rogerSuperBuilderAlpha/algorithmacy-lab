## What it argues

The founding text of online dispute resolution, and the source of the concept this cluster was searched for. Katsh and Rifkin argue that when dispute resolution moves online, the technology is not a neutral conduit for the process: it shapes what can be said, when, to whom, and with what consequences, and it does so with enough independent effect to warrant treating it as a party. They name it **"the fourth party"** — after the two disputants and the human third-party neutral — and argue that as the technology's capabilities grow, the fourth party takes over functions that previously belonged to the third, until in some designs there is no human neutral left and the system resolves the dispute itself.

The formulation is explicit: the fourth party can *displace* the third.

## Relation to the argument

**This is the answer to the critical question, and the paper must engage it.** Somebody in the mediated-communication literature has theorised a non-human entity holding a party position in a multi-party arrangement where determinations bind, and did it in 2001. Katsh and Rifkin's fourth party is not a channel, not a partner, not an agent serving one side, and not an object of interpretation — it is a positioned entity with its own effects on an outcome that binds both disputants. §9's claim that no existing framework supplies the third position is, stated flatly, **false**, and a reviewer who knows ODR will say so.

What survives, and it is substantial:

- **ODR is a dispute framework, not a coordination framework.** Its parties are in acknowledged conflict, know of each other, and have entered a process. The paper's worker is in ordinary coordination, does not meet her counterpart, and entered nothing.
- **ODR theorises the system's design, not the disputant's competence.** Katsh and Rifkin ask what the fourth party does and how to build it well. Nobody in ODR asks what capacity a disputant needs to fare well against an undisclosed fourth party, and the reason is that ODR presumes process transparency as a design norm.
- **The fourth party is disclosed.** Its role is announced, its procedure published, its neutrality an explicit design commitment. The platform gate discloses nothing, which is the condition §5 makes the paper's premise.

§9 should be rewritten to say the third position exists in ODR and that the competence question was never asked there. That is a stronger paper than one whose structural claim is refuted by a book from 2001.

## Caution

Practitioner-facing, pre-dating machine learning entirely; the fourth party of 2001 is case-management software and structured negotiation interfaces. The concept has been developed since — Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy (2017), Wing, Martinez, Katsh & Rule (2021) — and those are the citable modern statements. Rifkin is second author and does not appear on the later work.
