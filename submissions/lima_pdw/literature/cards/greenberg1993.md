## What it argues
Greenberg reorganises the justice field along two crossed dimensions rather than the conventional list of types. One dimension is the *focus* of the justice judgement: structural, meaning the formal arrangement, against social, meaning the treatment received from people. The other is the *determinant*: procedural against distributive. Crossing them yields four classes, and the two that were new are the social ones. **Interpersonal justice** is the dignity and respect with which a person is treated when an outcome is delivered. **Informational justice** is the adequacy of the explanations offered — whether the person is told why the decision was made and whether the account is truthful and thorough. Greenberg's argument for the split is that the two behave differently: explanations affect judgements of the *procedure*, while dignified treatment affects judgements of the *outcome*, and interventions that supply one do not substitute for the other. He supports this with evidence that adequate explanations mitigate reactions to unfavourable outcomes, including in layoff and pay-cut settings, and that the mitigation depends on the explanation's perceived sincerity.

## Relation to the argument
Informational justice is the justice literature's name for the thing §5 says is missing, and naming it that way lets the paper answer the reviewer's question directly. What a platform withholds when it delivers a determination without reasons is informational justice, and Greenberg's finding is that supplying it changes how people judge the *procedure* — which is a claim about perception, not about answerability, and the difference is exactly §5's virtue/mechanism line. A platform that produced adequate explanations would raise informational justice without creating a forum, and the paper should say so, because it shows that the justice constructs and the accountability constructs are measuring different objects even where they use the same words.

The two social classes also sharpen §5's common-understanding analysis. Interpersonal justice requires a party present enough to be respectful or not; §5 already says the counterpart is unmet. Informational justice requires an explanation from that party; §5 says the reviewer's reasoning is unavailable. The paper's withholding analysis and Greenberg's taxonomy line up cell for cell, which is worth one paragraph and would defuse the objection that the paper reinvented organisational justice without reading it.

## Caution
Not read in the original; the taxonomy is reconstructed from Colquitt's validation and the standard accounts. There is a persistent citation hazard here: Greenberg published two influential 1993 pieces, and the *OBHD* article on employee theft is frequently miscited for this taxonomy. Verify the chapter's editor, volume title and pagination from a library copy before use. The interpersonal/informational split remains contested, and some measurement work collapses them back into interactional justice. Greenberg's evidence that explanations mitigate reactions is about *reactions*, and it has been read cynically — an explanation that reduces grievance without changing the outcome is management of perception, which is a reading the paper should note rather than avoid.
