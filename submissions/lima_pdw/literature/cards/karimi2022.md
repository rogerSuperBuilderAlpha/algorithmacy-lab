## What it argues
Karimi and colleagues survey the recourse literature and impose a distinction the field had been blurring. *Contrastive explanations* — counterfactual explanations, nearest-flip points, "if your income had been higher the answer would have been yes" — describe a different world in which the model would have decided differently. *Consequential recommendations* prescribe actions in *this* world that would produce that outcome. The two come apart because the world has causal structure: acting on one feature changes others downstream, so the nearest counterfactual point is frequently not reachable by any sequence of actions, and following it can move the subject somewhere worse. Their argument is that recourse is properly a causal problem, requiring assumptions about the structural relations among features, and that recourse recommendations produced without those assumptions may be invalid. They also survey the field's other complications: recourse under model uncertainty and model updating, the cost and feasibility of actions, fairness of recourse across groups, robustness, and the strategic problem that publishing recourse invites gaming. Their closing assessment is that the field has strong formalism and weak empirical grounding, with almost no evidence about whether recommended actions produce the promised outcomes for real people.

## Relation to the argument
This survey is the honest state of the art on the question §5 puts to its own claim, and it comes out on the paper's side. The most technically developed programme for giving decision subjects a way to change outcomes concludes that (a) the guarantees require causal assumptions nobody can verify, (b) recourse degrades when the model updates, and (c) nobody has shown that it works for actual people. §5 says predictability is a state a worker can move herself toward and accountability is not; this survey shows that even the movable half is fragile, contingent on the modeller's choices, and unvalidated. A reviewer proposing recourse as the counterexample to §5 will find the field's own survey unhelpful to that position.

The distinction between explanation and recommendation is also useful to §7. Asymmetric interpretation produces a contrastive explanation — a private theory of what would have passed. Acting on it is a consequential recommendation, and Karimi and colleagues show the gap between the two is where the causal structure of the world lives. A worker's folk theory can be correct as a description of the boundary and useless as a plan.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth; do not attribute specific formalisms or results without pulling the survey. It is a computer-science survey and its unit is methods, not people — none of its claims are evidence about what decision subjects do. Recourse remains a technical property of a model plus an action set; it is not answerability, and the paper must not let the word slide. The causal-recourse programme it advocates is itself contested within the field. Cite as 55(5), article 95.
