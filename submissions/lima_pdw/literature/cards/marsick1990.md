## What it argues

Marsick and Watkins introduced the distinction that organizes this literature. **Informal learning** is learning that takes place outside formally structured institutional settings but is still, in some degree, intentional and under the learner's control — she decides to find something out and goes about it. **Incidental learning** is a by-product of some other activity, unintended and often not recognized as learning at the time: a by-product of task accomplishment, of interpersonal experiment, of trial and error, or of being told something in passing. Their model treats such learning as triggered by a jolt or surprise in ordinary work, proceeding through interpretation of the context, examination of alternatives, and action, and they argue that the conditions that make it more effective are proactivity, critical reflection, and creativity. The 2001 chapter updates the theory against subsequent research.

## Relation to the argument

The vocabulary §10 is reaching for. Between "instruction" and "participation" the paper has no name for what actually happened to the Lima participant, and Marsick and Watkins supply two. Her account of learning the gate is almost a textbook incidental-learning sequence: an expectation (a personal review from a staff member), a surprise (a bot instead, saying the actions were done incorrectly), interpretation of the surprise by another route, a changed action on the next build. Their "jolt" as trigger matters for the paper's design, because it predicts that learning is concentrated at moments of violated expectation rather than distributed evenly across sixteen weeks — which is a directly testable proposition against §11's dated ledger of rule changes, and a reason to time the mid-cohort interview around rule changes rather than around the calendar. Their conditions for effectiveness (proactivity, critical reflection) also name a plausible source of stratification that is neither talent nor position.

## Caution

An adult-education monograph from 1990, written before the workplace literature that surrounds it and before any platform context; the model is prescriptive and practitioner-facing as much as descriptive, and the informal/incidental boundary is acknowledged to be fuzzy, with the two terms often used interchangeably in subsequent work.
