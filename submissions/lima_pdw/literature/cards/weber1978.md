## What it argues

Weber's posthumous treatise distinguishes three pure types of legitimate domination — traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal — and develops **bureaucracy** as the ideal type of rational-legal authority: a fixed jurisdictional hierarchy of offices, each governed by written, codified rules; conduct according to calculable rules and files rather than personal discretion; officials appointed (not elected) on demonstrated qualification and organized in a chain of command with clearly bounded authority at each level. The bureaucratic ideal type is deliberately one-sided — a measuring rod against which actual organizations are compared, not a description of any one organization.

## Relation to the argument

Five uses. Four pair Weber with Simon (1997) to define Hierarchy — "managerial direction secures alignment through formal standard operating procedures" (line 37); "a codified administrative procedure administered by a designated hierarchical superior defines the formal bureaucracy... where rules remain legible and authority resides in identifiable human offices" (line 181); Table A1's mechanism cell (line 513); and the Appendix A synthesis sentence (line 522). A fifth, independent use applies the concept directly to the paper's own empirical setting: "Neither party occupies the role of a hierarchical superior administering a legible rule (Weber, 1978)" (line 290), arguing the computational gate in the course does not reduce to Weberian bureaucratic authority because no human office holds the rule. All five are conceptual applications of the bureaucracy ideal type (codified rules, hierarchical offices, legible authority) rather than quotations, so there is no verbatim-quote risk to check — the substance matches the standard reading of Weber's rational-legal type on every point: rules are legible/codified, authority is administered by an identifiable superior, and offices (not persons) are the locus of authority.
