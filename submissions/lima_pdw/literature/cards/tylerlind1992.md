## What it argues
Tyler and Lind reformulate the group-value model into the *relational model of authority*, and the reformulation is about what people are reading when they evaluate a procedure. Three relational judgements do the work. **Neutrality**: is the decision-maker unbiased, honest and acting on facts rather than on personal preference? **Trust**: can the authority's motives be trusted to consider the person's needs? **Standing**: does the authority treat the person with dignity and as a full member of the group, respecting her rights and position? Their argument is that these judgements, rather than outcome favourability or outcome control, drive people's willingness to accept decisions, to comply voluntarily and to regard an authority as legitimate. The model explains the fair-process effect without instrumentality: procedures matter because they carry relational information about the person's position, and that information is valuable independent of any outcome. The chapter also argues that authorities can secure voluntary compliance more cheaply through relational treatment than through sanctioning, which is why legitimacy is an efficient basis for authority.

## Relation to the argument
Standing is the model's own term, and it is the paper's term, and the coincidence is worth exploiting because it lets the paper connect the dissertation's inherited standing problem to a large empirical literature. Tyler and Lind's standing is a judgement the person makes about how an authority treats her; the companion review's standing is a position the arrangement confers or withholds. The paper can say the two are the perception and the fact of the same thing, and that the relational model shows people track it accurately.

The model also supplies the sharpest possible statement of why §6's institutional route must transfer standing rather than merely process. All three relational judgements require an *authority* — a party with motives to be trusted or distrusted, who can treat a person with dignity or fail to. A platform's determination arrives from no such party. That is not a low score on the three judgements; it is a case the model cannot score, which is diagnostic in the same way the multifoci problem is.

For §5 the point is the same and worth making once: an authority is the entity §5 says a worker cannot manufacture, and the entire relational literature presupposes one.

## Caution
Not read in the original; the three relational judgements are the standard rendering from citing literature, so quote nothing and verify before pinpointing. The model has been extensively tested and largely supported, but its scope is authorities within groups the person identifies with — courts, police, employers — and it has been criticised for assuming identification that platform workers do not have. It is a model of legitimacy and compliance, not of accountability; do not treat its standing construct as equivalent to Bovens's forum standing without marking the difference. It appears in a book series and is variously cited as a chapter and as an article.
