## What it argues

Berg reports a nationally representative Norwegian survey built around an explicit construct of *consumer competence*. Her operating premise is the one §4 denies: "to be a competent consumer, it is decisive to be informed about products and to be familiar with how markets function" — so consumers' self-reported effort to keep themselves informed about specific markets serves as the competence indicator. Three findings. Norwegian consumers rate their own competence as mediocre. Competence is not one thing but sorts into profiles — some consumers are price-conscious in daily purchases, others in yearly financial dispositions, a third group is community-oriented and active in ethical and environmental markets. And measured competence predicts how market-rational and reflective consumers' actual choices and practices are.

## Relation to the argument

Direct prior art on the market cell, and the most awkward kind: a named construct called consumer competence, measured at population scale, reported as unevenly distributed and outcome-relevant. Anyone auditing §4's "n/a — settled at low magnitude" entry will find this literature within one search. The paper needs a stated reason why it does not overturn the cell, and there is a good one available: Berg's competence is *market-specific* and largely a matter of how much effort a person puts into being informed about a market she chooses to enter, not a capacity the coordination form compels her to supply in order to be coordinated at all. A consumer who does not develop supermarket competence buys worse groceries; a platform worker who does not reconstruct the rule loses access to work. The row is ordinal, and this is evidence about where the market sits on it, not evidence that the market sits high.

Note also the profile finding, which supports §10's distribution question: competence for a form is not a single scalar, and different parties are competent at different pieces of the same form.

## Caution

Abstract depth only. The competence measure is self-reported *effort to stay informed*, which is a proxy of uncertain validity — a person who works hard at being informed may still be incompetent, and the outcome variables are also self-reported, so the predictive finding risks common-method bias. Single country, single time point, 2007 data, and a consumer-policy research tradition rather than a coordination-forms one. Useful as evidence that the question has been asked on the market side; not usable as a measured magnitude.
