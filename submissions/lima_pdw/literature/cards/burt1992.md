## What it argues

Burt turns Simmel's tertius into a theory of network advantage. A **structural hole** is a gap between two parties who are not connected to each other; a third party who bridges the hole controls the flow of information and the timing of contact between them, and captures the returns. Burt's two tertius strategies are Simmel's: *tertius gaudens*, profiting from the parties' separation, and its variants. His empirical claim is that individuals and firms rich in structural holes outperform those embedded in dense, redundant networks, because they see and time opportunities others cannot.

The mechanism is informational: the broker knows both sides and neither side knows the other.

## Relation to the argument

**The clearest statement of what the platform's position actually is, and it is not "intermediary" in any neutral sense.** A platform is a structural hole held open at scale. Its position depends on the worker and the counterpart not being connected to each other, and the returns to that position are exactly Burt's — control of information flow and of timing. §5's account of withheld conditions gets a mechanism from this: the conditions are not withheld by oversight or by technical limitation, they are withheld because the third party's advantage consists in withholding them.

That reframing does real work for §6 and §8. §8's possession argument says the worker owns nothing; Burt says why — the asset is the disconnection itself, and it can only be held by whoever sits in the gap. And it makes the paper's construct look less like a lament and more like a prediction: a competency that develops in workers on the far side of a deliberately maintained hole.

## Caution

Burt's broker is a strategic human or firm pursuing advantage, not a rule; his networks are of persons, and the tertius chooses. The theory has been criticised for treating brokerage as unambiguously advantageous and for underplaying its costs, and Obstfeld (2005) supplies the *tertius iungens* counter-case.
