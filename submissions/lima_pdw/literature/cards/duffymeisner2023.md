## What it argues

In-depth interviews with 30 social media creators sampled from historically marginalised identities and stigmatised content genres, on how they perceive and experience algorithmic (in)visibility. Their accounts converge on a shared understanding that platforms govern **unevenly**, through formal means (human and automated moderation) and informal ones (shadowbans, biased algorithmic boosts). The understanding is not idle: it is implicated in practices ranging from **self-censorship** to **concerted efforts to circumvent algorithmic intervention**.

## Relation to the argument

The two named practices sit at opposite ends of the outbound facet and the pairing is analytically useful. Self-censorship is intent specification with the loss maximised — the worker drops what the gate will not carry rather than repackaging it — while circumvention is the same facet with the loss minimised and the risk raised. Section 7 says part of the competency is knowing what can afford to be lost; Duffy and Meisner show the same population making that judgement in both directions, and show it being made under a belief about uneven governance rather than under a belief about a uniform rule.

The uneven-governance finding also bears on §10's scope condition. Section 10 assumes the workers being compared face the same withholdings; these creators believe they do not, and if that belief is correct then equivalently placed is harder to establish than the section allows.

## Caution

Thirty interviews, purposively sampled for marginalised identity and stigmatised genre — chosen precisely because their experience is atypical, which is a strength for the finding and a limit on generalisation. Perceptions of shadowbanning and biased boosts are unverifiable from outside; the paper studies the experience, not the mechanism. Normatively committed. Read at abstract depth.
