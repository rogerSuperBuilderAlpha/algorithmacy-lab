## What it argues

An experimental study of how people perceive algorithmic versus human managerial decisions across different task types. The organising finding is that the perception depends on what the decision requires: for tasks seen as mechanical, algorithmic decisions were perceived as about as fair and trustworthy as human ones; for tasks seen to require distinctly human judgement, algorithmic decisions were perceived as less fair and less trustworthy and provoked stronger negative emotion.

## Relation to the argument

The complement to the qualitative cluster and the source that keeps §7 from over-generalising. If perceived legitimacy of an algorithmic determination varies with the task, then so plausibly does the willingness to do the interpretive work the facets describe. A worker who thinks the gate is competent to judge what it is judging has reason to invest in reading it; a worker who thinks the judgement is illegitimate has reason to withdraw, which is `rahman2021`'s constrained reactivity and `eslami2019`'s exit branch. Section 10's stratified fluency treats variance as competency; Lee 2018 supplies a rival account in which variance is partly motivational and tracks beliefs about legitimacy. Section 11 should measure it.

## Caution

An online experiment with vignettes, not field behaviour, and perceptions rather than outcomes. The fairness and trust constructs are borrowed from organisational justice and carry that literature's measurement commitments. Nothing here is about opacity as such — the manipulation is decision-maker type, not disclosure — so the source bears on §7 by inference rather than directly.
