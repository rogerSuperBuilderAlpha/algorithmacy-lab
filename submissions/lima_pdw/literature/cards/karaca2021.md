## What it argues

Karaca, Çalışkan and Demir built the individual-level **readiness** measure that the AI literacy scales usually cite and rarely emulate. Their premise is occupational and specific: AI will not replace physicians but will take over many of their roles and generate new tasks, so medical students need to understand AI fundamentals, data science, mathematical concepts, and the ethical and medico-legal issues that come with them. No validated instrument existed to measure whether they were ready.

The construction is orthodox and well executed. Expert opinion gathered qualitatively defined the required competencies and framed the item pool; exploratory factor analysis ran on 568 medical students and confirmatory factor analysis on a further 329, all at two public universities in Turkey. An initial 27 items reduced to **22 items in four factors — cognition, ability, vision, and ethics** — explaining 50.9% of cumulative variance. Cronbach's alpha was .87. The CFA fit was acceptable: χ²/df = 3.81, RMSEA = .094, SRMR = .057, CFI = .938, NNFI (TLI) = .928. The authors position MAIRS-MS as a curriculum development tool for medical schools — needs assessment before a course, perceived readiness after.

## Relation to the argument

Neighbour, and the most useful card in this sub-cluster for the **role** half of the paper's competency definition.

Karaca et al. get one thing right that the general AI literacy scales do not: they define the competency for an individual **in a role**. Not a learner, not a user, not a non-expert — a physician-in-training, whose readiness is readiness for tasks that will exist in a specific occupation. That is half of the paper's criterion for competency, and it is worth conceding the point plainly rather than lumping MAIRS-MS in with the education instruments.

The other half fails, and so does the triad. **Vision**, the most interesting of the four factors, concerns the student's outlook on AI's future in medicine, which is anticipation about a technology rather than coordination with anyone. **Cognition** is knowledge, **ability** is skill, **ethics** is normative awareness. The patient — the one human being in medicine whose interests a diagnostic algorithm most obviously binds — does not appear as a party. This omission is striking rather than incidental: clinical AI is the textbook case of a system committing a determination binding on two parties who did not endorse it, since a risk score or a triage recommendation lands on physician and patient alike, and neither wrote it. An instrument built for exactly that setting still measures only the physician's relation to the machine. If the counterpart could survive anywhere in this literature, it would survive here. It does not.

That observation is more valuable to the manuscript than any of the general-scale cards, and §9 should use it: not "the scales forgot the counterpart" but "even in the one domain where the counterpart is unavoidable, the instrument records only the dyad." It is also a live comparison for the paper's dual-function claims about who owns what — the physician holds a licence and a role, the platform worker holds neither, and the readiness framing that half-works for the first will not transfer to the second.

Finally, *readiness* is not competency. Readiness is prospective and perceived; MAIRS-MS measures how prepared students *feel* for an AI-inflected future. Algorithmacy is exercised now, under a live system, whether or not the holder feels prepared, and it is acquired by participating rather than by being made ready. The manuscript should mark this distinction because "AI readiness" instruments have multiplied since 2021 and a reviewer may raise them.

## Caution

Perceived readiness, self-report, medical students only, two Turkish public universities, N = 568 then N = 329. RMSEA = .094 exceeds the conventional .08 threshold and the authors report it without much comment; the fit is acceptable rather than good. Four factors explaining 50.9% of variance leaves half unexplained. The scale is validated in Turkish; downstream English-language applications (for example among Jordanian medical students, doi:10.1177/23821205241281648) use translations whose equivalence is untested. Nothing here connects readiness scores to clinical performance with AI tools, so do not cite it for what readiness does. Read at extended-preview depth — the structured abstract is unusually complete, but the item wordings, the qualitative expert phase and the factor loadings need the open-access full text before anything is quoted.
