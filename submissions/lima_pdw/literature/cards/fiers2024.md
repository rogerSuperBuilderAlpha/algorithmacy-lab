## What it argues

Fiers approaches gig platforms through the digital-inequality tradition and asks a narrow question: what do digital skills actually look like on an online labour platform, and how do workers convert them into opportunity? The evidence is semi-structured interviews with 21 US online freelancers, coded for moments where a participant approached the platform differently *because of* a skill.

Three functions emerge. Digital skills let freelancers (1) optimise their use of platform tools as designed, (2) construct alternative pathways when those tools malfunction, and (3) imagine and blaze new paths beyond the platform's current functionality. The third is the paper's contribution. In a volatile environment where the interface changes, breaks or proves inadequate, skill is not competence with a stable tool but the capacity to improvise when the tool stops being what it was — which is why the paper frames the finding as *resilience* rather than proficiency.

## Relation to the argument

**Neighbour, and quietly useful to §8.** Fiers keeps the word *skill* and then describes something the manuscript's own definition of skill cannot hold. If a skill is trainable by instruction and dates with the tool, a capacity whose defining case is *the tool changing or breaking* is not one. That is the manuscript's argument for preferring competency, made independently and without the vocabulary, in a communication journal. Cite it in §8 as evidence that the field's own skill talk buckles under platform volatility, and the terminological argument stops looking like a stipulation.

Two discriminations hold. The construct is a worker–platform relation throughout: optimise the tools, route around the tools, go beyond the tools. The client never enters as a party being coordinated with. And the account is inventoried from interviews rather than derived from what the arrangement withholds, so it can say what skills do but not why these three and not four. The manuscript should also note the boundary: online freelancing, where the worker chooses and negotiates engagements, is a weaker case of binding determination than the dispatch-and-score regimes where algorithmacy is sharpest.

## Caution

Twenty-one US freelancers, qualitative, single-author, single-country; the three functions are analytic categories, not measured dimensions, and nothing here supports a distributional claim. Read at abstract depth — the abstract states the three functions verbatim, but the platform studied, the recruitment strategy and the coding scheme were not confirmed.
