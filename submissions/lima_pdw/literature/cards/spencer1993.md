## What it argues

The practitioner's consolidation of the McClelland-Boyatzis programme, built from a database of competency studies conducted by McBer over two decades. Spencer and Spencer define a competency as an underlying characteristic causally related to criterion-referenced effective or superior performance, and add the image that made the field's reputation: the **iceberg**, with knowledge and skill visible above the waterline and self-concept, traits, and motives below it, the submerged ones being harder to develop and more predictive. They supply a generic dictionary of around twenty competencies with behavioural indicators scaled by level, generic models for technical, sales, helping, and managerial jobs, and a procedure for building a model from behavioural event interviews with superior and average performers.

## Relation to the argument

Useful to the paper mainly as the standard against which its own instrument will be judged, and as the clearest statement of what a competency *is not* allowed to be if the word is used in its home tradition. Three commitments come with it: competencies are causally related to performance, criterion-referenced against identified superior performers, and partly dispositional, sitting below the waterline. §8's algorithmacy meets none of these cleanly. There is no identified superior performer at a gate whose votes are private; the causal claim runs the other way, since §10 says the competency develops through the participation whose outcomes it is supposed to explain; and the paper explicitly wants the competency to be acquired rather than dispositional. That is not fatal — it is an argument for saying that algorithmacy is a competency in the descriptive sense of a role-level capacity and not a competency in the modelling sense — but the distinction has to be drawn, because an HR-literate reviewer will read "competency" in the Spencer sense by default. The iceberg also supplies a useful contrast for §10's stratification: the competency-modelling answer to uneven distribution is submerged traits, and the paper's answer is differential participation, and those are rival explanations of the same variance that §11 should be able to separate.

## Caution

A consultancy handbook, not a research monograph: the underlying database is proprietary, the dictionary is a normative instrument sold for use rather than a validated taxonomy, and the causal language outruns the correlational evidence. Cite it for the definition, the iceberg, and the behavioural-event method, never as evidence about performance.
