## What it argues
Alkhatib and Bernstein take Lipsky's street-level bureaucrat — the caseworker who exercises discretion where policy meets a particular person — and ask what changes when an algorithm occupies that position. Their answer turns on a structural difference. A human street-level bureaucrat encounters cases policy did not anticipate and can improvise: she recognises that this situation is not what the rule was written for, and she can escalate, bend or make an exception, and can be argued with about whether to. A street-level algorithm cannot recognise the unanticipated case at all, because it has no representation of "outside the training distribution as a normative matter." It classifies the novel case as though it were a familiar one, silently, and it does so at scale and at speed. The authors' further point is temporal: the algorithm's decision boundary is fixed at training, so it enacts a past consensus on a present it cannot see, and the people who fall in the gap have no way to make the gap visible — there is no counterpart who could notice that the rule does not fit. They argue this is why appeals in algorithmic systems so often fail: the appeal is routed back into the same classifier, or to a human instructed to check whether the classifier was correctly applied, which is a different question from whether the rule should apply here.

## Relation to the argument
This paper names the mechanism §5 asserts, and names it in the vocabulary of street-level discretion that organisation theorists know. The reason a worker's skill cannot manufacture answerability is that there is no party in the loop capable of receiving the argument "this case is not what the rule is for." A human supervisor is exactly such a party, however badly she performs; the classifier is not one at all. That gives §5 a mechanism rather than a definition, which is what a sceptical reviewer will want — the claim stops being "accountability is by definition relational" and becomes "the position that would receive the objection is unoccupied."

For §6 it explains why the institutional route has to be *external*. An in-system appeal that asks whether the rule was correctly applied cannot answer the question the subject is raising, so the forum must sit outside the system that classified her. And for §7 it sharpens the boundary of asymmetric interpretation: the worker reconstructing the rule is reconstructing a boundary fixed in the past, and Alkhatib and Bernstein explain why the boundary will keep failing to fit her case rather than converging on it.

## Caution
Read at abstract depth; do not quote, and verify the Lipsky reading against the original before relying on it. This is a theory paper with illustrative cases, not an empirical study, and its claim about why appeals fail is argued rather than demonstrated — Vaccaro and colleagues supply the evidence. The Lipsky analogy has been criticised for overstating the discretion human bureaucrats actually exercise, which slightly weakens the contrast. The domain is public-service and platform decisions generally, not paid platform labour.
