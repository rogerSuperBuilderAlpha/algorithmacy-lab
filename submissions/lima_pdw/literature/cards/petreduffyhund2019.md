## What it argues

A content analysis of 105 news and technology articles plus seven platform guideline documents, across three cases where a platform publicly accused creators of manipulation: Google demoting Rap Genius for link-building in 2013, Facebook demoting "engagement bait" in 2017, and Instagram shutting down the botting service Instagress in 2017. The boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate gaming turns out to be nebulous and to shift with the platform's business interests, while both platforms and press describe it in strongly moral terms. The authors name the dynamic **platform paternalism**: platforms unilaterally issue rules, pronouncements and punishments that purport to serve the user network, acquiring moral authority on top of structural and economic power. The mechanism runs through three metaphor families — organic versus artificial, crime and deviance, cheating — all scaffolded by an authenticity ideal that obscures the platform's own curatorial role. Facebook's reversal is the clean illustration: it demoted as engagement bait the very calls-to-action it had recommended months earlier.

## Relation to the argument

The source that shows why **intent specification is hazardous work**, which §7's neutral phrasing about compression does not convey. A worker who packages a submission so the gate reads it correctly is doing something the gate's owner may reclassify as manipulation without notice and punish retroactively. That is a second-order withholding §5 does not currently name: not only is the rule undisclosed, the rule about which attempts to satisfy it are permissible is undisclosed, shifting, and enforced morally. Section 7 would be stronger for saying that the outbound facet includes judging how far one can go, and Petre, Duffy and Hund are the evidence.

The Facebook reversal is also the cleanest documented instance of an unannounced rule shift with a direct penalty attached, which is temporal tracking's occasion in its most consequential form.

## Caution

Discourse and content analysis of US news coverage and platform statements; no creator behaviour observed and no algorithmic mechanics established. The authors explicitly decline to define what counts as gaming, so cite for the boundary-drawing as a documented mechanism, not for any claim about which tactics are legitimate. The normative charge — that the boundary-drawing is undemocratic and self-interested — is the paper's own.
