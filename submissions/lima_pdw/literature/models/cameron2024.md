PDF on the dissertation shelf). "The Making of the 'Good Bad' Job: How Algorithmic Management
Manufactures Consent Through Constant and Confined Choices."

**Cameron is not cited anywhere in `PAPER.md`.** See the last section of this memo; that is a finding,
not an oversight to fix silently.

## The arc

The literature predicts that task standardization and surveillance under algorithmic management produce
the quintessential bad job. Workers report liking it. That contradiction is the opening — an anomaly in
her data set against a prediction in the literature, not a gap.

Her answer: algorithms segment the work into many small human–algorithm interactions, which scaffolds a
choice set. Workers make hundreds of narrow choices. Two tactic families — **engagement** (follow the
nudges) and **deviance** (manipulate the inputs) — are practical opposites and both produce consent,
and both leave workers seeing themselves as skillful agents.

## What to steal

1. **Open on an anomaly, not an absence.** "Most research argues X; I find, surprisingly, Y." Our §1
   has an anomaly available and the review has already said §1 must state it as the residual after skill
   and task are accounted for. Cameron's opening is the model for how to say *surprisingly* and then
   earn it.
2. **A comparison table that positions the phenomenon before any finding appears.** Her Table 1 puts
   on-demand work beside two older systems of production on six dimensions. Paper 1's six questions
   across five forms is the same instrument, and §2 currently narrates what a table would show. One
   table would let §2 shorten and would make the open cell visible rather than asserted.
3. **Researcher position stated as conduct, not as a disclaimer.** She drove for three years in a major
   U.S. city, on her own car and a rental, varying shifts — weekday mornings, the bar shift, airport
   runs timed to international arrivals, New Year's Eve twice — and ran "mini experiments" on herself.
   The honesty is in the specificity. The review's finding 7 says our role-duality paragraph is one
   clause where it should be a passage; this is what the passage looks like.
4. **Name the tactics in workers' terms, then sort them on one dimension.** Simple and complex,
   engagement and deviance. Two axes, four cells, every cell filled from incidents. If our later
   responses support named forms of conduct, this is the display — but see the caution below.
5. **Coding reported as rounds with what emerged in each.** Five rounds; open coding of one-fifth
   selected for maximum variation on gender, hours, tenure and location; three themes, then two more.
   That is four sentences and it satisfies a methods reviewer completely.

## What not to steal

- **Seven years, 160 hours of participant observation, 136 interviews with 63 drivers across 23
  cities, plus 112 conversational interviews.** Nothing about her display transfers at our N, and §6
  exists to say so.
- **Consent as the outcome.** Her dependent variable is why workers like a bad job. Ours is a
  competency. Her tactics are ways of *participating in* the arrangement; our operations are what a
  person supplies in the absence of a withheld condition. The two look similar on the page and are cut
  differently.
- **The four-cell tactic table, at this stage.** A table implies coverage. We have early responses.

## The finding this memo turned up

**Cameron's Table 1 gives a published geometry of the arrangement, in the venue our paper is aimed at,
and `PAPER.md` neither cites her nor answers it.** Her row reads: geometry of control arrangement —
manufacturing *dyadic*, service *triadic*, on-demand *triadic or quadratic*; and the on-demand system
of production is the "algorithmic labor triangle: app–worker–customer—(merchant)."

Our §3 closes with "In every case a person stands on one side and a system on the other… The missing
piece is a position, not a variable." That sentence is about *competence constructs*, and scoped that
way it survives — Cameron theorises consent, not competence. But it is the same exposure the 19 August
review found with Katsh and Rifkin's fourth party, arriving from a second direction and from inside
*ASQ*: someone has drawn the triangle, in print, three years ago.

Two consequences, both for the author:

- **The scope of the §3 close has to be stated.** "No competence construct gives the counterpart a
  position" is defensible. "Nobody has named the position" is not, and Cameron and Curchod are why.
- **Citing her helps more than it costs.** A paper arguing that the arrangement has three parties is
  stronger when it can say the geometry is already recognised — Cameron's triangle, Curchod's triad —
  and that what is missing is not the diagram but a competence written for it. That is the same repair
  the review proposed for the fourth-party literature: concede the antecedent, keep the question.

`PAPER.md` §2 for something else.
