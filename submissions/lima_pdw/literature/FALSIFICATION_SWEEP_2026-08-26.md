# Falsification sweep: the negative claim of PAPER_V2.md
**Date:** 2026-08-26. **Target:** the claim that, on four scope conditions, no competence construct yet written gives the human counterpart a position. **Method:** adversarial search outside the project's 387-source library, assuming the claim false. **Manuscript deadline:** 2026-09-10.

## Verdict up front

**No kill.** After searching management, HCI, communication, education, health, social work, applied linguistics, dispute resolution, online dating, and non-English literatures (German, Chinese, Spanish), I found no source that passes all three tests — (a) names or measures a capacity of a person, (b) includes a specific human counterpart as a party to the interaction, and (c) reaches that counterpart through a system that reads and binds both sides. The claim survives, but only because scope condition 3 (reads-and-binds) does the killing on every near-miss, and one June 2026 preprint gets two of three tests and must be cited before a reviewer finds it first.

## Near-misses ranked by threat

1. **JMIR #105459, AI-Mediated Relational Competence (June 2026 preprint).** The only construct found that is simultaneously a competence and counterpart-inclusive with an AI intermediary inside the encounter. Read without scope condition 3, the manuscript's sentence "no competence yet written gives the counterpart a position" is false against this preprint. It survives only because the construct's own premise — accountable human decision-making over disclosed clinical tools — is the negation of "commits decisions neither controls." Action: cite it in the Extant Constructs closing or in What This Paper Cannot Say as the eighth hearing, and say exactly why condition 3 excludes it. It is unreviewed and I could not retrieve authors or full text (page renders empty); it must be pulled and read before 10 September. A reviewer in health-adjacent OT will find it with one search.
2. **Yang & Liechty 2026 (social work).** Framing sentence puts trust, power, and rapport in the worker–client relationship at the base of AI competency — counterpart inside a competence framework, in a field whose real deployments (predictive risk models in child welfare) genuinely read both parties and bind. If the chapter's competency tiers write that arrangement in, it is a kill; from the visible material it stops at tools-the-worker-uses. Action: obtain and read pp. 545–570 before the workshop.
3. **The dating-app pincer.** Dredge & Anderson (2021) hold the counterpart-facing competence with no algorithm; Hu & Zhan (2024) and successors hold algorithm awareness with no counterpart — the same "nobody has both" shape the paper documents for labour platforms, sitting on a site where the intermediary indisputably reads both parties and commits matches. Two exposures: (i) the manuscript's shelf never mentions matching platforms for intimacy, and a reviewer can ask why the review's scope is platform work when the claim is universal ("no competence yet written"); (ii) this literature is one merger paper away from occupying the position — the claim is true today and fragile here. Action: either add a sentence acknowledging the dating literature as the same pincer on another site (strengthens the audit), or scope the claim to work coordination explicitly.
4. **Xi 2025 (applied linguistics).** Communicative competence revised for AI, humans still central, AI as delegate — fails condition 3 as a delegate, but it is a major-venue competence revision the paper does not cite, in the family (communicative competence) the paper leans on via Spitzberg.
5. **Pugh 2024, connective labor.** OT reviewers know this book. A capacity, a counterpart, and the algorithm as antagonist rather than intermediary. One clause distinguishing algorithmacy from connective labor (competence *through* the system vs. work the system *erodes*) would pre-empt the question cheaply.
6. **Bishop 2019/2020.** Algorithmic expertise with a binding recommender but a diffuse audience; also the strongest published account of competence *acquired by participation and gossip*, which touches the paper's "acquired by participation" property. Worth a citation in the properties subsection more than in the review.
7. **Bowker, MT literacy.** Counterpart present, intermediary reads both directions of the exchange, but transforms rather than binds. The cleanest illustration that condition 3, not condition 2, is carrying the whole claim.

## Prior use of the name

- **"Algorithmacy" — no independent prior academic use found.** Every substantive hit is the author's own: the rogerhuntphdcand.substack.com posts and algorithmacy.org — the "first global conference on algorithmacy," Port of Spain, 28–31 Oct 2026, convened by Roger Hunt (Bentley), co-organized with Hult and others. Two consequences the author should weigh, neither fatal: (i) AI-assisted search summaries already quote the Substack as "one recent analysis proposes algorithmacy" — the construct is publicly attributed before peer review, and the conference site's definition ("the competency through which a worker coordinates with another human through an algorithmic third party") differs from the manuscript's three-operation definition; a reviewer who finds both will see definition drift between the public materials and the paper. Reconcile them or be ready to explain the revision. (ii) The PDW is not blind, so self-disclosure is not a problem, but the paper's "names are cheap" posture sits oddly beside a branded conference; expect the question.
- **Close coinages, which the author must know cold:** "algocracy"/"algocratic" — coined by A. Aneesh (*Virtual Migration*, Duke, 2006; "Global Labor: Algocratic Modes of Organization," *Sociological Theory* 27(4), 2009), who distinguishes bureaucracy (legal-rational), market (price), and algocracy (code) as ruling principles — almost exactly Paper 1's comparative frame, two decades earlier, and a near-homophone of the paper's coinage. Danaher's "The Threat of Algocracy" (2016) carries the same word in governance. Neither is a competence construct, so neither touches the position claim, but a reviewer who hears "algorithmacy" will ask about Aneesh, and the answer should be ready: Aneesh names the *form's ruling principle*; algorithmacy names the *party's competence*. (Aneesh verified against publisher records; Danaher via secondary sources.)

## Closing ruling

The claim survives this sweep, and it survives for one reason: every construct that includes the counterpart hands the intermediary less than reads-both-and-binds (tool, delegate, channel, context, threat), and every construct that grants the intermediary that power excludes the counterpart. That is the paper's own diagnosis, and nothing found outside the shelf breaks it.

Three scoping obligations follow if the claim is to hold at the workshop:

1. **Cite the June 2026 preprint (#105459) and say why condition 3 excludes it.** It is the nearest thing in print to the position, it postdates the audit, and the negative claim reads as falsified without the condition. Retrieve the full text first.
2. **Read Yang & Liechty in full before asserting the claim to a room.** It is the one unresolved cell in the table above.
3. **Decide the claim's breadth.** As written ("no competence yet written") the claim ranges over intimacy platforms, clinical encounters, and social work, which the review never visits. Either bound the review's claim to coordination of work — the paper's actual terrain — or add one paragraph showing the same pincer on the dating site, which is the strongest available evidence that the gap is general. The current text does neither, and that is its largest remaining exposure.

The strongest surviving threat is not any single paper; it is that condition 3 carries the entire contribution, and three literatures (clinical AI, social work, dating) are each one construct away from meeting it. The paper should say so — a falsifiable durability statement costs a sentence and pre-empts the reviewer who would otherwise write it as an objection.
