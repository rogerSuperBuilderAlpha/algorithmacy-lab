# Context and region: two gaps filled — 26 August 2026

Research memo for [`manuscript/PAPER.md`](../manuscript/PAPER.md), against the workshop's
distinctive aim: dialogue between local realities and global debates "without reducing such
contexts to mere empirical sites." Two gaps. Part 1 finds the warrant for the manuscript's claim
that the construct's formation site is constitutive. Part 2 assesses the Latin American and
Caribbean platform-labour scholarship against the paper's specific claim — that existing
competence constructs leave the human counterpart outside — and separates what informs the
argument from what would decorate it.

Every citation below was checked against a publisher page, journal search record, or
institutional repository on 26 August 2026; the provenance line under each entry says which.
Quotations are reproduced only where the source's own page or abstract carries them; anything
marked *verify at page* needs the PDF opened before it enters the manuscript.

## Part 1 — The warrant for theorizing from the formation site

The manuscript asserts, without citation, that where a construct forms is a claim about where its
theory comes from, and that a cohort holding none of the usual explanations made the unevenness
visible. No single source states that argument whole. It decomposes into three claims, each with
a literature behind it: (1) unconventional sites are a recognized engine of construct formation;
(2) a site that exhibits a phenomenon with the usual explanations absent is a *strategic* site,
not a convenience; (3) treating an emerging-economy context as a place theory comes *from* rather
than a place theory is tested *in* is a live programmatic position in this workshop's own
journals. The best sources, in order of load-bearing weight:

### 1.1 Bamberger & Pratt (2010) — unconventional contexts reveal what conventional sites hide

Bamberger, P. A., & Pratt, M. G. (2010). From the editors: Moving forward by looking back:
Reclaiming unconventional research contexts and samples in organizational scholarship. *Academy
of Management Journal, 53*(4), 665–671.

- *Provenance:* AMJ "From the Editors" collection listing and ResearchGate record; title, year,
  and page range confirmed. Paywalled at AOM; PDF circulates on ResearchGate and Bentley's
  library carries AMJ. Obtainable before 10 September.
- *What it says:* an editors' essay in the field's flagship empirical journal arguing that
  organizational scholarship narrowed its sites to the conveniently conventional, and that
  unconventional contexts and samples — reclaimed deliberately — generate constructs and
  boundary insights that conventional sites cannot. Their operative rule: the context and sample
  must serve the theory, not the reverse.
- *What it licenses:* the manuscript's whole move. An AI-engineering cohort in Trinidad and
  Tobago is an unconventional site by any measure, and Bamberger and Pratt make choosing such a
  site a theory-building strategy with an AMJ imprimatur — provided the paper says what the site
  did for the theory, which the 26 August revision already does.

### 1.2 Merton (1987) — the strategic research site

Merton, R. K. (1987). Three fragments from a sociologist's notebooks: Establishing the
phenomenon, specified ignorance, and strategic research materials. *Annual Review of Sociology,
13*, 1–28.

- *Provenance:* Annual Reviews article page confirms title, journal, volume, year; the phrase
  quoted below is from the Annual Reviews record of the piece — *verify at page* before quoting
  in print. Available through Annual Reviews and JSTOR via library. Page range 1–28 per the
  Annual Reviews volume listing.
- *What it says:* strategic research materials are sites, objects, or events "that exhibit the
  phenomena to be explained or interpreted to such advantage and in such accessible form" that
  they make stubborn problems tractable. Merton's own examples run from Darwin's finches to
  medical residencies: the site earns its place by what it lets the investigator see.
- *What it licenses:* the precise sentence the manuscript already almost says. GauntleTT is a
  strategic research site in Merton's sense: the phenomenon — unevenness in how people read the
  same opaque system — appears there in accessible form because the explanations that hide it
  elsewhere are absent. This is the sharpest single warrant for "nowhere ordinary to hide."

### 1.3 Eisenhardt & Graebner (2007), with Siggelkow (2007) — revelatory sampling

Eisenhardt, K. M., & Graebner, M. E. (2007). Theory building from cases: Opportunities and
challenges. *Academy of Management Journal, 50*(1), 25–32.

Siggelkow, N. (2007). Persuasion with case studies. *Academy of Management Journal, 50*(1),
20–24.

- *Provenance:* both confirmed against the AOM journal pages and Semantic Scholar records; the
  Eisenhardt–Graebner phrase "unusually revelatory, extreme exemplars, or opportunities for
  unusual research access" and Siggelkow's argument that an organization may be chosen
  "precisely because it is very special in the sense of allowing one to gain certain insights
  that other organizations would not be able to provide" are reproduced across the records
  searched — *verify at page* (E&G p. 27; Siggelkow p. 20) before quoting. Both PDFs circulate
  openly; obtainable immediately.
- *What they license:* the sampling logic stated as method rather than apology. Theoretical
  sampling chooses the revelatory site, and revelation is exactly what the manuscript claims for
  the cohort. These are the citations a *Organization Studies* reviewer will expect to see next
  to a single formative site.
- *Honest limit:* neither states the manuscript's precise variant — a site where the
  *conventional explanations are unavailable*, so that residual difference must be the new
  construct. That claim is Merton's site logic plus a natural-control argument, and the
  manuscript should cite the composition (Merton 1987; Bamberger & Pratt 2010; Eisenhardt &
  Graebner 2007) rather than pretend one source owns it. Nobody found in this search states it
  better than the manuscript's own sentence does.

### 1.4 The context-and-theory program: Tsui, Whetten, Barkema et al.

Tsui, A. S. (2004). Contributing to global management knowledge: A case for high quality
indigenous research. *Asia Pacific Journal of Management, 21*(4), 491–513.

Whetten, D. A. (2009). An examination of the interface between context and theory applied to the
study of Chinese organizations. *Management and Organization Review, 5*(1), 29–55.

Barkema, H. G., Chen, X.-P., George, G., Luo, Y., & Tsui, A. S. (2015). West meets East: New
concepts and theories. *Academy of Management Journal, 58*(2), 460–479.

- *Provenance:* Tsui confirmed at Springer (APJM 21(4), 491–513); Whetten at Wiley/Cambridge
  (MOR 5(1), 29–55); Barkema et al. at the AOM page and LSE Research Online (58(2), 460–479 —
  the LSE eprint is open access). All obtainable before 10 September.
- *What they say:* Tsui argues that novel contexts in developing economies are where global
  management knowledge grows, not merely where Western theory gets tested — indigenous,
  contextualized research as contribution, not charity. Whetten supplies the distinction the
  manuscript needs in one phrase: *contextualizing theory* (theories applied in context) versus
  *theorizing about context* (context entering the theory's content). Barkema and colleagues
  generalize: management's paradigms formed on mid-century North American phenomena, and
  contexts that differ on the formative dimensions are where new constructs come from.
- *What they license:* the sentence "where a construct forms is a claim about where its theory
  comes from" stops being an assertion and becomes a position in a named debate. The paper
  theorizes *from* Trinidad in Whetten's second sense — the site's absence of sorting enters the
  construct's formation story, which is context in the theory, not context as backdrop.

### 1.5 The workshop-facing sources: this position in the sponsoring journals' own pages

Bothello, J., Nason, R. S., & Schnyder, G. (2019). Institutional voids and organization studies:
Towards an epistemological rupture. *Organization Studies, 40*(10), 1499–1512.

Ibarra-Colado, E. (2006). Organization studies and epistemic coloniality in Latin America:
Thinking otherness from the margins. *Organization, 13*(4), 463–488.

Alcadipani, R., Khan, F. R., Gantman, E., & Nkomo, S. (2012). Southern voices in management and
organization knowledge. *Organization, 19*(2), 131–143.

Srinivas, N. (2023). Locating the Global South in *Organization*. *Organization*. Advance/issue
details: DOI 10.1177/13505084231189264 — *volume and pages unverified; confirm before citing
with locators*.

- *Provenance:* Bothello et al. confirmed at SAGE (OS 40(10), 1499–1512; open version in the
  Loughborough repository). Ibarra-Colado confirmed via SAGE/Semantic Scholar (13(4), 463–488).
  Alcadipani et al. confirmed at SAGE (19(2), 131–143; open PDF at FGV). Srinivas confirmed at
  SAGE by DOI only.
- *What they say:* Bothello and colleagues, in *Organization Studies* itself, show what happens
  when a non-Western site is read only through constructs formed elsewhere — it appears as a
  void, a deviation from a Western baseline — and call for contextually grounded theorizing
  instead. Ibarra-Colado named the underlying condition epistemic coloniality: Latin America
  (the Caribbean included, on his own definition) consuming organization theory made elsewhere.
  Alcadipani, Gantman, Khan and Nkomo opened *Organization*'s special issue on Southern
  knowledge production; Srinivas tracks three decades of the Global South in that journal's
  pages.
- *What they license:* the manuscript can say that forming the construct in Trinidad — rather
  than importing a construct formed on Upwork freelancers and Chinese couriers and testing it
  there — is the response this workshop's own literature asks for. One sentence suffices; the
  reviewers at this PDW will know the debate. Note the fit with Bothello et al.: the platform-work
  literature's constructs treat an unsorted cohort as having *nothing* (no scores, no tenure, no
  task history), when what it has is the phenomenon unobstructed.

### What the manuscript should do (Part 1)

Three additions, all in `## The Site and the Researcher's Position`, none longer than a sentence
or two. Proposed wording, in the paper's voice:

1. After "…where a construct forms is a claim about where its theory comes from." add:

   > That claim has a literature. Tsui (2004) made novel contexts a source of management
   > knowledge rather than a test bed for it, and Whetten (2009) gives the difference its terms:
   > a context can sit outside a theory as boundary condition, or enter it as part of what the
   > theory is about.

2. After "…the difference had nowhere ordinary to hide." add:

   > A site like that is what Merton (1987) called a strategic research site — one that exhibits
   > the phenomenon in accessible form — and choosing one is the move Bamberger and Pratt (2010)
   > reclaimed for organizational scholarship and Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) call revelatory
   > sampling: the cohort's flatness on every ordinary explanation is what let the unevenness
   > show as a competency and not as a residue of prior sorting.

   (Two citations of the three suffice if the sentence runs long; Merton and Bamberger & Pratt
   carry the most weight.)

3. Where the paragraph turns to what the formation contributes, one workshop-facing sentence:

   > Forming the construct where it became visible, rather than importing one formed on sorted
   > platforms and testing it there, is also what the field's own accounting asks of
   > emerging-context research: a site read only through constructs formed elsewhere shows up as
   > lack (Bothello, Nason, & Schnyder, 2019; Ibarra-Colado, 2006).

References to add for Part 1 (all verified above): Bamberger & Pratt 2010; Merton 1987;
Eisenhardt & Graebner 2007; Tsui 2004; Whetten 2009; Bothello, Nason & Schnyder 2019;
Ibarra-Colado 2006. Barkema et al. 2015, Siggelkow 2007, Alcadipani et al. 2012, and Srinivas
2023 are bench strength — cite only if a reviewer asks or space allows.

## Part 2 — Latin American and Caribbean platform-labour scholarship

The regional literature is real, growing, and almost entirely in the power–precarity–resistance
lane the manuscript already maps through Cameron (2024) and Healy and Pekarek (2024). No source
found writes a competence construct, and none puts the human counterpart inside one. That is the
honest headline: the gap the paper names is global, and the regional literature *documents* the
same triad without theorizing a capacity for the trapped party. What follows sorts the strong
candidates by whether they inform the argument or would decorate it.

### 2.1 Sources that genuinely inform the argument

**Manky, O. (2026). Reimagining work security in Latin America's platform economy: Workers'
strategies amid urban violence. *New Technology, Work and Employment*. DOI 10.1111/ntwe.70005.**

- Peer-reviewed, Wiley; the search record shows a 2026 issue assignment (vol. 41) — *confirm
  volume/pages at the Wiley page before citing with locators*. Manky is at Universidad del
  Pacífico, **Lima** — a workshop-relevant provenance that is earned, not performed.
- Findings: 40 in-depth interviews with Lima ride-hailing drivers. Drivers value platforms not
  for job security but for mitigating physical risk — cashless payment, and *data-driven
  oversight of passengers and routes*. Workers "rework" the platform's own instruments.
- Bearing on the claim: **direct, from the other side.** Manky's drivers use the intermediary to
  read the human counterpart — the passenger judged through platform data. This is the triad's
  counterpart position entered in reverse: the region's nearest approach to a worker capacity
  aimed at the other human, framed as security rather than competence. The manuscript can cite
  it as exactly that — evidence that the counterpart-facing capacity exists in practice and
  still has no construct — and the citation happens to come from the city the workshop meets in.

**Grohmann, R., Pereira, G., Guerra, A., Abilio, L. C., Moreschi, B., & Jurno, A. (2022).
Platform scams: Brazilian workers' experiences of dishonest and uncertain algorithmic
management. *New Media & Society, 24*(7), 1611–1631.**

- Peer-reviewed, SAGE; confirmed at the SAGE page; open version at LSE Research Online
  (eprints.lse.ac.uk/115622). Authors span Brazilian institutions; Abilio is the theorist of
  "uberization," Grohmann now at Toronto.
- Findings: three case studies (illusive surge pricing for Uber drivers among them) arguing that
  scam — dishonesty and uncertainty — is a *structuring element* of algorithmic management in
  Brazilian platform labour, not a malfunction of it.
- Bearing on the claim: **partial but real.** It extends Rahman's opacity in a direction the
  manuscript's interpreting operation must survive: an intermediary that is not merely
  unexplained but possibly dishonest. A worker interpreting a scan-structured system is doing
  the manuscript's first operation under harder conditions. It does not touch the counterpart —
  the relation is worker–platform throughout — and the manuscript should say so if it cites it.

**ILO. (2025). *Decent work in the platform economy in the Caribbean*. International Labour
Organization.**

- Institutional report, not peer-reviewed; confirmed at ilo.org; free PDF (≈815 KB). Covers
  eleven Caribbean countries, **Trinidad and Tobago included**.
- Findings: platform work in the region is limited and uneven, concentrated in urban centres and
  tourism, dominated by location-based transport and delivery on global platforms; local
  platforms struggle to scale.
- Bearing on the claim: **direct, on the site argument.** The manuscript claims GauntleTT
  participants "arrived new to AI-mediated coordination." The ILO study is the evidence that
  this is a property of the labour market and not an assertion about individuals: there was no
  platform-sorted workforce in Trinidad and Tobago for the cohort to have been drawn from. One
  citation converts the site claim from stipulation to documented regional reality — which is
  dialogue with the local context, not decoration of it.

**Doon, R. A. (2025). Working without borders: Digital labor platforms and the emergence of gig
work in Trinidad and Tobago. In R. A. Doon (Ed.), *Sustainability and adaptability of gig
economies in global business* (pp. 1–34). IGI Global.**

- Book chapter, IGI Global — editorial review rather than full journal peer review; weigh
  accordingly. Confirmed via IGI Global and ResearchGate records. Doon is a Trinidadian
  economist (University of the West Indies affiliation). Obtainable via IGI or author copy on
  ResearchGate; allow a few days.
- Findings: documents the *emergence* of gig work and digital labour platforms in Trinidad and
  Tobago and the limited state of knowledge about it.
- Bearing on the claim: same duty as the ILO report, with a named regional scholar doing the
  documenting. As far as this search found, **it is the only academic treatment of platform
  labour specific to Trinidad and Tobago** — the honest citation for "the platform-work
  literature has barely reached the paper's formation site."

**Chigbu, B. I. (2026). Algorithmic management in the global gig economy: An interdisciplinary
systematic literature review and critical discourse analysis. *Frontiers in Sociology, 11*,
1743445.**

- Peer-reviewed, open access; confirmed at the Frontiers page, which carries the sentence:
  "Latin America and the Caribbean (two studies, 2%) remain significantly underrepresented" —
  of 103 empirical studies reviewed.
- Bearing on the claim: **direct, on the reference-list problem itself.** The manuscript's
  Anglo-American-and-Chinese source base is not an oversight peculiar to this paper; it is the
  documented shape of the literature. Citing the 2% turns the absence into a stated fact about
  the field — and gives the research agenda its regional direction with a number attached.

### 2.2 Sources that would be decoration here (and why)

Each of these is sound scholarship. None bears on whether competence constructs include the
counterpart, and bolting them onto the construct sections would be the gesture the call warns
against.

- **Gutiérrez Crocco, F., & Atzeni, M. (2022). The effects of the pandemic on gig economy
  couriers in Argentina and Chile: Precarity, algorithmic control and mobilization.
  *International Labour Review, 161*(3), 441–461.** (Confirmed via RePEc/Wiley.) Control and
  resistance — the Cameron/Healy–Pekarek lane, done well for Rappi and PedidosYa. Cite only if
  the paper wants one regional witness that the power lane runs through Latin America too; one
  clause at most.
- **Sánchez, D., Maldonado, O. J., Agudelo-Londoño, S. M., et al. (2024). Infrastructuring
  platform delivery work: Exclusions, coercions and resistance in delivery platforms' migrant
  work in Bogotá, Colombia. *Tapuya: Latin American Science, Technology and Society, 7*(1).**
  (Confirmed at Taylor & Francis; Tapuya is open access.) Venezuelan migrants working Rappi's
  infrastructures. Its one argumentative use: Bogotá's couriers arrive *heavily* pre-sorted — by
  migration status above all — which is a further instance of the manuscript's point that
  conventional platform sites deliver differences pre-explained. If cited, cite it for that,
  in the formation-site paragraph, not as regional colour.
- **Leyton García, J. A., Arriagada, A., Bonhomme, M., & Ibáñez, F. (2023). The gig economy in
  Chile: Examining labor conditions and the nature of gig work in a Global South country.
  *Digital Geography and Society, 5*, 100063.** (Confirmed via Bristol PURE/ScienceDirect.)
  Fairwork-framework conditions study; notes LAC is underexplored. Chigbu's 2% makes the same
  point with a sharper number.
- **Mogollón Barrionuevo, N. (2023). Trabajando bajo el control del algoritmo: El caso de los
  repartidores de Rappi en Lima Metropolitana en el contexto de pandemia. *La Colmena* (PUCP).**
  (Confirmed at revistas.pucp.edu.pe; open access.) Identifies the *customer evaluation system*
  as one of four control forms over Rappi couriers in Lima — the counterpart's judgment routed
  through the app, observed in the workshop's own city. Tempting, and the finding genuinely
  touches the paper's position claim; but La Colmena is a student-run sociology journal, and a
  construct paper at an OS/OT workshop should not hang a load-bearing claim on it. If the
  Spanish-language grounding is wanted, cite it transparently as what it is, in a footnote or
  alongside Manky — never alone.
- **Abílio, L. C., Grohmann, R., & Weiss, H. C. (2021). Struggles of delivery workers in Brazil.
  *Journal of Labor and Society*, DOI 10.1163/24714607-bja10012** — uberization and collective
  organization; and **Bonhomme (2025), NTWE, DOI 10.1111/ntwe.12308** on migrant platform-labour
  agency in Chile. Both belong to a future paper on responses and organizing, not to this one's
  construct argument.

### 2.3 The Anglophone Caribbean, specifically

Doon (2025) and the ILO (2025) study are what exists: an emergence chapter and an eleven-country
mapping. This search found **no peer-reviewed journal study of platform labour or algorithmic
management in Trinidad and Tobago**, and nothing from the Anglophone Caribbean on worker
responses to opaque algorithmic evaluation. The World Bank's *Working without Borders* (2023,
open access) carries global online-gig-work statistics with LAC coverage if a number is needed.
The scarcity is itself the paper's friend: it corroborates the unsorted-site argument, and it
makes the study in progress one of the first accounts of algorithmically mediated coordination
in the region — a sentence the research agenda can carry.

### What the manuscript should do (Part 2)

Engage where the argument touches the region; decline to garland. Concretely:

1. **Ground the site claim** in `## The Site and the Researcher's Position`, after "Participants
   arrived new to AI-mediated coordination":

   > That newness is regional fact, not stipulation: platform work in the Anglophone Caribbean
   > is recent, limited, and uneven, concentrated in transport, delivery, and tourism (ILO,
   > 2025), and its emergence in Trinidad and Tobago is only beginning to be documented (Doon,
   > 2025) — there was no platform-sorted workforce for the cohort to have been drawn from.

2. **Name the field's regional absence once**, either in `## What These Boundaries Share` or the
   agenda:

   > The hearings above run Anglo-American and Chinese because the literature does: a recent
   > systematic review of algorithmic management found two of 103 empirical studies from Latin
   > America and the Caribbean (Chigbu, 2026). The regional scholarship that exists works the
   > lane Cameron and Healy and Pekarek work — control, precarity, mobilization (Gutiérrez
   > Crocco & Atzeni, 2022) — and its nearest approach to the counterpart runs the other way:
   > Manky's (2026) Lima drivers use the platform's own data to read the passenger, a
   > counterpart-facing practice with no construct yet written for it.

   That last clause does real work: it is regional evidence *for* the gap, from the city the
   workshop meets in.

3. **Give the agenda a regional direction** in `# A Research Agenda`:

   > Whether the three operations appear where the arrangement has already sorted the workers is
   > a question for the region's platforms themselves — Rappi's couriers in Lima or Bogotá meet
   > an opaque verdict daily — and testing the construct there, with the scholars already
   > studying those sites, is the natural next study.

4. **Do not** add Rappi/iFood precarity citations to the Extant Constructs hearings. The seven
   hearings are construct hearings; none of the regional literature offers a competence
   construct to hear, and padding the list would misstate the literature while performing
   engagement — the exact reduction the call names.

New references this part proposes (verified above): ILO 2025; Doon 2025; Chigbu 2026; Manky 2026
(confirm final volume/pages); Gutiérrez Crocco & Atzeni 2022. Optional, with stated caveats:
Sánchez et al. 2024; Mogollón Barrionuevo 2023. One further note: the reference list's Scolari,
Guerrero-Pico, Piña, & Establés (2026) is led by a Latin American scholar but studies Spain — it
does not answer this gap, and no one at the workshop will mistake it for regional engagement.
