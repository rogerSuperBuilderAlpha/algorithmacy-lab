# Relational coordination: citation verification and flank test

**Date:** 2026-08-26 · **For:** PAPER.md, `# Extant Constructs`, opening paragraph · **Card:** `cards/gittell_relational_coordination.md`

The sentence under test:

> Constructs that include the counterpart already exist on another shelf: relational coordination names a quality of communicating and relating among parties doing interdependent work (Gittell, 2002), and it presupposes exactly what this arrangement removes, parties who can see, reach, and answer one another.

**Ruling up front: the sentence survives as written.** Two guard actions follow, neither a retreat: disambiguate which Gittell 2002 the reference list anchors, and keep one distinguishing sentence ready for the single AI-era application a reviewer might wave.

## 1. The citation, verified

Gittell published two relevant papers in 2002, so "(Gittell, 2002)" is ambiguous until the reference list commits.

**The anchor to use:**

> Gittell, J. H. (2002). Coordinating mechanisms in care provider groups: Relational coordination as a mediator and input uncertainty as a moderator of performance effects. *Management Science*, 48(11), 1408–1426. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.48.11.1408.268

This is the standard organizational citation for the construct, and its abstract — verified verbatim against the RePEc reproduction of the publisher record — describes "relational coordination, a communication- and relationship-intensive form of coordination." The manuscript's paraphrase ("a quality of communicating and relating among parties doing interdependent work") is faithful to it.

**The definition's exact home.** The sentence the literature quotes as *the* definition — "a mutually reinforcing process of interaction between communication and relationships carried out for the purpose of task integration" — carries the cite (Gittell, 2002, p. 301) in multiple independent citing papers, and p. 301 belongs to her *other* 2002 paper: Gittell, J. H. (2002). Relationships between service providers and their impact on customers. *Journal of Service Research*, 4(4), 299–311. https://doi.org/10.1177/1094670502004004007. Gittell's own later review (Bolton, Logan & Gittell, 2021, *JABS*, 10.1177/0021886321991597) renders the definition "a mutually reinforcing process of communicating and relating for the purpose of task integration" and credits it to "(Gittell, 2002a: 300)" — while listing 2002a as the *Management Science* paper, whose pagination contains no p. 300. That is an internal error in her own review; the page can only be the *JSR* paper.

**Dimensions, as she operationalises them** (verified in two Gittell-authored texts read in full: the Bolton–Logan–Gittell 2021 review and her 2011 Oxford Handbook overview as adapted in the 2013 Gittell–Suchman summary): three relationship dimensions — shared goals, shared knowledge, mutual respect — reinforcing and reinforced by four communication dimensions — frequent, timely, accurate, problem-solving. The validated Relational Coordination Survey (Gittell et al., 2000; Valentine, Nembhard & Edmondson, 2015) measures the construct as a network of ties between *work roles*, each role rating its communicating and relating with every other role.

**Provenance, stated plainly.** Verified against primary or author-authored text: the *MS* 2002 abstract (verbatim, RePEc); the seven dimensions, the roles-network operationalisation, and the "direct contact" mechanism (Bolton et al. 2021 full text, Brandeis-hosted PDF; Gittell–Suchman 2013 overview, ACGME-hosted PDF — both read in full). Taken from citing sources only, not confirmed against the primary PDF: the p. 301 placement of the definition sentence, and its exact wording. Neither 2002 primary PDF is legitimately obtainable open-access; the manuscript paraphrases rather than quotes, so nothing it asserts depends on the unconfirmed page.

**Recommended fix.** Keep the sentence; in the reference list, enter the *Management Science* paper. If the author ever wants the definition verbatim, add the *JSR* paper as 2002b and quote with "(Gittell, 2002b, p. 301)" only after sighting the primary page.

## 2. The flank test

The claim to falsify: relational coordination presupposes parties who can see, reach, and answer one another, so it cannot cover a setting where an opaque intermediary stands between them.

**Searched:** relational coordination (construct and survey) crossed with gig work, platform work, algorithmic management, AI, crowdwork, Upwork, online labour markets, ride-hailing, food delivery, virtual and distributed teams, remote work — via general web search (eight query variants), two semantic full-text searches over the Wiley corpus (Scholar Gateway), and the construct's own census: Bolton, Logan & Gittell (2021) systematically review *every* empirical study of relational coordination's predictors and outcomes, 1991–2019.

**Found: no application of relational coordination or its instrument to platform work, gig work, algorithmic management, or any setting with an opaque, binding intermediary.** The review's site list runs hospitals, airlines, schools, prisons, banking, construction — and telehealth, but telehealth is care delivered *through* a channel to a patient the clinicians can still address. What comes closest, in descending order of apparent threat:

1. **Hall, T. A., Gardner, J. W., & Wright, Z. A. (2025). Does artificial intelligence stimulate or diminish human interactions? An affordance perspective on AI, relational coordination, and performance. *Production and Operations Management*. https://doi.org/10.1177/10591478251407777** (OnlineFirst 2025; listed vol. 35(6), 2026). The one paper that puts "AI" and "relational coordination" in the same title. Behavioral lab experiment: three-person teams manage a simulated factory over ten rounds; the AI issues recommendations the team can discuss. Finding: the AI *stimulated* relational coordination — members used it as "a conversation starter and a relationship builder" (KU announcement quoting Hall). The AI is a shared advisory tool on the team's side of the work; the humans remain mutually visible and answer one another directly. It does not read both parties, rank them, or commit decisions. Far from opening the flank, it shows the construct travelling into AI-equipped settings *only where the mutual-visibility presupposition still holds* — but a hurried reviewer could cite the title, so the distinguishing sentence should be ready.
2. **Relational coproduction** (Gittell, 2016, *Transforming Relationships for High Performance*, Stanford UP): her own extension of the ties to customers, clients, and families. It strengthens the manuscript's premise — the construct includes counterparts by *direct* relation — and never meets an algorithm.
3. **Virtual and distributed applications:** Bozan (2017, HICSS 50) on collaborative environments in virtual, distributed teams; patient-portal networks (Otte-Trojel et al., 2017, cited in Bolton et al.); a Redalyc-indexed study of relational coordination through digital social networks in higher education. In each, technology is a channel between identifiable parties who address one another through it.
4. **The construct's own boundary evidence, running the manuscript's way:** in Gittell (2000, *IJHRM*), shared information systems that *replaced* direct contact rather than complementing it "were negatively associated with relational coordination" (Bolton et al., 2021, direct read). Where a system stands in for the contact, the construct's measured quantity falls. Bolton and colleagues also open their review noting that in services, "virtual interfaces require new forms of coordination" (citing Sklyar et al., 2019) — the theory's own custodians marking the setting as calling for something new.

**Ruling.** The sentence survives, unnarrowed. One reading note rather than a revision: "see, reach, and answer" must mean mutual identifiability and addressability, not co-presence — relational coordination has been measured across distance and virtual channels, and the survey works wherever a respondent can name the counterpart role and rate the communication with it. That is precisely what it cannot do when the counterpart's judgement arrives anonymized through the platform (Rahman's TalentFinder clients, whose feedback "will be kept anonymous and never shared directly with the freelancer"). The distinction doing the work is the manuscript's own: a channel two parties use versus an intermediary that reads and binds both. If the author wants one sentence of insurance, the Gittell (2000) information-systems finding converts the presupposition from assertion into the construct's own measured behaviour.

## 3. Adjacent constructs, checked

The contribution claim — no competence construct gives the human counterpart a position when a binding intermediary reads both parties — with each candidate carried as far into algorithmic settings as the literature goes:

- **Technology-mediated communication competence, the live Spitzberg line.** Koponen, Turunen, Laajalahti, Julkunen & **Spitzberg** (2025). Managers' technology-mediated communication competence: A theoretical framework. *Journal of Business and Technical Communication*, 39(3), 338–397. https://doi.org/10.1177/10506519251326558. Extends the 2006 channel model into digitalized workplaces; the technology remains the manager's channel to people she addresses directly. Not a threat — but it is the nearest thing to a reviewer's "the Spitzberg tradition kept going" and could earn a parenthetical beside Spitzberg (2006) in the CMC hearing.
- **Coordination mechanisms literature:** carried into platforms as *description of the platform's own coordinating* (algorithmic matching, Möhlmann et al., Kellogg et al. — already in the paper), never as a participant competence.
- **Boundary-spanning competence** (Levina & Vaast, 2005, *MISQ*): no algorithmic-management application surfaced; recent boundary work in platform settings concerns negotiating manager/worker/algorithm responsibility, not a counterpart-facing skill.
- **Team communication competence in virtual teams:** stays within teams whose members address one another; no extension puts an evaluating intermediary between the parties.
- **Measurement of algorithmic management** (Parent-Rocheleau et al., 2024, *HRM*, algorithmic management questionnaire): measures exposure to the intermediary, not competence toward the counterpart.
- **Cameron (2022). "Making out" while driving: Relational and efficiency games in the gig economy. *Organization Science*, 33(1), 231–252** — the single most citable near-miss: drivers doing relational work toward passengers under the app. It is a typology of engagement games, a mechanism of meaning and consent, not a competence construct, and it sits naturally beside the Cameron (2024) triangle the paper already cites as theories of power that write no competence.
- **Worker-to-worker cooperation on Upwork** (PACM HCI, 2024): peers, which the manuscript's boundary conditions already exclude.

Nothing found gives the counterpart a position through a binding intermediary. The contribution claim stands.
