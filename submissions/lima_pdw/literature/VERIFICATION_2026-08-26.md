## Class 1 — page anchors for quoted sources

### 1. Spitzberg (2006), JCMC 11(2), 629–666 — **CORRECTED**

The version-of-record PDF was obtained from Oxford Academic (saved to the library). Pages close as
follows: model components from p. 637 (motivation) and p. 638 (knowledge); the four skill clusters —
attentiveness, composure, coordination, expressiveness — **p. 638**; the motivation ratio **p. 640**;
context facets **p. 644**; outcome definitions and the working typology **pp. 647–648**; the measure's
simplification and the "four reliable factors ... roughly parallel motivation, knowledge, skills, and
outcomes" **p. 651**; co-interactants raising or lowering one another's competence **p. 650**.

**The correction.** The manuscript says: "Four outcome criteria close the model (appropriateness,
effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction), and the first and last are administered by the partner."
The article's working typology (p. 648) has **five** outcomes, with efficiency folded inside
effectiveness: "appropriateness, effectiveness (including task achievement and efficiency),
coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development." With five members, "the first and last"
also picks out the wrong pair. Text the manuscript should carry:

> Five outcome criteria close the model (appropriateness, effectiveness — subsuming task achievement
> and efficiency — coorientation, satisfaction, and relationship development), and two of them are
> administered by the partner: appropriateness is perceived legitimacy, satisfaction her affect.

Everything else in the two Spitzberg paragraphs checks out: the five-part model, the partner-facing
readings, and "the hypothesized structure did not appear as drawn" (p. 651: two recurring problems, the
measure "significantly simplified," four factors "roughly parallel" the model). One minor flag, author's
choice: the manuscript glosses motivation as "the ratio of approach to avoidance in interacting with
her"; the article's definition is "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in
a given CMC context" (p. 640) — context-indexed, not partner-indexed. The sentence is a paraphrase, not
a quote, and the surrounding claim (every part faces the partner) is fair to the model; tightening it is
optional.

## Class 2 — abstract-depth sources now carrying weight

### Spitzberg & Cupach (1984), *Interpersonal Communication Competence* — claim 1 **CONFIRMED**, claim 2 **CORRECTED**; book itself still unopened

The book remains a physical-library item (no open copy anywhere). Both load-bearing claims were run down
through the authors' own restatements and a contemporaneous validation study (ERIC ED279030, now in the
library), which reports the book's seven criteria and quotes it at p. 151.

1. **"Competence is not a property of a person but a quality of a relationship, produced jointly and
   judged by both parties" — CONFIRMED, and Spitzberg & Cupach (1984) carries it.** The book's criteria
   include "competence is an interdependent process" and "competence is an interpersonal impression";
   the book at p. 151: the model is "relational in the sense that it is sensitive to the implicit
   perceptions of the relationship held by the interactants." Spitzberg (2013, open access) restates it
   — "Competence is an impression, or an evaluative inference, rather than an ability" — and Spitzberg
   (2006: 650) attributes the joint-production dynamics (summative, compensatory, interactive effects)
   to the 1984 book. The manuscript's sentence, including its handoff to Spitzberg (2006) for the
   mediated setting, stands as written.

2. **"Measurement at one level licenses no inference at another" — CORRECTED as to source.** What the
   1984 book verifiably holds is that competence is *both molar and molecular* (criterion four in the
   book's own list). The no-free-inference formulation is the position of Spitzberg's later assessment
   work (the 2015 De Gruyter chapters; the 1989 handbook), and could not be verified as 1984 wording.
   Text the manuscript should carry (in "What This Paper Cannot Say"), either:

   > Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) hold that competence is at once molar and molecular — levels that do
   > not collapse into one another — and the design moves from incident-level interviews toward a scale.

   or keep the current sentence and cite it to Spitzberg (2015) alongside the book. A chapter-level
   check of the 1984 book would settle whether the stronger sentence can keep the 1984 cite alone.

### Teece, Pisano & Shuen (1997) — **CONFIRMED**

Full text read (mirror PDF saved). "The essence of competences and capabilities is embedded in
organizational processes"; "Capabilities cannot easily be bought; they must be built"; "The very essence
of most capabilities/competences is that they cannot be readily assembled through markets"; routines are
firm-specific asset clusters. The manuscript's compression — a capability is held by the party that owns
the routine, and the platform holds the ratings, the matching, and the rule — is a faithful position
attribution: capabilities live in the organization's own routines and cannot be held by a party who has
none of them. It is a reading, not a quotation, and the manuscript treats it as such.

### The insider-research paragraph — five sources

- **Bowen (2006) — CONFIRMED** (full text read, saved). Sensitizing concepts "start inquiry and do not
  sort it" is faithful to Bowen's Blumer quotation ("suggest directions along which to look," not
  "prescriptions of what to see") and to his "starting point for a qualitative study," worked through a
  study where coding revised the concepts.
- **Brannick & Coghlan (2007) — CONFIRMED** (publisher abstract). Role duality is one of the four named
  dynamics of insider research they explore, and their conclusion is reaffirmation, not apology.
- **Anteby (2013) — CONFIRMED** (publisher abstract). "The two dimensions should not be conceptualized
  as opposite ends of a continuum" is the abstract's own sentence.
- **Mercer (2007) — CONFIRMED** (author-final full text read, saved). The residue is hers: interviewees
  "already knew how I viewed appraisal, and this knowledge certainly affected the information they chose
  to give me, although precisely how I can never know"; even with her views suppressed, "they may still
  have tailored their responses in any number of other ways." One care point: her informants were
  fellow faculty, not her students — the manuscript attributes the residue, not the population, which is
  fine; a revision should not drift into implying she studied her own students.
- **Ferguson, Yonge & Myrick (2004) — CONFIRMED** (full text read). They prescribe agent-mediated
  separation — third parties approach students, and decisions not to participate are kept "confidential
  from faculty researchers" — and note that anonymity of participants *from the researcher* is the hard
  part. The manuscript's anonymous-intake design is a stronger form of the same separation; "the
  separation Ferguson, Yonge, and Myrick (2004) prescribe" is fair.

### Laupichler, Aster, Haverkamp & Raupach (2023) — **CORRECTED** (one word of precision)

Abstract obtained verbatim (gold OA): SNAIL "is also designed to enable the evaluation of AI literacy
courses' teaching effectiveness." The manuscript's first clause — built to evaluate whether AI-literacy
courses teach — is the article's own statement. The correction: the reported validation is an
exploratory factor analysis with **no external criterion**, so course efficacy is the scale's declared
*purpose*, not its validation criterion. The manuscript's "its criterion is what a course can install"
should become:

> its declared use is what a course can install

(or "its purpose is..."). The argumentative payload — algorithmacy predicts no course could install it —
is untouched.
