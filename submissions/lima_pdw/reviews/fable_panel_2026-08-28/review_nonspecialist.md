# Review: "Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation"

**Reviewer lens:** informed outsider — tech policy / platform-labor journalism background, no prior contact with organization theory, communication-competence literature, or any of the cited constructs. I am testing whether the paper's argument reaches someone who cares about the phenomenon but does not hold the academic apparatus.

---

## Verdict

**Needs work to be accessible.** The core idea survives translation — I can now explain algorithmacy to a colleague in two sentences, and I believe there is something real here — but the paper buries every concrete thing it owns (the Hult gate, the Trinidad cohorts, the pilot respondent who routed around the human counterpart entirely) beneath a 4,700-word literature gauntlet written in the paper's densest register, and it spends its scaffold vocabulary ("standing," "coordinative co-optation," "satisficing," "micro-foundation") before glossing any of it. **The single most important fix: open the paper with one concrete scene — a student's submission hitting the gate's concealed tally, or a courier's rating moving on a client's hidden feedback — and promote the routing-through-a-second-AI pilot incident from Appendix B into the main text.** The paper's best evidence that the construct is real is currently in a place a reader who gave up in the Spitzberg section will never reach.

---

## Step 0: Register and bar

The paper aims for accessible academic prose by its own house standard — plain, concrete, claim-first, named agents, every abstraction touching a case — in the register of the ASQ/Annals exemplars it cites. I am holding it to that bar: jargon is fine where it buys precision, and fatal where it hides a point a plain sentence could carry; a motivated non-specialist should be able to follow the whole argument without giving up.

## Part 1 — Argument clarity for a motivated non-specialist, section by section

### Abstract

The first sentence is a real definition, and I could parse it, but only on a second pass: "coordinates as a counterpart to another human party through an opaque, adaptive intermediary that evaluates and binds them both" stacks four abstractions before the sentence ends. What it means — you and another person are both being graded by a system neither of you can see into, and the grade sticks — is vivid; the sentence is not. Then the second sentence hands me three things I cannot possibly have: "Paper 1 of this dissertation," "coordinative co-optation" (undefined — and I still, at the end of the paper, do not know why the word "co-optation" is the right word for gig platforms; the Selznick seat metaphor that would explain it is in Appendix A), and "how a participant takes her place within it structurally." The word "standing" then does heavy work through the whole abstract, unglossed. The paper's own later gloss — "whether the enrolled actor is owed anything like an employee's protections, a buyer's exit right, or a co-opted party's hearing" (Algorithmacy section, ¶2) — is perfectly clear and should be where "standing" first appears, not 130 lines later.

What saves the abstract for me: the three operations (interpreting, specifying intent, keeping track) are named in plain verbs, and the paradox is stated flatly and honestly — "algorithmacy may improve a participant's own outcomes while doing nothing to make the arrangement itself more just." That sentence made me keep reading.

**Answer to the assigned question (1):** after abstract and introduction alone, yes, I could explain algorithmacy: *the skill of working with another person through an algorithm that grades you both and that neither of you can see — reading its verdicts, writing so your meaning survives its narrow channels, and noticing when its rules quietly change.* The literacy/numeracy/oracy analogy (Introduction, ¶4) is what delivered this; it is the single best expository move in the front matter. What I could *not* explain after the introduction: what "coordinative co-optation" is, why it is called that, what "standing" means, or what a "satisficing construct" is. The definition lands; the scaffold around it does not.

### Introduction

Paragraph 1 is good — hierarchy, market, network, "governed respectively by authority, price, and ongoing social relationships" is exactly the kind of compression an outsider can ride. Paragraph 2 is the roadmap and it is where I first stalled: "we formalize algorithmacy as a satisficing construct that incorporates the counterpart into an algorithmically bound triad, argued as the individual-level micro-foundation coordinative co-optation needs rather than as a completed account of participant standing." That is five pieces of unglossed apparatus in one clause. "Satisficing" gets its explanation (Simon's aspiration threshold) in the Theoretical Implications section, a very long way from here.

Paragraph 3 — the preview of the seven constructs — is actually a strong move for an outsider: it tells me in advance that the failures differ and what the one shared limit is. But note that its key sentence ("Where a counterpart appears, the intermediary is a channel, a delegate, or infrastructure to route around — never an autonomous party rendering a binding verdict") recurs nearly verbatim in "What These Boundaries Share." I noticed the repetition as a reader; the house style file bans exactly this.

What the introduction never does: touch a case. There is no gig worker, no student, no pull request, no rating in the entire front matter. The first concrete instantiation of the triad a linear reader meets is the Hult gate — at the Empirical Strategy section, roughly two-thirds through the main text. For a reader like me, who came for the phenomenon, that is far too long to run on architecture alone.

### The Coordination-Form Gap: Paper 1 in Brief

Short and genuinely good. "A zone, a term, a relation, a seat" is the best sentence in the paper — an outsider immediately understands what mature theory provides and what the fifth column lacks. The distinction between the standing question (not this paper's job) and the capacity question (this paper's job) is drawn cleanly here, better than in the abstract. If the abstract borrowed this section's language, the front matter would work.

### Extant Constructs

**Answer to the assigned question (2): yes, this section becomes a slog, and I can locate where I would have quit.** Table 1 is excellent — I got the entire argument of the section from the table in ninety seconds, and the "What's missing" column is written in plain English. The prose that follows then takes ~4,700 words to re-earn what the table gave me, in the densest register in the paper.

Specifically:

- **The Spitzberg section is where a motivated outsider gives up.** The second paragraph opens "Rejecting this assumption requires first addressing a reductive reading of Spitzberg that would render the critique trivial" — a defense against an objection I don't know enough to have. The fourth paragraph (reverse-coded items, attenuated subscale reliabilities, omnibus exploratory factor analysis) is insider psychometrics doing no work for the argument I'm following. I pushed through because reviewing was my job; a general reader stops here, on the *first* of seven constructs.
- **The Guzman & Lewis section** recovers somewhat (the "machine occupies the interlocutor's seat" idea is genuinely graspable), but the Gibbs structuration paragraph — "an organizational structuration," "methodological individualism" — sent me back to the table.
- **The Zhou and Rahman sections are the strongest hearings** for an outsider, because they touch cases: the courier compiling audio recordings and timestamped logs to appeal a bad rating, and appealing *to the platform, bypassing the customer entirely*, is the first moment the paper's abstract distinction (counterpart reached *through* the system vs. routed around) became a picture in my head. Rahman's freelancers smuggling clients off-platform, risking termination to restore direct contact, is the second. These two vignettes carry more persuasive weight than all the boundary architecture around them.
- **The repetition problem.** The same closing move — an actor achieves "ceiling performance" / "comprehensive mastery" across every dimension of the rival construct and still hits a "structural breakdown" — appears in the Spitzberg, Guzman & Lewis, Hancock, Long & Magerko, and Zhou sections. By the third occurrence I was skimming for it; by the fifth it read as a template. One reader-facing consequence: the hearings stop feeling like seven arguments and start feeling like one argument stamped seven times. Two full statements of the move, with the rest compressed, would hold me.
- **"What These Boundaries Share" rescues the section.** "Seven constructs, seven different failures, and one shared shape once Table 1 is read across rather than down" is exactly right, and the Manky finding — Lima drivers decoding opaque platform metrics *to judge whether a passenger is safe to pick up* — is the most arresting real-world fact in the main text. It proves the capacity exists in the wild, directed at a counterpart, with bodily stakes. It gets one sentence, at the end of the section most readers will have skimmed.

### The construct section

The Suddaby scaffolding is legible enough, and the clarification paragraph ("A construct describing what a person can do is not a claim about what she is owed") is the clearest statement of the paper's scope anywhere — I'd move its substance forward. The three operations are defined in plain verbs and I believed interpreting and specifying intent immediately. "Why Each Operation Requires the Full Triad" is the intellectually honest heart of the theory, and the pull-request example inside specifying intent ("phrasing a pull request's rationale... must satisfy the intermediary's legibility requirements and the counterpart's interpretive ones simultaneously") is the first concrete act in the theory core — one sentence, and it does more than pages of topology.

**Least convincing claim #1 (as an outside reader):** the triadic defense of *keeping track*. The argument — rule shifts are invisible as system signals and become visible only as changes in what the counterpart's behavior now rewards — is clever, but it reads as a patch applied after someone pointed out the operation is system-facing. "A participant who tracked only system-facing signals... would systematically miss the class of rule change this operation exists to detect" is asserted, not shown; one incident (from the pilot, from Trinidad, from anywhere) of a rule shift detected through a counterpart's changed behavior would convert me. The paper has field sites; it should have this story.

**Least convincing claim #2:** that the Hult classroom is an instance of the same phenomenon as Uber and Upwork rather than an artifact built to exhibit the author's construct. The "Why Hult Instantiates Coordinative Co-optation, Not a Hybrid Form" section is a good-faith answer, and flagging the two unconfirmed facts (reviewer-pair assignment; appeal rights) rather than asserting them raised my trust. But the underlying worry survives the argument: the researcher designed the opaque gate, then studies the skill people need to cope with opaque gates. The fourth study (non-builder settings, no institutional stake) is the real answer, and it is in Appendix C; the main text should say plainly, once, that the author knows the classroom cannot carry the generalization alone.

### The paradox

**Answer to the assigned question (3): it lands, and it is the best thing in the paper.** This is not an academic hedge, for three reasons a policy reader can verify from the text. First, the propositions are specific enough to be wrong — Proposition 3 (skilled participants absorb friction that would otherwise surface as visible breakdown or complaint, thereby stabilizing an arrangement that would otherwise register as unfair) is a real, uncomfortable claim about the political economy of coping skills, and it names the exact reason "just teach gig workers to read the algorithm" is a suspect policy program. Second, Proposition 4 hands the reader the policy lever — transparency mandates as a path that removes the need for the competency, making the construct's scope conditions "a lever rather than a fixed boundary." That is the sentence I would quote in a policy brief. Third, the propositions are wired into the research agenda (studies 3 and 4, Appendix C) rather than left as throat-clearing, and the conclusion circles back to them ("the reason for that caution, not a footnote to it"). My only complaint is billing: this material sits as a sub-subsection under Theoretical Implications, and the abstract's one clause about it undersells what is arguably the paper's most original contribution for anyone outside the construct-validation business.

### Empirical Strategy, Conclusion, Appendices

The Empirical Strategy section and Appendix B are — unexpectedly — the most readable prose in the manuscript. The gate description is concrete; the two-year GauntleTT arc (two Trinidad secondary schools, La Brea, the Member of Parliament, the Hearts and Minds gang-intervention partnership, cursorboston.com, Hult) is vivid and did more to convince me this research program is real than any theory section. The honesty is disarming in the right way: "two harness transcripts exist, one of them piloted; neither is coded, and neither is evidence for the construct."

**Answer to the assigned question (5):** yes, there is a vivid example — the pilot respondent who satisfied the "transmitting communicative intent" module *by routing an explanation through a second AI system rather than engaging the human counterpart*, a move the protocol had not anticipated and now explicitly probes for (Appendix B, Instrument Piloting). This is the single most convincing detail in the paper: the instrument met reality once and reality did something the theory didn't script. It is also the perfect miniature of the whole thesis — a participant treating the triad as a dyad, exactly the failure the construct predicts matters. It appears only in an appendix. Promote it.

**Answer to the assigned question (6):** the honesty does not undercut trust — it builds it. The flagged unknowns, the disconfirming-evidence column in Table B1 (the paper tells you what would prove it wrong, module by module), and the Bowen precedent (two of three sensitizing concepts discarded, reported as rigor) are the moves of someone who expects to meet data. What *does* strain trust is thinner: the lived material amounts to one read transcript, and the construct's real-world resonance (couriers, freelancers, drivers) is borrowed entirely from other people's fieldwork while the author's own site is a classroom he built. I come away believing the author has found a real *question* and built honest instruments for it; whether "algorithmacy" is a real, separable competency — rather than a plausible label for general adaptiveness plus technical skill — is exactly what the paper admits it cannot yet show, and the paper is better for admitting it.

## Part 2 — Jargon and clarity audit

### Precision jargon — leave it

- "opacity means the participant cannot directly observe the governing rule; adaptivity means the rule can shift dynamically" (Introduction). Terms defined at first use, then used consistently. This is what jargon is for.
- "Where the intermediary is opaque and binding, the counterpart is absent, external, or reduced to an outcome the system measures" (What These Boundaries Share). Dense, but every word is load-bearing.
- "discriminant validity," "latent correlation thresholds," pre-registration language (Semantic Relations; Appendix C). This is the actual currency of the measurement claim; a plain paraphrase would be less precise, not more honest.
- "Across every hearing, we interrogate a single structural question" — "hearing" for each construct's day in court is a live metaphor doing organizing work. Keep.
- The Rahman "invisible cage" vs. Weberian "iron cage" contrast — borrowed jargon, but glossed on arrival and genuinely clarifying.
- "A zone, a term, a relation, a seat." Compression, not jargon. The best kind.

### Obscuring jargon — flag, with rewrites in the paper's own voice

1. **Abstract, sentence 1.** "Algorithmacy is the competency through which an individual coordinates as a counterpart to another human party through an opaque, adaptive intermediary that evaluates and binds them both."
   → *"Algorithmacy is the competency a person exercises when she coordinates with another person through an algorithm neither of them can see into — an intermediary that evaluates them both and whose verdicts bind them both."*
   Same claim, same register, one abstraction fewer per clause, and "neither of them can see into" carries opacity before the technical gloss arrives.

2. **Abstract / Introduction, "standing" at first use.** "leaving open how a participant takes her place within it structurally."
   → *"leaving open what standing a participant holds within it — whether she is owed anything like an employee's protections, a buyer's exit right, or a partner's voice."*
   The paper already owns this gloss (Algorithmacy section, ¶2); it belongs at first use.

3. **Introduction, ¶2.** "we formalize algorithmacy as a satisficing construct that incorporates the counterpart into an algorithmically bound triad, argued as the individual-level micro-foundation coordinative co-optation needs rather than as a completed account of participant standing."
   → *"we formalize algorithmacy as a construct that does one job and stops — in Simon's sense, it satisfices: it names the capacity a participant needs inside the triad, and it leaves the question of her standing open."*
   "Satisficing" is worth keeping only if welded to its gloss where it first appears.

4. **Extant Constructs, opening sentence.** "A construct, within this theoretical framework, designates a formalized conceptualization of participant capacity — whether formulated as an operational competence, an infrastructural literacy, or an interpretive orientation toward an automated system."
   This defines "construct" circularly ("construct... designates a formalized conceptualization"). → *"By construct we mean any formal account of what a participant can do — a competence, a literacy, an interpretive orientation toward an automated system."*

5. **What These Boundaries Share.** "We establish this theoretical boundary across four explicit analytical conditions. The construct must operationalize an individual actor's behavioral and interpretive capacity; the human counterpart must function as an active, interdependent party... ; the computational intermediary must simultaneously evaluate and execute binding determinations upon both interactants — distinguishing this architecture from a transmission channel that merely conveys messages, an automated delegate that assists a focal sender, and a procedural fourth party operating within transparent dispute protocols; and the critique must substantiate an unpopulated theoretical position within organizational theory rather than a superficial disagreement over taxonomic labels."
   This is the paper's qualifying exam for its own gap claim, packed into one sentence a reader cannot hold. → Break into four short sentences or a displayed list: *"Four conditions set the boundary. The construct must describe what an individual can do. The counterpart must be an active party to the coordination — not a designer upstream, a peer network alongside, or a recipient downstream. The intermediary must evaluate and bind both parties — which rules out a channel, a delegate, and a transparent referee. And the gap must be a missing position in the theory, not a quarrel over labels."*

6. **Extant Constructs, Spitzberg ¶4** (the psychometrics paragraph: reverse-coded items, attenuated reliabilities, omnibus EFA). Nothing in the paper's argument uses this. → Cut to one sentence: *"Spitzberg himself reported that the measure's empirical structure came out simpler than the model, and called the research nascent."*

7. **What These Boundaries Share.** "Concurrently, empirical fieldwork has begun documenting this latent capacity in naturalistic organizational settings."
   Agentless and abstract, immediately before the paper's best fact. → *"Field researchers have already caught the capacity itself, unnamed: Manky (2026) shows Lima ride-hailing drivers (N=40) decoding opaque platform metrics to judge whether a passenger is safe to pick up."*

8. **The repeated boundary formula** ("An actor exhibiting comprehensive mastery across every dimension... experiences a structural impasse / structural breakdown / structurally unfillable parameters") across five of the seven hearings. Not jargon per se, but templated prose the reader learns to skip — the house style file's "uniformity of optimization" in miniature. Keep the two strongest instances (Spitzberg, Hancock, where the move is genuinely argued) and compress the rest to a clause each.

## Part 3 — Line-level revisions (highest-value flags, exact sentences)

1. **Abstract, sentence 1** — replace with: "Algorithmacy is the competency a person exercises when she coordinates with another person through an algorithm neither of them can see into — an intermediary that evaluates them both and whose verdicts bind them both."

2. **Abstract, sentence 2** — replace "leaving open how a participant takes her place within it structurally" with: "leaving open what standing a participant holds within it — whether she is owed anything like an employee's protections, a buyer's exit right, or a partner's voice."

3. **Introduction, insert after ¶1** (new concrete anchor, in the paper's own material): "The arrangement is easiest to see in a case this paper later studies: a student submits a pull request to a course's evaluation gate; her peers' reviews and upvotes are public, but the weighting that converts them into a verdict is concealed from everyone — submitter, reviewers, even the instructor, who holds no vote. She is coordinating with people she can see, through a rule she cannot."

4. **Introduction, ¶2** — replace the satisficing sentence with: "To resolve this gap, we formalize algorithmacy as a construct that does one job and stops — in Simon's sense, it satisfices: it names the capacity a participant needs inside the algorithmically bound triad, and it leaves the question of her standing open."

5. **Extant Constructs, opening sentence** — replace with: "By construct we mean any formal account of what a participant can do — a competence, a literacy, an interpretive orientation toward an automated system."

6. **What These Boundaries Share, four-conditions passage** — replace the single sentence with: "Four conditions set the boundary. The construct must describe what an individual can do. The counterpart must be an active party to the coordination — not a designer upstream, a peer network alongside, or a recipient downstream. The intermediary must evaluate and bind both parties, which rules out a channel, a delegate, and a transparent referee. And the gap must be a missing position in the theory, not a quarrel over labels."

7. **What These Boundaries Share, Manky sentence** — replace "Concurrently, empirical fieldwork has begun documenting this latent capacity in naturalistic organizational settings. Manky (2026) demonstrates that ride-hailing drivers in Lima (N=40) systematically decode opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness and ensure physical safety — an explicitly counterpart-directed interpretive capacity, mobilized for bodily security, that currently lacks formal theoretical conceptualization." with: "Field researchers have already caught the capacity itself, unnamed. Manky (2026) shows Lima ride-hailing drivers (N=40) decoding opaque platform metrics to judge whether a passenger is safe to pick up — the counterpart read through the system, with bodily stakes, and no construct to describe it."

8. **Empirical Strategy, "Instruments and Status"** — after "neither is evidence for the construct," add one sentence pulling the pilot incident forward: "One piloting result belongs in the main text because it miniaturizes the construct's stakes: the one fully read respondent satisfied the intent-transmission module by routing an explanation through a second AI system rather than engaging the human counterpart at all — a move the protocol had not anticipated and now explicitly probes for (Appendix B)."

9. **Spitzberg section, ¶4** — replace the psychometrics paragraph with: "Spitzberg himself reported that the measure's empirical structure came out simpler than the model he had theorized, and characterized research on the framework as nascent (p. 651)."

## Findings, ranked by cost to the reader

1. **No concrete case before the two-thirds mark.** (Introduction; first grounding at Empirical Strategy.) The paper's own house style demands every abstraction touch a case; the front matter touches none. A reader who came for gig workers and students runs on pure architecture for thousands of words. Fix: revision #3 above, plus promoting the pilot incident (revision #8).

2. **The Extant Constructs section loses the non-specialist at its first hearing.** (Spitzberg ¶2 and ¶4; Gibbs structuration paragraph.) Table 1 carries the whole argument in plain English; the prose then re-argues it at five times the length in the paper's densest register, with a boundary-verdict formula repeated across five subsections. Fix: lead each hearing with its plain verdict, cut the psychometrics paragraph, compress the repeated formula to two full instances.

3. **Scaffold vocabulary spent before it is glossed.** (Abstract; Introduction ¶2.) "Standing," "coordinative co-optation," "satisficing," "micro-foundation" all appear before their explanations; the clear glosses exist later in the paper. "Co-optation" itself is never motivated in this paper — the Selznick seat that explains the word sits in Appendix A. Fix: revisions #1, #2, #4, plus one sentence in the Paper-1-in-Brief section saying why *co-optation* is the inherited name.

4. **The paradox is under-billed.** (Sub-subsection of Theoretical Implications.) For any reader outside construct validation, Propositions 3 and 4 are the paper's most original contribution — competent coping stabilizes unfair arrangements; transparency is the policy path that makes the competency unnecessary. One clause in the abstract and a buried subsection undersell it. Fix: give it a full abstract sentence and a top-level section heading.

5. **The four-conditions sentence is unparseable.** (What These Boundaries Share.) The paper's qualifying exam for its own gap claim should be its clearest passage; it is one semicolon-chained sentence. Fix: revision #6.

6. **Verbatim repetition between Introduction ¶3 and What These Boundaries Share.** The "channel, a delegate, or infrastructure to route around" sentence appears nearly identically twice. Keep the later instance, vary the earlier.

7. **The keeping-track triadicity argument is asserted, not shown.** (Why Each Operation Requires the Full Triad.) One field incident of a rule shift detected through a counterpart's changed behavior would convert the paper's most vulnerable theoretical claim into its most persuasive. The author has two years of sites to draw one from.

8. **The builder's-classroom worry is answered in an appendix.** (Why Hult Instantiates...; Appendix C, study 4.) The main text should say once, plainly, that the author knows the self-built gate cannot carry the generalization and that study 4 exists for exactly that reason.

## Closing note

The moment that most made me believe this idea is real is small and sits in Appendix B: the one fully read pilot respondent satisfied the interview's intent-transmission module *by routing an explanation through a second AI system instead of engaging the human counterpart* — and nothing in the protocol had anticipated that a person could do this. That detail convinced me for a reason no amount of boundary architecture could: the theory met one live human being, and the human immediately did something the theory hadn't scripted but the construct retroactively explains — treating the triad as a dyad, coordinating with the system and abandoning the person on the other side. That is what an idea with real content looks like when it first touches the world. It is also, right now, invisible to any reader who does not finish the appendices.

The one thing only the author can supply: a single, fully told incident from the two years of fieldwork he already owns — one named-in-role participant (a La Brea student, a Boston submitter), one specific verdict from the gate, what she believed the hidden tally and her peers had done, and what she did next. The paper has Rahman's couriers and Manky's drivers; it has none of its own people on the page. One lived story — even explicitly flagged as uncoded, pre-thesis-cycle material, exactly as the paper's own honest bookkeeping would require — would do more to make "algorithmacy" feel like a discovered thing rather than a well-argued label than any revision a reviewer can propose.
