## Findings, ranked

### 1. §4, L128 — the competency anchor contradicts its own source

The manuscript writes:

> Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) treat communication competence as what a party is able to do in a
> coordination, as against what she did on any one occasion.

The card written from the Sage volume and the authors' 1989 restatement
([`../literature/cards/spitzberg1984.md`](../literature/cards/spitzberg1984.md), `extended_preview`)
records the opposite: competence is "**not a property of a person** but a quality of a relationship,
produced jointly, **judged by both parties**, and specific to the episode and relationship in which it
occurs."

The relational model is a rejection of competence-as-individual-ability. Citing it for an ability
residing in a party is the failure the August memo caught in the old §8 with Sandberg — anchoring on a
source written to refute the reading. The draft already handles Sandberg correctly two sentences
earlier: "That result stands; the rationalistic definition he wrote the article to refute does not."
This sentence is that repair left unfinished.

The repair is a gift. Their model requires the partner's judgement to constitute competence, and the
arrangement removes the partner from the position where she could judge.

**Replacement:**

> Spitzberg and Cupach (1984) went further in the other direction: for them competence is not a
> property of a person at all but a quality of a relationship, produced jointly and judged by both
> parties. That is the standard this arrangement cannot meet. The counterpart never reaches a position
> from which she could judge, and the competency named here is what a party supplies when the
> relational test has nobody to administer it.

One consequence from the same card: they hold that measurement at one level licenses no inference at
another (molecular behaviours, episodes, relationships). §5 plans a move from incident-level interview
data toward a scale. §6 should concede that level shift in a clause.

### 2. §2, L60 — the load-bearing hinge is unbolted

> Those sources still do not answer the six questions in the form's own terms with the completeness the
> older literatures reached. **One cell remains open.** The older forms already say how a person takes
> her place — by accepting a zone, reading a price, investing in a relation, or taking a seat.

Which cell? §2 lists Paper 1's six questions at L48: how the form operates, what it must deliver, what
conditions favor it, what instruments it employs, how it fails, how a researcher would recognize it.
**"How a person takes her place" is not among them.** It is a seventh question — which is what the
archived draft argued explicitly, under that name, with a five-condition admission rule.

The rewrite removed the scaffolding and kept the conclusion. As written, §2 establishes that
coordinative co-optation is under-answered on six questions, then asserts that the older literatures
answer a different question Paper 1 never asked, and treats the second as the open cell of the first.
§6's concession that "Paper 1's grades are a frame rather than a finding" does not cover it: the
problem is not the grades, it is that the open cell is not one of the graded cells.

Two ways out. Name the seventh question in one sentence and let it be an addition, or fold
person-integration into the **mechanism** cell — Paper 1 already defines mechanism as the operation
that secures the form *and the standing of the party that operation reaches*, which is where a zone of
acceptance and a posted price live. The second is tidier.

**Replacement:**

> One cell answers less than it should. Paper 1 defined a form's mechanism as the operation that
> secures it *and the standing of the party that operation reaches*, and it is the second half that
> goes missing here. The older forms say how a person takes her place — by accepting a zone, reading a
> price, investing in a relation, or taking a seat. Coordinative co-optation says what the algorithm
> does to the parties. It does not say what the person must be able to do in order to take part.

### 3. §3, L116 — the prior-art trap: the fourth party

> In every case a person stands on one side and a system on the other. Adding items will not move that
> line. The missing piece is a position, not a variable.

Katsh and Rifkin named **the fourth party** in 2001 — after two disputants and a human neutral — and
argued the technology can displace the third. Wing, Martinez, Katsh and Rule (2021, *Negotiation
Journal*) theorise a fourth party that triages, recommends, and in some designs decides. Both are
carded here, at `extended_preview` and `full_text`; the note on
[`../literature/cards/wing2021.md`](../literature/cards/wing2021.md) calls it "the single most
important card in this cluster… and the one that most damages the section as written."

Scoped to seven *competence constructs*, the claim survives — no ODR work is a competence construct.
But the paper never says it is scoped that way, and "the missing piece is a position" reads as a claim
about the literature at large. The concession costs one paragraph and buys an antecedent.

**Insert before the closing three sentences:**

> One literature has named a non-human party outright. Katsh and Rifkin (2001) called it the fourth
> party, after the two disputants and the human neutral, and argued that the technology can displace
> the third; Wing, Martinez, Katsh and Rule (2021) describe fourth parties that triage, recommend, and
> in some designs decide. That literature asks what the system owes — transparency, contestability,
> consent — and answers for its design. It does not ask what a party must be able to do when the system
> owes those things and delivers none of them, because online dispute resolution presumes a disclosed
> process. The question here begins where that presumption fails.

### 4. §3, L110 — a misquote, running in the direction of the argument

Verified against [`../literature/pdfs/sutherland2020.pdf`](../literature/pdfs/), p. 463. The source
reads:

> Clients' narratives conversely provided some verification as to which literacies or skills **were in
> fact valued** in gig workers.

The draft has "were apparent," which is lifted from the preceding sentence about workers' own accounts,
and appends "to them" outside the quotation marks. The splice softens the nearest rival in the paper's
favour, which is the worst direction for an error to run: "valued" makes the client an evaluator of
competence, a stronger counterpart presence than "apparent."

**Replacement:**

> clients were interviewed "to provide some verification as to which literacies or skills were in fact
> valued in gig workers" (p. 463)

### 6. §5 — "several responses," and the approval question

**How many?** L146: "We have obtained several responses." Then one is described. Pratt (2009), cited
for exactly this, argues that what counts as enough is a function of the question — but that argument
requires stating the actual N. "Several" reads as evasion where a number would read as honesty, and
this paper's credibility rests on being precise about how little it has.

> We have obtained *n* responses across the three protocols. One is inspectable at the incident level,
> and it comes from the student protocol in the Hult course.

**Under what approval?** L154 says the exempt determination — protocol 260511078, 11 May 2026 — covers
"a companion three-wave instrument… not yet fielded." The paper reports human-subjects data from a
different instrument and never says what covers it. The arm's own files disagree:
[`../interview/CONSENT.md`](../interview/CONSENT.md) and
[`../interview/INVITATION.md`](../interview/INVITATION.md) tell participants the interview study is
exempt under 260511078, while [`../PLAN.md`](../PLAN.md) §2 says the qualitative protocol "is a
separate application." Whether a May determination made on a three-wave survey design covers an
LLM-mediated interview with different data and a different collection mechanism is an IRB question,
not a repo question — and consent forms are already in participants' hands.

**Author-only, and it outranks every drafting task in this review.**

### 7. §5, L140 — the strongest objection gets one clause

"The investigator teaches the course, casts no vote in the peer gate, and keeps consent outside the
course" is correct as far as it goes. The objection an OS/OT reviewer raises is role duality plus
non-free consent: the investigator holds evaluative standing, so declining has a cost — and having
designed the gate, he is the person least able to see it as opaque. The answering literature is carded
here and none of it is cited: Brannick and Coghlan (2007) on insider research, Anteby (2013) on
distance and involvement not being ends of one continuum, Ferguson, Yonge and Myrick (2004) on faculty
recruiting their own students, Mercer (2007) for conceding the residual in someone else's words.

### 8. §4, L124 — Suddaby's third requirement is answered circularly

> The neighbours occupy the positions just described. They measure adjacent things; if they already
> measured the same thing, the semantic relation would collapse.

That asserts distinctness by assuming it. Suddaby asks for specified relations and what distinguishes
each. The paper already has the answer, in the wrong section: §3's Algorithmic Competency subsection
(L100) predicts a moderate correlation between Zhou's Understanding and interpreting, and between
Leveraging and specifying intent, with Embracing having no counterpart. That is exactly what the third
requirement asks for.

**Replacement:**

> The neighbours measure adjacent things, and the relations can be stated rather than asserted. Zhou
> and colleagues' Understanding should correlate moderately with interpreting and their Leveraging with
> specifying intent; their Embracing has no counterpart in the definition, because an attitude toward
> the platform's efficiency is not an operation performed through it. Rahman's reactivity is orthogonal
> on a different axis: he cuts by engagement, this construct cuts by direction and time. If any of
> those correlations came back high enough to collapse the distinction, the construct would not survive
> it.

### 9. §4, L126 — why three operations, and not four

"A competence that stopped at the system would need only the first" is the real derivation, and it is
good: it shows a counterpart-including competence needs more than interpretation. It does not show it
needs exactly three. The archived draft had a rule that generated the number and left an empty cell
visible; the rewrite kept the number and dropped the rule. Rahman's fifth component — *who influences
the score* — is the nearest thing to a fourth candidate in the paper, and saying why it does not become
one would do the work the orchestration cut used to do.

### 10. The cheapest available upgrade, still unused

§3 establishes the counterpart's absence by reading seven constructs. Three stocktakings establish it
across the field, all carded: Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) screened 96 and reviewed 50
algorithmic-literacy articles across four databases; Gagrčin, Naab and Grub across 169 studies; Iyamu
and colleagues chart AI literacy among workers against Nutbeam's model, whose *communicative* level is
literally "interacting with AI systems and explaining AI-mediated information," and report communicative
literacies infrequently assessed. One sentence converts "we read seven and none has it" into a
documented negative result.

Abidin (2016) is the crispest line available: visibility labour is the one construct with a real human
counterpart, and it deliberately excludes the algorithm. Every construct that centres the algorithm has
no counterpart; the one with a counterpart has no algorithm. Nobody has both.

## Prose

Three patterns earn a flag. Only the first is serious.

**The recycled formula — "Adding items" ×4, across four sections.** "Adding items stays inside cells
already defined without a counterpart" (§3 AI Literacy); "Adding items will not move a counterpart
already coded as an outcome" (§3 Algorithmic Competency); "Adding items will not move that line" (§3
close); "Adding items will not produce the position" (§4). This is the style file's named tell — the
same formula-phrase restated across sections. Keep the third, where it caps the section. The fourth
also does the work §4 most needs done, and does it by repeating a slogan.

**The four-beat anaphora — "still of the apparatus" ×4, §3 L98.** Deliberate hammer, and it works
twice. Four identical em-dash-plus-tag constructions in four consecutive sentences is the paired-dash
crutch at maximum. Break the last one:

> Remediating is repairing or supplementing the system's deficiencies. Four dimensions, four relations
> to the apparatus, and no relation to anyone the apparatus matched.

**The landing-line drumbeat in §3.** All seven subsections close on an epigram delivering the same
verdict, and two use the same verb phrase ("leave unasked whether…", "leaves unasked whether…"). The
verdicts must repeat; the shape need not. The one that breaks the pattern is the best line in the
paper: **"A rating that can close an account does not."** Six words after a long build.

**Not flagged, and why.** `rather than` (7 instances, 1.4/1k) is load-bearing every time. "A person
takes her place" (×5) across the five forms and "treats it as an object of" (×4) across the hearings
are declared analytic parallelism, the class the style file protects. The subsection openers are
backward hooks onto the previous close — cohesion mechanism 7, working as intended, and the
best-executed thing in the draft's prose. One verbatim repetition crosses the lock: "a client, an
evaluator, or another participant" appears at L42 (locked) and L68; vary the second.

## Closing

**The genuine strength is §3.** Seven constructs, one question asked of each — what does this make of
the algorithm, and where does the counterpart then sit — and each hearing gives the source its best
version before drawing the line. The Hancock paragraph is the model: it grants that AI-MC has three
positions, then finds the difference in standing rather than in counting. "Even a fully autonomous
agent, on their definition, still operates on behalf of a communicator. A rating that can close an
account does not." That is the paper's real argument, and it is better than the position-counting
around it. If §3's close and §4 ran on standing the way that paragraph does, findings 3 and 8 would
mostly dissolve.

**What only the author can supply:** the answer to the approval question in finding 6, and the number
in finding 7. Neither is a drafting task. And one more that no reviewer can fabricate — §5 reports what
the first participant *did*, but the paper's own claim is that competence is a *conception* of the
work, not a behaviour. What did she think the gate was doing? The transcript may already hold it. If it
does, that sentence is worth more to this draft than any repair above, because it is the only place
where the construct and the data touch.
