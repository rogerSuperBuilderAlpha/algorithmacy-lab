# Non-specialist reader review — PAPER_gdoc_merged_2026-08-29.md

*Reviewer stance: a well-read non-academic. I read serious nonfiction and long-form journalism. I have no training in organization theory, communication studies, or psychometrics. I read the entire manuscript, appendices included.*

---

## Verdict

**With real effort.** There is a genuine, graspable, even timely idea at the center of this paper, and I can state it in one sentence. But I only got it because I pushed through roughly four pages of prose that seemed designed to keep me out, and the one scene that would have handed me the idea in thirty seconds is buried at the bottom of the second page. A smart outsider who gives this paper the benefit of the doubt will come away understanding the core claim. Most won't give it that benefit, because the title, the abstract, and the first two paragraphs all read as a wall.

---

## Step 0

This paper names and defines a new skill — "algorithmacy" — the knack for working with another person when a black-box algorithm sits between you, grading both of you by rules neither of you can see and that quietly change. I understood this at the classroom example near the end of the Introduction (a student's code is passed or failed by a hidden formula that blends her peers' visible reviews), and not one sentence earlier.

## Part 1 — Where I got lost, and where I didn't

I walk through the paper in reading order. Each item is marked for how badly it broke my reading: **[wall]** stopped me cold, **[fog]** slowed me down, **[works]** carried me.

### 1. The title. [wall]

"Algorithmacy: A Competency for Coordinative Co-optation." Three of the five content words are jargon, and one of them ("Algorithmacy") is jargon you invented. "Co-optation" means something to me — being bought off, absorbed by the thing you opposed — and I couldn't connect that meaning to anything in the paper for several pages, because your usage (borrowed, I eventually learned, from Stark and Vanden Broeck) means something closer to "conscripted by a platform." A newspaper reader parsing this title gets: *made-up word: a skill for jargon jargon.* I understand a workshop title has to signal its literature, but nothing on the title line tells me what the paper is about.

### 2. The abstract's first sentence. [fog]

> "Algorithmacy defines the interpretive sensibility an individual exercises when coordinating with another person through an opaque, adaptive algorithmic intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties."

This is actually the best sentence in the abstract — every piece is technically decodable — but it front-loads five abstractions ("interpretive sensibility," "coordinating," "opaque," "adaptive," "intermediary") before I have any picture to hang them on. I read it three times. The third time, after finishing the paper, it was fine. An abstract shouldn't require the paper as a prerequisite.

### 3. The rest of the abstract. [wall]

> "…the emerging form of coordinative co-optation remains theoretically incomplete, leaving the structural standing and individual capacity of the enrolled actor unmodeled."

"Structural standing," "enrolled actor," "unmodeled" — I have no referents for any of these yet. And this:

> "Rather than exploring legal or institutional standing/sovereignty, this article supplies the behavioral micro-foundation required to coordinate within such triadic architectures."

"Behavioral micro-foundation" and "triadic architectures" in one clause, with a "standing/sovereignty" slash-pair I can't unpack. I stopped trying to understand the abstract here and moved on, trusting the body to explain. It eventually did — but an abstract that a reader skips is a failed abstract.

### 4. The pilot analogy. [works — with a caveat]

> "This capacity resembles an experienced pilot operating an automated flight-control system: the primary task is neither manual flight from origin to destination nor passive reliance on automation, but the cognitive readiness to monitor automated drift, interpret subtle telemetry shifts, and intervene decisively when the system encounters boundary conditions."

This was the first moment I had a picture. Monitoring an autopilot rather than either flying or dozing — I get that immediately, and it's a good image for "a distinct mode of attention." The caveat, which I only noticed later: the analogy is missing the very thing the paper says makes its subject new. There's no *other person* in the cockpit picture. The autopilot isn't grading the pilot, and there's no second human on the far side of it. Once I understood the paper's actual claim — the triangle is the whole point — the paper's own opening analogy turned out to describe the two-party situation the paper spends twenty pages saying is *not* its subject. A lay reader who anchors on the pilot will anchor on the wrong shape.

### 5. The literacy / numeracy / oracy lineage. [works]

> "Paralleling the established developmental lineages of literacy, oracy, and numeracy, this article introduces *algorithmacy*…"

"Oracy" was new to me, but I could guess it from its siblings, and the family resemblance did real work: literacy is what you need when text mediates life; algorithmacy is what you need when algorithms do. This is the right kind of coinage-justification and it lands for an outsider. (The later paragraph admitting the analogy's limits — "Printed texts, datasets, and static software interfaces do not evaluate or execute binding determinations upon their users" — is honest and also readable. Good.)

### 6. The Introduction's first paragraph. [works]

"Organization theory lacks an account of how individuals coordinate with one another when an automated platform mediates their relationship." Plain claim, plain words. The hierarchies/markets/networks triad is explained in a single clause each ("hierarchies governed by managerial authority, markets governed by price mechanisms, networks governed by ongoing social relationships") — that's exactly how to hand an outsider a field's furniture. I followed this paragraph completely.

### 7. The satisficing sentence. [wall]

> "To resolve this vacancy, we formalize algorithmacy to a satisficing threshold in Simon's (1997) behavioral sense, defining the individual sensibility required to navigate this triadic structure without making unearned claims about broader institutional justice."

I do not know what it means to formalize something "to a satisficing threshold," and the sentence gives me no way to find out. I registered it as "we're being modest somehow" and moved on. If the point is "we define the skill without pretending to settle whether the arrangement is fair," say that — it's a good point and I only found it, in nearly those words, fifteen pages later.

### 8. The classroom example. [works — this is the moment]

> "A student submits a software pull request to an automated evaluation gate. While her peers' qualitative reviews and upvotes remain publicly visible, the algorithmic weighting that converts those inputs into an authoritative pass-fail verdict remains concealed from everyone: the submitter, the peer reviewers, and the instructor, who holds no vote. The focal actor must coordinate with human peers she can observe through an evaluative rule she cannot see."

This is where I understood the paper. One concrete scene, one closing sentence that compresses the whole idea — "coordinate with human peers she can observe through an evaluative rule she cannot see." Everything the abstract failed to give me is here, in four sentences, on page two. Why it works: named actors (a student, her peers, an instructor), a familiar setting, one visible thing and one hidden thing, and a final sentence that states the paradox in twelve plain words. This paragraph should be the paper's first paragraph. I'll come back to this in Part 3.

### 9. "The Coordination-Form Gap." [fog]

> "Mature organization theory defines a coordination form by specifying both its primary operational mechanism and the formal standing of the participants it governs."

I could parse this only because the bulleted list right after it rescues me — Hierarchy/Market/Network/Classical Co-optation, each with a one-line human picture (an employee taking direction, a buyer who can walk away, a partner in a relationship, an outsider given a seat at the table). The list works; the framing sentences around it ("Established scholarship evaluates coordination forms across six core dimensions: operational mechanics, required integrative deliverables, favoring conditions, primary instruments, failure modes, and empirical identification criteria") are a customs declaration I skimmed. And this section is where "co-optation" finally got defined for me — Selznick's outsider-given-a-seat — which retroactively explained a quarter of the title. That definition arrives too late.

### 10. The seven-construct review (Extant Constructs through Gig Literacies). [fog, ~10 pages of it — the single biggest drag on the reading experience]

This is the longest stretch of the paper and it nearly lost me. The structure is fine — seven rival frameworks, each shown to miss either the other human or the binding algorithm — and Table 1 is genuinely helpful; I used its "Structural Vacancy" column as a map. The prose is the problem. Every construct gets three to five paragraphs written at maximum density, in near-identical rhythm, with the same vocabulary recurring like a drumbeat: "structural," "constitutive," "vacancy," "boundary," "triadic," "operationalize," "analytical frame." Sample sentences that stopped me:

> "Coorientation and relationship development operate as irreducibly bilateral dynamics; appropriateness, defined as the 'perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context,' requires a conscious human perceiver; and satisfaction, operationalized as 'positive affect associated with fulfillment of positively valenced expectancies,' demands an interacting subject capable of affective resonance."

> "…reconfigures the technical apparatus as an active interlocutor across functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions."

> "Consequently, their framework conceptualizes competencies enacted adjacent to a digital intermediary rather than communicative and coordinating action executed through an opaque, binding triadic architecture."

I don't doubt these sentences are precise for their intended audience. But over ten pages of this register, my reading degraded from *understanding* to *pattern-matching*: I stopped decoding sentences and started scanning each subsection for its verdict.

What kept me going through this section — and this is worth the author noticing — was the buried concrete material:

- **The courier's appeal** (Zhou et al. discussion): "when confronted with an unjustified customer evaluation, a courier compiles objective documentation (such as audio recordings or timestamped delivery logs) and submits an administrative appeal directly to the platform's automated apparatus." A real person doing a real, slightly desperate thing. I understood the point — the customer who wronged him is never part of the process — instantly.
- **The invisible cage vs. the iron cage** (Rahman discussion): fixed legible bureaucratic rules versus shifting hidden ones. A vivid inherited metaphor, well used. "Even if a freelancer makes headway in uncovering one or a few components of the evaluation algorithm, other facets will remain opaque" — I felt that.
- **Freelancer P39** (Sutherland et al. discussion): a worker whose rating couldn't be fixed inside the platform "systematically migrated established client relationships off-platform to preserve economic viability." One person's escape route, and it perfectly illustrates the paper's contrast between escaping the algorithm and getting good at working through it.
- One abstract sentence in this section that genuinely worked for me, because it's a clean two-sided contrast in plain categories: "AI-mediated communication governs symbolic messages, whereas algorithmacy governs binding institutional verdicts." Messages versus verdicts — I can hold that.

Every one of those concrete moments is somebody else's field data, retold. They are also the only oxygen in the section.

### 11. "What These Boundaries Share." [fog, one bright spot]

> "Demarcating this theoretical boundary requires satisfying four analytical conditions that distinguish triadic coordination from adjacent organizational models."

By this point sentences like that were washing over me. But the **Lima drivers** paragraph woke me up completely: ride-hailing drivers "systematically interpret opaque platform metrics to infer passenger trustworthiness and ensure physical safety." Reading a black-box score to decide whether it's safe to pick someone up — that's the paper's skill, deployed to stay alive. It's the single most compelling piece of evidence in the manuscript that this skill is real and matters, and it gets three sentences at the tail of a section most readers will be skimming by then.

### 12. The construct section opener. [wall]

> "Construct clarity within organizational theory requires establishing a precise definition, delineating explicit scope conditions, demarcating semantic relationships with neighboring constructs, and demonstrating theoretical coherence (Suddaby, 2010)."

A sentence about the rules for writing the sentences that follow. Pure inside baseball; I skipped it. Likewise: "This conceptual independence holds because the constitutive operations of algorithmacy remain invariant across candidate resolutions of institutional standing." I *think* this means "the skill is needed whatever legal status the worker has," and the following sentences confirm it (the hearing-right example is actually good), but the topic sentence itself is unreadable.

### 13. The three operations. [works]

Interpreting, specifying intent, keeping track. The trio is the graspable heart of the paper, and the bulleted definitions, though wordy, decode: figure out what the black box and the hidden person meant; phrase what you say so it survives the machine and still reaches the person; notice when the rules move. The best supporting passage is the dual-audience one:

> "A single communicative act, such as drafting the rationale for a technical contribution or submitting an administrative proposal, must satisfy the computational intermediary's parsing rules and the human counterpart's interpretive expectations concurrently…"

Writing one paragraph for two readers, one of whom is a machine that grades you — anyone who has ever tuned a résumé for the applicant-tracking software *and* the hiring manager knows this problem in their bones. The paper never reaches for an everyday anchor like that, and it should.

The *keeping track* justification also works: hidden rule changes "register only through altered counterpart behavior" — you learn the algorithm changed by noticing the people changed. That's a genuinely interesting observation, stated almost plainly.

### 14. "Semantic Relations and Hypothesized Covariation." [wall — and I knew it wasn't for me]

"Nomological network," predicted correlation bands, HTMT ratios later in Appendix C. I recognized this as machinery for a different reader and skipped it without guilt. Fine — but note that from here to the Empirical Strategy, the paper alternates between specialist machinery and the occasional plain paragraph, and the plain paragraphs are what carried me.

### 15. The propositions — where the stakes finally show up. [works, but buried]

Does the paper ever say why a normal person should care? Yes — twice, both late, both under anti-invitational headings. First, under "A Paradox the Construct Raises Rather Than Resolves": being good at this skill helps *you* but fixes nothing — "individual mastery compensates for structural deficits without altering the underlying opacity of the regime." Second, Proposition 2: because the skill is unevenly distributed among workers the platform treats as identical, it creates "persistent earnings, safety, and evaluation disparities that platforms attribute to individual merit rather than structural informational asymmetries." In plain words: there is a hidden skill sorting gig workers into winners and losers, the platform calls the outcome merit, and nobody can see the sorting. *That* is a story an educated outsider cares about — it touches inequality, AI, and work all at once — and it appears on roughly page fifteen, phrased as a numbered proposition. Proposition 3 adds the policy hook: make the algorithms more transparent and people need less of this compensatory skill. Stakes exist. They are hidden about as well as the platform's weighting formula.

### 16. Empirical Strategy and the pilot anecdote. [mostly fog, one unforgettable moment]

The methods prose is heavy going ("the core action research project" vs. "the formal research project," insider positionality, abductive coding — I trusted rather than followed it). But this stopped me cold, in the good way:

> "…a participant navigating the intent-specification module resolved the communicative constraint by routing their explanation through an external language model to optimize parsing legibility, entirely bypassing direct engagement with the human peer counterpart."

A student, asked to explain their work to a human peer through the system, instead fed their explanation to an AI to make it *machine*-readable and skipped the human altogether. That is the most 2026 sentence in the paper, it's from the author's own pilot, and it complicates the paper's own construct in a way that feels alive: the person under study responded to a human-plus-machine audience by optimizing for the machine and adding *another* machine. I would open a magazine article with this. Here it's one sentence in a subsection called "Instruments and Status," restated later in Appendix B as "Identification of Secondary Intermediary Routing" — a phrase that does to that story what the weighting algorithm does to peer reviews.

### 17. Appendix B's program history. [works — and made me trust the author]

Trinidad and Tobago secondary schools; a youth program called GauntleTT in La Brea run with a Member of Parliament, a ministry, and a community policing initiative called Hearts and Minds; a Boston open-enrollment sprint; then Hult. This is the only place the paper feels like it was written by someone who *did something* — two years of running real cohorts in real places — rather than by someone arranging citations. None of this texture reaches the main text. The main text says "piloted across developmental cohorts," which is what "we taught coding to teenagers in Trinidad" looks like after the algorithm compresses it.

### The graspable idea, stated

Yes, there is one, and here is my one-sentence version: **When an algorithm neither of you can see or appeal sits between you and the person you're working with — grading you both, matching you both, and changing its rules without notice — getting things done takes a distinct, learnable skill: reading the black box's verdicts for what the hidden person meant, writing for the machine and the human at once, and noticing when the rules have silently moved.** I could not have written that sentence from the abstract. I wrote it from the classroom paragraph, the three operations, and the courier.

## Part 2 — Voice audit

**Where a real person is visible:**

- The classroom pull-request scene (Introduction). Someone watched this happen. The detail that the *instructor has no vote* is the kind of thing you only include if it struck you in real life.
- The pilot anecdote of the student routing their peer explanation through an LLM. Nobody invents that detail; it has the specific wrongness of the real.
- The Lima drivers reading opaque metrics for physical safety, and the courier with his audio recordings — borrowed field data, but chosen by someone with an eye for the human particular.
- Appendix B's cohort history — La Brea, GauntleTT, Hearts and Minds, the note that the Trinidad iteration didn't retain logs and had to be reconstructed retrospectively. That last admission is the single most human methodological sentence in the paper.
- The honest self-limitations, when stated plainly: "The literacy analogy supplies historical grounding rather than operational architecture." "This article formulates the theoretical architecture… rather than its definitive empirical validation." A person deciding what not to claim.

**Where it reads as generated filler:**

- The register never moves. From the abstract to Appendix C, nearly every sentence has the same shape: abstract-noun subject, Latinate verb, two or three stacked modifiers, a citation. "Demarcating this theoretical boundary requires satisfying four analytical conditions…" and "Construct clarity within organizational theory requires establishing a precise definition, delineating explicit scope conditions, demarcating semantic relationships…" could swap papers with a thousand others. Real thinking has variable pressure — it speeds up where the writer is sure and slows where they're working something out. This prose is at uniform pressure throughout, which is exactly what machine-assisted academic writing sounds like, whatever its actual provenance.
- The paper repeats its thesis formula — "an opaque, adaptive intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties" / "an active human counterpart and an opaque, binding intermediary" — something like fifteen times in nearly identical wording. The first three times, it's a definition. By the tenth, it reads as a template token being pasted, and I started skipping any sentence containing "opaque" and "binding" in proximity, which means I started skipping the thesis.
- Sentences that narrate the paper's own virtue rather than saying anything: "To maintain methodological transparency, two specific operational parameters of the gate mechanism warrant explicit identification…" — just identify them. "The methodological dualism is complementary rather than contradictory" — a sentence reassuring me about a tension I hadn't felt.
- Section openers that are about the act of writing the section: the Suddaby sentence, "Evaluating candidate constructs for algorithmic coordination requires analyzing the precise structural role each framework assigns to the computational intermediary." These read as scaffolding left standing after the building was done.

The paper is most alive exactly where it touches a particular person doing a particular thing, and most dead where it defends territory. Roughly nine-tenths of it defends territory.

## Part 3 — My top three asks

### Ask 1 (top pick): Open the paper with the classroom scene, and let the definition arrive as its punchline.

The paragraph that made me understand the paper is stranded at the end of the Introduction, after five paragraphs of abstraction it would have rendered unnecessary. Move it to the front, sharpen it slightly, and let every abstract term be introduced *after* the reader has its referent. Suggested rewrite of the paper's opening (replacing the current first paragraph of "Orientation and Theoretical Motivation"):

> A student submits her code for review. Her classmates read it, comment on it, and vote — all in public, where she can watch. Then an algorithm none of them can inspect weighs those votes by a hidden formula and returns a verdict: pass or fail. Her instructor holds no vote and cannot override the outcome. The formula, meanwhile, shifts as the shared codebase grows. To succeed, she must coordinate with people she can see through a rule she cannot — reading the algorithm's verdicts for what her peers meant, writing explanations that a machine will parse and a person will understand, and noticing when the hidden rule has quietly changed.
>
> A growing share of work now runs through arrangements like this one. The Uber driver and her passenger, the freelancer and his client, the seller and her buyer are matched, scored, and sometimes expelled by systems neither party can see and neither party controls. Organization theory has names for coordinating through a boss, a price, or a relationship. It has no name for the skill this student is exercising. This paper names it — *algorithmacy* — and defines what it consists of.

Everything currently in the opening — the pilot analogy included — can follow or go. (I'd let the pilot analogy go: it lacks the second human, and once you open with the student you don't need it.)

### Ask 2: Define "coordinative co-optation" in one plain sentence the first time it appears — or keep the term out of the title and abstract.

The term carries the paper's whole framing and is never glossed for anyone who hasn't read Paper 1 or Stark and Vanden Broeck. One sentence would do it, placed at first use in the Introduction, e.g.: *"Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) call this fifth arrangement co-optation: the platform does not hire you, buy from you, or befriend you — it enrolls you, on terms it sets, scores, and can revoke."* Selznick's classical sense (the outsider given a seat at the table) should be glossed in the same breath, since a lay reader's intuition for "co-opt" points at Selznick and then misfires on your usage. And reconsider the title: "Algorithmacy: The Skill of Working Through an Algorithm That Judges You Both" is not less accurate than the current title; it is only less armored.

### Ask 3: Give every construct in the seven-construct review a one-sentence plain-language verdict up front, and promote the stakes to the Introduction.

Two connected fixes for the middle third, which is where outsiders will be lost:

(a) Open each of the seven subsections with a single plain sentence an outsider can hold — the deep engagement can follow unchanged. Model, for the Spitzberg section: *"The classic theory of online communication skill assumes the technology is just a channel — a phone line between two people who judge each other directly; it has no place for a channel that is also the judge."* For Zhou et al.: *"The best existing measure of algorithmic skill tests how well a courier reads the platform — but in its model, the customer is only a rating, never a person to be understood."* Table 1 proves the author can compress this way; the prose should inherit the table's clarity, not fight it.

(b) The paper's real stakes — a hidden skill sorts formally identical workers into winners and losers while the platform calls it merit, and Lima drivers use this skill to stay physically safe — currently live in Proposition 2 and a late literature paragraph. Two sentences carrying that content belong in the Introduction, where they would answer the question every non-specialist asks by page two: why does naming this skill matter? It matters because you cannot measure, teach, or compensate for an inequality that has no name.

## Closing note

The most real moment in the paper is the student who, asked to make her explanation legible to a human peer through the machine, fed it to a second machine instead and never addressed the peer at all. It is the paper's thesis, its complication, and its era, in one observed behavior — and the author saw it happen in his own pilot.

The thing only the author can supply: the paper's examples of real people are all borrowed — Rahman's freelancers, Zhou's couriers, Manky's drivers. But the author spent two years running these gates himself, from secondary schools in Trinidad to La Brea to Boston to Hult, and the main text contains not one moment from any of it. One student, named or not — what she submitted, what the gate said, what she did next, what she said about it — from Trinidad or La Brea, told in the main text with the specificity the LLM-routing sentence proves he can command, would do more to convince a reader that algorithmacy exists than the entire seven-construct review. No reviewer can invent her. He was in the room.
