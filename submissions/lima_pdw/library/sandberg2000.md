## What it argues

The rationalistic approaches that dominate competence research treat human competence at work as a specific set of attributes — the knowledge and skills used in performing a particular job — and then set about listing them. Sandberg argues that this inverts the order of explanation. Using phenomenography, an interpretative method that maps the qualitatively different ways people conceive of a phenomenon, he studies engine optimizers and finds three distinct conceptions of the work. The conceptions are not variants of one competence held in different amounts; each delimits a different set of attributes as essential and organizes them into a different structure. The abstract states the result directly: the meaning work takes on for workers in their experience of it, rather than a specific set of attributes, is what constitutes competence, and the particular way of conceiving of work delimits certain attributes as essential and organizes them into a distinctive structure of competence at work. The methodological consequence is that an attribute inventory cannot be the starting point, because which attributes matter is downstream of how the work is understood.

## Relation to the argument

§8 makes this one of two anchors for the word "competency," and it is the anchor doing the harder work. Paper 2's first facet, asymmetric interpretation, is not a quantity of understanding a worker has about the system; it is a conception of what the system is doing, and the sentence §8 rests on Sandberg — "the worker's competence *is* her conception of what the system is doing" — is a direct application of his result rather than an analogy. The fit runs deeper than the citation currently shows. Sandberg's finding that different conceptions organize different attribute sets is the theoretical warrant for §7's admission rule, which decides what counts as a facet by what the form withholds rather than by what workers can be observed doing; both refuse to start from an inventory. It also underwrites §12's caution about the instrument, since a scale built on items assumes a single conception across respondents, and Sandberg's whole point is that the assumption can fail within one job at one site. Verdict: promote from anchor to warrant — this is the source that licenses the derivation discipline, not just the choice of noun.

## Caution

Abstract-and-secondary grounded; the three conceptions are reported in the secondary literature but their content is not verified here, and the study site is described as engine optimizers without the employer confirmed from the source. A sharper tension deserves stating: Sandberg's method is interpretative and his conclusion is that competence is constituted in conception, which sits awkwardly beside §11's three-wave panel with a fixed item set. Paper 2 can hold both — the facets are derived from the form and the instrument measures their expression — but a reviewer trained in the interpretative tradition will read the pairing as borrowing Sandberg's authority for a construct and then discarding his method. §12 is the place to say which parts of the account a variance design can and cannot carry.
