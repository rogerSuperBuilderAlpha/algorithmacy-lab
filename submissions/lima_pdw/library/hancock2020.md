## What it argues

The article defines AI-Mediated Communication as "interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals," and distinguishes it from computer-mediated communication, where the technology transmits messages a human authored. Smart replies, predictive text and emerging video manipulation are the worked examples. AI-MC is then dimensionalized: magnitude, from minor correction to whole-message generation; media type, across text, audio and video; optimization goal, since these systems are built to achieve interpersonal outcomes such as appearing trustworthy or attractive; autonomy, from user-selected suggestions to fully autonomous operation; role orientation, targeting the sender's production or the receiver's analysis; and synchronicity, covering asynchronous and real-time exchange.

The agenda has four strands — the design of AI-MC systems and the questions of agency attribution and disclosure they raise; effects on language, including alignment, norms and dialect homogenization; interpersonal dynamics such as self-presentation, impression formation, trust and intimacy; and policy, culture and ethics. Three ethical considerations close it: bias and fairness, since training on existing communication may ossify social power structures; transparency, since it is unclear when disclosure of AI involvement is required; and manipulation, since the boundary between acceptable self-presentation and deception shifts by context. The article's stated purpose is to force a re-evaluation and possible expansion of CMC's key theories now that an agent sits inside the exchange.

## Relation to the argument

§9 files this as the construct that makes the algorithm **an agent serving the worker**, acting on her behalf and augmenting or modifying what she sends, and the definition read verbatim supports that exactly — "operates on behalf of a communicator" is the source's own phrase and the one §9's clause paraphrases. It is also the strongest of the five for §9's dyad diagnosis, because AI-MC is the case where a third element genuinely enters the exchange and *still* resolves into two positions: the agent is folded into the sender's side rather than standing between the parties, so the account has a sender-plus-agent and a receiver. Paper 2's triad differs precisely there. On a platform the system does not act on the worker's behalf; it interprets both parties and commits determinations neither endorsed, which is a position no dimension in this paper's list can hold. The autonomy and role-orientation dimensions are worth naming in the manuscript if the clause is ever expanded, since they are where AI-MC comes closest and where the difference is cleanest. Verdict: correctly placed, and the best available foil — the construct that puts an agent in the exchange and still cannot reach the third position.
