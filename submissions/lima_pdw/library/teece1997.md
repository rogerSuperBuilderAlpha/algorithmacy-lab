## What it argues

The dynamic capabilities framework asks how private firms create and capture wealth under rapid technological change. Competitive advantage is located in distinctive processes — the firm's ways of coordinating and combining — shaped by its asset positions, notably its portfolio of difficult-to-trade knowledge assets and complementary assets, and by the evolutionary paths it has adopted or inherited. Path dependence is amplified where increasing returns hold. Whether an advantage erodes depends on the stability of market demand and on the ease of replication internally and imitation by competitors. The framework's normative upshot is stated sharply in the abstract: under rapid technological change, wealth creation depends far more on honing internal technological, organizational and managerial processes than on strategizing in the sense of keeping rivals off balance, raising their costs and excluding entrants.

## Relation to the argument

§8 uses this as the head of the category it must refuse. The word "capability" carries three commitments Paper 2 cannot accept for algorithmacy — the firm as the unit, the routine as the thing held, and firm-level advantage as the explanandum — and Teece, Pisano and Shuen state all three about as cleanly as the literature does. The abstract's own language helps: processes, asset positions and paths are the *firm's*, and the individual enters only through microfoundations as a component of a firm-level capacity. That is what lets §8 make its possession objection without caricature. Verdict: cite as the canonical statement of the capability class, for the purpose of being ruled out; the exclusion is structural, not a criticism of the framework.

## Caution

The card rests on the abstract, which is unusually informative for this paper but still an abstract; the microfoundations claim §8 leans on ("its microfoundations treat the individual as a component of a firm-level capacity") is a fair reading of the framework's subsequent development rather than something the 1997 abstract says, and the later microfoundations literature — Teece's own 2007 paper and after — is where that claim is actually made. If §8's sentence is challenged, the 1997 paper is not where the answer is. Note also that Paper 2 cites this alongside Barney (1991) as though the resource-based view and dynamic capabilities were one position; they are related but distinct, and Teece's framework was in part a response to the static character of the resource-based account. The distinction does not damage §8's argument, since both are firm-level and possession-based, but the sentence should not imply they are the same claim.
