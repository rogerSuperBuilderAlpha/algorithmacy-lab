## What it argues

Spitzberg builds a model of CMC competence along five lines — motivation, knowledge, skills, context and outcomes — and offers it explicitly as "a metaphorical typology for organizing existing CMC research" and "a first step in examining individual differences in the domain of CMC relationships and media choice." Motivation is "the ratio of approach to avoidance attitudes, beliefs, and values in a given CMC context." Knowledge is "the cognitive comprehension of content and procedural processes involved in conducting appropriate and effective interaction in the computer-mediated context." Skills resolve into four clusters — attentiveness, composure, coordination, expressiveness. Context has five facets: cultural, chronological, relational, environmental and functional. The outcome criteria are appropriateness ("the perceived legitimacy or fit of a message to the context"), effectiveness ("the degree to which preferred objectives are optimized"), satisfaction, coorientation and efficiency.

The measurement half is candid about its own state. An a priori measure was written from the model and revised across studies by Harper, Bubaš, Bunz, and Van Slooten and Spitzberg; negatively worded items attenuated reliability, especially in short subscales, and the context, message and media factors proved less multidimensional than the model anticipated. The measure was then simplified for reliability and parsimony and, at the time of writing, "is at present being prepared for data collection." The only psychometric result reported is from an online Croatian study, where factor-analyzing all items yielded four reliable factors roughly paralleling motivation, knowledge, skills and outcomes. No sample sizes, no alphas, and no convergent, discriminant or predictive validity evidence are given. The author states that research on the model "is nascent" and that the theory does not compete with theories of media effects, applying to interpersonal rather than mass communication.

## Relation to the argument

The source supports that reading exactly. Every component is defined relative to a human interlocutor reached *through* a medium, and the outcome criteria are judged on the message rather than on the mediation, so the third position Paper 2 needs — a system that interprets both parties and commits determinations neither endorsed — has no place to sit. §8 also cites it as "the nearest prior case of a competence construct built for a mediated channel," and that is right, with the emphasis on *channel*.
