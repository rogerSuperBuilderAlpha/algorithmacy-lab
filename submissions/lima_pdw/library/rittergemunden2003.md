## What it argues

Firms with close ties to customers, suppliers, research institutes and competitors innovate more successfully, and the authors ask what makes some firms able to build and exploit such ties when others are not. Their answer is a company-specific ability they call network competence, defined as a two-dimensional construct: "(a) the degree of network management task execution and (b) the extent of network management qualifications possessed by the people handling a company's relationships" (p. 748). Tasks divide into relationship-specific work on a single dyad and cross-relational work on the portfolio, the latter organised as planning, organising, staffing and controlling. Qualifications divide into specialist and social, where the social half — self-reflectiveness, sense of justice, cooperativeness — is singled out because business relations run on interpersonal interaction. Task execution and qualification are held to be mutually dependent, which is the stated reason for combining them into one construct rather than two.

A LISREL analysis of 308 German mechanical and electrical engineering companies supports the model. Network competence raises the extent of interorganizational technological collaboration and raises product and process innovation success, so the construct improves both the means and the ends. Four organizational antecedents predict it: access to resources, network orientation of human resource management, integration of intraorganizational communication, and openness of corporate culture. The discussion draws the conclusion that networking is a company-wide responsibility — "the ability to manage in networks is inseparable from the company itself" — and that developing network competence is a firm's choice, while being embedded in a network is not.

## Relation to the argument

This is the load-bearing precedent for Paper 2's §4, and for the paper's central move. The seventh question — what competency does a coordination form demand of the party it coordinates? — is answered in exactly one of the three settled columns, and this is the paper that answers it. §4 uses it twice: as proof that the question is answerable at all, and as the template for what Paper 2 then does for co-optation ("this paper does for co-optation what the network literature did for networks"). The construct's internal shape is also a usable model, because Ritter and Gemünden derive their dimensions from what network coordination requires — reading a counterpart, sustaining a relation without contract or command — rather than from what practitioners report doing, which is the same derivation discipline §7 applies through the admission rule. The four antecedents are a further parallel: they are conditions of the organization, not of the individual, which is the structural reading Paper 2 gives the withheld conditions. Verdict: cite as the named precedent for the row, and as the closest available model of a competency read off a coordination form's demands.

## Caution

**The construct is firm-level, and Paper 2's row is not.** Ritter and Gemünden measure "a company's degree of network competence," survey companies, and place the qualifications in "the people handling a company's relationships" rather than in a coordinated party as such. Paper 2's §4 table asks what each form demands of the coordinated party and answers "Yes, for three decades" in the network row; that is defensible when the coordinated party in a network is itself a firm, but §8 then commits algorithmacy to the individual, and the precedent does not carry the level with it. What transfers is the form of the argument, not the unit. Say so, or a reviewer who knows this literature will say it first. Beyond that: the sample is German engineering firms, the design is cross-sectional and self-report, innovation success is self-assessed, and the LISREL fit licenses consistency with the model rather than causal direction.
