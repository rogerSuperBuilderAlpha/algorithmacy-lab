## What it argues

AI is increasingly embedded in user-facing technology while public understanding of it stays thin, and the paper treats that gap as an HCI problem with two halves: what competencies users need in order to interact with and critically evaluate AI, and how to design learner-centred AI that builds those competencies. The method is synthesis — interdisciplinary literature read into a conceptual framework organized thematically — rather than empirical study. The output is a definition and two lists. AI literacy is "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace." Seventeen core competencies follow, running from Recognizing AI — "distinguish between technological artifacts that use and do not use AI" — and Understanding Intelligence — critically analyzing what makes an entity intelligent, including differences among human, animal and machine intelligence — through data literacy, the human role in AI, and the steps of machine learning, to Ethics, which asks the learner to identify and describe different perspectives on privacy, employment, misinformation, the singularity, ethical decision making, diversity, bias, transparency and accountability, and Programmability, "understand that agents are programmable." A set of design considerations accompanies them — explainability, embodied interactions, contextualizing data, promoting transparency, unveiling gradually, opportunities to program, milestones, critical thinking, identity and values, support for parents, social interaction, learners' interests, acknowledging preconceptions, new perspectives, and a low barrier to entry.

## Relation to the argument

§9 files this as the construct that makes the algorithm **an object of knowledge**, and the reconstruction confirms the reading down to the wording: the competencies are things a person knows or can discuss *about* AI systems, and the paper's own examples ask a learner to distinguish, analyze, identify and describe. §9's dismissal — "knowing how a recommender works predicts nothing about getting a counterpart to receive what you meant" — is fair to the source, since nothing in the seventeen addresses a third party. The framework also supplies §9's structural point in its cleanest form. AI literacy's second clause, "communicate and collaborate effectively with AI," puts the machine in the counterpart's chair, so the construct is not merely silent about the triad but committed to a dyad by its own definition. Verdict: correctly placed and doing real work; if the clause is expanded, quote the definition, because the phrase "collaborate effectively with AI" makes §9's case without argument.

## Caution

Not read in the original, and the count is contested in the citing literature: Gu and Ericson report seventeen and Marx and colleagues quote a "Competency 17," while an ACM-submitted review of generative-AI literacy states sixteen. Seventeen is the better-supported number and is what this card uses, but verify against the paper before it is printed. The full list of seventeen is not reconstructed here — seven are named. Two further notes. Marx and colleagues, who accept the framework, criticize this class of literacy framework for being "not yet formalized through rigorous research, not tied to behavior-based competency models, nor assessed with validated measures"; that criticism is useful to §9 but cuts toward Paper 2 as well, since algorithmacy is at the same stage. And the AI-literacy line §9 addresses is now more crowded than the single citation implies, with several validated scales published since 2023; the manuscript's own rival-scale problem sits here too, alongside the Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang and Hou (2025) algorithmic-competency scale that `literature/REFERENCES.md` flags.
