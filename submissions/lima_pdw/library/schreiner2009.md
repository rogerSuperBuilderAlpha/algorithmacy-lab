## What it argues

Two streams had grown up around alliance capability — one on how firms develop it, one on what it consists of — and the second had received the lesser share of attention. This paper takes up the second. Building on prior research and the authors' own fieldwork, it conceptualizes alliance management capability as a multidimensional construct comprising three distinct but related skills for managing an individual alliance once it is running: coordination, communication and bonding. The conceptualization is then tested in a framework linking the capability to outcomes at both the alliance and the firm level, using survey and secondary data from a large sample of interfirm relationships between software service providers and three major global software vendors. The authors report general empirical support for the three-part conceptualization and for its predictive validity against certain alliance outcomes.

## Relation to the argument

§4 cites this beside Ritter and Gemünden as the second named construct in the network column, and it does that work: it is a second, independently developed, measured answer to the question of what a party needs in order to coordinate through relations that neither price nor command secures. Its three dimensions are also close in spirit to Paper 2's derivation discipline, since coordination, communication and bonding are read off what an ongoing alliance requires rather than assembled from a competence inventory. ## Caution

**It is a capability, and §8 rules capability out of the family.** §8's decisive objection is possession: "a capability is held by the party that owns the routine, and this party owns nothing." Schreiner, Kale and Corsten are explicit that alliance management capability is a firm's, held and developed by the firm and predicting firm-level advantage — which is precisely the class §8 excludes. Citing it in §4 as evidence that the seventh question is answerable, and then in §8 excluding its whole category, is not a contradiction, but the two sections are using the same word in ways a reader will notice. Either mark the §4 citation as firm-level precedent for the *form of the question* rather than for a competency, or add half a sentence in §8 acknowledging that the network column's precedents are capabilities and saying why algorithmacy cannot be one. Also: the card rests on the abstract alone, so the three dimensions' operational definitions, the sample size, and which outcomes the capability did *not* predict are unverified here — "certain alliance outcomes" is the abstract's own hedge and should not be smoothed over when the paper is cited.
