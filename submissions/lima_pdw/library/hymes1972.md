## What it argues

Hymes writes against Chomsky's competence/performance distinction, which had confined competence to "tacit knowledge of language structure" (p. 271) and consigned actual speech to performance as an "imperfect manifestation of underlying system" (p. 272). He reads that restriction as "almost a declaration of irrelevance" for sociolinguistics (p. 270), one that "omits almost everything of sociocultural significance" (p. 280), and argues that rules of use are acquired alongside structural knowledge rather than grafted on later (p. 279). Since rules imply competence, a theory that admits stylistic rules of performance has already conceded the point.

What he puts in place of the binary is a richer competence with four kinds of knowledge, each paired with an "ability for use": what is *possible* (systemic possibility, grammaticality in syntax but also in culture and communication), what is *feasible* (psycholinguistic reality, constraints on memory and perception), what is *appropriate* (situational judgement, acceptability in context), and what is *performed* (actual occurrences). The ability-for-use side gathers motivation, affective and volitive factors, and capacities in interaction such as composure and presence of mind. Performance is then a third category rather than the other half of a pair: actual use and events, shaped by the interaction between an individual's competence, the competence of others, and the properties of the event. The essay's stated aim is a linguistic theory addressing "the capacities of persons, the organisation of verbal means for socially defined purposes, and the sensitivity of rules to situations" (p. 292).

## Relation to the argument

§8 cites Hymes for the competence/performance distinction that licenses the name "competency" — what a party is *able* to do in a coordination, as against what she did on any occasion. The source supports that, and supports more of Paper 2 than the citation claims. Hymes's third dimension, appropriateness in context, is a competence about a *situation* rather than about a system, which is the distinction §9 needs when it separates algorithmacy from AI literacy: knowing how a recommender works is knowledge of an object, while judging what is appropriate here is knowledge of a coordination. His insistence that performance depends jointly on the individual's competence, the competence of others, and the properties of the event is the closest thing in the lineage to §9's triad complaint — the counterpart is in Hymes's account from the start, and the two-position constructs §9 criticises drop what he had. Verdict: the warrant is real and currently under-drawn; one clause in §8 could carry the appropriateness dimension and one in §9 could claim descent for the counterpart's presence.

## Caution

Not read in the original. Every quotation above comes through Whyte (2019), a specialist source that cites pages and states that its table's wording derives from Hymes's text, but it is a source with its own purpose — language teaching and assessment for specific purposes — and it presents Hymes in order to argue that later applied-linguistics adaptations simplified him. That is convenient for Paper 2's use and should therefore be treated with a little suspicion rather than none. Before any of the page-cited quotations enters the manuscript, the chapter itself must be checked; pagination also varies across printings, and secondary sources disagree over whether the chapter ends at 285 or 293. Paper 2's reference list gives 269–293, which matches the range the fuller secondary sources use, so the entry itself is sound; what is unverified is the page attached to any quotation drawn from it.
