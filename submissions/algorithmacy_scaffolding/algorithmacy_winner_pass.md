# The Politics of Algorithmacy: Navigating Coordinative Cooptation from the Docket to the Screen

In controversies surrounding the expansion of algorithmic systems into contemporary life, there is no prescription offered with greater regularity, or with greater moral self-satisfaction, than the demand for "literacy." Faced with platforms that invisibly triage news, opaque language models that simulate fluent reasoning, and dispatch engines that manage laboring bodies across urban space, educators, policymakers, and technologists converge upon a common palliative: if only the public were taught to read the code, inspect the dataset, or polish the syntax of their prompts, the balance of power would be restored.

This view has an obvious wisdom. It serves as a necessary corrective to the mystification of practical artifice, puncturing the naive fatalism that treats complex software as an incomprehensible force of nature. Those who do not perceive that computational systems are manufactured artifacts, designed and deployed by specific institutions to advance specific interests, have not understood the problem at all.

Yet I take the corrective to suffer a disabling flaw: it mistakes an engine of coordination for a sheet of inscribed text. Text sits passively upon a page, awaiting the interpretive faculties of an autonomous reader. A platform does nothing of the sort. Platforms do not merely convey signs; they enroll participants, solicit their actions, extract their behavioral exhaust, and steer their conduct toward synthetic optimization targets whose parameters remain hidden. To insist that an individual confronting such an apparatus requires "literacy" is rather like advising a defendant entering an adversarial courtroom that what they truly need is a primer on phonics. Survival inside an active system of governance is not the same skill as deciphering an inert symbol.

When Andrew Wilkinson sought in 1965 to elevate the study of spoken language, he recognized that existing categories were insufficient. He coined the term *oracy* to sit alongside *literacy* and *numeracy*, designating a distinct physical and cognitive capacity to produce and interpret the spoken word (Wilkinson, 1965). Today, the pervasive installation of algorithmic coordination across public and private life demands a corresponding addition to our lexicon. I propose the concept of **algorithmacy**. Where literacy is the sensibility required to navigate an inscribed text, and numeracy the sensibility to parse numerical relations, algorithmacy is the know-how required to read, contest, and steer systems that enroll people by co-opting their agency—*coordinative cooptation*, in the sense developed below.

This competence is not computational thinking in the narrow sense of writing code. It is the capacity to deduce an invisible optimization target from its behavioral residues, to anticipate self-reinforcing feedback loops, to maintain an accurate mental model of an opaque execution environment, and to resist the subtle adaptations of human ends to technical means.

In what follows I trace the genealogy, cognitive architecture, and institutional affordances of this sensibility. I begin by examining coordinative cooptation as an organizational form, drawing upon David Stark's taxonomy of organizational coordination. I then turn to the cognitive sciences to establish that the gulf between novices and experts in literacy, oracy, and numeracy follows a remarkably consistent pattern—a pattern that illuminates why novices are so systematically subjugated within algorithmic regimes.

To demonstrate that this dynamic is not a novel invention of Silicon Valley, I examine three historical antecedents: the procedural machinery of the Anglo-American common-law courtroom, the administrative allocation of state largess, and the computerized foreign exchange trading rooms that replaced the gold standard. Finally, confronting recent human-computer interaction research, I show that naive transparency interventions fail where tested, and I advance four architectural affordances designed to scaffold novice users into genuine algorithmic agency.

## Coordinative Cooptation as an Organizational Form

In modern organizational sociology, coordination has long been understood through a classical triad: hierarchies command, markets price, and networks collaborate. As David Stark and Pieter Vanden Broeck (2024) have observed, however, digital platforms operate through an organizational logic that fits none of these descriptions: **platforms co-opt**.

The pedigree of the term belongs to Philip Selznick, who in his classic 1949 study of the Tennessee Valley Authority defined cooptation as "the process of absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence" (Selznick, 1949, pp. 13–16). Selznick distinguished between *formal* cooptation—in which outside groups are granted the public appearance of participation while the real locus of administrative decision remains tightly held—and *informal* cooptation, wherein organized community factions (such as the Farm Bureau) master the agency's internal machinery and turn its policies toward their own ends (Selznick, 1949).

The platform generalizes this absorption, but strips away its representative apparatus. As Stark and Vanden Broeck explain, the modern platform operator "benefits from [the user's] agency without her acting as its agent... The platform operator leverages rather than delegates" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). The platform does not issue bureaucratic commands, for it retains no employment relation with its vast armies of contributors and drivers. It does not merely clear a market at a competitive price, for it sets rates, suppresses counter-offers, and manipulates the matching mechanism. Nor does it foster network collaboration based on mutual trust and reciprocity. Instead, it enrolls external, autonomous human beings into an architecture that extracts their energy, steers their decisions, and monetizes their transactions, all while rendering institutional accountability "distributed, deflected, and denied" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14).

This arrangement marks a sharp departure from the organizational forms that preceded it. In earlier work, Stark (2009) demonstrated that innovative enterprises thrive on *heterarchy*—the lateral coexistence of multiple, rival orders of worth. In a heterarchy, actors negotiate among competing evaluative frameworks (market efficiency, professional pride, technical elegance, civic virtue). The human competence rewarded in such settings is dissonance-tolerance: the capacity to translate between disparate registers and broker compromise among incommensurable values.

Platforms systematically extinguish this heterarchical friction. They collapse all plural evaluations onto a singular, synthetically computed metric—a five-star rating, a driver acceptance percentage, a 100-point Job Success Score, or an opaque engagement coefficient. In place of human negotiation over what is worthy, the platform substitutes what A. Aneesh (2009) terms *algocracy*: rule by code that leaves "an environment in which there are only programmed alternatives" (Aneesh, 2009). Authority is embedded in the software's interface; because there is no visible human commander to petition and no alternative pathway through the screen, the system demands no political legitimacy in the traditional sense. The bank teller, the delivery courier, and the social media consumer simply comply because the software makes any other mode of conduct structurally impossible.

To survive within such an apparatus without being wholly co-opted, an individual must possess algorithmacy. Where network savvy rewards the cultivation of personal relationships, algorithmacy rewards the interpretive modeling of a non-human, running computation. It is the competence to look upon a platform's singular score or procedural funnel, discern the unstated optimization target that generated it, anticipate how that target shifts in response to user behavior, and construct counter-strategies from within the programmed alternatives.

## The Cognitive Architecture of Foundational Sensibilities

To understand why this competence is so unequally distributed, and why the uninitiated are so readily channeled by algorithmic systems, I turn to the cognitive sciences. Over the past half-century, an extensive body of empirical research has mapped the architecture of human expertise across literacy, oracy, and numeracy. Set side by side, these literatures show the same trap. In every symbolic domain, the novice is caught serial-decoding surface features; working memory floods; strategic thought never starts. The expert, by contrast, relies on automatized, modular chunks, rapid local dependency-checking, and dynamic mental models of the problem space.

```
                     A CONTINUUM OF COGNITIVE PROCESSING
                     
   Novice Bottleneck                                  Expert Fluency
   =================                                  ==============
   * Serial, linear decoding                          * Structural chunking (Ellis; Pawley & Syder)
   * Working memory swamped by surface                * Working memory freed for strategic goals
     features (Yarlas & Sloutsky; Inglis & Alcock)      (Kellogg; Flower & Hayes)
   * Local, cosmetic tinkering (Sommers)              * Dynamic mental models & warrant-checking
   * Trapped in "programmed alternatives" (Aneesh)    * Tactical navigation of the engine (Galanter)

```

In the study of **literacy and written composition**, Marlene Scardamalia and Carl Bereiter (1987) uncovered a fundamental dichotomy between two composing strategies: "knowledge-telling" and "knowledge-transforming". The novice writer engages in knowledge-telling—a linear retrieval strategy in which ideas are retrieved from memory in associative trains and transcribed onto the page in the exact sequence they occur. As Nancy Sommers (1980) documented in her granular study of student versus experienced writers, novices operate under a rigid "thesaurus philosophy" (Sommers, 1980, p. 381). They perceive words as the fundamental unit of discourse; their revisions remain doggedly local, cosmetic, and lexical. When something is structurally broken in their essay, they tinker with synonyms, convinced that the underlying meaning is already fixed and requires only translation.

Experienced writers, by contrast, subordinate knowledge-telling to knowledge-transforming. They revise recursively across global, structural levels, treating the draft not as a transcript of thought, but as a malleable instrument designed to satisfy an imagined reader's rhetorical expectations (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1987).

Crucially, Ronald Kellogg (2008) demonstrated that this divide is governed by working-memory capacity. The cognitive burden of simultaneously coordinating basic spelling, syntax, and sentence generation overwhelms the novice's central executive (Flower & Hayes, 1981; Kellogg, 2008). Yet this limitation is not an immutable ceiling: Kellogg noted that a mere eight minutes of structured instruction could prompt undergraduate writers to shift their attention from lexical substitutions to global revisions, proving that novices often possess structural faculties that remain dormant until the attentional competition of surface features is suppressed (Kellogg, 2008, p. 8).

In the study of **oracy and language acquisition**, a parallel mechanism governs fluency. In Willem Levelt's cognitive architecture of speech production, fluent speech requires the seamless pipelining of conceptual preparation, grammatical encoding, and sound-form articulation—what linguists call morpho-phonological articulation (Levelt, 1999, pp. 87–88). Mature native speakers achieve this through what Andrew Pawley and Frances Syder identified as lexicalized sentence stems—hundreds of thousands of pre-packaged, multi-word chunks that are retrieved from long-term memory as whole units (Pawley & Syder, 1983, p. 191).

Because human working memory is bounded by a strict "one-clause-at-a-time" encoding constraint, these formulaic sequences place speech production on "automatic pilot," freeing conscious executive attention for higher-level discourse planning and rhetorical nuance (Pawley & Syder, 1983, p. 202).

Nick Ellis (1996) formalized this process through sequence learning: expertise develops as learners cease assembling utterances word-by-word through declarative rules and begin relying on consolidated formulaic sequences (Ellis, 1996, p. 91). When Jihyun Kahng (2014) examined lower- and higher-proficiency L2 speakers through stimulated recall, this cognitive rift was confirmed empirically. Lower-proficiency learners paused repeatedly within clauses, reporting that their working memory was entirely consumed by retrieving declarative grammatical rules—agonizing over verb tenses, articles, and prepositions. Advanced speakers, conversely, paused at clause boundaries, deploying automatized chunks while directing their conscious attention to monitoring thematic coherence (Kahng, 2014).

In the study of **numeracy and mathematical validation**, the cognitive divide exhibits identical contours. For decades, educators assumed that mathematical expertise was characterized by sweeping, non-linear skimming. When Matthew Inglis and Lara Alcock (2012) deployed eye-tracking to record mathematicians and undergraduates validating proofs, they shattered this assumption. Novices spent 55% of their total dwell time fixating directly on algebraic formulae, compared to only 45% for research mathematicians (Inglis & Alcock, 2012, p. 371). The mathematicians did not engage in wide, global leaps across the page; rather, they expended their attention on the surrounding explanatory prose, executing rapid, local back-and-forth saccades between consecutive lines to infer and verify implicit inferential warrants.

Alexander Yarlas and Vladimir Sloutsky (2000) revealed the underlying representational pathology. When novices are presented with mathematical problems, they categorize and solve them according to surface-level features (specific digits, operational signs, or surface formats). Experts categorize the identical problems according to deep structural principles (such as commutativity or associativity). In a telling experimental manipulation, Yarlas and Sloutsky unmasked the deep principles by holding all surface features constant; under these sanitized conditions, novices successfully solved the problems based on deep relational principles over 90% of the time. Yet the moment irrelevant surface features were reintroduced, their performance plummeted to 35% (Yarlas & Sloutsky, 2000). Novices possess relational competence, but their attention is inexorably hijacked by salient surface notations.

Across every traditional literacy, the pattern is the same. Novices do not fail for lack of higher-order capacity. They fail because the interactive medium confronts them with salient, low-level surface details that exhaust their working memory.

Transposed into the realm of algorithmic coordination, the implication—on the reading I am defending—is stark. When an ordinary user encounters a conversational generative AI or a platform interface, they are confronted with a surface that is consummately fluent, frictionless, and anthropomorphic. Just as the student writer fixates on synonyms and the math student stares at numbers, the algorithmic novice fixates on the surface prose of the chatbot or the cheerful notifications of the gig platform. They are cognitively disarmed, lacking the mental models required to look behind the screen and evaluate the running procedural engine that holds them in its grasp.

## The Historical Machinery of Cooptation: Law, Largess, and Liquid Screens

It is a common conceit of modern Silicon Valley that the dilemmas of algorithmic governance are entirely unprecedented—that never before in human history have individuals been subordinated to opaque, rule-based systems of coordination. I take this to be historical myopia. Long before the silicon microprocessor was conceived, complex societies devised institutional engines designed to enroll, coordinate, and extract compliance from broad populations through specialized procedural rules. In each instance, these systems established an acute divide between a mandarin class possessed of the specialized sensibility to navigate the engine, and a broad public that was merely processed by it.

Consider, first, the machinery of the Anglo-American **common-law courtroom**. In his 1930 lectures on legal method, Karl Llewellyn laid bare the procedural engine of precedent. The common law, Llewellyn observed, operates on a principle of institutional repetition—officials doing what officials have done before. Yet this doctrine is fundamentally "Janus-faced" (Llewellyn, 1930, pp. 70–76). It possesses two contradictory techniques: a *strict* view, which acts as a "surgeon's knife" to whittle an unwelcome precedent down to its narrowest facts, and a *loose* view, which capitalizes on welcome precedents by expanding their dicta to maximum generality (Llewellyn, 1930, pp. 70–76).

Llewellyn acknowledged that wielding this double-edged instrument requires rigorous, learned training: "only finer minds, minds with sharp mental scalpels, can do this work... The strict doctrine, then, is the technique to be learned" (Llewellyn, 1930, pp. 70–76). For the uninitiated outsider, the doctrine appears as a monolithic, binding cage: "the past will bind him" (Llewellyn, 1930, pp. 70–76). But for the skilled practitioner who possesses the knife, precedent is a plastic, steerable medium.

In 1974, Marc Galanter provided the sociological foundation for Llewellyn's insight. Analyzing why the "haves" come out ahead in litigation, Galanter bifurcated legal actors into "One-Shotters" (individuals who have only occasional recourse to the courts, such as an injured tenant or a criminal defendant) and "Repeat Players" (institutions engaged in constant, recurrent litigation, such as insurance firms or the state) (Galanter, 1974, p. 97).

The advantage of the Repeat Player lies not merely in wealth, but in a specialized structural competence. Repeat Players have low stakes in any single case; consequently, they can litigate strategically to "play for rules," settling cases that might produce unfavorable precedents and aggressively pushing cases that will entrench favorable legal interpretations (Galanter, 1974, pp. 100–101). The One-Shotter, by contrast, is trapped in immediate survival; they cannot afford to play for the rules of the system, but must simply endure whatever outcome the engine visits upon them.

The legal system was thus an early apparatus of coordinative cooptation: it enrolled citizens into binding adjudications while reserving the ability to manipulate the underlying procedural machinery to a closed guild of legal repeat players.

A second historical antecedent emerged with the rise of the **modern administrative state**. In his landmark 1964 essay, "The New Property," Charles Reich warned that American society had undergone a silent structural transformation: government had become the nation's largest dispenser of wealth. Livelihoods were no longer anchored exclusively in tangible physical property, but in state-allocated largess: occupational licenses, welfare entitlements, corporate subsidies, franchises, and government contracts.

Reich recognized that this shift instituted a profound power asymmetry between the granting agency and the dependent recipient. The state dispensed wealth on its own terms, adjudicated breaches through internal tribunals that served as arms of the agency rather than independent courts, and revoked sustenance on the basis of undisclosed or arbitrary determinations.

The psychological effect upon the novice citizen was paralyzing: "Seeking to stay on the safe side of an uncertain, often unknowable line, people dependent on largess are likely to eschew any activities that might incur official displeasure" (Reich, 1964, p. 751). The citizen became an anticipatory conformist, censoring their own speech and conduct to preserve their standing in the eyes of an opaque administrative apparatus.

Only licensed professionals—doctors, real estate brokers, and corporate contractors equipped with retained legal counsel—possessed the institutional algorithmacy to decipher the agency's boundaries, contest its revocations, and secure their "new property" against arbitrary dispossession. For the ordinary welfare recipient, the administrative apparatus remained an unmapped, coercive maze.

A third, highly technological genealogy can be traced in the **decentralization of international finance**. Throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the international monetary system was anchored in the metallic discipline of the gold standard. Under this regime, the value of money was tethered to a physical, public benchmark; the ordinary holder of a currency required no specialized navigational competence, for parity was an institutional fixture backed by statutory gold weights.

As Barry Eichengreen (2019) has demonstrated, however, the rise of universal suffrage, unionization, and domestic welfare demands in the post-war era made this rigid convertibility politically unviable. Governments could no longer prioritize external currency pegs over domestic employment. Between 1971 and 1973, the Bretton Woods system was dismantled, capital controls were progressively abolished, and the monetary system shifted from fixed parities to a floating regime governed by continuous cross-border capital mobility.

What made this floating regime operational, as Karin Knorr Cetina and Urs Bruegger have shown, was an unprecedented technological intervention: the introduction of the computerized screen (Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002a, 2002b). Prior to the 1970s, currency trading was conducted via decentralized networks of telephone calls and telex dispatches; traders spent their days "finding the market" by telephoning counterparties across global capitals (Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002b, p. 394).

In 1973, Reuters introduced the computerized "Monitor" system, followed in 1981 by automated dealing screens. Almost overnight, the currency market ceased to reside in physical offices or bilateral networks; it became what Knorr Cetina and Bruegger term an "appresentation-based, globally centered platform or flow structure"—a market made present on-screen as a continuous, centered flow (Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002b, p. 391).

The market was compressed into an array of glowing CRT monitors displaying presorted, anonymized bids and asks, continually rank-ordered by algorithmic brokerage engines. Within this synthetic environment, human participants developed a novel form of expertise: what Knorr Cetina terms an "informed tracing... grounded more in a continually 'worked' and prepared structure of feeling than in modes of calculation" (Knorr Cetina, 2009). Floor traders became virtuoso navigators of an electronic stream.

Yet outside the investment banks, the ordinary economic citizen—the small business owner, the worker, the traveler—faced fluctuating prices and volatile inflation driven by a running global computation they could neither see nor comprehend. Numeracy in money was no longer enough; the monetary system now required an elite algorithmacy that resided exclusively on trading desks.

## The Anatomy of Algorithmic Subjection: How Novices Are Trapped

In each of these historical precursors, the procedural engine remained confined to specific institutional arenas: the courthouse, the bureaucratic bureau, the trading floor. The contemporary platform revolution, accelerated by generative artificial intelligence, represents the universalization of this dynamic. Today, everyday social, informational, and labor interactions are mediated by systems of coordinative cooptation. Yet when ordinary people interact with these systems, the novice/expert divide—as the cases below show—remains deeply entrenched. Without interface scaffolds designed to bridge this chasm, novices are subjected to three systematic failure modes.

```
                  THREE MODES OF ALGORITHMIC SUBJECTION
                  
  1. The Attentional Mask (Yarlas & Sloutsky)
     Conversational polish and frictionless scrolling distract working
     memory, preventing users from seeing latent execution states.
                             |
                             v
  2. The Invisible Cage & Experimental Reactivity (Rahman)
     Opaque, moving targets (e.g., 100-point Job Success Scores) induce
     blind, superstitious tinkering and chronic anxiety.
                             |
                             v
  3. The Forced Funnel (Rosenblat & Stark; Zamfirescu-Pereira)
     Suppression of counterfactuals (15-second timers; blind dispatch;
     monolithic prompt boxes) forecloses systematic hypothesis testing.

```

The first failure mode is **the attentional mask**. Just as Yarlas and Sloutsky found that arithmetic novices fail to deploy deep principles whenever salient surface digits are present, users of algorithmic interfaces are systematically distracted by surface polish. In social media feeds, the continuous, seamless cascade of personalized content conceals the ranking algorithm.

In their empirical investigation of Facebook users, Motahhare Eslami and colleagues (2015) discovered that 62.5% of participants were entirely unaware that their News Feeds were curated at all. When posts from friends failed to appear, these unaware novices attributed the absence to their own scrolling habits or assumed their friends had stopped posting. The smooth surface of the interface masked the underlying triage.

In conversational AI, this masking is even more insidious. As J.D. Zamfirescu-Pereira and colleagues (2023) demonstrated in their study of how non-AI experts attempt to design prompts, novices inevitably view the model through a "social lens" (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023). Because the system emits grammatically immaculate natural language, novices treat it as an intentional human agent. They rely on conversational politeness, attempt to give high-level narrative instructions without concrete exemplars, and expect the system to remember previous interactions. Trapped by the anthropomorphic surface, they fail to perceive that they are addressing a stateless probabilistic next-token generator. Their working memory is consumed by conversational etiquette rather than systematic prompt specification.

The second failure mode is **the trap of experimental reactivity**. When users eventually realize that an algorithm is steering them, but are denied any legible model of its parameters, they fall into what Hatim Rahman (2021) terms "the invisible cage" (Rahman, 2021). In his longitudinal ethnography of high-skilled freelance workers on a platform that replaced transparent ratings with an opaque "Job Success Score," Rahman observed that workers were plunged into profound paranoia.

Because the platform concealed the score's inputs and updated it dynamically, workers could neither decipher the rules nor learn from their mistakes. They were forced into "experimental reactivity" (Rahman, 2021)—blind, superstitious trial-and-error. They arbitrarily varied contract lengths, lowered prices, tweaked job pitches, and turned to user forums for speculative gossip, hoping to guess what the algorithm favored. Rahman observed that this blind reactivity actually tightened the platform's hold over the workers, leaving them in sustained anxiety while the platform extracted compliant labor. The novice senses the presence of the cage, but having no instrument with which to map its bars, their exploratory energy is entirely co-opted.

The third failure mode is **the forced funnel**. In classic studies of algorithmic management, platforms deliberately construct interfaces that eliminate the user's capacity to formulate counterfactual comparisons. Alex Rosenblat and Luke Stark (2016) showed that Uber enforces "soft control" over drivers through blind ride acceptance: the interface presents a ride request with a flashing 15-second countdown timer, withholding the passenger's destination and fare. The driver cannot compare the ride offered against alternatives; the time pressure forecloses deliberation, and canceling rides risks deactivation.

Min Kyung Lee and colleagues (2015) captured the counterfactual in their interview with a driver who had previously driven traditional municipal taxis: the taxi dispatch radio allowed him to hear all incoming requests across the city and choose strategically, whereas the platform's dispatch interface abolished this choice set, forcing him into a passive, one-off transaction.

The platform achieves compliance by engineering what Aneesh (2009) called an environment with "only programmed alternatives"—a single path forward, accompanied by artificial urgency.

## Cognitive Governance: Four Interaction Affordances for Algorithmic Agency

If the subjection of the novice is built into the architecture of the interface, then the liberation of the novice cannot be achieved by moral exhortation or conventional classroom literacy. It requires **cognitive governance**: the deliberate design of interaction affordances that act as cognitive scaffolding.

Just as Hodds, Alcock, and Inglis (2014) showed that a 15-minute booklet on self-explanation could sharply alter proof reading by stimulating warrant-seeking saccades (Hodds et al., 2014, p. 38), software interfaces can be engineered to shift users from passive cooptation toward expert algorithmacy.

To avoid the romantic fallacies that plague so much design discourse, however, I ground these affordances in hard empirical findings. Recent human-computer interaction research shows that intuitive transparency designs often backfire badly—and sometimes reverse the intended effect. An effective scaffold must not merely expose raw data; it must restructure the user's cognitive engagement with the running computation.

### 1. Seamful Boundary Disclosure over Raw Probability Exposure

A common assumption among transparency advocates is that systems should directly display their internal confidence—for instance, by showing numeric confidence scores or color-coding token probabilities. Yet the empirical trials reported below show that this approach fails where it was tested—and in the strongest trial, it was the worst-performing intervention.

In a rigorous 2025 CHI study, Jenny Bo, Sijia Wan, and Ashton Anderson tested token-level uncertainty highlighting on GPT-4o advice across logical reasoning and estimation tasks. Shading low-probability tokens red or pink was the *worst-performing* intervention tested: it worsened participants' confidence calibration, induced irrational algorithmic aversion, and significantly lowered perceived accuracy, whereas a plain static disclaimer outperformed it (Bo et al., 2025). Novices do not know how to interpret token log-probabilities; raw statistical confidence confuses human mental models rather than clarifying them. Similarly, Emilee Rader, Kelsey Cotter, and Janghee Cho (2018) proved that brief text explanations of feed algorithms raise stated awareness but actually *lower* users' perceived agency and fairness, leaving them feeling "disconnected from power" (Rader et al., 2018).

The genuine design requirement is what Upol Ehsan and colleagues (2024) term **Seamful XAI**. Rather than attempting to render a complex model seamless or displaying raw probability exhaust, interfaces must strategically surface the "seams"—the infrastructural mismatches between a model's training assumptions and its real-world deployment environment (Ehsan et al., 2024).

A seamful interface reveals to the user where the system's operational boundaries lie: when a model is extrapolating beyond its training distribution, when an administrative dispatch rule is prioritizing platform liquidity over driver proximity, or when natural-language uncertainty should be framed through clear epistemic hedges (e.g., "I'm not sure, but..."; Kim et al., 2024). Seamful disclosure gives the user peripheral vision, transforming the system from an oracle into a fallible instrument whose boundary conditions are legible and contestable.

### 2. Counterfactual and Branching Exploration

Novices remain passive because the interface presents a single, deterministic stream. To develop algorithmacy, users require affordances that allow them to explore the counterfactual space: *What did the system suppress? What would happen if I perturbed this parameter?*

A strong empirical demonstration of this affordance was provided by Motahhare Eslami and colleagues (2015) through **FeedVis**. FeedVis altered Facebook's interface by placing two columns side-by-side: "Shown Stories" (the curated feed) alongside "All Stories" (the unfiltered chronological stream), rendering invisible algorithmically hidden posts as prominent white "holes". The effect on unaware users was transformative.

Upon seeing what the algorithm had silently omitted, participants experienced a profound shift from passive consumption to active interrogation. They systematically altered their engagement, moving 43% of their friends into explicit custom categories, and at a six-month follow-up, 83% maintained more active, sovereign navigational habits (Eslami et al., 2015).

In the domain of generative AI, Ian Arawjo and colleagues (2024) achieved a similar breakthrough with **ChainForge**. By replacing the single chat box with a node-based flow environment that evaluates P prompts across M models and N responses simultaneously, ChainForge operationalized non-linear hypothesis testing (Arawjo et al., 2024). Novice users who were previously fixated on single outputs suddenly discovered model divergences, bias shifts, and hallucination patterns.

Branching affordances break the forced funnel, restoring the structural visibility that municipal taxi dispatchers once enjoyed over gig platforms.

### 3. Modular Visual Chunking

In written composition and language learning, experts operate through automatized structural chunks that relieve working memory. Novice computer users, however, are routinely thrust into unconstrained, free-form text areas (such as the raw prompt box), which provide no cognitive scaffolding whatever. The user is left in the position of a pilot staring at an unlabeled cockpit.

To bridge this gap, interfaces must substitute visual chunking for syntactic tinkering. In their foundational study of **AI Chains**, Tongshuang Wu, Michael Terry, and Carrie Cai (2022) demonstrated the power of decomposing complex tasks into modular, chained operations (ideation, classification, drafting, and filtering).

In a controlled within-subjects trial, interacting with scoped modular steps significantly outperformed a standard sandbox across transparency ($5.4$ vs. $3.8$), perceived control ($6.2$ vs. $4.5$), and output quality (Wu et al., 2022). Most critically, logged behavioral data revealed that visual chaining cut aimless trial-and-error—what the authors termed "rolling the dice" through unedited re-prompts—from 51% to 36%, while increasing active curation of text from 41% to 77% (Wu et al., 2022).

By packaging the running computation into visible state machines and bounded intermediate transformations, modular chunking offloads representational maintenance from human working memory, allowing novices to reason about the overall architectural pipeline rather than wrestling with surface phrasing.

### 4. Cognitive Forcing Functions as Intentional Friction

Commercial platform design chases "frictionlessness." That chase is, on my reading, the primary technology of coordinative cooptation. By making consumption seamless, platforms encourage users to rely on rapid, unreflective cognitive heuristics, establishing habits of passive compliance.

To foster algorithmacy, interfaces must introduce **reflective friction**. Zana Buçinca, M. B. Malaya, and Krzysztof Gajos (2021) proved that conventional explanations do not reduce human overreliance on faulty AI advice, because users simply adopt a blanket heuristic that the machine is competent.

To disrupt this heuristic, the authors introduced **cognitive forcing functions (CFFs)**: interface designs that force the human into active analytical cognition before revealing algorithmic recommendations. In their "decide-first" condition—where users were required to commit to their own provisional judgment before the AI's advice was unmasked—overreliance on incorrect machine suggestions was cut dramatically (Buçinca et al., 2021).

Crucially, Buçinca and colleagues (2021) discovered an acute trade-off: users explicitly disliked the cognitive forcing functions, rating them as more complex and less preferred than the seamless interfaces that facilitated their errors.

Here, as I see it, lies the central political dilemma of interaction design: platforms have an overwhelming commercial incentive to optimize for seamlessness, because seamlessness maximizes cooptation. Scaffolding algorithmacy requires the courage to introduce intentional friction—seams that slow the user down, force deliberate evaluation, and preserve human sovereignty within the interaction loop.

## Conclusion: The Post-Gutenberg Imperative

When the movable-type printing press emerged in mid-fifteenth-century Europe, it did not merely accelerate the production of books. It fundamentally revolutionized the structure of public authority. Prior to Gutenberg, literacy was the jealously guarded possession of an elite clerical and scribal guild. The common individual was related to the written word solely as an audience member: they gathered in the cathedral to hear scriptures read aloud and interpreted by ecclesiastical authorities. The church was the exclusive institutional interface between the human soul and the inscribed text.

Print shattered that monopoly. By mechanizing text production, it rendered inscribed knowledge pervasive. In so doing, it converted literacy from a specialized guild privilege into an existential requirement for civic, religious, and economic life. The individual who could not read the vernacular Bible, parse the legal contract, or inspect the market gazette ceased to be merely uneducated; they were stripped of agency, cast out from the self-governing citizenry, and condemned to total dependence upon institutional interpreters.

The contemporary deployment of algorithmic coordination and artificial intelligence constitutes, on the strong analogy I am drawing, an inflection point of a similar order. For decades, the computational engines that coordinate modern life were managed by a modern clerical guild: software engineers, quantitative analysts, and corporate platform operators. The ordinary public interacted with these systems from a position of passive subjection. But today, as generative models mediate our writing, algorithmic managers allocate our work, and opaque curation engines structure our public sphere, the luxury of computational ignorance has evaporated.

To remain an uninitiated novice within an algocratic regime is, on a strong reading, to accept a condition of voluntary vassalage. It is to allow one's desires to be shaped by synthetic optimization targets, one's livelihood to be judged by unanswerable metrics, and one's reasoning to be guided by the seductive surface of artificial fluency.

Conventional computer literacy, with its naive focus on syntax, coding blocks, and digital mechanics, will not save us. We do not need an entire society of software engineers; we need a society endowed with **algorithmacy**. We need citizens who possess the tactical sensibility to detect coordinative cooptation, to demand seamful transparency over black-box smooth surfaces, to navigate through counterfactual exploration, and to wield the knife of institutional critique with the precision of Llewellyn's seasoned advocate.

Whether the technologies we build will nurture this sensibility or actively suppress it is not a technical question; it is a political one. Technical artifacts can be designed to concentrate authority into opaque, centralized hierarchies, or they can be arranged to distribute competence, foster reflexivity, and support human autonomy. If our interfaces continue to prioritize frictionless compliance, they will entrench the platform as a central apparatus of subjugation. But if we insist on embedding cognitive scaffolds within the architecture of our software, we may yet realize what I take to be the true promise of modern technics: an institutional order where machines coordinate complexity, while human beings retain command of their own collective destiny.
