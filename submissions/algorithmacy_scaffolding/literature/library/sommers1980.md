# Sommers, N. (1980). Revision strategies of student writers and experienced adult writers. *College Composition and Communication*, 31(4), 378–388.

**Identifier:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/356588; https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc198015930 (NCTE) · **Read depth:** full_text (JSTOR scan via the CUNY City Tech mirror; the OCR of p. 387 is fragmentary in my copy) · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Sommers opens with the same target Flower and Hayes attack a year later: current models "are linear; they separate the writing process into discrete stages," and what the linear structure "fails to take into account ... is the recursive shaping of thought by language; what it fails to take into account is revision" (p. 378). Her diagnosis is that the linear models are modeled on speech, which "is irreversible" (p. 379, quoting Barthes), so revision becomes an afterthought. The study is a case-study design: "twenty freshmen at Boston University and the University of Oklahoma with SAT verbal scores ranging from 450–600" and "twenty experienced adult writers from Boston and Oklahoma City" who were "journalists, editors, and academics" (p. 380). Each wrote three essays and rewrote each twice; changes were coded by four operations (deletion, substitution, addition, reordering) and four levels (word, phrase, sentence, theme), and interviews yielded a "scale of concerns" per writer (p. 380).

Students revise words. "The students understand the revision process as a rewording activity. They do so because they perceive words as the unit of written discourse" (p. 381); they hold a "thesaurus philosophy of writing" (p. 381). Underneath is a belief that "the meaning to be communicated is already there, already finished, already produced, ready to be communicated, and all that is necessary is a better word" — "For the students, writing is translating: the thought to the page, the language of speech to the more formal language of prose, the word to its synonym" (p. 382). They notice lexical but not conceptual repetition, "solve the immediate problem, but blind themselves to problems on a textual level" (p. 382), and revise only as much as their sense of "inspiration" requires (p. 382). The students are not unwilling: "On every occasion when I asked students why they hadn't made any more changes, they essentially replied, 'I knew something larger was wrong, but I didn't think it would help to move words around.' ... The students view their compositions in a linear way as a series of parts" (p. 383), and they stop "when they decide that they have not violated any of the rules for revising," rules that are "lexically cued and rigidly applied" (p. 383).

Experienced writers revise structure. "The experienced writers describe their primary objective when revising as finding the form or shape of their argument" (p. 384); one says "I need to keep writing a first draft until I figure out what I want to say. Then in a second draft, I begin to see the structure of an argument" (p. 384). Their second objective is the reader: "experienced writers imagine a reader (reading their product) whose existence and whose expectations influence their revision process" (p. 385). Quantitatively, "the heaviest concentration of changes is on the sentence level, and the changes are predominantly by addition and deletion. But, unlike the students, experienced writers make changes on all levels and use all revision operations," and the operations students skip, "reordering and addition — seem to require a theory of the revision process as a totality" (p. 386). Hence: "Unlike the students, the experienced writers possess a non-linear theory in which a sense of the whole writing both precedes and grows out of an examination of the parts" (p. 386), and "The experienced writers see their revision process as a recursive process — a process with significant recurring activities — with different levels of attention and different agenda for each cycle" (p. 386).

## Relation to the argument

This is the empirical novice/expert study the scaffolding paper's table needs, and it is the source Flower and Hayes themselves cite for the claim that student revisers are "locked in by the myopia in their own goals" (1981, p. 379). Sommers supplies, in the participants' words, the novice's linear parts-model (p. 383), the lexical unit of attention (p. 381), and the belief that meaning pre-exists the text (p. 382); and for the expert, the structural objective (p. 384), the imagined reader (p. 385), and the explicit self-description "recursive" (p. 386). The paper can use pp. 382–383 for "surface syntax" and pp. 384–386 for "non-linear structural planning" and "manipulating rhetorical goals" without stretching the source. The reader-modeling result also feeds Kellogg's third stage, knowledge-crafting.

## Caution

The sample is small and unmatched: twenty first-semester freshmen against twenty professional journalists, editors, and academics, so age, education, and occupational selection all travel with "experience," and Sommers reports no inferential statistics, only coded frequencies and interview excerpts. The paper is also partly a rhetorical argument (Barthes, Saussure, Culler) rather than a psychological one; a reader from cognitive science will want the process data, which live in the 198-page NIE report (ERIC ED220839, 1982). That report's ERIC abstract gives eight students and seven adults, not twenty and twenty; which figure describes which phase of the study is unresolved and should be checked before either number is cited. Finally, the linear/recursive contrast here is drawn from what the writers *say* about revision, supplemented by change counts; it is not a moment-by-moment process record, and Baaijen and Galbraith (2018) show that keystroke-level linearity and self-reported strategy can come apart.
