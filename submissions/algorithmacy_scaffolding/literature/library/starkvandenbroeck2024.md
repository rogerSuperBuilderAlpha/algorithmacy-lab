# Stark, D., & Vanden Broeck, P. (2024). Principles of Algorithmic Management. *Organization Theory*, 5(2), 1–24.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.1177/26317877241257213 · **Read depth:** adapted_from_verified_card · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** reused_verification — verified previously against the typeset SAGE article of record in `dissertation/research/library/stark2024.md` (full-text read, published pagination); adapted here, page numbers carried over · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Platforms manage assets, activities, and users that sit outside the firm, and that topology forces a style of management the old triad cannot describe. The paper's signature line is the one this course paper builds on: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (p. 1, repeated p. 5). Co-optation is what algorithmic management does. The authors set it against scientific management and collaborative management along five dimensions (Table 1, p. 14): organizational form (factory / project / platform), object of management (supervise labor / coordinate specialists / co-opt many types of user), ideology (efficiency / flexibility / immediacy-totality), modality (standardized / diversified / synthetic), and accountability (vertical / horizontal / twisted).

The mechanism that makes co-optation neither hierarchy nor network is stated through the eBay rating: "she is not acting as a subordinate to whom eBay has delegated authority. The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent … The platform operator leverages rather than delegates" (p. 14). Accountability on a platform is "neither hierarchical nor heterarchical. It is distributed, deflected, and denied" (p. 14). Against the network form, "reputation takes the form of a score rather than being built through interactive engagements … instant reputation matters more than trust" (Note 7, p. 18). The unit of analysis is "algorithmic practices—not algorithms alone but configurations of humans and machines" (p. 4), and the closing agenda asks readers to treat "technology as organization" and to study the genre forms of ratings and rankings.

## Relation to the argument

This card supplies the coordination mode that algorithmacy is a competence *for*. The paper's construct is the sensibility to navigate the "platforms coopt" mode, and Stark and Vanden Broeck define that mode with enough precision to say what a navigator must be able to do. Three of their claims translate directly into the novice/expert gradient. First, the co-opted party is enrolled without delegated authority and steered by a score, so the navigator's task is to model an optimization it does not own — the driver who anticipates surge, the seller who infers the ranking function. Second, accountability is "distributed, deflected, and denied," which a novice experiences as opacity and an expert treats as a procedural surface with regularities to learn. Third, the network's coordinating media, trust and reciprocity, do not apply on a platform, so network savvy does not transfer; the score is a different kind of object to read, and the paper can use that non-transfer to argue algorithmacy is a distinct competence rather than a rebranded social skill.

The closing agenda — "technology as organization," the genre forms of ratings and rankings — is where the paper's HCI scaffolding section attaches. If the rating is a genre, then interface design that exposes its grammar is a scaffold for moving novices toward expertise, and the paper can present its design considerations as one way to work the agenda the authors leave open.

## Caution

Stark and Vanden Broeck theorize the platform, not the user's competence. Nothing in the article proposes a novice/expert distinction, a learning curve, or a scaffolding remedy; those belong to this paper and must be attributed to it, with the article cited for the form's definition only. The piece is conceptual, its examples illustrative (eBay, MyJohnDeere, ride-hail, a megachurch database), so it licenses vocabulary rather than empirical findings about how co-opted users behave. The authors are explicit that the analysis "is certainly not advocacy" (p. 1); "deflected and denied" is analytic, and the paper should not read it as an indictment. A Spanish-language version appeared in *Revista MAD* 51 (2024), 1–30, with the same argument; cite the English article of record. The `stark2009` card carries the heterarchy antecedent and should be paired with this one wherever the paper contrasts network competence with algorithmacy.
