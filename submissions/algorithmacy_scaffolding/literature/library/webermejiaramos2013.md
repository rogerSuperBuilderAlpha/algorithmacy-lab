## What it argues

Weber and Mejía-Ramos accept Inglis and Alcock's (2012) findings on surface features and warrant-seeking but contest the claim that mathematicians do not skim a proof before reading it closely. Their argument is methodological: "inferring individuals' strategy use by averaging their reaction times across multiple trials is problematic" (p. 464), citing Siegler (1987) on the perils of averaging over strategies. Reanalysing Inglis and Alcock's raw data (shared by the authors), they report that the distribution of initial-reading ratios across 68 trials is not normal (Shapiro–Wilk W = .958, p = .02) and looks bimodal, "a larger distribution with a mean of about .35 and a smaller distribution with a mean of .80" (p. 468); on Proof 5, "eight of the 12 mathematicians had IR ratios less than .35" (p. 469). They conclude the data are "consistent with the claim that mathematicians use two initial reading strategies to validate proofs, with the more common one being an IS [initial skim] strategy" (p. 470), while conceding that "a demonstration of consistency does not necessarily mean that our account is correct" and that ".35 … is substantially less than .50, but still perhaps greater than one would predict for someone using a skimming strategy" (p. 470). They also distinguish "zooming out" (a form of understanding) from "skimming" (a reading process), and say Inglis and Alcock tested only the latter (p. 465).

Inglis and Alcock's response argues that Weber and Mejía-Ramos's unit of analysis mixes by-subject and by-item sampling and "allows generalization neither to the population of mathematicians nor to the population of proofs" (p. 473), and that a single early last-line fixation "can easily be meaningless—perhaps the result of an irrelevant head movement or of returning from a blink" (p. 473). Inspecting all twelve fixation plots for Proof 5, they hold that "around 30% is the lowest proportion of reading time that any participant can be said to have taken for their initial read-through," and both sides agree that calling that skimming would "seem strange" (p. 473). Both sides agree the proofs were too short to settle the question.

## Relation to the argument

This exchange is the direct check on the "experts scan non-linearly" half of the section's claim. Read charitably to the section, Weber and Mejía-Ramos give it a foothold: a majority of expert trials may have involved a faster first pass before detailed reading. Read strictly, neither side supports non-linear scanning: the dispute is whether experts do a *fast linear* first pass, and the agreed floor is a first pass taking about 30% of reading time. The safe formulation for the section is that experts check dependencies between adjacent steps more thoroughly than novices (uncontested) and *may* precede this with a quick sequential pass (contested); no eye-movement evidence in this literature shows experts jumping around a proof's dependency structure.

## Caution

Both pieces are brief reports reanalysing one small dataset (12 mathematicians, six short proofs), so the disagreement is about inference from thin data rather than new evidence. Weber and Mejía-Ramos's own view rests partly on mathematicians' introspective reports, which Inglis and Alcock (2012, pp. 373–374) argue are unreliable for exactly this behaviour. Citing this exchange in support of a clean expert/novice dichotomy would invite the reply that the experts themselves cannot agree on what experts do.
