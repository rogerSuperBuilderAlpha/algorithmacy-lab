# Reich, C. A. (1964). The New Property. *The Yale Law Journal*, 73(5), 733–787.

## What it argues

Government had become the country's largest dispenser of wealth, and the law had no theory of what that wealth was. "Government is a gigantic syphon. It draws in revenue and power, and pours forth wealth: money, benefits, services, contracts, franchises, and licenses" (p. 733). Reich catalogues eight forms of largess — income and benefits, jobs, occupational licenses, franchises, contracts, subsidies, use of public resources, and services (pp. 734–37) — and shows that they are "steadily taking the place of traditional forms of wealth," so that "the wealth of more and more Americans depends upon a relationship to government … allocated by government on its own terms, and held by recipients subject to conditions which express 'the public interest'" (p. 733).

The recipient's side of that relation is the article's core. Grantors write the terms, adjudicate breaches of their own terms, and owe no process: the procedures "represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient," and "the tribunal is likely to be an arm of the granting agency rather than independent and impartial" (p. 751). The effect on conduct is anticipatory: "Seeking to stay on the safe side of an uncertain, often unknowable line, people dependent on largess are likely to eschew any activities that might incur official displeasure" (p. 751). Courts had protected occupational licences first because they were "intimately bound up with the individual's freedom to earn a living" (p. 741), and Reich generalizes that wedge into a demand for fair procedure across all largess: hearing, contest, a record, judicial review, and no denial "on the basis of undisclosed reasons" (p. 783). The middle of the article calls the arrangement feudal (pp. 769–70); the close quotes the framers — "a power over a man's subsistence amounts to a power over his will" — and ends "We must create a new property" (p. 787).

## Relation to the argument

Reich is the paper's first witness that the shift is not new. Sixty years before the platform, he described livelihood held at the discretion of an institutional grantor whose line is "uncertain, often unknowable," whose tribunal is its own arm, and whose reasons need not be disclosed. That is the novice's experience of a co-opting system stated in 1964 terms: the recipient cannot see the rule, so the recipient over-complies. Reich's prescription — disclosed reasons, a record, a hearing — is a demand to make the grantor's decision procedure legible, which the paper can read as a scaffolding demand made before there was a word for it.

The expert side is also in the article. Reich's protected class — "Doctors, lawyers, real estate brokers, and taxi drivers" (p. 741) — are people who already knew how to work a licensing board, and the lawyer in particular is the professional navigator of the grantor's own procedures. The paper's claim that algorithmacy was once a specialist's competence and is now a general requirement finds its 1964 form here: the licensed professional had it, the welfare recipient did not, and the law protected the first before the second. Reich's aside that "franchises in private businesses … status in private organizations" would need safeguards next (p. 786) names the private grantor the paper is concerned with and leaves the extension to others.

## Caution

Reich is writing about *government* largess, and his procedural apparatus — due process, judicial review of agency action — runs against state actors; nothing in the article makes a private platform a grantor in his sense, and the state-action doctrine blocks the transfer. The paper should cite him for the structure of the relation and the novice's predicament, not for a legal conclusion. His programme was also largely rejected in doctrine after *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) and *Board of Regents v. Roth* (1972), so present it as the framing that made the question askable, not as settled law. Reich frames the remedy as a right, not a competence; the paper's reframing of disclosed reasons as scaffolding is its own move and must be marked as such.
