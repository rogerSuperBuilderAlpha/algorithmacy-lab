## What it argues

The citation as given in the paper — "Quinby, B. et al. (2021)" — is wrong in the first initial. The only 2021 Quinby-led work on mathematical notation and working memory is by **Francis** Quinby (Trent University, Department of Mathematics) with Kim, Pollanen, Burr and Reynolds; Crossref, Springer and DBLP (conf/hci/QuinbyKPBR21) agree on authors, title, pages and venue. There is no "B. Quinby" in this literature. Full text is paywalled on SpringerLink and has no open repository copy that I could find; the abstract below is from the Springer chapter page.

Abstract: "Typesetting 2-dimensional mathematical notation can present challenges to users who rely upon WYSIWYG (what you see is what you get) word processing editors, which allow for direct manipulation of text. These editors use various models to represent 2-dimensional mathematical structure within the 1-dimensional word processing environment. … we hypothesize that two distinct models, structure-based (Microsoft Word Equation Editor) and free-form (MC²: Mathematics Classroom Collaborator) handle different types of mathematical structure with varying degrees of success. To test this hypothesis, an eye-tracking study was conducted to compare how these two models affect task efficiency for mathematical 'expert' and 'novice' users, as well as working memory interference, and cognitive load. The study required users to transcribe mathematical expressions containing three types of structure: linear (1-dimensional), exponential, and rational (fractions). Handwriting was used as a control. Results showed superior performance by the structure-based model for the transcription of fractions, while the free-form model displayed ameliorated performance for the transcription of exponents. Handwriting was found to be significantly more efficient, but cognitive effects were inconclusive. Few differences were found with respect to user mathematical experience level."

The companion paper, Quinby, Pollanen, Reynolds and Burr (2020), "Effects of digitally typesetting mathematics on working memory" (HCII 2020, LNAI 12186, pp. 69–80, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-49044-7_7), is the one whose abstract actually speaks to working memory: transcribing expressions required "significantly fewer transitions" back to the stimulus when handwritten than when digitally typeset, with no difference in fixation duration, which "supports the hypothesis that digitally typesetting mathematics causes significant working memory interference."

## Relation to the argument

This source does not say what the section needs it to say. It is a human–computer-interaction study of *transcribing* two-dimensional notation into typesetting software, with handwriting as the control. It shows that forcing 2-D notation through a 1-D input model costs efficiency and (in the 2020 companion) increases working-memory interference relative to handwriting. It does not study problem solving, does not study scratchpads, and reports "few differences" between expert and novice users. The most it can support is the negative form of the claim: stripping notation of its two-dimensional structure imposes a working-memory cost, so the spatial layout of notation is doing cognitive work. It cannot support "expert reasoners offload working-memory strain into 2D structural notation" as an expert/novice difference. If that claim is to stand, it needs a different source — the cognitive-offloading literature (e.g., Risko & Gilbert 2016, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) or work on external representations in problem solving — and Quinby et al. should be cited only for the narrower point.

## Caution

Abstract-only; the body, sample size, and the "inconclusive" cognitive-load results were not checked. It is a conference chapter in an HCI series, not a cognitive-science journal, and its expertise manipulation found little. The first-initial error suggests the citation was reconstructed from memory rather than from the source; the reference list entry needs correcting to "Quinby, F., Kim, S., Pollanen, M., Burr, W. S., & Reynolds, M. G. (2021)."
