# Kahng, J. (2014). Exploring utterance and cognitive fluency of L1 and L2 English speakers: Temporal measures and stimulated recall. *Language Learning*, 64(4), 809–854.

## What it argues

Kahng compares 15 L1 English speakers with 31 Korean learners of English on temporal measures of a spontaneous speech task, then runs stimulated recall with 17 of the learners, split into lower (n = 9) and higher (n = 8) proficiency groups. The L1 and L2 groups "were different in speed, length of run, and silent pauses. In particular, a striking group difference in silent pause rate within a clause was found, consistent with the claim that pauses within clauses reflect processing difficulties in speech production" (abstract). Silent pauses per minute within clauses were 6.6 for L1 speakers and 13.8 for L2 speakers, while pauses at clause boundaries were nearly identical (8.5 vs. 8.0) (General Discussion, Table 7). Within-clause silent pause rate "had the most striking group difference ... and a significant negative correlation with speaking scores," and Kahng reads it, following Kircher et al. and Pawley and Syder, as reflecting "difficulty in speech planning and lexical retrieval," adding that L2 speakers "may pause in the middle of a clause even for more general planning such as deciding content of message ... and syntactic encoding ... because their speech planning processes are not automatized and often go through a process of trial and error during speech production" (General Discussion).

The stimulated recall is where the declarative/procedural contrast becomes data. The lower group reported 122 issues to the higher group's 75; grammar made up 31.1% of the lower group's comments against 18.7% for the higher group, vocabulary 18.9% against 12.0%, while message content dominated for the higher group (50.7% vs. 33.6%) (Qualitative Study, Table 5). "The lower proficiency learners remembered their problems regarding sentence construction and choice of tense-aspect, articles, and prepositions. They reported they were often thinking about specific rules, L2 declarative knowledge they had learned, for instance, about how to make a sentence using comparatives or whether to use a gerund or an infinitive. On the other hand, the majority of higher proficiency learners' comments on grammar involved monitoring their sentence structure, especially with complex clauses" (Qualitative Study). Example 8 is a lower-proficiency learner who "wanted to make a sentence which compares the golf cost between Korea and the US ... but it wasn't easy to put the sentence together" (Qualitative Study). Higher-proficiency learners "often mentioned having trouble remembering" their thoughts, which Kahng takes as consistent with automatized processing that leaves no declarative trace (General Discussion). She concludes that "the lower proficiency learners almost always seemed to think about L2 declarative knowledge or rules while speaking in the L2 (e.g., deciding on the words, tense-aspect, function words such as articles, prepositions, and putting words together to construct a sentence)," supporting Kormos's proposal of an L2 declarative knowledge store in the speech production model (General Discussion). The theoretical frame is Levelt's blueprint as adapted by Segalowitz (2010) with seven "fluency vulnerability points" at microplanning, grammatical encoding, lemma retrieval, morphophonological encoding, phonetic encoding, articulation and self-perception (Theoretical Background).

## Relation to the argument

This is the closest thing to direct evidence for the paper's novice: an L2 speaker who, by her own report, assembles sentences word by word from remembered rules about tense, articles and comparatives, and who pauses inside clauses to do it. It also gives the expert side in the same study — higher-proficiency learners whose grammar comments are about monitoring complex structure and who cannot recall the rest — and it embeds both in Levelt's architecture, so the paper can say where in production the difference lives. Use it as the empirical anchor between DeKeyser's theory and Ellis's chunking account.

## Caution

Small samples (17 in the recall study), an in-house proficiency test, one L1 background, and no L1-speaker recall for comparison; Kahng lists all of these. Stimulated recall reports what learners can verbalize, which the declarative/procedural model itself predicts will favour the lower group — the design partly builds in its result, and Kahng half-concedes this ("This difference may not be very surprising"). The higher-proficiency learners are advanced L2 users, not native experts, so the study shows a proficiency gradient among learners rather than a novice/native contrast on the cognitive side. Repair is measured but is not among the strongest discriminators; do not cite this paper for "rapid discourse repair" in experts.
