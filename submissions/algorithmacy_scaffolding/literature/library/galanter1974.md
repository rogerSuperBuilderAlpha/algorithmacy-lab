# Galanter, M. (1974). Why the "Haves" Come Out Ahead: Speculations on the Limits of Legal Change. *Law & Society Review*, 9(1), 95–160.

## What it argues

The article asks "under what conditions can litigation be redistributive," and answers by looking at the parties rather than the rules. Galanter's own abstract announces "some conjectures about the way in which the basic architecture of the legal system creates and limits the possibilities of using the system as a means of redistributive (that is, systemically equalizing) change," taking litigation "in the broadest sense of the presentation of claims to be decided by courts (or court-like agencies) and the whole penumbra of threats, feints, and so forth, surrounding such presentation." His 1983 retrospective compresses the thesis: "Patterns of litigation, legal services, legal institutions, and legal rules are traced to the pervasive effects of differences in the capabilities of disputing parties — especially the differences between recurrent (usually organizational) users of the system and occasional (usually individual) users." Those are the repeat players (RPs) and one-shotters (OSs), terms the article introduced (p. 97) and that Galanter says are what it is most often cited for.

The RP's advantages, set out at pp. 98–102 according to Guy (2022), include economies of scale in building a litigation record, developed expertise and ready access to specialists, a bargaining reputation that gives credibility against opponents, informal relations with the institutions, and — the point most cited — an interest in the rule component of a case rather than the immediate outcome. RPs "play for rules": they settle weak cases and litigate strong ones to judgment in order to "trade off tangible gain in any one case for rule gain" (pp. 97, 100–01, per Cummings 2017). Having lost or conceded, an RP can "structure future transactions in light of the outcome" (Gilad 2010, summarizing). The OS, whose stake is large relative to its size and who will not be back, cannot do any of this. Two institutional features compound the gap: the adversarial system's passivity, which assumes parties equally able to bring evidence and argument, and court overload, which presses claimants toward inferior settlements (Gilad 2010). Beyond party capability, "the rules of the game tend to favor older, culturally dominant interests" (p. 123, per Hanretty 2013). Galanter's conclusion is that rule change alone will not redistribute, because "the system is so constructed that changes in the rules can be filtered out unless accompanied by changes at other levels" (p. 149, per Cummings 2017); litigating and lobbying must be complemented by organizing, services, and new institutional forms (p. 150). He treats lawyers as RPs but did not expect representation to offset the OS's disadvantage substantially (pp. 114–19, per Gilad 2010). In 1983 he described the article's approach as one "that emphasizes the capabilities and goals of the 'customers' who use legal institutions, rather than the concerns of the professionals who staff them."

## Relation to the argument

Galanter is the paper's sociological evidence that the courtroom already sorted its users into those who had the algorithmacy and those who did not. His OS/RP distinction is a novice/expert distinction defined by recurrence of use, and his account of the RP's edge is an account of competence rather than law: advance intelligence, expertise, knowing which cases to settle and which to litigate, playing the system for rules while the novice plays it for the outcome in front of him. That is what the paper means by navigating a procedural engine. Llewellyn (`llewellyn1930`) shows the technique the expert applies to a single precedent; Galanter shows what the possession of that technique does across many cases, and he shows it from the user's side, which is the side the paper's novice/expert framing takes.

Two further uses. The RP who "structures future transactions" in light of past outcomes is, on a platform, the operator itself — the ultimate repeat player, which writes the terms and also learns from every case — so Galanter's asymmetry gives the paper a way to say that the platform's co-opted users are one-shotters facing a party that plays for rules by construction. And Galanter's remedy — that rule change is filtered out unless the supply of capability to have-nots also changes — is the sociolegal ancestor of the paper's scaffolding claim: exposing the rule does nothing for the novice unless the competence to use it is also supplied, which is what interface scaffolding is for.

## Caution

The article's full text has not been read; every page-cited quotation above is reproduced from citing literature that supplies the page, and the paper must check each against the JSTOR or Cambridge Core text before quoting. The abstract and the 1983 commentary have been read directly and can be cited as given. Galanter calls the article's assertions "speculative and tentative" in its own abstract; the empirical follow-ups partly confirm the RP advantage (Songer and Sheehan 1992; Songer, Sheehan and Haire 1999; Hanretty 2013 for government litigants) and partly dispute it (Lempert 1999 argues the "haves" and "repeat player" categories are too entangled to separate; Gilad 2010 finds the advantage weaker in informal dispute resolution). The article is about parties, not lawyers' skill as such; the reading of RP capability as "expert algorithmacy" is the paper's, and the platform-as-ultimate-RP extension is the paper's too. Galanter's 1983 note that he had "become aware of its flaws" and never produced the planned enlargement should keep the paper from treating the 1974 framework as his final word.
