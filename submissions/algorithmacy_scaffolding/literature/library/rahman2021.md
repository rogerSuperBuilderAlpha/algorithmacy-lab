## What it argues

When a platform rates workers by criteria it hides and changes without notice, workers experience an *invisible cage*: control they feel but "cannot decipher or learn from." The study draws on 98 interviews over three years, 2,053 discussion-board messages, platform documents, and the author's own registered-user observation of a large high-skill freelance platform ("TalentFinder"). In 2015 the platform replaced a transparent five-star rating, which had inflated to the point that 90% of freelancers sat above 4.0, with an opaque 100-point Success Score computed from undisclosed inputs. Workers responded in two ways. *Experimental reactivity* meant testing tactics to raise the score — different project types, contract lengths, job categories, pitch styles. *Constrained reactivity* meant limiting exposure to protect it — declining new clients, taking platform-met clients off-platform, withdrawing. Which a worker chose turned on platform dependence and whether they had suffered a score setback. The opacity was deliberate, adopted to prevent gaming and preserve differentiation, and it was defended by an algorithm that detected attempts to exchange contact information. Rahman sets the construct against Weber's iron cage: rules there are fixed and knowable, here they are hidden and moving, and the reactivity often tightens the platform's hold rather than loosening it, leaving workers in sustained "paranoia" years later.

## Relation to the argument

The gig-work case's evidence on what happens when workers attempt the exploration the paper's affordances would support, but without any affordance. Experimental reactivity *is* counterfactual exploration done blind: workers vary inputs and watch a score, with no way to isolate which change mattered, because the criteria shift under them. This grounds **branching/counterfactual exploration** by showing the demand for it and the cost of its absence — the experiments are uncontrolled, so learning does not accumulate, and the constrained-reactivity path is the worker giving up on learning altogether. It also sets the hardest limit on **intentional frictional transparency**: a seam that reveals a criterion is worthless if the criterion changes next month, so the affordance must expose the *stability* of the target as well as the target. The five components the discussion names as making the cage invisible — criteria, execution interval, magnitude of change, impact on reputation, and who influences it — are a checklist of what **exposing optimization targets** would have to disclose to be more than partial. The rating-inflation history is a caution the paper should carry: the transparent five-star system failed by its own lights, and the platform's turn to opacity was a response to that failure, not gratuitous.

## Caution

A single platform, pseudonymized, in high-skill freelance work rather than location-based gig work, so scheduling and dispatch are not the object; the relevance is to opaque *evaluation*, which drives task allocation indirectly through visibility and client selection. The reactivity typology is inductive from one setting.
