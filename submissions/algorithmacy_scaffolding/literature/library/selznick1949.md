# Selznick, P. (1949). *TVA and the Grass Roots: A Study in the Sociology of Formal Organization*. Berkeley: University of California Press.

**Identifier:** https://archive.org/details/tvandgrassrootss00selzrich · **Read depth:** adapted_from_verified_card · **Source-tier:** primary_academic_book · **Evidence basis:** reused_verification — verified previously in `dissertation/research/library/selznick1949.md` against the full Internet Archive OCR text (definitional chapter pp. 13–16 and the Conclusion's "Empirical Argument Restated," pp. 262–264); adapted here · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Selznick studies the Tennessee Valley Authority to show how a formal organization is shaped by the informal commitments it takes on to survive. The mechanism is cooptation: "the process of absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence." He splits it in two. Formal cooptation absorbs outside groups through avowed relationships — appointments, contracts, advisory bodies — to shore up legitimacy or secure participation; what is shared is the responsibility for power, not power itself, so "the forms of participation are emphasized but action is channeled so as to fulfill the administrative functions while preserving the locus of significant decision in the hands of the initiating group." Informal cooptation responds to specific centers of community power, draws organized interests into the actual leadership, and stays unpublicized, because admitting capitulation to particular interests would itself erode legitimacy. The TVA's "grass-roots" doctrine became the vehicle: routing the agricultural program through the land-grant colleges, the extension service, and the Farm Bureau built a constituency that constricted the Authority's later choices and displaced its conservation aims toward those agencies' priorities. The Conclusion records that under its agriculturists' pressure "the TVA gradually altered a significant aspect of its character as a conservation agency," and that the voluntary-association device "represents a sharing of the burdens of and responsibility for power, rather than of power itself."

## Relation to the argument

Selznick gives the paper the pedigree of Stark's verb, and something more useful: a case in which cooptation ran in both directions depending on the competence of the coopted. The formally coopted party gets the forms of participation while "the locus of significant decision" stays with the initiator — that is the novice's position, enrolled and channeled, participating without power. The informally coopted party, the Farm Bureau and the extension service, knew the TVA's administrative machinery well enough to turn it toward their own priorities, and the Authority's goals were displaced as a result — that is the expert's position, enrolled but working the enrolling organization from inside. Selznick thus shows, in a 1940s public agency, the gradient the paper claims platforms have made universal: the same coopting structure yields channeled participation for those who cannot read it and leverage for those who can.

The formal/informal distinction also marks how far the platform case has moved. Selznick's cooptation absorbs actors into a leadership structure; Stark's platform routes assets and behavior through optimization without seating anyone (`starkvandenbroeck2024`). The paper can use that migration to say why the competence changed shape: working a board seat and working a ranking are different skills, though both are ways of not being merely channeled.

## Caution

The term migrated. Selznick's cooptation centers on absorbing actors into an organization's leadership; the platform "co-optation" the paper builds on seats no one, so the lineage claim must mark the shift rather than treat the two as one construct. The reading of informal cooptation as the coopted party's expertise is the paper's, not Selznick's; he frames it as the organization's capitulation to community power centers, and the paper should present the competence gloss as its own inference from his account of goal displacement. The boundary disputes over formal versus informal cooptation come from the reception (Thompson and McEwen 1958; Eriksson 2018), not the text, and should be cited to them. Dated single-case sociology: valuable as the construct's origin, not as evidence about contemporary platforms.
