## What it argues

Wilkinson's monograph introduced the term "oracy" for competence in speaking and listening, coined on the model of literacy and numeracy, and argued that spoken language deserved the same educational attention as the written. The ERIC abstract records that it "suggested modifications of current assumptions both about the nature of the spoken language and about its functions in relation to personality development." The book surveys what linguistics had contributed to English teaching in Britain, examines the environmental factors that shape oral ability — early upbringing, schooling, social context, mass media, cultural identity — and sets out approaches to teaching spoken English together with methods of assessing oral expression and listening comprehension, with sample test materials. It is a work of English education, not of media theory. A 1966 NCTE edition exists, so US catalogues may give 1966; 1965 is the original.

## Relation to the argument

Wilkinson supplies the lexical warrant for the paper's sequence. The paper compares novice/expert architectures across literacy, oracy, and numeracy and then adds algorithmacy as a fourth term; that comparison assumes the four are parallel competences, and Wilkinson is where the parallel was first drawn — "oracy" was built on "literacy" and "numeracy" by design. His monograph also treats oracy as something with assessable levels: it proposes tests of oral expression and listening comprehension. A competence that can be tested is a competence with novices and experts, which is the shape the paper needs each term to have before algorithmacy can be added to the set.

The paper should cite him at the point where the sequence is introduced, credit the coinage, and say in the same sentence that it is extending the word-forming pattern to a competence Wilkinson did not contemplate.

## Caution

Verified at abstract level only; no passage of the book has been read, and the summary above rests on the ERIC record and one review listing. Do not quote the monograph. Wilkinson's oracy is an educational competence in speaking and listening, narrower than anything the paper means by algorithmacy, and the paper must mark the extension.

One further limit matters for this paper specifically. Wilkinson is *not* a source for the historical sequence in which literacy moved from a scribal or clerical monopoly to a universal requirement after print; that claim belongs to Goody, Ong, Eisenstein, and the literacy historians, and the source card names Goody (1977) and Ong (1982) as the relevant neighbours. Wilkinson coined a word; he did not write the history the paper's "newly universal" claim depends on. If the paper wants that history cited, it needs a separate card.
