# Shepherd, M. D., & van de Sande, C. C. (2014). Reading mathematics for understanding—From novice to expert. *The Journal of Mathematical Behavior*, 35, 74–86.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmathb.2014.06.003 · **Read depth:** abstract_only · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** secondary_report · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Full text was not accessible: ScienceDirect presented a CAPTCHA, which I did not complete; the academia.edu copy that Google Scholar indexes sits behind a Cloudflare challenge; Semantic Scholar lists no open-access version. What follows is the publisher abstract (via ASU Pure and Google Scholar) plus how two Loughborough eye-movement papers that I read in full characterise the study.

Abstract: "As students progress through the college mathematics curriculum, enter graduate school and eventually become practicing mathematicians, reading mathematics textbooks and journal articles appears to become easier and leads to increased proficiency and understanding. This study was designed to begin to understand how mathematically more advanced readers read for understanding in mathematical exposition as it appears in textbooks compared to first-year undergraduate students. Three faculty members and three graduate students participated in this study and read from a first-year graduate textbook in an area of mathematics unfamiliar to each of them. The observed reading strategies of these more mathematically advanced readers are compared to observed reading strategies of first-year undergraduate students from an earlier study. The reading methods of the faculty level mathematicians were all quite similar and were markedly different from those that have been identified for undergraduate students, as well as from those used by the graduate students in this study. A Mathematics Reading Framework is proposed."

Panse, Alcock and Inglis (2018, accepted manuscript p. 4) summarise the findings: "Task-based interview studies have illuminated the detail of reading behaviors in graduate students and experts, suggesting that experienced readers often 'read the meaning' rather than literal symbols, and that they perform frequent comprehension checks and make thorough attempts to rectify failures (Shepherd & van de Sande, 2014)," and later (p. 22) that "students pay less attention than experts to comprehension failures (Shepherd & van de Sande, 2014)." The "earlier study" of undergraduates is Shepherd, Selden and Selden (2012, *Mathematical Thinking and Learning*, 14, 226–256), which found that first-year students who were good general readers still "pay insufficient attention to detail and do not respond effectively to confusion and errors" (Panse et al. 2018, p. 4).

## Relation to the argument

The source supports a novice/expert contrast in *reading* mathematical text, and the "read the meaning rather than literal symbols" phrase is a natural partner to Inglis and Alcock's finding that novices dwell proportionately longer on formulae. It gives the section a second modality (task-based interviews with a textbook, versus eye tracking with short proofs) pointing the same direction. It does not speak to linear versus non-linear reading order in any way I could verify, and it does not concern diagrams or notation offloading. Use it for comprehension monitoring and symbol decoding, not for scan paths.

## Caution

Abstract-only, with the substantive characterisation taken second-hand from Panse et al. (2018). The expert sample is three faculty members and three graduate students — six people — reading one text, and the undergraduate comparison is imported from a different, earlier study rather than run in parallel. A hostile reader will call this a case study and will note that a six-person interview study cannot establish a general novice/expert distinction. Any quotation of the paper's own words beyond the abstract must wait for the full text; do not attribute "read the meaning" to Shepherd and van de Sande with a page number until it has been checked in the original.
