# Rader, E., Cotter, K., & Cho, J. (2018). Explanations as mechanisms for supporting algorithmic transparency. *Proceedings of the 2018 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (CHI '18), Montréal.

**Identifier:** 10.1145/3173574.3173677 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted PDF, emileerader.com/papers/rader_chi18.pdf, read end to end) · **Source-tier:** conference_proceedings · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Brief textual explanations of a feed algorithm raise awareness but do little for the judgments transparency is supposed to enable. The authors ran a between-subjects online experiment (Qualtrics panel, August 2017; 681 US non-expert Facebook users after exclusions) in which each participant read one of four 200-word explanations of the News Feed, or a control text about Facebook the company. The four types come from the recommender-systems literature plus two the authors add: *What* (the feed is ranked, not chronological; lower stories get missed), *How* (the algorithm scores each story from behavioral data), *Why* (information overload makes ranking necessary; the goal is relevant, high-quality stories), and *Objective* (Facebook tests and revises the algorithm from user feedback). All content was drawn from Facebook's own News Feed FYI blog so that no participant was deceived. Outcomes were self-report measures grouped under four functions of transparency: awareness, correctness, interpretability, accountability.

Before the manipulation 56% agreed that Facebook shows them every story from friends and Pages. Every explanation then raised agreement that the *system* has agency over the feed, and every one raised "Understand Why"; but no explanation shifted *user* agency, since none linked the ranking to the user's own scrolling or visiting. Only *What* moved correctness beliefs (more intention to check friends' Timelines; stronger sense of missing wanted stories) and only *What* lowered perceived fairness. *How* made the feed seem *more* random at serving interpersonal and informational goals and reduced belief that the UI controls do anything; *Why* reduced belief that content actions such as liking affect the feed. *Objective* moved nothing beyond awareness. The discussion draws the lesson flatly: awareness "is necessary but not sufficient"; explanations "place a tremendous burden on individuals" to work out what to do; in a system where the algorithm has more agency than the user, transparency is "disconnected from power"; and explanations built from what a company already discloses tend to leave users feeling the feed is less fair and less controllable than the control group did.

## Relation to the argument

The feed case's cautionary source, and the one that makes the case for interactive rather than textual affordances. It bears on **exposing optimization targets** as a controlled test of that affordance in its weakest form: the *Why* explanation names the target (relevance under overload) and the result is higher stated understanding alongside *lower* belief that one's own actions matter. Naming the target without a handle on it reduces felt control. Read against `eslami2015`, where hands-on exploration of the curated-versus-unfiltered feed raised felt control at follow-up, the contrast supports the paper's move from explanation to **branching/counterfactual exploration** — the explanation tells, the branch lets the user test — and it supplies the four-function framework (awareness, correctness, interpretability, accountability) as a rubric for what each affordance should be scored on. The finding that *How* explanations made the feed seem more random also warns that partial mechanism disclosure can degrade a novice's model, which is the risk **intentional frictional transparency** must be designed against: the seam has to lead somewhere. Finally, the null on user agency across all conditions is a design specification in itself: any scaffolding affordance must connect the algorithm's behavior to the user's own inputs, or it will not move the belief that matters for algorithmacy.

## Caution

Effects are short-term, self-reported, and small (most R² between 0.08 and 0.26); the authors call the study exploratory and note the explanations differ from one another in several ways at once, so effects attach to whole texts, not to isolated features. The sample excluded social-media and computing experts by design and is not representative. Explanation content was bounded to Facebook's public statements, so the study cannot say what a fuller or less favorable explanation would do. Do not read the *How* result as "mechanism disclosure is harmful"; it shows that a 200-word data-scoring gloss with no link to user controls lowered perceived control.
