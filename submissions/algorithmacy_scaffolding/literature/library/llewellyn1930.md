# Llewellyn, K. N. (1930). *The Bramble Bush: On Our Law and Its Study*. New York: Oceana.

## What it argues

Precedent is a habit of officials before it is a doctrine. "In the large, precedent consists in an official doing over again under similar circumstances substantially what has been done by him or his predecessor before"; its foundation is "the official analogue of what, in society at large, we know as folkways, or as institutions, and of what, in the individual, we know as habit." The bar is what turns the habit into a system: where courts might neglect their own records, "the lawyer searches the records for convenient cases to support his point, presses upon the court what it has already done before, capitalizes the human drive toward repetition by finding, by making explicit, by urging, the prior cases."

The lecture's core is that the doctrine of precedent is "two-headed. It is Janus-faced. That it is not one doctrine, nor one line of doctrine, but two, and two which, applied at the same time to the same precedent, are contradictory of each other." The strict view confines an unwelcome case to its facts — "a surgeon's knife," "the recognized, legitimate, honorable technique for whittling precedents away." The loose view takes a welcome case for everything it said, including dicta — "a device for capitalizing welcome precedents." The same lawyer in the same brief uses "the technically strict one, to cut down half the older cases that he deals with, and … the loose one, for building with the other half."

Llewellyn is explicit that the strict technique is hard and learned. "It is not easy thus to carve a case to pieces. It takes thought, it takes conscious thought, it takes analysis." He suspects the strict view became the orthodox one because "only finer minds, minds with sharp mental scalpels, can do this work," and he draws the consequence: "If you can handle this, then you can handle both. Not vice versa. The strict doctrine, then, is the technique to be learned." The same asymmetry applies on the bench: "An ignorant, an unskillful judge will find it hard to use: the past will bind him. But the skilful judge … has the knife in hand, and he can free himself." His instruction to students follows — read each case "for its maximum value as a precedent" and "for its minimum value," and then "you have now the tools for arguing from that case as counsel on either side of a new case." He closes on the outsider: people "who think that precedent produces or ever did produce a certainty that did not involve matters of judgment and of persuasion … simply do not know our system of precedent in which they live."

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper's primary text for common-law litigation as a procedural algorithmacy, and it states the novice/expert split in the author's own words. The courtroom runs on an engine — officials repeating what officials did — and the engine has two contradictory operating rules that a trained party can apply selectively while an untrained party cannot apply at all. Llewellyn names the competence ("the technique to be learned"), locates it in a class ("finer minds, minds with sharp mental scalpels"), states the asymmetry it produces (the unskilful judge is bound, the skilful judge freed), and describes the outsider's condition (living inside a system of precedent without knowing it). Each of those is a sentence the paper's historical section can quote to show that a coordination system whose rules only specialists could work existed long before the platform, and that the difference was one of trained sensibility rather than access.

The lecture also supplies the paper's claim about what expertise consists in. The expert reads the same case twice — for its maximum and its minimum — and then predicts which reading a court will choose from "all that you know of individual judges, or of the trends in specific courts … or in the times at large." That is inference about an opaque decision procedure from its past outputs, which is the same cognitive move the paper attributes to the platform expert modelling a ranking from its behavior. Llewellyn's "you must turn, for purposes of prediction, to the reactions of the judges to the facts" is the legal-realist form of reading the algorithm rather than the rulebook.

## Caution

The read covers one lecture (pp. 70–76 of the 1930 edition) from a course-site transcription, not the book, and the transcription carries minor OCR faults ("lessended," "lie can free himself"); check quotations against a printed edition before press. Later editions repaginate — the 1951 Oceana edition and the 2008 Quid Pro edition with Stewart Macaulay's introduction — so cite the edition consulted and its page numbers. Llewellyn is a legal-realist lecturer instructing first-year students, and the lecture is normative and programmatic, not an empirical study of how lawyers acquire skill; the paper should cite him for the structure of the technique and the asymmetry it produces, and reach for Galanter (`galanter1974`) for the sociological evidence that recurrent users of the system do better. The line "what these officials do about disputes is, to my mind, the law itself" is elsewhere in the book (the opening lecture, p. 3 of the 1930 edition per secondary sources) and was not in the excerpt read; do not cite it from this card. Llewellyn later softened that line (the 1951 foreword calls it "unhappy words"), which a hostile reader will know.
