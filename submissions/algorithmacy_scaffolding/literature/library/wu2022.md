# Wu, T., Terry, M., & Cai, C.J. (2022). AI Chains: Transparent and Controllable Human-AI Interaction by Chaining Large Language Model Prompts. *Proceedings of the 2022 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '22)*, Article 385, 22 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3491102.3517582 · arXiv:2110.01691v3 · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v3, camera-ready) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top HCI venue (CHI); best-paper-honourable-mention-class systems paper, ~700 citations · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Breaking a task into chained LLM steps, each with a scoped primitive operation (classification, ideation, rewriting, split points, compose points, etc.) and editable intermediate data, improves both output quality and the user's sense of transparency and control — without changing the model. The interface shows a Chain view (flow chart of operations and data layers) and a Step view where each step's prompt, inputs and outputs are editable in place. A within-subjects study with 20 practitioners at a large software company (UX designers, linguists, data analysts, non-ML engineers) compared Chaining with a GPT-3-Playground-style Sandbox on two tasks, both on the same underlying model (LaMDA). On seven-point scales Chaining scored higher on "helped me think through the task" (6.0 vs 3.6, p < .001), control (6.2 vs 4.5, p < .001), collaboration (5.7 vs 4.6, p = .04) and transparency (5.4 vs 3.8, p = .002); two blinded raters preferred Chaining outputs 85% and 80% of the time. Logged interactions show the behavioural shift: in Sandbox participants re-ran the model unchanged 51% of the time ("rolling the dice"), in Chaining 36%; curation of existing text rose from 41% to 77%. Qualitatively, nine participants said the scoped steps calibrated their expectations of the model, users compared parallel paths to infer what phrasing worked, and isolated steps served as "unit tests" that localised a global failure to one stage. Costs: nine noted a steeper learning curve, four lost track of how a local edit affected the end result, and three preferred Sandbox for free exploration.

## Relation to the argument

The strongest empirical support in this library for affordance 3 (visual chunking: modular workflow blocks and state-machine-like steps instead of raw prompt tinkering), and it is the paper that later work (ChainForge, PromptChainer, LangFlow) builds on. It also speaks to affordance 1 in a specific sense: the paper's transparency gain comes from scoping each step's objective, not from exposing a confidence score, which is a different mechanism from the one the essay proposes and should be labelled as such. The "compared parallel paths" behaviour is a small-scale instance of affordance 2 arising inside a chunked interface. For the novice/expert frame, the cleanest quote is the Sandbox user who felt like "sitting down in front of the controls of an airplane, all the knobs are there but I don't know what to do with them" — the raw prompt box is the novice's unstructured problem space, and chunking is what gives it structure.

## Caution

Twenty participants, one company, ninety minutes, and the default Chains were authored by the researchers; the authors admit the learning-curve and lost-big-picture complaints may stem from using pre-built rather than self-authored Chains. Participants had at least seen an LLM demo, so this is not a pure-novice sample, and no expert comparison exists. The model is a 2021 non-dialogue LaMDA; the single-prompt baseline's weakness on multi-objective tasks may be smaller with current instruction-tuned models, which weakens the outcome-quality result but not the transparency/control result. Do not read "transparency" here as confidence exposure.
