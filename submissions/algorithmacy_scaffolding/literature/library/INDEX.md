# Algorithmacy Scaffolding — Contextual Bibliography (index)

42 sources, one file per entry. Grouped by the section of the paper each source serves, not
alphabetically — for "why is this in the library and what does it do for the argument," structure
matters more than the author's surname. Each entry: citation, why it's here, how it contributes,
and a status flag where the verification wasn't clean. Full detail — quotes, page numbers, method,
sample sizes — lives in the linked card; this page is the map, not the evidence.

**Status flags:** ✓ verified in full against a primary source, no caveats that affect use as cited.
⚠ usable but carries a real caveat (abstract-only, adapted from elsewhere, contested finding). ✗
citation or attribution problem — do not cite as currently written without fixing it first.

## 1. Literacy — novice/expert writing

- **[Flower, L., & Hayes, J. R. (1981). A cognitive process theory of writing.](flowerhayes1981.md)** ✓
  The paper that licenses "recursive" for skilled composing and "linear" for the model it rejects — the
  source of the writer-based-prose/retrieval claim for novices and the goal-hierarchy account of
  experts. Note: "knowledge-telling" is Bereiter & Scardamalia's term, not theirs — don't misattribute it.

- **[Sommers, N. (1980). Revision strategies of student writers and experienced adult writers.](sommers1980.md)** ✓
  The actual novice/expert *study* behind the table's composition row — twenty freshmen against twenty
  professional writers, coded revisions. Supplies, in the writers' own words, the novice's word-level
  "thesaurus philosophy" and the expert's structural, reader-directed revision. Flower & Hayes cite it.

- **[Scardamalia, M., & Bereiter, C. (1987). Knowledge telling and knowledge transforming.](scardamaliabereiter1987.md)** ⚠
  The paper's own citation for the knowledge-telling/knowledge-transforming pair — but the chapter
  itself was unreachable (HathiTrust search-only). The card is built from the companion book's Preface
  and two secondary characterizations (Kellogg 2008, Baaijen & Galbraith 2018). Needs a library-copy
  check before any page-numbered quotation goes into a draft.

- **[Kellogg, R. T. (2008). Training writing skills: A cognitive developmental perspective.](kellogg2008.md)** ✓
  Converts the two-model contrast into a working-memory capacity story — which is exactly the premise
  an HCI scaffolding argument needs (offload representation-holding, behavior should shift without
  waiting a decade). Also the source of the complication: expert scientists split between linear
  planners and recursive redrafters, and the linear strategy often produces the better text.

- **[Baaijen, V. M., & Galbraith, D. (2018). Discovery through writing.](baaijengalbraith2018.md)** ⚠ (partial read)
  The direct empirical challenge to "associative retrieval" as the novice mechanism: keystroke-logged
  evidence that global revision and controlled sentence production pull in *opposite* directions on
  text quality, complicating any table that bundles both into "expert." Results/Discussion sections
  were paywalled — cite the abstract-level findings only until the full text is obtained.

## 2. Oracy — novice/expert language production

- **[DeKeyser, R. M. (2007). Skill acquisition theory.](dekeyser2007.md)** ✗ (citation ambiguity)
  The theoretical source for the rule-bound novice: declarative knowledge assembled step by step,
  proceduralized fast, then slowly automatized. Two different "DeKeyser 2007" items exist in the SLA
  literature; the 2007 text itself was unreachable, so this card verifies against a 2025 rewrite instead
  and flags every quotation as needing a library check against the original pagination.

- **[Ellis, N. C. (1996). Sequencing in SLA: Phonological memory, chunking, and points of order.](ellis1996.md)** ✓
  The load-bearing source for "expert = automatized multi-word chunks," with the actual mechanism
  (chunking under the power law of practice) rather than just the observation. Caution: Ellis's novice
  sequences phonological/lexical material — he does not describe a *rule*-driven novice, so don't cite
  him for the "declarative grammar rules" half of the claim; that's DeKeyser's or Kahng's job.

- **[Pawley, A., & Syder, F. H. (1983). Two puzzles for linguistic theory.](pawleysyder1983.md)** ✓
  The origin of the whole formulaic-fluency line (Ellis and everyone after cites it). The one source
  that states both poles in the same place: the grammar-book learner producing unidiomatic-but-correct
  strings, and the fluent speaker retrieving whole stems and spending the freed capacity on discourse
  planning. Also the empirical source for "novice under cognitive load" (the one-clause-at-a-time limit).

- **[Levelt, W. J. M. (1999). Producing spoken language: A blueprint of the speaker.](levelt1999.md)** ✓
  Supplies the architecture (conceptual prep → grammatical → phonological → phonetic encoding) and the
  mechanism of "monitoring and repair" the paper needs to name — but says nothing about novices,
  experts, or L2 speakers. Cite for the architecture; cite Kahng for the novice/expert difference within it.

- **[Kahng, J. (2014). Exploring utterance and cognitive fluency of L1 and L2 English speakers.](kahng2014.md)** ✓
  The closest thing to direct evidence for the paper's novice: an L2 speaker who, by her own
  stimulated-recall report, assembles sentences word by word from remembered rules about tense and
  articles. Gives both poles (lower- vs. higher-proficiency learners) inside Levelt's architecture.
  Caution: the "experts" here are advanced L2 learners, not native speakers — a proficiency gradient, not a novice/native contrast.

- **[Tavakoli, P., & Uchihara, T. (2020). To what extent are multiword sequences associated with oral fluency?](tavakoliuchihara2020.md)** ⚠
  The modern quantitative test of the Pawley–Syder/Ellis claim — more formulaic material tracks faster
  articulation and fewer mid-clause pauses. The verbatim-vs-flexible finding (novices copy a chunk
  whole; C1 speakers vary it) is the paper's best evidence for a graded rather than binary competence.
  Caution: three of five headline correlations don't survive the authors' own correction — do not lean on the repair-fluency finding.

## 3. Numeracy — novice/expert mathematical reasoning

- **[Inglis, M., & Alcock, L. (2012). Expert and novice approaches to reading mathematical proofs.](inglisalcock2012.md)** ✓
  The anchor source for "novice fixates on notation" — quantitative and direct (55% vs. 45% dwell time
  on formulae). Caution: the paper's own finding is *local* back-and-forth between adjacent lines
  seeking warrants, with explicitly **no** evidence of global scanning — "non-linear scanning across
  proof dependencies" overstates what this source shows; say local dependency-checking instead.

- **[Yarlas, A. S., & Sloutsky, V. M. (2000). Problem representation in experts and novices.](yarlassloutsky2000.md)** ✓
  The cleanest, most quantitative support in the entire library for "novice = surface features, expert =
  deep principles" (10–20% vs. ~80% principled choices, ability-matched control group). Its sharpest
  contribution: novices *have* the relational knowledge and use it when surface features are
  neutralized — an argument for scaffolds that suppress surface salience, not for teaching principles novices already hold.

- **[Hodds, M., Alcock, L., & Inglis, M. (2014). Self-explanation training improves proof comprehension.](hoddsalcockinglis2014.md)** ✓
  Turns the Inglis & Alcock contrast into a scaffolding result: a 15-minute self-explanation booklet
  shifts novices toward the expert reading pattern (more between-line saccades) with a large
  comprehension gain. Direct precedent for "a cheap external prompt changes a default pattern."

- **[Weber, K., & Mejía-Ramos, J. P. (2013) / Inglis & Alcock (2013), reply and response.](webermejiaramos2013.md)** ⚠
  The methodological dispute over whether experts do a fast linear skim before close reading. Neither
  side supports "non-linear scanning" — the disagreement is about whether the first pass is fast-linear
  or not, with an agreed floor of ~30% of reading time. Cite for the honest state of dispute, not to settle it either way.

- **[Stylianou, D. A., & Silver, E. A. (2004). The role of visual representations in advanced mathematical problem solving.](stylianousilver2004.md)** ⚠ (abstract-only)
  Supports "experts use visual representations as dynamic exploratory tools; novices mostly don't
  construct them at all" — but this is about representations solvers *construct*, not diagrams they're
  given, so it complements Inglis & Alcock rather than restating it. Full text was paywalled; abstract-only.

- **[Shepherd, M. D., & van de Sande, C. C. (2014). Reading mathematics for understanding.](shepherdvandesande2014.md)** ⚠ (abstract-only)
  A second modality (task-based textbook-reading interviews, not eye-tracking) pointing the same
  direction: experts "read the meaning" and catch comprehension failures; novices don't. Full text
  paywalled — read at second hand via a citing paper (Panse et al. 2018). Does not speak to scan order or notation offloading.

- **[Quinby, F., et al. (2021). An evaluation of two-dimensional digital input models for mathematical structure.](quinbyetal2021.md)** ✗ (misattributed & off-topic)
  This is what the essay's "Quinby, B." citation actually resolves to once corrected — and it does not
  support the offloading claim it was attached to. It studies *typesetting software input models*, found
  "few differences" by expertise, and calls its own cognitive-load results inconclusive. The
  offloading-into-scratchpads claim needs a real replacement (the card names Risko & Gilbert 2016 as a candidate).

## 4. Theoretical framing — coordinative cooptation & algorithmacy

- **[Stark, D., & Vanden Broeck, P. (2024). Principles of Algorithmic Management.](starkvandenbroeck2024.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  The paper's central theory source — "hierarchies command, markets contract, networks collaborate, on
  platforms they are co-opted" is the line the whole essay is built on. Defines what a navigator of that
  mode must be able to do: model an optimization it doesn't own, read a score-based reputation instead of built trust, work an accountability structure that is "distributed, deflected, and denied."

- **[Stark, D. (2009). The Sense of Dissonance: Accounts of Worth in Economic Life.](stark2009.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  The boundary case showing algorithmacy is not the same thing as network savvy. Heterarchy (Stark's
  earlier construct) rewards negotiating among several plural valuations; platform co-optation rewards
  inferring a single computed metric. Set beside `starkvandenbroeck2024`, it lets the paper say precisely
  what a network expert does *not* already know when they arrive on a platform.

- **[Reich, C. A. (1964). The New Property.](reich1964.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  The paper's first witness that the shift isn't new: sixty years before the platform, livelihood held
  at the discretion of a grantor whose rule is "uncertain, often unknowable" and who owes no procedure.
  Reich's protected class (doctors, lawyers, licensed professionals) is the 1964-era expert who already
  knew how to work a licensing board. Caution: his procedural apparatus runs against *government* actors — cite him for the structure of the relation, not a legal conclusion about platforms.

- **[Selznick, P. (1949). TVA and the Grass Roots.](selznick1949.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  The pedigree of Stark's verb "coopt," and a case that already shows the competence gradient: the
  formally coopted party gets participation without power (the novice's position), while the informally
  coopted party (the Farm Bureau) knew the TVA's machinery well enough to bend the agency's own goals
  (the expert's position). Caution: the term migrated — Selznick's cooptation seats people in a
  leadership structure, Stark's platform seats no one; mark the shift, don't equate the two.

- **[Aneesh, A. (2009). Global Labor: Algocratic Modes of Organization.](aneesh2009.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  Names algocracy — governance by code, distinct from bureaucracy and market — and describes it from the
  novice's side: an environment "in which there are only programmed alternatives." His two populations,
  the coders and the coded, are the novice/expert split in its purest form. His closing comparison of
  code to all-purpose money is the paper's bridge from numeracy to algorithmacy.

- **[Wilkinson, A. (1965). Spoken English (the "oracy" coinage).](wilkinson1965.md)** ⚠ (abstract-only; scope caution)
  Supplies the lexical warrant for the paper's four-term sequence — "oracy" was built on "literacy" and
  "numeracy" by design, which licenses adding "algorithmacy" the same way. **Important:** Wilkinson is
  *not* the source for the print-press/scribal-monopoly history the essay also wants — that claim needs
  Goody, Ong, or Eisenstein. Cite him for the naming convention only.

## 5. Historical instances of institutional algorithmacy

- **[Knorr Cetina, K., & Bruegger, U. (2002). Global Microstructures: The Virtual Societies of Financial Markets.](knorrcetinabruegger2002.md)** ⚠ (adapted, partial)
  The strongest find for the FX case: a sociology-of-finance account of currency trading becoming a
  "globally centered platform or flow structure" through the 1970s–80s (exchange controls abolished from
  1971, Reuters Monitor 1973, Reuters dealing 1981). Gives the "computed" half of the claim and an
  explicit novice/expert gradient in the trading room itself. The AJS article proper was verified at
  abstract level only — quotations come from a companion piece, cited separately in the card.

- **[Eichengreen, B. (2019). Globalizing Capital: A History of the International Monetary System.](eichengreen2019.md)** ⚠ (secondary characterization)
  Supplies the "ended" half of the FX claim — why the gold-standard discipline broke down (democratized
  suffrage made pegged rates politically unsustainable). Not read directly; reconstructed from two
  reviews and the publisher description. Contested by Schwartz's review — present as the leading account, not the settled one.

- **[Llewellyn, K. N. (1930). The Bramble Bush.](llewellyn1930.md)** ✓ (partial read)
  The paper's primary text for common-law litigation as procedural algorithmacy, read in full for the
  precedent lecture. States the novice/expert split in the author's own words: precedent doctrine is
  "Janus-faced," the strict technique is "the technique to be learned," and "an ignorant, an unskillful
  judge will find it hard to use ... the skilful judge has the knife in hand."

- **[Galanter, M. (1974). Why the "Haves" Come Out Ahead.](galanter1974.md)** ⚠ (secondary quotations)
  The sociological evidence that the courtroom already sorted users into those with the competence and
  those without: repeat players "play for rules," one-shotters can't. Extends neatly to the platform as
  the ultimate repeat player. Full text unread — page-cited quotations are taken from citing literature and need a direct check before quoting.

## 6. HCI case study — algorithmic feed controls (recommended primary anchor)

- **[Eslami, M., et al. (2015). "I always assumed that I wasn't really that close to [her]."](eslami2015.md)** ✓
  The single strongest empirical grounding in the design section: a built and tested artifact (FeedVis)
  that directly instantiates three of the four proposed affordances — counterfactual curated-vs-unfiltered
  view, a chunked three-bin "Friend View," and frictional revelation of the algorithm's existence.
  Reports both the behavioral response (43% of friends re-categorized) and the awareness gap (62.5%
  didn't know the feed was curated at all).

- **[Rader, E., & Gray, R. (2015). Understanding user beliefs about algorithmic curation.](radergray2015.md)** ✓
  Maps the mental models novices hold *before* any tool intervenes — six "intuitive theories" from
  Passive Consumption to Speculating about the Algorithm, an empirical ladder from novice to expert. The
  "leave clues" design recommendation is a direct precedent for frictional transparency: seed the
  expectation-violations that trigger theory formation rather than hiding or fully explaining the algorithm.

- **[Rader, E., Cotter, K., & Cho, J. (2018). Explanations as mechanisms for supporting algorithmic transparency.](radercottercho2018.md)** ✓
  The cautionary counterpart to Eslami: a controlled experiment (n=681) showing that *text* explanations
  raise awareness but lower felt control and fairness — "disconnected from power." Makes the case for
  interactive/branching affordances over static explanation, and supplies a four-function rubric
  (awareness, correctness, interpretability, accountability) for scoring any proposed affordance.

## 7. HCI case study — algorithmic management / gig work (secondary, contrast case)

- **[Kellogg, K. C., Valentine, M. A., & Christin, A. (2020). Algorithms at work.](kellogg2020.md)** ✓
  The management-theory frame: the "6 Rs" (restrict, recommend, record, rate, replace, reward) and a
  three-part decomposition of opacity (intentional secrecy, required technical literacy, ML opacity).
  Scopes what a scaffolding affordance can actually fix — technical literacy — versus what it can't (a
  deliberate policy of secrecy, or ML opacity no interface will resolve).

- **[Lee, M. K., Kusbit, D., Metsky, E., & Dabbish, L. (2015). Working with machines.](lee2015.md)** ✓
  The clearest single source for the novice/expert gradient priced inside one workforce ("more knowledge
  more advantage") — drivers who understood the dispatch rule built workarounds; those who didn't lost
  earnings and ratings. Caution: also reports drivers "did not feel entitled to" more control over the
  algorithm — a finding a design argument for these affordances has to engage, not skip past.

- **[Rosenblat, A., & Stark, L. (2016). Algorithmic labor and information asymmetries.](rosenblatstark2016.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  The foundational field study for the gig-work case, showing the algorithmacy gap being manufactured,
  not merely tolerated: blind ride acceptance removes the counterfactual branch entirely, the surge map
  is keyed to the wrong referent (passenger location, not driver's), and communication runs one way. Every proposed affordance has a documented negative case here.

- **[Rahman, H. A. (2021). The invisible cage.](rahman2021.md)** ✓ (adapted)
  What happens when workers attempt the exploration the paper's affordances would support, but without
  any affordance: "experimental reactivity" is counterfactual exploration done blind, with no way to
  isolate what worked because the platform's rating criteria move under them. Sets the hardest limit on
  the whole design section — a seam that reveals a criterion is worthless if the criterion changes next month.

## 8. HCI case study — AI/prompt interfaces (weakest grounding; use with named caveats)

- **[Zamfirescu-Pereira, J. D., et al. (2023). Why Johnny Can't Prompt.](zamfirescupereira2023.md)** ✓ (no expert baseline)
  The anchor source for the novice/expert claim in this case study — but its own "expert" column is
  imported from prior programming/ML literature, not measured. Documents genuine novice struggles
  (opportunistic exploration, over-generalizing from one example, a social lens carried over from
  human instruction) and is the origin of the "seamful vs. seamless" design vocabulary this case study borrows.

- **[Arawjo, I., et al. (2024). ChainForge.](arawjo2024.md)** ✓ (qualitative only)
  The closest peer-reviewed match to the branching/what-if affordance — its Table 1 lists non-linear
  branching as an explicit design goal, and a case study shows counterfactual model comparison producing
  calibration. Evaluation is qualitative with no linear baseline; even self-described experts struggled to scale up from single prompts to systematic testing.

- **[Wu, T., Terry, M., & Cai, C. J. (2022). AI Chains.](wu2022.md)** ✓
  The strongest empirical support anywhere in the library for the chunking affordance: a within-subjects
  study (n=20) where chaining scoped LLM steps beat a sandbox on transparency, control, and output
  quality, with a logged behavior shift away from "rolling the dice." Caution: its transparency gain
  comes from scoping each step's objective, not from exposing a confidence score — a different mechanism than the essay's affordance 1.

- **[Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M. B., & Gajos, K. Z. (2021). To Trust or to Think.](bucinca2021.md)** ✓
  The theoretical and empirical root of the frictional-transparency affordance — cognitive forcing
  functions (hide, delay, decide-first) measurably reduce overreliance on wrong AI advice. Two costs the
  essay has to carry: users dislike the designs that help them, and a plain confidence-percentage
  baseline (the essay's affordance 1 in its simplest form) did nothing on its own.

- **[Kim, S. S. Y., et al. (2024). "I'm Not Sure, But...".](kim2024.md)** ✓
  A pre-registered, well-powered test of natural-language hedging as confidence exposure — it works, but
  modestly, and the effect is specific to *wording*, not to numeric or visual confidence display. Supports "surface uncertainty" but not "surface it as a number."

- **[Bo, J. Y., Wan, S., & Anderson, A. (2025). To Rely or Not to Rely?](bo2025.md)** ✗ (contradicts affordance 1)
  The direct empirical test of confidence exposure in its strong form — and it fails. Token-level
  confidence highlighting on GPT-4o was the *worst*-performing intervention tested: it worsened
  calibration and induced aversion to the AI, while a plain one-sentence disclaimer outperformed it. This
  is the finding the design section has to answer if affordance 1 stays in the essay as written.

- **[Ehsan, U., et al. (2024). Seamful XAI.](ehsan2024.md)** ✓ (formative, not evaluative)
  The peer-reviewed home of the "seams" vocabulary the essay uses for frictional transparency — gives it
  a definition, a design procedure, and a rationale (reveal infrastructural mismatches, not just model
  internals). No control condition and no behavioral outcome measure — the authors call their own
  findings formative. Use for the concept and procedure, not as evidence that seamful interfaces work.
