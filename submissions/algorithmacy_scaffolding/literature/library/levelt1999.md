# Levelt, W. J. M. (1999). Producing spoken language: A blueprint of the speaker. In C. M. Brown & P. Hagoort (Eds.), *The Neurocognition of Language* (pp. 83–122). Oxford: Oxford University Press.

## What it argues

This chapter is Levelt's own update of the *Speaking* (1989) model. The blueprint runs conceptual preparation → grammatical encoding → morpho-phonological encoding → phonetic encoding → articulation, with a self-perception loop feeding back (pp. 87–88). The first two stages are "the semantic/syntactic system" and the last three "the phonological/phonetic system," and Levelt insists these are separate systems, bipartitioned in evolution and in child development, so that "although the skilled adult speaker normally shows fluent co-ordination of these two underlying systems, the rift between them never disappears entirely" (p. 86). Processing is incremental: each component "can start working on the still incomplete output of the current processor," so the components are "normally simultaneously active, overlapping their processing as the tiles of a roof. When we are uttering a phrase, we are already organizing the content for the next phrase" (p. 88). He presents this as "a basic consensus in the language production literature" (p. 88).

Two points bear on stored units. Phonetic encoding draws on a "syllabary," a "repository of syllabic gestures that the infant began to build up by the end of the first year of life. Sometimes new or infrequent syllables have to be composed, but mostly speakers can resort to their syllabary" (p. 88). And the lexicon holds more than words: idioms "are encoded by going from a single concept to a complex idiom lemma with its own syntactic properties," and "probably, the amount of idiom and collocation in the mental lexicon is of the same order of magnitude as the number of words" (p. 95). Grammatical encoding is "largely lexically driven": the selected lemma brings its syntactic frame, and building a surface structure "is like solving a set of simultaneous equations" among the frames (pp. 94–95).

Monitoring gets its own section. "There is no more complex cognitive-motor activity than speaking": lemmas "come at a speed of two to three per second in normal conversation," articulation at "10–15 consonants and vowels per second," yet error rates run about one per thousand words; "the effort of keeping control is more apparent from hesitations, dysfluencies, and fresh starts that abound in normal speech. We are continuously monitoring what we produce or what we are about to produce" (p. 112). Monitoring runs through "the same speech comprehension system that we use for listening to others"; "if we notice trouble in the speech we are producing, in particular trouble that may have communicative consequences, we can stop and correct ourselves" (p. 88). The conceptual system can reject a message before or during formulation (the "Tell me, uh what—d'you need a hot sauce?" example, pp. 112–113), while form errors are caught through the "perceptual loop," with an external branch via overt speech and an internal branch via inner speech (p. 113). Experiments with Wheeldon show the internal loop monitors "the phonological score, the string of syllabified phonological words" (p. 114). Conceptual preparation is itself language-specific: an English speaker "must always think of the temporal properties of a state or event before expressing it," which Chinese speakers need not, so the model incorporates Slobin's "thinking for speaking" (pp. 93–94).

## Relation to the argument

Levelt gives the paper its stage model of speech production and its account of what "monitoring and repair" are — a perceptual loop that parses one's own inner and overt speech and can interrupt at any stage. He also licenses the idea that fluent production relies on stored, retrievable units at two levels (the syllabary and idiom/collocation lemmas), which is the mechanism behind Pawley and Syder's and Ellis's chunks. What Levelt does not do is compare novices and experts, or say anything about second-language speakers. The model is of the skilled adult native speaker; the L2 adaptation with "fluency vulnerability points" is Segalowitz's (2010) and Kormos's (2006), and Kahng (2014) is the empirical study that tests it. Cite Levelt for the architecture and the monitor; cite Kahng for the novice/expert difference within that architecture.

## Caution

The chapter is a consensus statement plus Levelt's own lab results; the incrementality and modularity claims were and remain contested on the amount of feedback between stages (Levelt says as much, p. 89, and the connectionist alternative from Dell is cited but not engaged). The lemma stratum itself was later challenged (Caramazza's work) and the "internal loop" account of monitoring has rivals (production-based monitors). None of this touches the paper's use, but a reader in psycholinguistics will not accept "Levelt says" as settling anything about the monitor's locus. The chapter contains no learner data and no fluency measures.
