# Baaijen, V. M., & Galbraith, D. (2018). Discovery through writing: Relationships with writing processes and text quality. *Cognition and Instruction*, 36(3).

## What it argues

The paper tests the problem-solving account of writing (Flower & Hayes; Bereiter & Scardamalia) against Galbraith's dual-process account, using keystroke logging rather than think-aloud protocols. It restates the problem-solving account in the terms the scaffolding paper uses: "Knowledge-telling involves the linear production of ideas in the order that they are retrieved from memory and the spontaneous translation of these ideas into words. Knowledge-transforming, by contrast, involves modifying the order in which ideas are produced, and deliberating over how to formulate these ideas to satisfy rhetorical goals. The result is that experts develop their understanding of the topic, whereas novices do not." It then names the gap: "Conspicuous by its absence, however, is research into relationships with the development of the writer's understanding. ... This fundamental assumption of the problem-solving account remains to be tested."

The rival account rejects retrieval as the mechanism of text production. "The problem-solving models characterize this as a knowledge-telling process in which ideas are retrieved from memory, and then translated into words. By contrast, the dual-process model characterizes this as a knowledge-constituting process in which semantic content is synthesized out of subconceptual units, and ideas are gradually constructed over a series of such syntheses." Discovery therefore has two sources with different relations to quality: dispositionally guided text production, "unrelated to text quality," and explicit rhetorical planning, which "is equivalent to the knowledge-transforming process with the crucial qualification that, because it operates on pre-existing ideas, it does not by itself lead to the creation of new content. Discovery is at a maximum when the two processes ... are combined."

Method: 84 Groningen students (78 analyzed), selected as high or low self-monitors, randomly assigned to outline or synthetic planning, then 30 minutes writing an argumentative newspaper article logged with Inputlog; two principal components — a "global linearity" scale ("Consistent with the problem-solving account linear text production is assumed to reflect knowledge-telling; nonlinear text production reflects knowledge-transforming") and a "sentence production" scale from controlled (long pauses, clean bursts) to spontaneous (short pauses, revised bursts); understanding rated before and after; quality rated by two judges (r = .84). Result, from the abstract: "The results support the dual-process account: Discovery was related to spontaneous sentence production and global revision of text, which had opposing relationships with text quality." The Discussion opens: "The results support the dual-process model. First, the fact that increased understanding was associa[ted] ..." (sentence truncated in my extraction).

## Relation to the argument

This is the complication the scaffolding paper must answer if it keeps "associative retrieval" in the novice column. Baaijen and Galbraith accept the linear/non-linear contrast at the global level — their own linearity scale maps knowledge-telling onto linear production — but they deny that text production is retrieval at all, and they find that the two things the table bundles into "expert" (non-linear global revision and deliberate sentence-level control) pull in opposite directions on quality. The safe use is to cite this paper for the operationalization of linear versus non-linear composing with keystroke logs, and to cite it as evidence that "recursive" and "high quality" are not the same thing. The dual-process account also offers the algorithmacy analogy a second novice route: understanding can arise from spontaneous engagement with the system as well as from deliberate planning.

## Caution

The sample is university students only, so this is not a novice/expert comparison; it manipulates planning condition and self-monitoring within one population and infers about strategies, not about developmental stages. "Discovery" is a one-item self-rating of understanding before and after writing. I did not read the full results section or the later Discussion, so any specific coefficient or interaction must be checked against the article before citation. Galbraith is the author of the dual-process model being tested, and the model's connectionist framing is a theoretical commitment rather than a measured mechanism; a reader sympathetic to Bereiter and Scardamalia can accept the keystroke findings while rejecting the "subconceptual synthesis" gloss.
