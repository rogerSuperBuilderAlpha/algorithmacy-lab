## What it argues

The AJS article examines, from participant observation, interviews, and trading transcripts in the interbank currency-trading floors of global investment banks, "regular patterns of integration that characterize the global social system embedded in economic transactions." The authors call these patterns "global microstructures" — global in scope, microsocial in character — and argue that "features of the interaction order, loosely defined, have become constitutive of and implanted in processes that have global breadth." They draw on Schutz to develop temporal coordination as the basis of the intersubjectivity found in these markets, distinguish cambist (trading) markets from producer markets, and posit "a form of market coordination that supplements relational or network forms of coordination" (abstract).

The companion *Current Sociology* article gives the history that the paper's FX section needs. The candidate system "emerged in the late 1970s": "In the 1970s, first the USA (1971), then major European countries, including Britain by 1979, and finally Japan in the early 1980s, abolished exchange controls, effectively eliminating the Bretton Woods Agreement of fixed exchange rates in place since 1944 and allowing foreign exchange trading for purposes of speculation" (p. 389). Daily turnover in traditional FX instruments rose "from $36.4 billion in 1974 to $1.5 trillion" in 1998 (p. 389, citing BIS). The authors' thesis is that "currency markets before 1980 were indeed effectively structured in terms of networks. Contemporary foreign exchange markets, however, are better understood as appresentation-based, globally centered platform or flow structures" (p. 391). Before screens, a trading room "was a room with desks and phonelines and a calculating machine," and "activities on the floor centered around 'finding the market', that is finding out what the price of a currency was and who wanted to deal" (p. 394). Screens began to present the market "only in 1973, when the British news provider Reuters first launched the computerized foreign exchange system 'Monitor'"; dealing stayed on phone and telex "until 1981, when dealing services also developed by Reuters went live" (pp. 394–95). The result: "After the introduction of the computerized screen quotes, 'the market' no longer resided in a network of many places, but only in one, the screen, which could be represented identically in all places. It acquired a presence and profile of its own" (p. 395). Each trader "faces up to five computer screens, which display the market and serve to conduct trading" (p. 393); the screen "is a building site on which a whole economic and epistemological world is erected" (p. 395).

The 2009 lecture adds the mechanism inside the screen. An electronic broker "rank orders" the entered bids and offers, "shows the highest bid … and lowest ask price onscreen," "suppresses" the inputting bank's identity, and stores unrevealed limit orders, so that "the tradable prices seen onscreen are presorted, sequentialized indications of select market participants' interests." Traders "trade 'by the seat of their pants,' based on a 'feeling for the market,'" and spot trading "tends to be a form of informed tracing … grounded more in a continually 'worked' and prepared structure of feeling than in modes of calculation."

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper's primary source for the FX instance of institutional algorithmacy, and it delivers the instance in two steps that the paper should keep apart. The first step is the end of metallic discipline: exchange controls abolished from 1971, Bretton Woods gone, speculative trading permitted. That step made currency a market. The second step is what made the market a computed surface: Reuters Monitor in 1973, Reuters dealing in 1981, then electronic brokers that sort, rank, anonymize, and match. Knorr Cetina and Bruegger use the word "platform" for the outcome, and their contrast — network before 1980, "globally centered platform or flow structures" after — is Stark's network-to-platform contrast drawn a generation earlier in a single market (`starkvandenbroeck2024`).

The novice/expert gradient is in their ethnography. The old expert "found the market" by telephone; the new expert reads a presorted screen that hides who is behind each price, and does so with a "prepared structure of feeling" rather than explicit calculation. That description of expert performance — fast, embodied, non-deliberative — matches the expert pole of the cognitive-architecture literature the paper's spine rests on, and the trader's screen is a working example of an interface on which a novice would see only numbers. Outside the trading floor, the corporate treasurer, the importer, and the traveller face the same floating currency without the screen or the feel, and that asymmetry is the paper's claim in miniature: the float created a computed coordination system, and the competence to navigate it was held by specialists. The electronic broker also gives the paper a co-optation reading of FX: traders enroll in a matching system they do not own, which sets the order in which their interests become prices.

## Caution

The AJS article itself has been verified at abstract level only; its title, venue, pagination, and abstract are confirmed through Crossref and OpenAlex, but every page-cited quotation above comes from the *Current Sociology* companion or the 2009 lecture, and the paper must cite those pieces for those passages, not the AJS article. The historical account concerns institutional interbank trading in global banks, not retail or corporate currency users; the extension to the treasurer and traveller is the paper's. The authors never say "algorithm" or "computational"; "appresentation," "flow," and "scopic system" are their terms, and the paper should quote them and gloss the translation. The float (1971–73) and the screen (1973–81) are distinct events with distinct causes — the paper must not write as if abandoning gold made currency computational; it made currency tradable, and Reuters made it visible. The phrase "informed tracing" and the "seat of their pants" quotation are Knorr Cetina's later reflection, not the 2002 data, and should carry a 2009 citation.
