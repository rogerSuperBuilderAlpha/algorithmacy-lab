## What it argues

I could not open the Rosenberg chapter. Cambridge has no online edition, HathiTrust holds it as search-only and would not render in this session, and no mirror exists. Everything below about the two models therefore comes from two kinds of source: the Preface to the same authors' 1987 book, which I read in full from the authors' own institute PDF, and the characterizations in Kellogg (2008) and Baaijen and Galbraith (2018), both of which I read in full and both of which cite the book rather than the chapter.

The Preface states the program the chapter executes. The research asks "Is writing basically the same process for beginners, with a few parameters set differently, or does it follow a qualitatively different mode?" and answers that "there are distinctly different strategies that involve writers in different kinds of thinking when they write. These differences have structure and are not simply a matter of more of this or less of that. Producing discourse without a conversational partner — which is what writing amounts to — is a formidable task for novices. To cope with it they devise a simplifying strategy. Expertise in writing does not come from refining this strategy or from mastering the use of it. Expertise comes from subordinating the simple strategy to another that is a great deal more complex" (Preface, unpaginated PDF, second page). The Preface also warns against reading the two-model claim as a denial of children's competence: "We are struck by how much more dependent novices are than experts on environmental supports on particular genres — most particularly the personal experience narrative — and on the topic of writing" (same page). The chapter titles reported for the book in library records — "Two Models of Composing Processes" and "Knowledge Telling and the Problem of Inert Knowledge" — name the two strategies; I have not seen those chapters.

The secondary reports agree on the content of the two models. Baaijen and Galbraith (2018) quote the authors' own summary from Scardamalia, Bereiter, and Steinbach (1984, p. 178): knowledge telling is "a simple think-say process of composition, reflected in such previously noted tendencies as that of making the order of presentation correspond to the order of idea generation and limiting revision to cosmetic improvements," whereas "the expert ... carries on a two-way process of information transfer, which results in the joint evolution of the composition and the writer's understanding of what he or she is trying to say." Baaijen and Galbraith then gloss: "Knowledge-telling involves the linear production of ideas in the order that they are retrieved from memory and the spontaneous translation of these ideas into words. Knowledge-transforming, by contrast, involves modifying the order in which ideas are produced, and deliberating over how to formulate these ideas to satisfy rhetorical goals." Kellogg (2008, p. 6) reports that "the verbal protocols collected by Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987) of children clearly document the essential focus on the author's representation rather than the text and reader representations. The text produced is essentially a restatement of their thoughts," and (p. 7) that protocols of knowledge-transforming writers "reveal extensive interactions among planning, language generation, and reviewing"; he adds (p. 11) that the authors "turned to graduate student writing to provide clear illustrations of knowledge-transforming."

## Relation to the argument

This is the source that gives the scaffolding paper its two column headings. The Preface supplies, in the authors' own words, the exact shape the paper wants: not more-or-less of one process but a qualitatively different strategy, with expertise defined as subordinating the simple strategy to a complex one rather than discarding it. That last clause matters for algorithmacy: the expert still has knowledge telling available and uses it as a component. The Preface's remark about novices' dependence on "environmental supports" is a direct bridge to the HCI scaffolding section — the design question is which supports let a novice run the complex strategy before it is internalized.

## Caution

The chapter itself is unverified. Every quotation above is either from the book's Preface or from a secondary source quoting a 1984 paper; none is from pp. 142–175 of the Rosenberg volume. Before the paper cites this chapter for any specific claim — a figure, a protocol excerpt, a page number — someone must read it (a library copy of the Rosenberg volume or of the Erlbaum book, or the 2013 Routledge reissue, DOI 10.4324/9780203812310). Two further limits. The evidence base is children versus adults, largely elementary-school protocols against graduate-student protocols; the novice/expert contrast is confounded with age, which Kellogg (2008) tries to unpick and Baaijen and Galbraith (2018) treat as a problem-solving assumption never directly tested. And Galbraith's line of work argues the knowledge-telling description of text production is wrong even for novices — content is synthesized during production, not retrieved — so a hostile reader can accept the two-strategy claim while rejecting "associative retrieval" as the mechanism of the simple one.
