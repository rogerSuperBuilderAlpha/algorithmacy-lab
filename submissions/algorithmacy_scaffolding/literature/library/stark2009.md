# Stark, D. (2009). *The Sense of Dissonance: Accounts of Worth in Economic Life*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press.

**Identifier:** ISBN 9780691152486; https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400831005 · **Read depth:** adapted_from_verified_card · **Source-tier:** primary_academic_book · **Evidence basis:** reused_verification — verified previously in `dissertation/research/library/stark2009.md` from the publisher description, a *Socio-Economic Review* 9(3) review, and forty citing-paper contexts (full text not accessed there either); adapted here · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Organizations gain, rather than suffer, when they hold several competing standards of value at once instead of consolidating onto a single metric. Stark draws on Dewey's pragmatism, where a perplexing situation is an opening for inquiry, and treats friction among rival criteria of worth as the source of the reflexivity that lets a firm search, discover, and adapt under uncertainty. The central concept is heterarchy — the lateral coexistence of multiple orders of worth — set against hierarchy and market price as coordinating principles. Three ethnographic cases carry the argument: a Hungarian machine-tool firm across the end of communism, a New York new-media startup through the dot-com collapse, and a Wall Street arbitrage trading room reconstituted after 9/11. In each, dissonance among valuations drives the recombination that lets the organization cope with disruption. The citing literature converges on the same three ideas — competing registers of value coexist and generate friction, the friction is productive, and "what counts" is plural — which is what the summary above rests on.

## Relation to the argument

This book is the boundary case that shows algorithmacy is not network savvy. Heterarchy keeps plural orders of worth in the hands of participants, who negotiate among them; the competence it rewards is dissonance-tolerance and the ability to argue one valuation against another. Stark's later co-optation form collapses valuation onto one owned metric that the platform sets and computes (`starkvandenbroeck2024`). The competence that form rewards is different in kind: inferring the single worth the platform computes rather than negotiating among several. Set side by side, the two Stark constructs give the paper a clean way to say what the expert on a platform knows that the expert in a network does not, and why moving from one setting to the other resets a person to novice.

The arbitrage trading room is the second use. Stark's traders are already experts at reading a computed, screen-borne market, and their reflexive search is a mature form of the sensibility the paper calls algorithmacy. The trading-room case therefore links this card to the FX cards (`knorrcetinabruegger2002`, `eichengreen2019`) and supports the historical claim that the competence existed in expert pockets long before platforms made it a general requirement.

## Caution

The characterization rests on the publisher description, one review, and citer contexts, not the full text; the three cases are accurate in outline, but the paper should not quote Stark's ethnographic detail from this card. The book predates and does not contain the platform co-optation construct; cite `starkvandenbroeck2024` for that, and this book only for heterarchy and the orders-of-worth apparatus. The heterarchy/co-optation contrast drawn above is the paper's reading, not Stark's own; he does not describe co-optation as an anti-heterarchy. The novice/expert gloss on the trading room is likewise the paper's extension.
