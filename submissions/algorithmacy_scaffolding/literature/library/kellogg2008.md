# Kellogg, R. T. (2008). Training writing skills: A cognitive developmental perspective. *Journal of Writing Research*, 1(1), 1–26.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.17239/jowr-2008.01.01.1 · **Read depth:** full_text (open access PDF from jowr.org) · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Kellogg turns Bereiter and Scardamalia's two models into a three-stage developmental account and adds a working-memory mechanism. "The novice writer progresses from a stage of knowledge-telling to a stage of knowledge-transforming characteristic of adult writers. Professional writers advance further to an expert stage of knowledge-crafting in which representations of the author's planned content, the text itself, and the prospective reader's interpretation of the text are routinely manipulated in working memory" (p. 1). He is explicit about the evidential status of each: "The first two — knowledge-telling and knowledge-transforming — are well documented. A third stage — knowledge crafting — is more speculative" (p. 3). Knowledge-telling "consists of creating or retrieving what the author wants to say and then generating a text to say it" (p. 6); the child's protocols show "the essential focus on the author's representation rather than the text and reader representations. The text produced is essentially a restatement of their thoughts" (p. 6). Knowledge-transforming "involves changing what the author wants to say as a result of generating the text. It implies an interaction between the author's representation of ideas and the text representation itself" (p. 6), and its protocols "reveal extensive interactions among planning, language generation, and reviewing" (p. 7).

The mechanism is executive attention. "The key point made here is the heavy demands made on working memory by planning, sentence generation, and reviewing processes limit not only the coordination of these basic cognitive processes, but also the maintenance and use of the three distinct representations underlying the composition of expert writers" (p. 5). In Flower and Hayes's terms, "limited executive attention must be allocated to the monitor component instead of to the basic processes of planning, translating, and reviewing" (p. 11). Progress is slow — "Approximately a second decade of practice is needed to advance from knowledge-telling to knowledge-transforming" (p. 11) — and the remedy is demand reduction through long-term working memory, relative automatization, deliberate practice, and cognitive apprenticeship (pp. 14–20).

Two passages complicate any clean novice/expert split. On revision, Kellogg reads Sommers (1980) as showing that freshmen "seemed to view their assignment primarily as an exercise in knowledge telling" (p. 7), but then reports that college students revise globally after "8 minutes of instruction" (Wallace et al. 1996) and infers that "when left to their own devices, college students invest their available working memory resources as best they can, but still fail to maintain the reader representation needed in making deep structural changes to the text. Because students can, with minimal instruction, change their focus of attention to the reader's perspective, they apparently know how to revise globally as well as locally" (p. 8). On experts, "experienced scientists show a wide range of individual composing strategies (Rymer, 1988). Whereas some use a linear strategy of extensive planning during prewriting before starting a draft, others jump right in with a very rough draft and revise endlessly" (p. 10), and outlining before drafting — a deliberately linear separation of processes — improves text quality (Kellogg 1988, p. 16).

## Relation to the argument

Kellogg is the source that converts the two-model contrast into a capacity story, which is what an HCI scaffolding argument needs: if the novice's linearity is a working-memory economy rather than ignorance, then interfaces that off-load representation maintenance (showing the text, showing the reader's view, holding the plan) should move behavior toward the expert pattern without waiting a decade. The p. 8 passage is the strongest single support for that design premise: eight minutes of instruction shifted college students to global revision. The paper's table can cite p. 6 for "linear knowledge-telling," pp. 6–7 for "recursive knowledge-transforming," and p. 5 or p. 11 for the working-memory constraint.

## Caution

Kellogg's third stage is by his own account "speculative" (p. 3) and should not be cited as established. His developmental timeline (two decades, three stages) is a synthesis rather than a longitudinal result, and the 10-year expertise rule he imports from Ericsson has since been contested in the deliberate-practice literature. The p. 10 observation that expert scientists split between linear planners and recursive redrafters cuts directly against any table that assigns "linear" to novices and "recursive" to experts as a trait: on Kellogg's account experts choose a strategy, and the linear one is often the better-performing one for text quality. A hostile reader will also note that the paper is a position piece with no new data, and that its key empirical support for novices (Bereiter & Scardamalia's child protocols) confounds expertise with age.
