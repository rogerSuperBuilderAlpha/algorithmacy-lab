# Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M.B., & Gajos, K.Z. (2021). To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 5(CSCW1), Article 188, 21 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3449287 · arXiv:2102.09692v1 · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v1, matches CSCW article numbering in header) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top venue (PACM HCI / CSCW); ~700 citations, the origin paper for "cognitive forcing functions" in HCI · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Explanations do not cure overreliance because people rarely engage analytically with each one; they form a heuristic about the AI's competence and follow it. Drawing on dual-process theory and on clinical debiasing, the authors test three cognitive forcing functions (CFFs) — *on demand* (the AI suggestion is hidden until clicked), *update* (decide first, then see the AI and revise), and *wait* (a 30-second "AI is processing" delay) — against two simple explainable-AI baselines (explanation; explanation plus a confidence percentage) and a no-AI condition, on a nutrition task with a simulated 75%-accurate AI (N = 199, MTurk). On instances where the AI was wrong, CFF participants outperformed simple-XAI participants (overall correct 0.09 vs 0.03, d = .37; carb-source detection 0.27 vs 0.08, d = .66) and overrelied less on the detection sub-task (0.48 vs 0.64), though everyone with AI still did worse than no-AI on those instances. The trade-off is explicit: trust and preference correlated negatively with performance on incorrect predictions, and participants rated the designs that helped most as more complex and less preferred. A Need-for-Cognition audit found the CFFs benefited high-NFC participants more, which the authors flag as an intervention-generated inequality.

## Relation to the argument

This is the theoretical and empirical root of affordance 4 (intentional friction — seams that make users interrogate rather than comply). It supplies the mechanism (System 1 heuristics about AI competence; friction as a forcing function into System 2), the taxonomy of friction designs (hide, delay, decide-first), and the two costs the essay must carry: users dislike the designs that help them, and the benefit accrues unevenly by cognitive motivation. It also weakens affordance 1 on its own terms: the *uncertainty* baseline — a confidence percentage shown alongside the explanation — did not differ from plain explanation on any metric, so exposing a confidence number without friction did nothing here. For the novice/expert frame, the NFC moderation is the closest this literature comes to an individual-difference result, and it cuts the wrong way for scaffolding: the intervention helps the already-motivated most.

## Caution

The task is ingredient substitution, not generative AI; the AI is simulated; the sample is MTurk with 49 of 260 excluded for selecting ingredients not on the plate. The paper predates LLM interfaces entirely, so any transfer to prompt engineering is by analogy. Effect sizes on the aggregate overall-decision measure are small (d = .03 across all instances; the gains appear only when the AI is wrong). The three CFFs did not differ from one another, so the paper cannot say which kind of friction is best. Pair it with Bo et al. (2025) before claiming friction produces *appropriate* reliance rather than merely less reliance.
