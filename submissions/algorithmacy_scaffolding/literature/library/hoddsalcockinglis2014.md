# Hodds, M., Alcock, L., & Inglis, M. (2014). Self-explanation training improves proof comprehension. *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education*, 45(1), 62–101.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.45.1.0062 · **Read depth:** full_text (author preprint, https://mjinglis.github.io/files/JRME_SelfExpl_Paper.pdf; page numbers below are preprint pages) · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Three experiments test whether "a simple booklet containing self-explanation training, designed to focus students' attention on logical relationships within a mathematical proof, can significantly improve their proof comprehension" (abstract, p. 2). The theoretical setup restates the expert/novice picture from Inglis and Alcock (2012): undergraduates "devoted more of their attention to algebraic parts of proofs and less to the surrounding text (in which logical claims are often made explicit)" and "shifted their attention around less often than mathematicians; that mathematicians made significantly more moves from one line of a proof to another in a manner consistent with an attempt to understand the logical relationships between these lines" (pp. 5–6). The authors' hypothesis is that novice failures "are not of understanding but of execution" and that "considerable improvement might be possible with a relatively light-touch intervention that encourages students to conduct their reading more thoroughly" (p. 6).

Experiment 1: trained students scored 18.2 (SD 4.2) versus 14.2 (SD 4.0) on a comprehension test, "a very large effect size, d = 0.950," with study time as a covariate (p. 27). Experiment 2 is the eye-movement study. Trained students had longer mean fixation durations on their second proof, 301 ms versus 276 ms, F(1,23) = 14.234, p = .001 (p. 32), and made more between-line saccades after controlling for both their first-proof saccade count and total reading time, F(1,22) = 10.394, p = .004, ηp² = 0.321 (p. 33); "we can rule out the possibility that self-explanation training merely increases the time a student spends reading a proof" (p. 34). Experiment 3 moved the booklet into a lecture as a 15-minute self-study activity; the comprehension gain persisted at delayed post-test (d = 0.410 immediate, d = 0.350 delayed, p. 38).

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper that turns the Inglis–Alcock expert/novice contrast into something a scaffolding argument can use. It treats between-line saccades as the behavioural marker of attention to logical dependencies and shows that a 15-minute prompt shifts novices toward the expert pattern on that marker, with a comprehension gain. For algorithmacy the parallel is direct: the novice "linear, surface-feature" reading is a default, not a ceiling, and a cheap external prompt that directs attention to dependencies changes it. It also reinforces the correct reading of Inglis and Alcock — the expert signature is local linking between lines, not global scanning — since that is the measure the training moved.

## Caution

The eye-tracking experiment has a modest sample (the ANCOVA degrees of freedom imply roughly 26–28 analysed participants) and one institution; Experiments 1 and 3 are larger. The reading-behaviour measure is a proxy: more between-line saccades is taken to mean more warrant-seeking, which is an inference, not an observation. The preprint page numbers will not match the published version, so page citations should be re-checked against the JRME PDF before use. The self-explanation booklet targets proof reading specifically; whether the same light-touch prompt works when the "text" is an algorithmic system rather than a proof is an open question the section would be raising, not answering.
