## What it argues

The international monetary system's history is the history of governments discovering that internal and external stability conflict and failing, mostly, to resolve the conflict through cooperation. Eichengreen covers four regimes — the classical gold standard, the interwar period, Bretton Woods, and the post-Bretton Woods "nonsystem" — and ties each to the state of international capital mobility at the time. The gold standard before 1914 held because governments could subordinate domestic goals to convertibility: in Schwartz's summary of his argument, its success rested on "limited suffrage and the weakness of labor unions and of labor-oriented political parties," so that authorities "could face down a challenge from the masses, when defense of the convertibility of domestic currencies … required raising interest rates and contracting economic activity." After the First World War, "universal suffrage and the rise of a politically robust labor movement have constrained the ability of government authorities to elevate external over internal stability," and no pegged regime has since been able to survive sustained pressure to pursue employment over parity. Bretton Woods began with "officially sanctioned capital controls"; "when the era ended, the controls had been eroded by the emergence of liquid international financial markets." The move from pegged to floating rates in the 1970s follows from that erosion: growing capital mobility made pegs indefensible. The post-1973 arrangement is a nonsystem — "managed floating by large industrialized countries, an effort to establish monetary union in Western Europe, currency boards and nominal exchange-rate anchors in less developed countries" — and the third edition extends the story through the global financial crisis, the euro crisis, the rise of China as a monetary power, and the renewed controversy over the dollar's role.

## Relation to the argument

Eichengreen supplies the "ended" half of the paper's FX claim, and Knorr Cetina and Bruegger supply the "computed" half (`knorrcetinabruegger2002`). The paper says currency moved from metallic discipline to a decentralized computed system that requires specialized navigation; the first clause needs a political-economy account of why the discipline ended, and this is the standard one. The mechanism is also usable for the novice/expert argument. Under gold, the ordinary holder needed no navigational competence: parity was a fixed weight, and the rule was the whole of the system. Under floating, value became the moving output of a market, and the competence to hedge, price, and read it — the trader's, the treasurer's, the central banker's — became a specialist's. The shift Eichengreen narrates is therefore the moment numeracy in money stopped being enough, which is the historical template the paper applies to algorithmacy: a coordination system's rules move out of a fixed public standard and into a running computation that only trained parties can follow.

Eichengreen's causal story is also the paper's caution against technological determinism. The float came from democratic politics and capital mobility, not from computers; the screens arrived afterward to make a market that politics had already created. The paper's historical section should say so, and cite him for it.

His central causal claim — that democratization made fixed rates unsustainable — is contested: Schwartz's review argues that government involvement undermined the gold standard rather than sustaining it and that its crises stemmed from banking legislation, not gold shortage, and she questions "whether democracy truly necessitates floating rates." The paper should present his thesis as the leading account, not the settled one.
