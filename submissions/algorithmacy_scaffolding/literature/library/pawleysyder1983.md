# Pawley, A., & Syder, F. H. (1983). Two puzzles for linguistic theory: Nativelike selection and nativelike fluency. In J. C. Richards & R. W. Schmidt (Eds.), *Language and Communication* (pp. 191–226). London: Longman.

## What it argues

Pawley and Syder pose two problems a generative grammar cannot answer: how a native speaker picks the natural sentence from among many grammatical paraphrases (nativelike selection), and how a speaker sustains fluent multi-clause speech when "human capacities for encoding novel speech in advance or while speaking appear to be severely limited" (nativelike fluency) (p. 191). Their answer to both is that "fluent and idiomatic control of a language rests to a considerable extent on knowledge of a body of 'sentence stems' which are 'institutionalized' or 'lexicalized'" — clause-length units "whose grammatical form and lexical content is wholly or largely fixed" (p. 191), numbering in the hundreds of thousands for a mature speaker (p. 192). The speaker "is able to retrieve these as wholes or as automatic chains from the long term memory; by doing this he minimizes the amount of clause-internal encoding work to be done and frees himself to attend to other tasks in talk-exchange, including the planning of larger units of discourse" (p. 192).

The processing constraint is stated as an empirical generalization from their New Zealand conversational corpus: "the largest unit of novel discourse that can be fully encoded in one encoding operation is a single clause of eight to ten words" (p. 202), the "one clause at a time constraint." Fluent speakers pause at clause boundaries and rarely mid-clause; the speaker must "regularly ... encode whole clauses, in their full lexical detail, in a single encoding operation and so avoid the need for mid-clause hesitations" (p. 204). Multi-clause fluent units, which seem to break the constraint, "generally consist partly or wholly of a familiar collocation" (p. 208). "Speakers show a high degree of fluency when describing familiar experiences or activities in familiar phrases. It is notorious that speakers are at their most hesitant when describing the unfamiliar" (p. 208). The transcripts of F (the hostel draughtsman) and Q (a PhD candidate under oral examination) show what non-fluency looks like in a native speaker: F "has not planned its syntactic and lexical content more than a phrase or — at most — a single clause ahead" (p. 201); Q, though a practised speaker, "does this planning a few words at a time" when the subject matter is novel (p. 202).

The learner appears explicitly. For someone who has "learned the rules of sentence construction in a new language from a grammar book" it is "a common experience to find that most of one's productions are, to the native ear, unidiomatic. Each sentence may be strictly grammatical. The trouble is that native speakers just do not say things that way" (pp. 194–195). And "it is a characteristic error of the language learner to assume that an element in the expression may be varied according to a phrase structure or transformational rule of some generality, when in fact the variation (if any) allowed in nativelike usage is much more restricted" (p. 215; examples "You are pulling my legs," "John has a thigh-ache"). The theoretical section grounds dual storage in a claim about the mind: "people are not good ... at performing a number of different mental acts simultaneously or in rapid succession; however, they do possess an enormous memory capacity" (pp. 217–218), so holistic storage trades memory for processing. Note 10 adds the phrase the paper will want: with memorized clauses "the speaker can attend to planning tasks while leaving the production of the current clause 'on automatic pilot'" (p. 222). Note 12 records that with practice on a description task speakers do not talk faster, but "the length of fluent (pause-free) units increases significantly" (pp. 222–223, citing Goldman-Eisler).

## Relation to the argument

This is the origin of the formulaic-fluency claim that Ellis (1996) and the entire later phraseology literature cite, and it is the one source that describes both poles of the paper's contrast in the same place: the book-learned novice producing grammatical but unidiomatic strings rule by rule (pp. 194–195, 215), and the fluent speaker retrieving stems whole and spending the freed capacity on discourse planning (pp. 192, 208, 222). It also supplies the paper's "under cognitive load" premise directly, since the one-clause-at-a-time constraint is a capacity limit, and shows that even natives fall back to word-by-word planning when the content is unfamiliar (Q, p. 202) — which is a useful reminder that the novice/expert line is about the fit between stored routines and the task, not about the person.

## Caution

The evidence is a small, informally analysed corpus of New Zealand and Australian English conversation from the 1970s; the "eight to ten words" figure and the pause statistics (p. 202) are reported without inferential tests, and Note 7 admits Goldman-Eisler found much shorter fluent runs in harder tasks. The "hundreds of thousands" of sentence stems is a guess from listing (pp. 213–214), not a count. The authors concede there is "no simple operation" for deciding what counts as a lexicalized stem (p. 212); a hostile reader will say the construct is unfalsifiable at the edges. The learner passages are observational asides, not learner data. The chapter is about native competence and says nothing about repair strategies beyond the transcribed reformulations.
