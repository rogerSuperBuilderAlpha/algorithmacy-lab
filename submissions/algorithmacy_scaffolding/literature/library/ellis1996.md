# Ellis, N. C. (1996). Sequencing in SLA: Phonological memory, chunking, and points of order. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 18(1), 91–126.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263100014698 (PDF read from the author's site: https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nickellis-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/1284/2021/07/Ellis1996Chunking.pdf) · **Read depth:** full_text (pp. 91–116 read closely; the closing section on generative grammar, pp. 116–120, skimmed) · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Ellis argues that "much of language acquisition is in fact sequence learning" and that "the resultant long-term knowledge base of language sequences serves as the database for the acquisition of language grammar" (p. 91). The paper runs at three grain sizes — phoneme sequences inside words, word sequences inside phrases, and the abstraction of grammar from a stock of stored phrases — and ties all three to the same mechanism: rehearsal in phonological short-term memory consolidates sequences in long-term memory, and consolidated sequences in turn let the learner perceive incoming speech in larger chunks (pp. 107–108). Chunking, in Miller's sense, "is the development of permanent sets of associative connections in long-term storage and is the process that underlies the attainment of automaticity and fluency in language" (p. 107).

The novice/expert contrast runs through the paper. On word associations, "novice language learners are bound up in the orthographic and phonological aspects of vocabulary," giving clang and syntagmatic responses where native speakers give semantic ones (p. 94); lexical items "are first represented as ordered phonological strings, then there is a focus on their collocations and their sequential probabilities in word strings, and only later are these patterns of occurrence analyzed to allow syntactic and semantic classification" (p. 94). On discourse, Ellis takes over Sinclair's idiom principle and Pawley and Syder's lexicalized sentence stems: "Speaking natively is speaking idiomatically using frequent and familiar collocations, and learners thus have to acquire these familiar word sequences" (p. 97), and conversational speech "is broken into fluent units of complete grammatical clauses of 4–10 words," a high proportion of which "are entirely familiar memorized clauses and clause sequences" (p. 97). His clock example is the clearest statement of the expert's phenomenology: once a pattern is chunked, "it is the patterned chunk that they will be aware of in working memory, not the individual components," just as an adult reading a watch "is aware of the time and has no immediate access to lower level perceptual information concerning the design of the hands or numerals" (p. 109).

The cognitive-load claim rests on Daneman and Case (1981) and on Ellis's own "headache" example. Before morpheme sequences are lexicalized, learners "had to retrieve the verb stem (*pum*) and the appropriate markers (e.g., *a-* and *-tay*), order them appropriately in STM, and produce the result," so discontinuous morphology "imposed less working memory load" only once chunked (p. 106). The child who lacks the chunks [hed] and [eik] "has to attend to a much longer sequence of chunks," so "attention is distributed over a much larger number of chunks and there is concomitantly a much greater chance of errors in sequencing" (p. 110); "the more the units of language come as packaged wholes, the greater the possibility of attentional focus and resultant association" (p. 111). The conclusion states the payoff: "The more automatic their access, the more fluent is the resultant language use, concomitantly freeing attentional resources for analysis of the meaning of the message, either for comprehension or for production planning" (p. 115). Ellis also fixes the relation between formulas and rules: the syntactic rules abstracted from experience "may be used in comprehension/production of new or difficult structures, whereas idiomatic analysis operates as a default: Linguistic analysis is as deep as necessary and as shallow as possible" (p. 116).

## Relation to the argument

This is the load-bearing source for the "expert = automatized multi-word chunks" half of the paper's claim, and it supplies the mechanism (chunking under the power law of practice, pp. 107–108) rather than just the observation. It also supports the novice half indirectly: the novice attends to more, smaller units, sequences them in a capacity-limited store, and errs more. Ellis does not, however, describe the novice as assembling utterances from "declarative grammar rules." His novice is sequencing phonological and lexical material; grammar is what emerges later from the stored sequences. If the paper wants the rule-driven novice, it needs DeKeyser (skill-acquisition theory) or Kahng (2014), not Ellis, and it should not cite Ellis as if he said it. The paper can, on Ellis's own page 116, present the expert as someone whose default mode is retrieval of stored sequences with analysis held in reserve for the novel or difficult case — which is exactly the shape of the algorithmacy analogy.

## Caution

The paper is a theoretical synthesis, not a study; the individual-differences evidence (phonological STM predicts vocabulary and grammar, pp. 102–106) is drawn from other work, mostly on children and on L1. The idiom-principle claims are asserted with corpus citations (Sinclair, Butler) rather than tested here. Ellis is an avowed empiricist and says so (pp. 100–101, 116–120); a reader from the UG side will treat the "grammar from sequences" thesis as contested, and it still is. The paper also predates the large fluency literature; its statements about attentional freeing are inferences from chunking theory, not measured. Nothing in the paper concerns discourse repair.
