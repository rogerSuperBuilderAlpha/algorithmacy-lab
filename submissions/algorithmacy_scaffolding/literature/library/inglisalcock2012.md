# Inglis, M., & Alcock, L. (2012). Expert and novice approaches to reading mathematical proofs. *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education*, 43(4), 358–390.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.43.4.0358 · **Read depth:** full_text · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

The paper reports "the first direct comparison of the proof validation behavior of research-active mathematicians and beginning undergraduate students" (p. 359). Eighteen first-year mathematics undergraduates and twelve research-active mathematicians at Loughborough validated six short purported proofs on screen while a Tobii T120 recorded their eye movements (p. 367). Three findings carry the paper.

First, on validity judgments: "contrary to previous suggestions, mathematicians sometimes appear to disagree about the validity of even short purported proofs" (abstract, p. 358). Proof 2, "described as 'The Real Thing' by Selden and Selden," was judged invalid by 5 of the 12 mathematicians (p. 379).

Second, on where attention goes: "the students spent proportionately longer fixating on the formulae than did the mathematicians (55% of the students' time was spent dwelling on formulae compared to 45% of the mathematicians' time)" (p. 371), and undergraduates "had longer mean fixation durations on these areas, 433 ms versus 343 ms" (p. 371). The authors note that the absolute dwell time on formulae was similar for both groups (370 s vs 349 s); "the interaction effect was driven by the mathematicians spending longer dwelling on the parts of the proof that were not formulae" (p. 371). They read this as "compelling evidence in favor of Selden and Selden's (2003) hypothesis that a major cause of validation difficulty is the tendency of undergraduates to focus on the surface features of arguments rather than their underlying logical structure" (p. 379).

Third, on reading order: mathematicians made more between-line saccades, "78.8 per proof, than it was for undergraduates, at 53.3 per proof" (p. 375), or 23% vs 18% of all within-proof saccades. The extra movement was local, not global: "This difference seemed to occur primarily because the mathematicians moved back and forth between consecutive lines more than the undergraduates, not because they moved their attention around the proof at a more global level" (p. 375). On Proof 6, mathematicians made 14.9 more warrant-seeking x → x−1 → x sequences on transitions that required a warrant than on those that did not, versus 5.0 for undergraduates (p. 378). The authors test Weber and Mejía-Ramos's (2011) "zooming out" strategy and reject it for this data: the mean proportion of reading time elapsed before the first fixation on a proof's last line was 50% for mathematicians and 66% for undergraduates (p. 372), and "we found no evidence that either mathematicians or undergraduates engage in zooming out, either at the beginning of or during an attempted validation" (p. 380). Their summary: mathematicians "appear to expend more effort inferring implicit between-line warrants and attend less to algebraic manipulations" (p. 384).

## Relation to the argument

This is the anchor source for the novice-fixates-on-notation half of the claim, and it supports that half directly and quantitatively. It supports the expert half only in a specific form. Experts do move around the proof more than novices, but the paper's own finding is that the extra movement is back-and-forth between *adjacent* lines in search of implicit warrants — a zooming-in strategy, executed more thoroughly. The paper explicitly finds *no* evidence of non-sequential, whole-proof scanning. If the algorithmacy section says experts "scan non-linearly across proof dependencies," it should be phrased as local dependency-checking between consecutive steps, not as global skimming or jumping across the proof. The paper also supplies a useful bridge to the scaffolding argument: the authors propose that light-touch training which prompts readers to "(a) decide whether a warrant is required, (b) infer an appropriate warrant, and (c) evaluate the inferred warrant" (p. 382) could close much of the gap, which Hodds, Alcock and Inglis (2014) later tested.

## Caution

Sample is small (12 experts, 18 novices) and from one university; proofs were short (four to nine lines) and read on screen; the task was validation rather than comprehension (pp. 381–382, the authors' own list). Weber and Mejía-Ramos (2013, JRME 44(2), 464–471) reanalysed the raw data and argued that the distribution of "initial reading" ratios is bimodal and consistent with mathematicians often skimming first; Inglis and Alcock (2013, JRME 44(2), 472–474) replied that single early last-line fixations are noise and that no participant finished a first pass in under about 30% of reading time. A hostile reader who wants experts to be non-linear scanners will cite Weber and Mejía-Ramos; one who wants them to be careful line-by-line readers will cite the original. Either way, the paper cannot be cited for global non-linear scanning. Panse, Alcock and Inglis (2018) later found negligible differences between reading-for-validation and reading-for-comprehension, which eases the validation-only limitation. The absolute-dwell-time point (p. 371) also matters: novices did not spend *more* time on symbols in absolute terms; experts spent more time on the surrounding prose.
