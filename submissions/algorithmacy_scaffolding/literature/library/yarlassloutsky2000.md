## What it argues

The citation is real and correctly attributed; it is a six-page CogSci proceedings paper, with a companion "Part 2. Underlying processing mechanisms" (same proceedings, pp. 475–480) that I did not read. The abstract: "Two experiments examined the content of novice and expert representations for both surface and deep structural elements of arithmetic equations. Experiment 1, which used a forced-choice categorization task in which surface features of equations (e.g., digits) competed with deep structural principles of mathematics (associativity and commutativity), found that experts were more likely to focus on principles in their judgments than were novices, who focused more often on surface elements. Experiment 2, using a similar task, introduced trials in which only principled elements varied. Novices were able to focus on principled elements in this case, but failed to transfer these representations when surface features were re-introduced. These findings indicate that novices had knowledge of the principles, but that they did not attend to them when competing surface features were present."

The framing sentence the section presumably leans on is in the introduction: "while experts are more likely to focus on hidden relational properties of a problem, novices are more likely to focus on less important surface features of a problem." Experiment 1 pitted commutativity/associativity against digits, sign, and number of terms in a three-card match-to-target task across five samples (first/second graders, sixth graders, 25 psychology undergraduates, 16 history graduate students as an age-and-ability control, and 20 mathematics graduate students as experts). Explanation-consistent principled choices: undergraduates 10.66% (associativity) and 20.44% (commutativity); history graduates 26.39% and 36.81%; mathematics graduates 80.00% and 77.22% (Table 1). The history-graduate control lets the authors "eliminate the possibility that general ability or development account for expert-novice differences." Experiment 2 "unmasked" the principles by holding surface features constant: undergraduates then chose on commutativity 90.79% of the time, but once surface features were reintroduced this fell to 35.09% (Table 2), so "even when participants knew the principle in question, they often focused on surface features." The conclusion: differences "do not stem from a lack of knowledge of deep principles by novices" but "may stem from different processing mechanisms underlying the construal of problem representations."

## Relation to the argument

This is the cleanest support in the set for "novices represent by surface features, experts by deep relational principles," with a numeric gap (roughly 10–20% versus 80% principled choices) and an ability-matched control group. Its most useful contribution to an algorithmacy argument is the Experiment 2 result: novices *have* the relational knowledge and can deploy it when surface features are neutralised, but surface salience wins the attentional competition when both are present. That is an argument for scaffolding that suppresses or reorganises surface features, not for teaching the principles.

## Caution

The domain is elementary arithmetic identities, not proof reading or algorithmic systems, and the "experts" are mathematics graduate students; the transfer to reading proofs or navigating algorithmic interfaces is an analogy the section has to make itself. It is a conference paper, not a journal article, and the samples are small (16–25 per group). The eScholarship PDF at qt2rs4b7j3 carries a cover sheet titled "Accentuation of category Differences: Revisiting a Classic Study" by Andrews and Livingston — a cataloguing error on eScholarship's side; the body is unambiguously Yarlas and Sloutsky Part 1. Cite the proceedings pages (1006–1011, confirmed on the OSU Cognitive Development Lab publications page) rather than the mis-labelled PDF.
