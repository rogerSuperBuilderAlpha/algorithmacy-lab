# Rader, E., & Gray, R. (2015). Understanding user beliefs about algorithmic curation in the Facebook News Feed. *Proceedings of the 33rd Annual ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (CHI '15), 173–182.

**Identifier:** 10.1145/2702123.2702174 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted PDF, emileerader.com/papers/filterbubble_CHI2015_final.pdf, read end to end) · **Source-tier:** conference_proceedings · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Users build causal theories of the News Feed from symptoms, and those theories feed back into the system because users are both its consumers and its producers. The study analyzes 464 open-ended responses from a Mechanical Turk survey (April 2014) that asked, "Do you feel like your Facebook News Feed always shows you every post created by your Facebook friends?" Seventy-three percent answered No, 8% Yes, 18% Maybe. Inductive coding of the explanations yields six overlapping patterns of "intuitive theory": *Passive Consumption* (103 responses; no evidence either way, sometimes inferring from volume alone), *Producer Privacy* (45; friends' audience settings explain absences), *Consumer Preferences* (130; the feed will not show what I want without my intervention through hiding, following, sorting, lists), *Missed Posts* (208; a friend mentioned a post I never saw, or I visited a Timeline and found posts), *Violating Expectations* (216; posts out of order, low turnover, the same people over and over), and *Speculating about the Algorithm* (223; an entity — "Facebook," or in 42 cases "an algorithm" — infers my preferences from popularity, interaction, and profile visits). The most sophisticated respondents already reasoned counterfactually ("If you start 'liking' more posts it may show more posts that I want to see") and some had built workarounds, such as a Friend list named "Everyone" to defeat the ranking.

The implications section makes two design claims. First, the feed's two goals — not missing important stories, and generating engagement — can diverge from the user's goals, and where they diverge the feedback loop produces outcomes such as a passive user seeing steadily less from friends. Second, users "can't help but informally reverse-engineer the algorithm," so designers "may be able to leave clues for users to help them inadvertently form intuitive theories that may be more friendly to the intended operation of the algorithm." The paper closes by naming the variable the scaffolding paper cares about: "Future work should investigate the relationship between varying degrees of algorithmic literacy and system-level outcomes."

## Relation to the argument

A second anchor for the feed case, complementary to `eslami2015`: where Eslami et al. intervene with a tool, Rader and Gray map the mental models novices already hold before any tool arrives, which is what a scaffolding design has to start from. It grounds **exposing optimization targets** most directly. The mismatch section states the two design goals of the News Feed and shows respondents guessing at them through popularity bias and interaction signals; an affordance that names the target would replace that guessing. The six intuitive theories are also an empirical ladder from novice to expert — Passive Consumption at the bottom, Speculating about the Algorithm at the top — that the paper can use to say what "scaffolding toward expert-level algorithmacy" is scaffolding *between*. The "leave clues" recommendation is a direct precedent for **intentional frictional transparency**: the authors propose that designers seed the expectation violations that trigger theory formation, rather than either hiding the algorithm or explaining it in full. The Violating Expectations pattern (216 responses) documents which symptoms already do this work — out-of-order posts, low turnover, over-represented friends — and so tells a designer which seams are legible to novices.

## Caution

Self-reported beliefs from a Mechanical Turk sample the authors chose *because* it is more algorithm-literate than the general population; the 73% No rate is therefore an upper bound on awareness, and it sits well above the 37.5% Aware figure in `eslami2015`, which used a quota sample of ordinary users. The study had no access to what respondents' feeds actually displayed, so no theory can be scored for accuracy; different respondents may also have been in different A/B conditions. Counts across the six themes are relative indicators, not population estimates, and one response can carry several codes. The feedback-loop argument is theoretical, illustrated by single respondents, not measured. Cite it for the taxonomy of intuitive theories and the "leave clues" design stance, not for prevalence.
