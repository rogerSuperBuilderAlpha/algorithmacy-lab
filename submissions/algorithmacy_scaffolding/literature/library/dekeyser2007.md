The one sentence from that volume I can verify at second hand, as quoted in the 2025 chapter, is its definition of practice as "specific activities in the second language, engaged in systematically, deliberately, with the goal of developing knowledge of and skills in the second language" (DeKeyser 2007a, p. 1, as quoted in DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025).

## What it argues

Skill acquisition theory, as DeKeyser presents it (in the 2025 text, which keeps the 2007 chapter's structure of theory, constructs, evidence and exemplary study), holds "that the learning of a wide variety of skills shows a remarkable similarity in development from initial representation of knowledge through initial changes in behavior to eventual fluent, spontaneous, largely effortless, and highly skilled behavior," and that one set of principles covers all of them (2025, "The Theory and Its Constructs"). Development runs through three stages, "declarative, procedural, and automatic, as Anderson" labels them (2025). Declarative knowledge is knowledge *about* the skill, usually "transmitted in verbal form from one who knows to one who does not." Proceduralization — "using declarative knowledge to build procedural knowledge" — is fast, "complete after just a few trials/instances," and DeKeyser's own 1997 experiment found it "essentially complete after the first 16-item block of practice items" (2025). Its payoff is the sentence the paper wants: "Proceduralized knowledge has a big advantage over declarative knowledge: It no longer requires the individual to retrieve bits and pieces of information from memory to assemble them into a 'program' for a specific behavior; instead, that 'program' is now available as a ready-made chunk ... to be called up in its entirety each time the conditions for that behavior are met" (2025).

Automatization is the long tail after that. "A large amount of practice is needed to decrease the time required to execute the task ('reaction time'), the percentage of errors ('error rate'), and the amount of attention required (and hence interference with/from other tasks, or more generally 'robustness')"; "automaticity is not an all-or-nothing affair" (2025). The learning curve follows the power law, which DeKeyser reads as "first a shift from declarative to procedural knowledge (achieved rather quickly, hence the rather steep initial section of the curve) followed by a much slower process of automatization of procedural knowledge" (2025). Two qualifications matter for any borrowing of the theory. The resulting knowledge "is much more specific than at the beginning, and in fact, so highly specific that it does not transfer well, even to what may seem quite similar tasks" — comprehension practice does not yield production skill (2025). And the theory does not claim conversion: "explicit knowledge never 'becomes' implicit through practice"; "all that is claimed is that existing declarative knowledge, via practice, plays a causal role in the development of procedural knowledge" (2025).

## Relation to the argument

This is the source for the paper's novice: someone holding rules in declarative form who must "retrieve bits and pieces of information from memory to assemble them into a 'program'" each time, at a cost in time, errors and attention that interferes with concurrent tasks. It also frames the expert as the end of a practice curve rather than as a different kind of mind, which fits the paper's scaffolding conclusion (experts are made by practice under the right conditions). Two cautions for the argument itself. First, DeKeyser's chunk is a compiled production rule, not Ellis's stored word sequence; the two literatures agree that experts retrieve rather than assemble, but they do not agree on what is retrieved, and the paper should not blur them. Second, the transfer-specificity point cuts against any quick claim that "algorithmacy" acquired on one platform carries over to another.

## Caution

I could not obtain the 2007 chapter; every quotation above is from the 2025 revision, which restates the same theory with updated citations. Before any quotation is attributed to DeKeyser (2007) with a page number, someone must check it against pp. 97–113 of the Erlbaum volume (library copy or Taylor & Francis e-book). The theory itself is contested inside SLA: Paradis and the implicit-learning camp deny that explicit rule knowledge feeds procedural skill at all (DeKeyser addresses this directly in 2025), and usage-based theorists such as Ellis locate the novice in statistical sequence learning rather than in declarative rules. A hostile reader will also note that the strong laboratory evidence (DeKeyser 1997) used an artificial language, and that the declarative-first picture describes instructed adult learners, not naturalistic acquisition.
