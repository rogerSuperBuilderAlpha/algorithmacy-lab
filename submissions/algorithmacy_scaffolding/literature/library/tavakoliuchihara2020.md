# Tavakoli, P., & Uchihara, T. (2020). To what extent are multiword sequences associated with oral fluency? *Language Learning*, 70(2), 506–547.

## What it argues

The study tests, on 56 learners across four CEFR levels (Low B1 to C1) taking a speaking test, whether use of multiword sequences (MWSs: two- and three-word n-grams drawn from the 30,000 most frequent in a native corpus) is associated with utterance fluency measured as speed, breakdown and repair. The theoretical warrant is Levelt via Kormos: "speakers with a large repertoire of MWSs can retrieve longer units of word constituents with similar processing cost to that needed for retrieval of single-word items," which lets them "'buy' processing time in preparation for other processing needs," whereas "speakers with a smaller repertoire of MWSs ... may exhaust cognitive resources in an attempt to retrieve every constituent item of the whole multiword unit" (Background Literature). The L2 lexicon is quoted, from Skehan et al., as "smaller, less organised, likely slower in access, less elaborated with syntactic and collocational information, and contains a narrower repertoire of formulaic language" (Background Literature).

The abstract reports that "(a) high-frequency n-grams correlated positively with articulation rate; (b) n-gram proportion correlated negatively with frequency of mid-clause pauses; and (c) n-gram association strength correlated positively with frequency of end-clause pauses and negatively with repair frequency," and that "whereas lower proficiency speakers used these n-grams verbatim, C1 level speakers used them competently in a variety of forms." The results section is more guarded than the abstract. The high-frequency-trigram factor correlated with pruned articulation rate at r = .396 (p = .003) and unpruned at r = .325 (p = .014); but the mid-clause-pause correlation (r = −.271, p = .041), the end-clause-pause correlation with mutual information (r = .302, p = .024) and the repair correlation with mutual information (r = −.308, p = .021) all "miss reaching the corrected alpha level" of .017 and are offered as "small associations" that "could tentatively imply" fewer repairs and more end-clause pausing among speakers who use strongly associated sequences (Results, Table 4). The authors' own summary states the pattern plainly: greater MWS use went with "speech rate and fewer pauses mid-clause" but not with "pauses in between clauses," and "while lower proficiency speakers relied more on verbatim repetition of the MWSs borrowed from the task, high-proficiency speakers seemed more likely to avoid exact repetition from the instruction and use MWSs more creatively" (OASIS summary).

## Relation to the argument

This is the modern quantitative test of the Pawley–Syder/Ellis claim, and it supports the paper's expert profile in outline: more formulaic material, faster articulation, fewer mid-clause breaks, and — tentatively — fewer repairs. The verbatim-versus-flexible finding is the most useful piece for the algorithmacy analogy, since it separates the novice who copies a routine whole from the expert who owns it well enough to vary it; that distinction is what Pawley and Syder call knowing "the grammar of that stem" (1983, p. 214). Cite it as convergent evidence, not as proof.

## Caution

The effects are small (r ≈ .3–.4), the sample is 56, and three of the five headline correlations do not survive the authors' own Bonferroni correction, though the abstract lists them as results; a careful reader will notice the gap between abstract and table. Only bigrams and trigrams were counted, proficiency ranged only from B1 to C1 (no beginners, no natives), and the speech came from a test task whose instructions supplied some of the n-grams. Repair fluency is the weakest link: the negative repair correlation is non-significant after correction, so the paper cannot lean on this study for the claim that experts repair faster or less.
