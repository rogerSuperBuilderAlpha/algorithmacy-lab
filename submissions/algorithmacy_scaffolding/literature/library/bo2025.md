# Bo, J.Y., Wan, S., & Anderson, A. (2025). To Rely or Not to Rely? Evaluating Interventions for Appropriate Reliance on Large Language Models. *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '25)*, 24 pages.

## What it argues

Reliance interventions on LLM output reduce over-reliance but generally fail to produce *appropriate* reliance, because they suppress useful reliance along with the harmful kind. Four hundred Prolific participants answered two LSAT logical-reasoning questions and two bean-jar estimation questions, first alone, then again after GPT-4o advice (correct and incorrect advice sampled at equal rates) shown in one of four forms: Control, a static *Reliance Disclaimer* ("Remember to verify this information…"), *Uncertainty Highlighting* (tokens with low log-probability shaded red/pink), or *Implicit Answer* (the reasoning shown but the final answer withheld). Using relative LLM-reliance, relative self-reliance and their ratio, the only intervention that improved appropriate reliance was the plain disclaimer, and only on LSAT (ARR 1.62 vs 1.17, p = .04). Uncertainty Highlighting and Implicit Answer raised self-reliance but pulled reliance down even when the advice was good; on the estimation task Implicit Answer cut reliance on good advice from 0.91 to 0.67. Confidence was miscalibrated everywhere — people grew *more* confident after wrong reliance decisions than after right ones — and Uncertainty Highlighting was the one condition where that miscalibration reached significance. All three interventions lowered perceived helpfulness; highlighting alone lowered perceived accuracy and confidence too.

## Relation to the argument

This paper is the direct empirical test of affordance 1 in its strong form (expose the model's own confidence — here, token log-probabilities) and of affordance 4 (friction: disclaimer, withheld answer), on an actual frontier LLM, and it complicates both. Token-level confidence exposure, the closest thing in the literature to "exposing the optimization target," was the *worst* performer: it induced aversion, worsened calibration, and the authors note users would have to know that highlighting reflects token not semantic uncertainty. The cheapest friction (a sentence of disclaimer) beat the sophisticated one. The paper's own conclusion — that "highlighting log probabilities to calibrate confidence did not have the intended effect on humans, who may have found the added information too confusing to optimally leverage" — is the sentence the essay's design section has to answer. For the novice/expert frame it is suggestive that the interventions requiring interpretation skill (highlighting) hurt untrained users most, which is consistent with a scaffolding argument only if the scaffold *teaches* the reading of the signal rather than merely presenting it.

## Caution

Four questions per participant, ten-minute sessions, fixed advice with no follow-up prompting, and advice quality held at 50% regardless of the model's real tendencies — none of which resembles iterative prompt work. The two-stage design (answer first, then see advice) is itself a cognitive forcing function, so the Control condition already contains friction. Highlighting used raw generation probability, which prior work says is a poor proxy for what users care about; the authors concede this. Static disclaimers may fade with alarm fatigue in deployment. Results diverged sharply between the two tasks, so the authors warn against generalising any single intervention's effect.
