# Flower, L., & Hayes, J. R. (1981). A cognitive process theory of writing. *College Composition and Communication*, 32(4), 365–387.

**Identifier:** https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc198115885 (NCTE); JSTOR copy read via the Baruch CUNY mirror · **Read depth:** full_text · **Source-tier:** primary_journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Flower and Hayes replace the stage model of composing with a process model. The stage model (Rohman's Pre-Write/Write/Re-Write, Britton's Conception/Incubation/Production) fails because "stage descriptions of writing ... model the growth of the written product, not the inner process of the person producing it" (p. 367); "writers are constantly planning (pre-writing) and revising (re-writing) as they compose (write), not in clean-cut stages" (p. 367). The replacement rests on four points, stated on p. 366: writing is "a set of distinctive thinking processes which writers orchestrate or organize during the act of composing"; these processes "have a hierarchical, highly embedded organization in which any given process can be embedded within any other"; composing is "a goal-directed thinking process, guided by the writer's own growing network of goals"; and writers "create their own goals" both by generating sub-goals and "by changing major goals or even establishing entirely new ones based on what has been learned in the act of writing." The model itself has three parts — task environment, long-term memory, and the writing processes of Planning, Translating, and Reviewing "under the control of a Monitor" (p. 369) — and the authors stress that the diagram "does not specify the order in which they are invoked" (p. 369). Embedding is the mechanism that makes the process non-linear: "A given process may be called upon at any time and embedded within another process or even within another instance of itself" (p. 375); when an entire process nests inside itself, that is "known technically in linguistics as recursion," though "it is much more common for writers to simply embed individual processes as needed" (p. 375). The evidence base is think-aloud protocol analysis of writers composing on assigned tasks such as an article about one's job for *Seventeen* magazine (p. 368).

The novice/expert contrast runs through the paper as a set of predictions rather than a headline finding. On retrieval: "The phenomena of 'writer-based' prose nicely demonstrates the results of a writing strategy based solely on retrieval. The organization of a piece of writer-based prose faithfully reflects the writer's own discovery process and the structure of the remembered information itself, but it often fails to transform or reorganize that knowledge to meet the different needs of a reader" (pp. 371–372). On surface load: "For children and inexperienced writers, this extra burden may overwhelm the limited capacity of short-term memory. If the writer must devote conscious attention to demands such as spelling and grammar, the task of translating can interfere with the more global process of planning what one wants to say" (p. 373). On local versus global attention: "one of the earmarks of a basic writer is a dogged concern with extending the previous sentence and a reluctance to jump from local, text-bound planning to more global decisions" (p. 371). On goals: "Good writers often give themselves many such instructions and seem to have greater conscious control over their own process than the poorer writers we have studied" (p. 377); "Poor writers will frequently depend on very abstract, undeveloped top-level goals ... Alternatively, poor writers will depend on only very low-level goals, such as finishing a sentence or correctly spelling a word," so the predicted difference lies "in both the quantity and quality of the middle range of goals they create" (p. 379). On exploration: "Even the poor writers we have studied often seem adept at the exploration part of this process, even to the point of generating long narrative trains of association — sometimes on paper as a final draft. The distinctive thing about good writers is their tendency to return to that higher-level goal and to review and consolidate what has just been learned through exploring" (p. 382).

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper that licenses "recursive" as the descriptor for skilled composing and "linear" for the stage model it rejects, and it is the source for the claim that the novice writes by association and retrieval. The scaffolding paper's table can cite p. 371–372 for retrieval-driven, writer-based prose; p. 373 for surface syntax consuming attention; p. 379 for the goal-level contrast; and p. 382 for the "long narrative trains of association" that the poor writer hands in as a draft. The passage on p. 375 is the correct anchor for "recursive," and it comes with a qualification worth reproducing: full recursion (a process nested in itself) is rare; ordinary embedding of sub-processes is the norm. The paper also hands the algorithmacy analogy its mechanism — a growing network of self-made goals that the writer "pops" back up to (p. 379) — which is the thing a novice at a platform lacks.

## Caution

Three limits. First, the term "knowledge-telling" does not appear in this paper; it belongs to Bereiter and Scardamalia, whom Flower and Hayes cite (pp. 374–375). A card or table that attributes knowledge-telling to Flower and Hayes 1981 is misciting. Second, the authors call the theory "a working hypothesis and springboard for further research" (p. 366), and the good/poor contrasts on p. 379 are framed as what "one might predict," not as results; the paper reports no sample size, no coding scheme, and no statistics. A hostile reader will ask for the empirical study behind each contrast, and the answer is mostly the companion chapters (Hayes & Flower 1980; Flower & Hayes 1980 "The Cognition of Discovery"), plus Sommers 1980 and Perl 1978 for the basic-writer observations. Third, the model is explicitly not a novice-linear/expert-recursive dichotomy: pp. 380–381 argue that novices who "free associate on paper" are also running process goals ("write it as it comes"), so the difference is in the goals, not in the presence or absence of recursion. The scaffolding paper should phrase the novice column as "recursion driven by low-level or absent middle-range goals," not "no recursion."
