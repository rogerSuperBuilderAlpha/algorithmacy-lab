## 1. Maric (2025), "Organization beyond organizations: the front- and backstage of digital platforms"

**Record.** Doctoral thesis, Johannes Kepler University Linz, Institute of Organization Science, June 2025; first supervisor Elke Schüßler, second Robert Bauer (title page). Cumulative thesis: a 25-page framing text (pp. 6–24) plus five manuscripts (pp. 31–135). Printed page numbers equal PDF page numbers throughout (checked at pp. 5, 24, 44, 73, 79, 81, 96, 101).

**What I read.** Framing text pp. 1–24 in full. Paper 1 (restaurants in Linz, English, pp. 31–53): Methods and Initial Findings through Conclusion, pp. 40–49, in full. Paper 3 (BJIR 2025, pp. 71–89): pp. 72–81 by page and by searches for *client*, *triang*, *accountab*, *neutral*, *responsib*. Report 4 (German, pp. 90–106): pp. 96, 101–102, and searches for *Verantwort*, *Bewertung*, *Kompetenz*. Paper 2 (German AMR version of paper 1, pp. 54–70): searches only. Paper 5 (pp. 107–135) is the manuscript of Schüßler, Maric & Dobusch (2026, *Information and Organization*), read in full in S14; I searched it here for *perceiv*, *blame*, *hold … accountable* and found no user attribution, as S14 reported.

### 1.1 Does it treat users, workers or counterparts as attributing (or failing to attribute) actorhood or responsibility to the platform?

**Yes, in one place, and it is the thesis's first contribution.** Restaurant owners are shown failing to see the platform as a strategic actor, and the thesis reads this as users internalizing the platform's neutrality narrative:

> "this narrative is not only something that digital platforms communicate but something that is also perceived and believed by affected users like the restaurant owners who do not perceive food delivery platforms as a threat or a competitor" (p. 24)

The evidence is paper 1: "Restaurant owners perceived delivery platforms as mere service providers whose service can be used or not, and did not engage with them strategically." (p. 41). Three limits matter for the introduction:

- The attribution is **strategic and business-model level** (is the platform a competitor?), not the placing of responsibility for an outcome. Actorhood here is the three-dimension institutionalist construct, "identity, intentionality, and accountability" (p. 11), not Schoeneborn et al.'s charging and discharging. The thesis cites Dobusch & Schoeneborn (2015) and Schoeneborn et al. (2019), not Schoeneborn et al. (2026), which postdates it.
- Paper 1's findings are **preliminary and unpublished** ("At this point, the analysis of our data is still ongoing", p. 41; presented at EGOS/SASE/WK ORG 2022, p. 4). 29 interviews (p. 13).
- The thesis also claims audience-dependent actorhood: platforms are "minimizing agency when engaging with users while performing as comprehensive organizational actors when interfacing with investors" (p. 3). This is asserted in the framing text; I found no paper in the thesis that analyses investor communication.

**Counterpart blame appears once, as a provider's anticipation.** One restaurant owner fears that customers will blame the restaurant, not the platform, for delivery quality the platform controls:

> "I'm afraid that our food will be delivered in poor quality and customers will be angry with us. Not with Lieferando" (p. 44)

The framing text generalizes this as delegated sanctioning: "This places the burden of customer dissatisfaction, like complaints about cold or damaged food, primarily onto restaurants and delivery riders." (p. 21). This is a fourth instance, beside Lee et al. (2015, p. 1608), Curchod et al. (AAM p. 17) and Muralidhar et al. (AAM p. 21), of the blamed party's account of a counterpart misattributing the system's part. The customer's own view is not recorded.

**Responsibility toward the platform: asserted, not observed.** Paper 3 (Maric, Schuessler & Thäter, BJIR, a conceptual analysis of existing research) states that "Extant studies have well documented how difficult it is for workers and clients alike to hold the platform accountable for their action, as platforms pro-actively employ the narrative of being a 'neutral party'" (p. 79; repeated p. 81). The framing text calls this "the fundamental unaddressability of digital platforms" (p. 15). Paper 3 names the structure: "platforms create triangular relationships between the platform, clients and freelancers" (p. 73); report 4 calls it a "trilaterale Beziehung" (p. 102).

### 1.2 Does it ask what participants must be able to do?

**Only at the organizational level, and as strategy.** Paper 1 proposes four "fields of action" for restaurants (Figure 1, p. 49) and says two of them require "the allocation of time and resources … as well as the development of platform-specific knowledge and competencies" (p. 48). The competence is to see the platform as a strategic threat and respond collectively or through regulation. Paper 3's remedies are design and regulation (transparency, co-determination, ombuds offices, pp. 79–81). Nowhere does the thesis ask what a person must be able to do to place responsibility for an outcome and go on working with the counterpart.

### 1.3 Does it occupy any part of the introduction's gap?

- **Capacity half (§1.1 of the ruling): not occupied.** Nearest is p. 48, an organizational capability to perceive the platform strategically.
- **Actorhood half: partly occupied, from the user's side.** §1.3 item 5 of the ruling says the introduction may not claim that nobody has studied individuals' attribution of collective actorhood to platforms because of Buhmann et al. (2021). Maric (2025, p. 24, from p. 41) is a second, qualitative instance: users who do *not* attribute strategic actorhood to the platform. The introduction does not make the forbidden claim, so nothing breaks.
- **Outcome-by-outcome placing between the two people: not occupied.** The p. 44 owner shows the expectation; the thesis does not follow it.
- **Join of the two OT conversations:** the thesis cites Stark & Pais (2020) (pp. 6, 8, 35) but neither Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024) nor Hinds & von Krogh (2024). Row X8 (scoped to *Organization Theory*) is unaffected.

### 1.4 Edits

None required. Two options for the author:

1. **Review section (later in the paper):** cite Maric (2025) beside Schüßler, Maric & Dobusch (2026) for user-side non-attribution of actorhood (p. 24, from p. 41) and for counterpart blame anticipated by a provider (p. 44). Distinction: strategic perception of the platform, not placing responsibility for an outcome; preliminary data.
2. **HOLD rivals passage (Complication):** Maric could join "Lee et al. (2015) and Curchod et al. (2020) record what the blamed driver or seller bears" only as a restaurant owner's fear, which that sentence does not describe. I do not recommend adding it there.

Record (from the title page; no database record checked): Maric, S. (2025). *Organization beyond organizations: The front- and backstage of digital platforms* [Doctoral thesis, Johannes Kepler University Linz].

## 2. Stelmaszak, Joshi & Constantiou (2026), row G6

**Record from the PDF.** Stelmaszak, M., Joshi, M., & Constantiou, I. (2026). Artificial intelligence as an organizing capability arising from human–algorithm relations. *Journal of Management Studies, 63*(2), 335–365. https://doi.org/10.1111/joms.70003. Printed page = PDF page + 334.

**Author's sentence (G6):** "Organizing now increasingly depends on relations between people and learning algorithms (Stelmaszak et al., 2026)."

- **"Learning algorithms": supported.** AI "resides in a system of multiple, complex, and multifaceted relations among human and algorithmic actors (by which we mean learning algorithms as we explain in detail later)" (p. 337); the algorithmic actors "are often referred to as learning algorithms" (p. 343).
- **"Increasingly": supported.** "AI is increasingly embedded in organizational life, shaping how decisions are made, knowledge is produced, and actions are coordinated" (p. 335); "sustained attention to human–algorithm relations that give rise to AI will become increasingly more relevant" (p. 360).
- **"Organizing … depends on relations": a fair compression.** The paper's claim is that AI is "a connective, codependent, and emergent capability of a system of relations – among human and algorithmic actors – to organize" (p. 337). Organizing depends on these relations to the extent that AI enters it. That is what "increasingly" carries.

**Verdict: stands; no edit.** Flag 4 in the introduction and §4 item 6 of the ruling close. Add pages if wanted: (Stelmaszak et al., 2026, pp. 335, 337).

## 3. Cameron (2024), rows A7 and K2

**Record from the PDF.** *Administrative Science Quarterly, 69*(2), 458–514, matching the introduction. Printed page = PDF page + 457. Data: "a seven-year study that includes participant observation (as both a driver and rider), longitudinal interviews (n = 136)" (p. 460); "136 interviews with 63 drivers" (p. 471).

### 3.1 Row A7 ("ride-hailing (Cameron, 2024; Lee et al., 2015)")

**Holds.** Cameron studies ride-hailing drivers under algorithmic management and frames the point of production as triadic: "the site of the worker–app–customer interaction constitutes a clearly defined system of production at which consent is manufactured" (p. 465). The rider appears as rater with sanctioning power: "In short, riders could fire drivers." (p. 477), with deactivation "with limited information provided by RideHail and no means of recourse" (p. 477). A driver on complaints: "But here, is nothing. It's working with a machine." (p. 488).

Caveat: Cameron's analysis is of the worker–algorithm relation. She records no driver placing responsibility between rider and platform for an outcome, and the rider's view comes only from her own participant observation.

### 3.2 Row K2 ("As workers develop algorithmacy to navigate these pressures, their adaptive competence inadvertently preserves the platform's opaque authority.")

**Supported in substance, on three points:**

- Skill produces consent: both engagement and deviance tactics "elicit consent … and both contribute to workers seeing themselves as skillful agents" (abstract, p. 458).
- Consent keeps the system running: "An algorithmic management system in which individuals have circumscribed choices while working is crucial to keeping the platform system running smoothly, because workers can invest in their work and develop a sense of mastery." (p. 494)
- The structure stays hidden: "this choice-based consent can mask the more-structurally problematic elements of the work" (p. 458); "Thus, workers' interaction with the algorithmic management system is on its terms, not workers'." (p. 494). Even deviance, which amplifies conflict, still yields consent (p. 495).

**Three differences the author should know:**

1. Cameron's skill is skill with the algorithm, in a worker–algorithm relation. The triadic reading ("algorithmacy") is the author's extension.
2. What is preserved in Cameron is consent and the "good bad" job; the opacity she cites is of owners and managers who "hide behind an opaque management system" (p. 493, citing Chai & Scully, 2019, and Vallas & Schor, 2020). "Opaque authority" is a fair gloss of pp. 493–494 taken together.
3. Cameron does not use "inadvertently". Consent is "active, enthusiastic participation" (p. 458); that it masks the structure is her analysis, not the workers' intent. "Inadvertently" is consistent with that.

**Minimal edit (optional, author's sentence):** "…inadvertently preserves the platform's opaque authority **[C: (cf. Cameron, 2024, pp. 493–494)]**." The "cf." marks that Cameron's skill is dyadic. The ruling's K2 note ("possible support at abstract level only") is now superseded by the full read.

## 4. Orlikowski & Scott (2014), row A7

**Text.** LSE accepted manuscript (LSE Research Online 57602), not the version of record; AAM page = PDF page − 2. Pages below are AAM pages; **VoR pages owed.** Record matches the introduction: *Organization Science, 25*(3), 868–891.

**A7 ("hotel reviews (Karunakaran et al., 2022; Orlikowski & Scott, 2014)"): holds.** The study compares the AA's inspector scheme with TripAdvisor, where a proprietary algorithm ranks hotels on user ratings and "TripAdvisor is careful not to reveal any details about the algorithm" (p. 24). The authors find accountability unlocatable: the algorithmic apparatus "Produces inscrutable assessments and accountabilities that cannot be clearly located" (Table 2, p. 48), and its scale and momentum "make identifying a "doer behind the deed" problematic" (p. 42). Staff feel it: "It keeps the staff on their toes all the time, because they are all terrified of a bad review." (p. 27). The authors warn that treating these apparatuses as immaterial "makes it all too easy for responsibility to be eclipsed" (p. 40).

**Caveats for how the paper uses it:**

1. The evaluated party is the **hotel**, mostly through owners and managers; staff appear only as recipients of managers' review meetings (p. 27). The frontline worker named in a review is Karunakaran et al.'s material (AAM p. 31), not Orlikowski & Scott's.
2. The counterpart is an **anonymous one-off reviewer**: "unseen and unnamed adversaries who run away immediately and have no stake in the future" (p. 42). The introduction's premise that the two people "depend on each other" (row A3) fits this case weakly. A few hoteliers respond by turning on the counterpart, joining Guestscan, a "guest blacklisting service" (p. 26).
3. The study is about valuation, not about how hoteliers place responsibility between TripAdvisor and guests.

No edit.

## 5. Vesa & Tienari (2022), row X4

**Record from the PDF.** Vesa, M., & Tienari, J. (2022). Artificial intelligence and rationalized unaccountability: Ideology of the elites? *Organization, 29*(6), 1133–1145. https://doi.org/10.1177/1350508420963872 (online 2020; store folder `vesa2020`).

**The two sentences.**

- Vesa & Tienari (p. 1137): "how can a human decision-maker be held accountable for decisions that occur faster and with a larger number of inputs than they can physiologically comprehend and react to?"
- Stark & Vanden Broeck (p. 14, checked in the store's PDF this stage): "But who or what is accountable when an algorithm exercises decisions that occur faster and with a vastly larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend and react to (Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137)?"

**Finding: a close paraphrase with a borrowed clause, cited.** The clause "decisions that occur faster and with a [vastly] larger number of inputs than [a human / they] can physiologically comprehend and react to" is Vesa & Tienari's, with one word added and the subject changed. The question is changed: Vesa & Tienari ask how a *human decision-maker* can be held accountable; Stark & Vanden Broeck ask *who or what* is accountable when *an algorithm* decides. Stark & Vanden Broeck signal the debt with a page citation but no quotation marks.

**Should the intro attribute the wording? Yes.** The introduction quotes Stark & Vanden Broeck verbatim and replaces their citation with "[…]", which hides that the distinctive clause is Vesa & Tienari's. Minimal edit to Claude's X4 sentence:

> "…can physiologically comprehend and react to […]?" (p. 14~~)~~, after Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137)

Vesa & Tienari then enter the reference list (record above).

**One further find, for the author.** On the same page Vesa & Tienari state the crumple-zone point in accountability terms: "Accountability is, ultimately, always a human burden." and "it is questionable if such accountability can be informed – or whether the responsibility is in the end more of a scapegoat nature" (p. 1137). ## 6. Zheng & Zhu (2026) and Buhmann et al. (2021): the HOLD sentences

Both texts in the store are still abstracts. Zheng & Zhu's capture is the Emerald abstract page, truncated in the Design section. Buhmann et al.'s is the CBS Research Portal abstract of the **ICA 2021 conference paper** (45 pp., no file), not the AoM Proceedings abstract the introduction cites.

### 6.1 Complication HOLD, sentence 2

> "Fu et al. (2025) and Zheng and Zhu (2026) measure whom a customer holds responsible after a service failure."

**Not quite as written.** Neither abstract shows a measure of *whom*, a choice among parties. Zheng & Zhu measure "responsibility attribution to the platform in the failure incidents (Studies 2 and 3)"; Fu et al. find consumers "more likely to attribute responsibility to the platform". Both measure how much responsibility goes to the platform. Zheng & Zhu's participants also "imagined placing an order" (scenario experiments), and their failures are worker-caused. **Edit:**

> "Fu et al. (2025) and Zheng and Zhu (2026) measure ~~whom~~ how far a customer holds the platform responsible after a service failure."

### 6.2 Complication HOLD, sentence 4 (Buhmann et al.)

> "Buhmann et al. (2021) test how calling an organization a "platform" bears on the collective actorhood individuals attribute to it"

**Close, with one shift.** The fuller abstract: "metaphors (such as platform, app, player, technology, etc.) used in organizational portrayals affect cognitive attributions of collective actorhood", and the finding is that "the use of anthropomorphic metaphors in organizational portrayals lead to higher levels of cognitive attributions of collective actorhood". "Platform" is one metaphor among several, and the result is stated for anthropomorphic metaphors, which "platform" is not. **Edit:**

> "Buhmann et al. (2021) test how calling an organization a "platform" ~~bears on~~ rather than a "player" lowers the collective actorhood individuals attribute to it"

This follows the title ("Player or Platform?") and the abstract's direction. If the author prefers not to state a direction from an abstract, use instead: "test how the metaphors used to portray an organization, "platform" among them, bear on the collective actorhood individuals attribute to it".

**Two record discrepancies to settle before citing:** (a) the ICA abstract says "three empirical studies"; S14 reports the AoM Proceedings abstract as "four empirical studies" (`VERIFY_S14.md`). (b) The ICA title reads "How Rhetorical Figures Affect the Attribution of Collective Actorhood to New Forms of Organizing"; the introduction's reference gives the AoM title "How metaphors affect the attribution of actorhood to new forms of organizing". Cite one version and use its abstract. The ICA abstract adds a line worth having: platforms are chosen "because here the question of organizational actorhood and responsibility is particularly salient".

### 6.3 Concern HOLD sentence

> "Where the platform intermediates heavily, or is shown assigning the work, customers are more likely to place responsibility on the platform (Zheng & Zhu, 2026), "even after recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem" (Fu et al., 2025)."

**Misattributes one condition.** "Intermediates heavily" is Fu et al.'s condition ("high (vs. low) intermediation platforms"), not Zheng & Zhu's; the parenthesis gives both conditions to Zheng & Zhu. "Shown assigning the work" fits Zheng & Zhu's algorithm-directed dispatch framing ("orders automatically assigned to workers"). **Edit:**

> "Where the platform intermediates heavily (Fu et al., 2025), or is shown assigning the work by algorithm (Zheng & Zhu, 2026), customers are more likely to place responsibility on the platform~~ (Zheng & Zhu, 2026)~~, "even after recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem" (Fu et al., 2025)."

Optional precision: Zheng & Zhu's effect "is stronger among low-PDB consumers and attenuates among high-PDB consumers" (power distance belief). The sentence need not carry it, but "customers" generalizes past it.

## 7. Fu et al. (2025): what the HOLD sentences may claim from the abstract alone

The store holds the article-page abstract, truncated at capture after the moderators; S14d read the full Crossref abstract. Full text needs an institutional sign-in we cannot perform.

**The abstract licenses:**

- The phenomenon and its name: a "locus-responsibility shift", "a form of scapegoating in which consumers transfer responsibility for service failures to a platform, even after recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem".
- The evidence base: "A text analysis of 24,183 consumer complaints and 4 experimental studies".
- The three conditions: consumers on "high (vs. low) intermediation platforms", those with "severe (vs. mild) service failures", and those with "encounter (vs. core) service issues" are "more likely to attribute responsibility to the platform".
- The frame: attribution theory, multi-actor service contexts, consumers as attributors.

**It does not license:**

- Effect sizes, how "intermediation" was operationalized, or which result comes from complaints and which from experiments.
- Anything about the provider's side, or about what either party must be able to do. Absence in an abstract is not absence in the paper.
- Hence the Complication's closing sentence ("None of them asks what the two people must be able to do…") **cannot be certified for Fu et al.**, nor for Zheng & Zhu or Buhmann et al., from abstracts. What the abstracts do show is the side: all three measure the customer's or observer's attribution. A form the abstracts carry: "All of them measure the attribution from one side, the customer's or an observer's; none, in what they report, asks…". Whether "in what they report" suits the register is the author's call. Otherwise the sentence stays HOLD until the full texts are read.
- The citation year: online 2025, issue 29(1) dated 2026 (ruling §4 item 1). Unchanged.

**The quotation in the Concern HOLD sentence is exact** against the abstract and may stay, cited to the abstract.
