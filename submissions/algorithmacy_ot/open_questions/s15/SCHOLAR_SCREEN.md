## 1. Result

**Nothing in the Scholar results occupies the gap. The S14 verdict stands.** No hit treats a participant's placing of responsibility among platform, algorithm and counterpart as charging or discharging the platform's actorhood. No hit treats that placing as something a person must be able to do to keep working with the other.

Three finds bear on the introduction. None changes the verdict.

1. **Chong, Yoo & Cheshire (2024, CHI), read in full, is the nearest peer-reviewed case so far.** Consumers say in their own words that they docked a driver's tip for "the app's fault", and one gave up trying to gauge responsibility at all. The authors' remedy is design: platforms should "surface accountability to consumers". They never ask what the consumer must be able to do. This is a stronger source for the Concern block's case than Meng et al. (2026, preprint). It also sharpens the paper's distinction (§3).
2. **The *Business & Society* item is a call for papers, not an article.** The anchor authors' circle (Schoeneborn, Berkowitz, Dobusch, Vásquez, de Bakker) issued it in 2025, and full papers were due 30 Sept 2026. Its framing names "platform companies disavowing responsibility". The Scholar snippet shows a question on how strategic efforts to downplay or amplify actorhood "affect responsibility attributions". This does not occupy the gap, since no paper has appeared. It is a timing risk: work on this join is probably now under review there (§3).
3. **Ohme et al. (Communication Theory), read in full, already moves platform communication "from a dyadic to a triadic" model, citing Simmel's third.** Their intermediary is a journalist, influencer or AI agent that serves the source and recipient of content. It does not decide between them. They raise responsibility nowhere, and "communicative abilities" only as a limitation to explore (p. 10). The paper must cite it where it brings in Simmel and the triad.

Khan (2026) could not be read past the abstract, which is paywalled. On the abstract it theorizes the algorithmic system, not the platform, as the "third actor" between service firm and frontline staff. It does so through system properties. Nothing in the abstract concerns attribution, actorhood, or what the people in the triad must be able to do. It is marked HOLD.

## 3. What this means for the introduction

- **Gap verdict: unchanged.** The join stays open: participants placing responsibility, the platform's actorhood, and the capacity this needs.
- **Concern block, case sentence (optional).** Chong et al. (2024) give the counterpart-blamed case in the customer's own words, peer-reviewed. P11: "It was obviously more of the app's fault than the driver's fault but I was just kind of having a rough day." (§4.2). Meng et al. (2026) remain the two-sided preprint. Whether to add Chong beside Lee et al. (2015) is the author's call. Pages must come from the VoR, because the Consensus text has no page numbers.
- **HOLD rivals passage.** Add Chong et al. (2024) to "Outside the journal the parts of the question are studied apart". It is the only screened work in which participants describe *failing* to sort responsibility (P2 gave up and tipped everyone the same), and its authors answer with design ("surface accountability to consumers", §5.1.3). The distinction holds: the remedy they offer is information from the platform, not a capacity of the people. Tang, Ma & Yoon (2021) is a workshop *proposal*, with no results, that asks "whether or not customers can identify a worker's responsibility". It is the closest wording to the capacity half, but as an experiment on accuracy. Cite it in the review at most.
- **"neither follows a decision to the people on either side of it" / §1.3 item 1 of RULING_S14.** Chong et al. are a further study of the everyday case, so the bar on claiming it unstudied holds more firmly.
- **Course of action (Simmel / "irreducible triads").** Cite Ohme et al. where the paper brings in Simmel's third and the move from dyad to triad, and mark the difference. Their intermediary is an optional service-provider between sender and receiver of content, which can be "bypassed" (p. 6). The platform's third sets the standing of both people and cannot be bypassed. This joins Rahman et al. (2024, preprint p. 6) as a prior Simmel reading the paper owes (RULING_S14 §1.4).
- **Common ground / Concern (platform-side discharge).** Rubel, Castro & Pham (2019) name the platform's move "agency laundering", with "Uber's driver interfaces" among the cases (abstract). It sits beside Stark & Vanden Broeck's "deflected" and Öztürkcan's "algorithmic scapegoating" (S14 row 20). It is optional in the introduction; a reviewer from ethics may expect it in the review.
- **Timing risk from the B&S call.** The anchor's own circle has solicited papers on how collective actorhood is "constituted, performed, avoided, and denied" (SAP post) and how strategic downplaying "affect[s] responsibility attributions" (Scholar snippet). Papers were due 30 Sept 2026. Before submission the lead should re-run a check for *Business & Society* special-issue articles. The introduction's sentence "Schoeneborn et al. (2026) state its general form and leave it open" remains true of the published record today. I would not cite a call for papers in the paper.
