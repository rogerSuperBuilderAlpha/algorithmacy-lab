## C-A. Partner or medium: technology between people

**Verdict: holds with reframing, for Stark and Vanden Broeck alone.** The answer does not take up our call.
It contradicts our call, and it rests on a triangle we drew ourselves. Reframe it as a correction of our
p. 16 "communication partner" line, grounded in our p. 14 triangle, and drop Whyte et al.

**Objections**

1. *Stark & Vanden Broeck: the answer denies the premise of the question.* We asked for research on "this
   new sociality—when the partner with which we are communicating is an algorithm" (p. 16), and we set it
   against Yates, for whom technology was "a medium for communicating orders" (p. 16). We had already said
   "a line is crossed … when technologies are no longer merely the means of communication among parties
   but themselves become communication partners (Esposito, 2022)" (p. 9). The proposed answer says the
   system is neither counterpart nor channel and calls treating it as a counterpart a failure mode
   (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, "Two failure modes"). That is a rejoinder to our call, not an answer to it. I
   would cite it as disagreeing with us.
2. *Stark & Vanden Broeck: the answer covers only part of the cases we named.* Our examples of
   communicating "not by means of technology but with technology" are Duolingo, Happify, "chatting with
   Jesus Christ" on textwith.me, purchases and travel bookings (p. 16). Most of them have no human
   counterpart. Algorithmacy requires "another person" on the far side of the intermediary
   (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, one-sentence definition), so it says nothing about the cases that motivated our
   sentence.
3. *Stark & Vanden Broeck: the triad is ours, so the answer's novelty lies elsewhere.* We wrote that Stark
   and Pais "observe a triangular structure (Vallas & Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and
   producers and users at the other corners" and that "the platform benefits from her agency without her
   acting as its agent" (p. 14). A paper that offers the triad as its answer to our p. 16 call has to say
   that we supplied the triad on p. 14. What it adds is the participant's ability, and the paper should
   claim that and only that.
4. *Stark & Vanden Broeck: level.* We wrote "we operate at the organizational level" (p. 2). Algorithmacy is
   "an individual's capacity" (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, "Level of analysis"). The new sociality we flagged
   is a relation between an organizational form and its users. The answer moves our question down one
   level without saying so.
5. *Whyte et al.: the "represented people" are colleagues on a video call, not people behind an
   evaluator.* Our phrase is "interacting with represented people as well as other co-located people"
   (p. 14). We define the online practice as "a site of routinized social interaction with represented
   others" (p. 3), and representation means that "the place, person and social interaction are represented,
   turned into digitally encoded data" by the video conference (p. 6). The video conference neither
   evaluates nor binds anyone. In algorithmacy's own terms it is a channel, a moderated dyad, which is the
   picture the answer sets aside.
6. *Whyte et al.: the question is about hybrid co-presence, a neighbouring problem.* The call asks how
   people organize when some participants are on screen and others are in the room. Our concepts are
   presence, proximity and participation (pp. 6–9), and our open questions concern "how coherence is
   created across these practices" (p. 14). An ability for working through an opaque, binding, adaptive
   intermediary answers none of these. The only thing in our text near algorithmacy is the remark that
   "images, audio and other data become accessible to large software providers" (p. 14), which concerns
   data extraction, not verdicts.
7. *Both askers: the answer tells each of us that our picture is a failure mode.* The proposed answer says
   "the two failure modes in algorithmacy are the two pictures each taken as complete" (`CANDIDATES.md`,
   C-A). Stark and Vanden Broeck hold the partner picture (p. 16). Whyte et al. hold the medium picture
   (p. 6). Neither of us asked what lies between partner and medium, so the pairing builds a dilemma from
   two papers that never met.

**Strongest objection.** Stark and Vanden Broeck asked for research on technology as a communication
*partner* (p. 16; also p. 9), and algorithmacy answers by declaring that picture a failure mode, so it
contests the call rather than takes it up.

## C-B. Legitimacy tests co-produced by humans and technology

**Verdict: shoehorned.**

**Objections**

1. *Our question is about how the test is constructed, and the answer is about one contender's skill.* We
   wrote that algorithmic systems "play a growing role in curating and disseminating information,
   indirectly influencing which justifications gain visibility", which suggests "the need to examine how
   legitimacy tests are co-produced by both human and technological actors" (pp. 18–19). The object is the
   test: its emergence, complexification and settlement (Figure 1, p. 9). The proposed answer describes
   what one organization must be able to do inside the test. Even if it holds, it cannot "explain how
   humans and algorithms co-produce the test" (`CANDIDATES.md`, C-B), because co-production concerns the
   technological actor's contribution and the answer concerns the human's.
2. *In our account the platform does not hold the verdict.* Audiences do. We define legitimacy, after
   Suchman, as "a generalized perception or assumption" held by "evaluating audiences" (p. 3). We call the
   algorithm's influence "indirect" (p. 19). Algorithmacy needs an intermediary that "evaluates and binds"
   both parties and holds the verdict (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, "The structure it answers to"). A curating
   feed does not bind the organization or its challengers, so the answer has to change the definition. The
   brief's scoring rule caps such a change.
3. *"Competent agency" in our text belongs to organizations and concerns orders of worth.* Our third
   contribution is "organizations' 'competent agency' in connecting situational evidence with higher-order
   principles" (p. 15). We define competence as "the ability of actors to navigate plural value systems,
   adapt to evolving controversies and strategically deploy justifications that resonate with key
   audiences" (p. 17) and write that "Organizations that exhibit competent agency" complexify (p. 17).
   Algorithmacy is a person's ability with an intermediary, and its three parts never mention orders of
   worth. Calling it "the ability that 'competent agency' (p. 17) needs" swaps our genus (a cultural
   competence of collective actors) for another (an individual's technical-relational ability).
4. *The counterpart in our framework is plural, public and adversarial, not a person doing interdependent
   work with the organization.* Our contenders are a focal organization and "challenging audiences": NGOs,
   regulators, media, MPs, unions (Table 2, p. 10; p. 9). Algorithmacy is "the ability to do interdependent
   work with another person" (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`). A legitimacy test is a dispute, not joint work.
5. *The answer's first part reinstalls the approach we argued is insufficient.* "Interpret outcomes it
   cannot see into" to find a cause is attribution. We place situational attributions and response
   strategies in the prior crisis-communication literature (p. 4) and say that this literature "has tended
   to remain at the level of organization–audience interactions, focusing primarily on cognitive and
   dyadic relational processes" and is "insufficient because [it does] not bring broader society and
   shared cultural norms … into the core of theoretical explanations" (p. 16). An answer built on a
   person's causal interpretation moves our question back to where we said the field was stuck.
6. *"Survive the platform's curation" is not our criterion of success.* In our account a justification
   succeeds through resonance with societally valued orders of worth, the power of the actor and the
   credibility of material moves (pp. 13–15). Visibility is a precondition we flag; it is not the test.
7. *The answer misses the case in our paper that is closest to it.* The UK Post Office scandal (pp. 11–12,
   15) is the one case where a technological actor, the Horizon IT system, produced the "false losses" that
   triggered the test (p. 11). That is co-production by a technological actor. The proposed answer is about
   platforms curating visibility and passes the Horizon case by.

**Strongest objection.** We asked how human and technological actors co-produce the legitimacy *test*,
in which audiences hold the verdict and the algorithm acts "indirectly" (pp. 3, 19), and the answer
supplies an individual's ability inside a binding triad our framework does not contain.

## C-C. Blind following and emergent interdependence

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** Re-enter at the team level and in process terms, tie "keeping track of a
rule that changes without notice" to progressive encapsulation, take seriously the branch in which
emergent interdependence becomes "less necessary", and anchor the binding condition in our cases where
GenAI evaluates (pp. 4, 6), not in the coordination guidance of p. 7.

**Objections**

1. *We asked a two-branch question with no answer preferred, and the answer takes one branch.* The
   question continues: "Or might emergent interdependence be less necessary as the AI reliably identifies
   more nuanced requirements for coordination inside and outside the team?" (pp. 7–8). We wrote that "our
   stance toward GenAI is neutral" (p. 2). The proposed answer treats blind following as a failure by
   definition. It has to say when following the AI *is* the right call, or it answers a narrower,
   normatively loaded question than ours.
2. *Emergent interdependence is a team-level property; the answer is about individual members.* The
   construct comes from Mayo (2022), studying "a core team and a shifting group at the periphery", where
   emergent interdependence "was organic and associated with better patient outcomes" (p. 7). The answer
   says interdependence "is disrupted where members lack the ability and kept where they hold it"
   (`CANDIDATES.md`, C-C). That is an aggregation claim. The paper must say how individual holdings sum to
   a team property.
3. *We placed the cause in encapsulation, not in members' deficits.* Our mechanism is that GenAI "might
   absorb entirely the function of informal coordination and minimize the relations between team members"
   (p. 7). The colleague "drops from view" because encapsulation removes the relation, not because the
   member collapsed a triad. An answer that locates the cause in members' ability reverses our causal
   arrow.
4. *We asked for process theory; the answer offers a variance claim.* We wrote "we call for process
   theories on emerging technologies to complement variance theories on AI" (p. 11) and asked for "a process
   theory that maps relations and functions at the outset and at the output stage" (p. 4). "People with more turns at
   it hold more of the ability" (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`) is a variance statement about who holds more.
5. *A fixed three-position triad sits awkwardly in a relational view.* Our framework, following Bailey et
   al., moves "away from an entity-based view of technology" (Abstract, p. 1) and away from "viewing
   technologies as fixed entities in fixed relations" (p. 4). Algorithmacy names three fixed positions:
   participant, counterpart, intermediary. A team of many members with a core and a periphery, plus an AI
   present in many teams at once (p. 11), is not one participant facing one counterpart.
6. *The GenAI in our passage guides; it does not bind.* In the team coordination section GenAI synthesizes
   needs "into simple statements that prompt each participant to reflect" (p. 7). Guidance one can follow
   blindly is advice, not a verdict. Our text does contain binding cases: GenAI "determining job
   assignments or pay increases" (p. 4) and an evaluation algorithm in hiring that becomes "increasingly
   refined and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). The answer should be built on those cases.
7. *What does fit.* Algorithmacy's third part, "keeping track of a rule that changes without notice",
   corresponds closely to what we call progressive encapsulation: GenAI "can continually expand the 'black
   box'" and does so progressively "as the GenAI learns and updates its model" (pp. 5–6). We also ask how
   teams can "evolve critical coordination practices when other practices become encapsulated" (p. 8). An
   answer that presents interpreting and tracking as such practices, which teams evolve over time, would
   take up our question in our terms.

**Strongest objection.** We asked a neutral, two-branch question about a team-level property driven by
encapsulation (pp. 2, 7–8), and the answer converts it into a one-branch claim that individual members'
ability decides the outcome.

## C-D. Expertise and the division of labour

**Verdict: shoehorned.**

**Objections**

1. *Hsu & Bechky: we named the ability, and it is domain expertise.* The proposed answer says the
   arrangement "calls for an ability none of the askers names" (`CANDIDATES.md`, C-D). We named it: "the
   expertise that will be needed to critically analyse, question and channel this powerful technology"
   (p. 12). In our account that expertise is craft knowledge, "rooted in their knowledge and experiential
   learning", built through an apprenticeship structure of writers' rooms and set visits (p. 9).
2. *Hsu & Bechky: our problem is retention across a career system, and the answer offers a new ability.* We
   worried that "as veterans retire and new screenwriters are not experiencing writers' rooms, this loss of
   expertise … will have an impact on the creative output of the industry" (p. 9). The challenge is to
   *retain* expertise that the career ladder used to reproduce. That is a sector-level problem: change
   happens "at the level of individual work tasks, skills and specializations, career ladders,
   organizational decision-making and broader market structures" (p. 11). Algorithmacy answers a
   different question: what new ability does the arrangement call for?
3. *Hsu & Bechky: our "channel this technology" is a person–technology relation with no counterpart.* The
   expert in our sentence analyses, questions and channels the technology. There is no second human whom
   the expert must reach through the system. Algorithmacy's triad is absent from the passage.
4. *Hsu & Bechky: our prediction runs against "rising relational demand".* We predict that "the number of
   creatives needed throughout the production process will substantially decrease" (p. 9), that writers'
   rooms "will continue to dwindle" (p. 9), and that the endpoint is "occupational narrowing" (p. 11,
   Figure 1). We also report that generative AI benefits novices more than experts (pp. 6–7). If
   algorithmacy claims that task deskilling and rising relational demand go together, it contradicts our
   forecast and has to argue against it, not cite us as support.
5. *Hsu & Bechky: our article is about social categorization.* Our abstract sets the aim as examining
   "generative AI's broader implications for the social construction of categories" (p. 1). The expertise
   sentence sits in the closing discussion of that argument (p. 12). The article type is "Conversations and
   Controversies", a commentary on Phillips et al. (p. 1). The answer takes a closing sentence and leaves
   our argument behind.
6. *Hinds & von Krogh: our question is organizational and already lists the relational outcome.* We asked
   how progressive encapsulation "evolves across levels and functions in organizations" and shapes the
   division of labour (p. 10). Our options are "hyper-specialization", "hyper-generalization" or an
   amalgam (pp. 10–11). Our hyper-generalization case has humans attending to "systems engineering,
   supplier negotiations, strategy, and leadership" (p. 11), several of which are relational tasks. "As a
   system takes over tasks, what a person must be able to do shifts toward working with a counterpart"
   restates our hyper-generalization branch at the individual level. It does not tell us which branch
   obtains or why.
7. *Both askers: the answer is too thin to test.* The proposed answer never names the three parts or the
   triad. It says only that "an ability" is called for. Neither of us could check it against our cases.

**Strongest objection.** Hsu and Bechky named the ability at stake, domain expertise reproduced through
apprenticeship, and asked how to *retain* it (pp. 9, 12), so "an ability none of the askers names"
misreads the call.

## C-E. Organization and communication as one field

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** Present the oracy–literacy–algorithmacy sequence as a contribution to
the communication field that we said is conjoined with the platform form, entering through our Yates
paragraph (pp. 15–16). Offer the participant's capacity as one component of that field, not as the field
itself, and confront our p. 16 partner line.

**Objections**

1. *The field we called for is a field of study, and its objects are genres, technologies and
   interfaces.* Our model is Merton and Lazarsfeld's twinned research programme, and Lazarsfeld's arm
   studied "mass communication" with new methods, the focus group and the Program Analyzer (p. 15). Our
   agenda "considers genre forms, for example, the genre forms of ratings and rankings", and technologies,
   "the screen and the smartphone … machine learning" (p. 16). "Conjoined" means that "organizational
   design is inseparable from design of the digital interface" (p. 15). A typology of individual capacities
   is one input to such a field. It is not the field.
2. *The answer's sequence clashes with ours.* Algorithmacy orders the forms "oracy … literacy …
   algorithmacy" (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, "A sequence of forms"). In our Table 1 (p. 14) the sequence runs
   factory, project, platform. Yates ties bureaucratic, systematic management to "written communications"
   (p. 15), which aligns literacy with our first column. Collaborative management, our second column,
   magnified "communication within and across specializations without recourse to managerial levels"
   (p. 7), which looks more like direct talk than like text. If the paper wants to sit beside our table, it
   must explain why oracy comes first in its sequence when talk-heavy collaborative management came second
   in ours.
3. *We placed the algorithm on the partner side.* The paragraph that follows our "conjoined" sentence says
   we are interested in "algorithmic practices in which the technologies are themselves our communication
   partners" (p. 16). The answer's triad puts the system in neither the partner nor the medium position.
   That is a defensible move, but it is a disagreement with our framing of the field, and the paper must
   say so (see also C-A, objection 1).
4. *Level.* We "operate at the organizational level" (p. 2) and offer no "theory of the 'algorithmic
   society'" (p. 2). The communication field corresponding to an organizational form is a meso-level
   object. An individual capacity can enter it only as its participant-side component, and the answer
   should say that it fills one cell, not the field.
5. *Our user is not a stable person who accumulates turns.* We wrote that "the user—as nothing more than
   an ensemble of data points—has no personhood from the algorithm's perspective" and that the challenge
   is managing "the multiplicity of profiles represented even on a single platform and in the case of a
   distinct individual", with reclassifications "perhaps different tomorrow morning" (p. 9). An ability
   that grows with "more turns at it" (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`) assumes a continuous participant whom the
   platform does not see. The paper should say which of the two, the person or the profile, holds the
   ability.
6. *What does fit.* Our genre of "ratings and rankings" (p. 16) and our "twisted" accountability, in which
   ratings "circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped feedback loops" that let
   the operator "deflect accountability" (p. 14), are exactly where a participant must interpret a rating
   and ask whether it reflects the platform or the counterpart. Entered there, the answer speaks our
   language.

**Strongest objection.** The "corresponding field of communication" we called for is a field of study
whose objects are genres, technologies and interfaces conjoined with an organizational form (pp. 15–16),
so a typology of individual capacities answers one component of it, not the call.

## C-F. The co-opted person's side

**Verdict: holds with reframing, for Justesen & Plesner alone.** Recast the three parts as a named,
visible form of what we call compensating work (pp. 12–14). Keep our normative stance that such work is
out of sight and devalued, and drop the claim that "pacifying the algorithm" is a failure. Drop Hinds &
von Krogh, whose question the answer concedes it does not answer. The title's "co-opted person" is Stark
and Vanden Broeck's term (p. 14), not either asker's, so the candidate should either enter through Stark
or retitle.

**Objections**

1. *Hinds & von Krogh: the answer concedes that it answers a different question.* We asked "what are
   individuals' psychological responses to the new relational fabric in which they are embedded?" and
   listed the outcomes we meant: "stress, creativity, sense of control, psychological safety, turnover
   intentions", "a loss of orientation", "an unmaking of meaning at work" (p. 11). The proposed answer says
   algorithmacy concerns what the person must be able to do, "which comes before the question of how the
   person responds" (`CANDIDATES.md`, C-F). An antecedent of our question is not an answer to it.
2. *Justesen & Plesner: we treat these tasks as devalued labour, and the answer turns them into an
   advantage.* Invisible work in our sense is "out of sight" and "devalued economically, socially, or
   morally" (pp. 8–9). We use it as a thick concept with "both descriptive and evaluative dimensions" and a
   "normative dimension" (pp. 8, 20). The answer says that "people with more turns at it hold more of the
   ability than others in the same formal position" (`CANDIDATES.md`, C-F). That makes the burden a
   resource that stratifies. Our call is to "account more fully for the emotional costs, mental loads and
   unintended tasks" (p. 22), and a competence account counts the gains, not the costs.
3. *Justesen & Plesner: the answer calls a failure what we ask to make visible.* Citing Bucher et al.
   (2021), we describe workers who put in "cognitive, social and emotional effort … aimed … at pacifying
   the algorithm" as invisible emotional labour and cleaning work (p. 15). Algorithmacy names "pacifying
   the algorithm" as its first failure mode (`ALGORITHMACY_BRIEF.md`, "Two failure modes"). Read from our
   side, the paper blames the worker for doing work we were trying to get recognized.
4. *Justesen & Plesner: the intermediary often contains people.* Our compensating and connecting work far
   from the core shows that platforms run on hidden human labour: Ayesha, who vets an Uber transaction
   "two oceans away" and is "entirely invisible to both Uber customers and drivers" (pp. 10–11), and the
   "fauxtomation" in which "real workers remain out of sight in the service of an illusion that AI systems
   are autonomous" (pp. 12–13). Algorithmacy's triad treats the intermediary as a system. Our account
   implies a hidden fourth party, and the person interpreting an outcome may be reading another worker's
   judgement.
5. *Justesen & Plesner: our call asks for empirical and methodological work.* The sentence closes a
   paragraph asking for "more empirical studies … to shed light on the various lived experiences of people
   engaged with invisible digi-work" (p. 22), after questions about netnography, time studies and document
   studies (pp. 21–22). A theory article that names an ability does not account for costs or loads. It
   supplies a category that such studies could use, and it should claim only that.
6. *Justesen & Plesner: what does fit.* Our compensating work is "activities that compensate for errors,
   limitations, or other shortcomings related to digital technologies that promise more than they can
   deliver" (p. 12), and our examples include caseworkers who become "supporters, teachers and IT problem
   solvers" for citizens using self-service systems (p. 13). Interpreting an outcome, specifying intent so
   it survives a system, and tracking a changing rule are plausible named forms of this work. Framed as
   naming invisible work, so that it becomes visible and valued, the answer joins our project instead of
   inverting it.

**Strongest objection.** Justesen and Plesner asked for an account of the *costs* of digitalization's
unintended tasks, framed as devalued, invisible work (pp. 8–9, 22), and the answer turns those tasks into
an ability that advantages those who hold more of it, while calling the "pacifying" work they defend
(p. 15) a failure.
