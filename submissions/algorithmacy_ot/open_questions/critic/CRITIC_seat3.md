## C-A. Partner or medium: technology between people

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** The paper should enter as a contest of Stark and Vanden Broeck's partner
premise (p. 16), using the triangular structure from their own article (pp. 4, 14) as the foothold. It should
drop Whyte et al. (2023), whose question algorithmacy does not answer. And it should claim the competence
only, because the third position already has owners.

**Objections**

1. **The asking article already holds the third position.** Stark and Vanden Broeck describe "a triangular
   structure (Vallas & Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the
   other corners", in which ratings "circulate among them and the platform operator" (p. 14). Earlier they
   credit Curchod et al. (2020) with finding "power asymmetries" in "triangular relations among platform
   operators, producers, and end users" (p. 4). The algorithm also stands between users, "interacting with
   the user and facilitating her interactions with other users" (p. 9). Their partner sentence on p. 16 is
   one picture among the three they draw, not their whole account. So the proposed answer's first move, a
   third position between partner and channel, repeats the asker.
2. **Curchod et al. already supply a first version of the competence.** The gap check's abstract-only reading
   of Curchod et al. reports sellers who "exploit their practical knowledge of the algorithm to increase their
   agency" inside the buyer–platform–seller triangle (`GAP_CHECK.md`, C3 #2). Stark and Vanden Broeck cite
   that article twice (pp. 4, 14). Practical knowledge of the algorithm, held by a party to a triangular
   relation, sits close to algorithmacy's "interpreting" part. The same abstract finds "an implicit
   coalition between buyers and the platform owner", which works against "binds both". Hong et al. (2026)
   name gig workers' "algorithmic skills" in three dimensions (`GAP_CHECK.md`, C3 #7), and their customer is
   absent. Algorithmacy adds a counterpart on the far side, three named parts, and the cycle of turns. Those
   additions are real but narrow, and the paper must state them against Curchod et al. by name.
3. **The corpus already carries a triadic theory of communication with non-human thirds.** Dion et al.
   (2025), writing from the CCO Montreal School, quote Smith: "communication is about holding two things
   together in a relationship through a third being" (p. 8). They hold that "binary relations are meaningless
   without a third term that provides interpretive grounding" (p. 15), and they study the "co-orientation,
   and inter-action of a few humans and other-than-human agents" (p. 15, citing Cooren et al. 2023). A reader
   from that school will see algorithmacy's triad as a special case of Thirdness. The paper has to say what
   evaluating and binding add to co-orientation.
4. **OT already answers the partner half of the question.** Grodal et al. (2024) explain when a technology
   is perceived as a participant: people anthropomorphize it (p. 4), and the technology's affordances
   moderate that perception (p. 5) (record, Key constructs). Phillips et al. (2024) sort the AI's relational
   position from a "digital assistant" that stays "very much subordinate to the human partner" to "an
   artificial person who is added to a team of humans" (p. 9; Table 1, p. 10). These are the corpus's
   standing replies to Stark's partner sentence, and the paper's contest of the partner premise must meet
   them, not only Esposito (2022), whom Stark cites (p. 16).
5. **The third party's positions already have names in OT.** Brennecke et al. (2025) restate tertius
   iungens, gaudens and separans, with the gaudens "interposing themselves between their contacts who may
   not trust one another or lack the context to coordinate directly" (p. 13). A platform that leverages
   ratings (Stark, p. 14) reads naturally as tertius gaudens, so a typology built on "what stands between"
   two actors competes with the brokerage typology in the same journal.
6. **Whyte et al. pose a different question, and Uhlin is their rival, not algorithmacy.** The "represented
   people" of OQ-whyte2023-09 are participants on a video-conference screen, and the authors call the
   video-conference meeting "a pervasive medium of organizing" (p. 14). That is the moderated dyad, the
   channel picture algorithmacy sets aside. Whyte et al. name Uhlin (2022) as the interlocutor on the same
   point (p. 14). Nothing in the passage evaluates or binds anyone.
7. **Pick-up: the triad is visible, the competence is not.** Zelles et al. describe a platform that "stands
   formally independent of both parties" (p. 17, full text). Bujold et al. call algorithmic management "a
   technological intermediary between workers and organization" (abstract only; card under revision). The
   gap check lists Pfadenhauer and Kirschner's dyad-to-triad move (2017, p. 7, full text), Snyder's
   worker–customer–algorithm triad (2025, abstract), and Edwards et al., who hold partner and medium at once
   (2026, abstract). None names the competence. That supports the narrow claim in objection 2 and nothing
   wider.

**Single strongest objection.** The third position is already in the asking article: Stark and Vanden
Broeck's triangular rating structure (p. 14) and their reading of Curchod et al. (p. 4) put the platform
between producer and user. Curchod et al.'s sellers already use "practical knowledge of the algorithm" in
that triangle. Algorithmacy can therefore claim only the competence's triadic content, its counterpart and
its three parts, and not the position.

## C-B. Legitimacy tests co-produced by humans and technology

**Verdict: shoehorned.**

**Objections**

1. **The asker's own construct already names the three parts, at the asker's level.** Patriotta et al.
   define competence as "an actor's ability to assess evolving situations, select resonant OOWs, and adapt
   their justifications as new information emerges" (p. 14). They define competent agency as "the ability
   of actors to navigate plural value systems, adapt to evolving controversies and strategically deploy
   justifications that resonate with key audiences" (p. 17). Assessing a situation, selecting what will
   resonate and adapting as information arrives map one-to-one onto interpreting, specifying intent and
   keeping track. The proposed answer gives competent agency a new name, adds a platform, and calls the
   result an explanation of co-production.
2. **The competent actor is an organization, and the counterpart is an audience.** The paper says so:
   "Organizations that exhibit competent agency are able to recognize when their initial home-domain
   justifications lose traction" (p. 17). Its cases are Vattenfall, Volkswagen, McDonald's, the Post Office
   and Uber (pp. 10–14). The brief defines algorithmacy as an individual's ability to do interdependent work
   with another person. Applying it here changes both level and counterpart, which the brief's scoring rule
   forbids.
3. **The call asks a field-level question, and field-level rivals already answer it in the corpus.**
   Patriotta et al. want to know how algorithmic systems, which curate "which justifications gain
   visibility", "shift the balance of power away from traditional elites" (p. 19). Orlikowski and Scott
   (2023) already show human reviewers and algorithms co-producing evaluation. Reviews and ratings "are then
   algorithmically aggregated into overall rankings", and accreditation schemes "lose capacity to
   effectively coordinate and regulate" (pp. 9–10). Hsu and Bechky (2024) cite Sharkey et al. (2023) for
   online review aggregators that disrupt "status hierarchies which heretofore have been stabilized by
   intermediaries such as expert critics" (p. 12). Pozner et al. (2023), whom Patriotta et al. cite, suggest
   that on social media "users serve as scandal entrepreneur, media reporter, publics, and audience
   simultaneously" (p. 15). Patriotta et al. themselves point to Wang and Tracey (2024) on platform
   affordances (p. 19) and to Karunakaran et al. (2022) on "democratized platforms for public criticism"
   (p. 2). Each of these explains co-production. Algorithmacy explains none of it, because a person's
   capacity is not a mechanism of field-level evaluation.
4. **The article's better opening fails the definition too.** The Post Office Horizon case (pp. 10–11) is the
   one place in the article where an opaque system stands between people and produces verdicts. Horizon
   reported false losses, and staff were accused. Sub-postmasters needed exactly "interpreting an outcome to
   find its cause (system, counterpart, or both)". But the Post Office chose Horizon and defended it as "an
   extremely robust system" (p. 11). The intermediary therefore belongs to one party, which breaks "neither
   participant chose the intermediary". The legitimacy test was the Post Office's, not the sub-postmasters'.
5. **No pick-up exists to rescue it.** None of the five works citing Patriotta et al. was carded, and no gap
   search was run. Nothing outside the corpus shows a citing author reading the call at the individual
   level.

**Single strongest objection.** Patriotta et al. already define competent agency as the ability to "assess
evolving situations, select resonant OOWs, and adapt their justifications as new information emerges"
(p. 14), which is algorithmacy's three parts held by an organization facing audiences. The proposed answer
renames the asker's own construct, changes its level, and leaves the field-level question of co-production
to Orlikowski and Scott (2023, pp. 9–10).

## C-C. Blind following and emergent interdependence

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** The paper should claim only the individual, triadic version of a remedy
that already exists at team level (Steiger et al., Yuh et al.) and as an individual capacity without a
counterpart (Scherer and Neesham). It should also move the anchor from the guidance passage (p. 7) to Hinds
and von Krogh's brokerage passage (p. 9), where the GenAI actually stands between people.

**Objections**

1. **OT already has an individual-capacity answer to blind following.** Scherer and Neesham (2023) name
   "infantilization": patterns that "induce subordination of individuals' judgement to external judgement by
   systems, algorithms and/or machines that 'know better'" (p. 6). Their remedy is a capacity. It joins
   Kantian introspective maturity to Habermasian socialized maturity ("reversibility of standpoints,
   inclusion", p. 17), so that citizens can "recognize infantilization pressures when they feel them, even
   when the pressures are exercised via impersonal systems" (p. 17). Reversibility of standpoints is an
   other-regarding capacity. Algorithmacy's claim to keep the colleague in view has to show what it adds
   beyond socialized maturity. The answer is a named counterpart reached through the system, but the
   proposed answer never says so.
2. **The asker supplies a team-level rival in the next paragraph.** Hinds and von Krogh invoke Weick and
   Roberts' "heedful interrelating", "collective mental models bult in situ and held by team participants",
   and they ask how encapsulation might hide "coordination practices from the view of team members" (p. 8).
   They pose the remedy at the team: "teams' ability to evolve critical coordination practices" (p. 8).
   Algorithmacy moves heedful interrelating down to the individual. That move has to be defended, because
   the question sits with Mayo's (2022) core and periphery, a team-level phenomenon (p. 7).
3. **In this passage the GenAI evaluates no one and binds no one.** The AI gives "guidance" (p. 7) and
   condenses "specialists' needs … into simple statements that prompt each participant to reflect" (p. 7).
   The brief's triad needs an intermediary that "evaluates and binds" both parties. The brokerage passage
   comes closer: GenAI brokers resources "between resource holders and resource takers", and team members'
   "actions are always recorded and under surveillance" (p. 9). That is the passage the paper should cite.
4. **Pick-up rivals answer the question at team or organization level, and empirically.** Steiger et al.
   find teams calibrating "what GenAI may influence, what it should contribute, and how strongly they intend
   to rely on its output" (p. 15, full text). Reliance peaks in the oracle role, when the team is exhausted
   (p. 13). That is a rival explanation of blind following through team capacity, not individual ability.
   Hubert places the switch in evidentiary artefacts: without them, responses swing "entre contournement et
   dépendance aveugle" (p. 25, full text). Johnsson and Nikolausson document the colleague dropping from
   view: "the number of occasions where knowledge is articulated and circulated within the team decreases"
   (p. 45, partial read).
5. **The gap check finds a near-rival that the paper must cite.** Yuh et al. (2026) train for
   "human-AI-human collaboration" against automation bias (abstract). Their competence is "collaborative—not
   merely individual" (body text), and it is aimed at scrutinizing the AI's output (`GAP_CHECK.md`, C2 #1).
   González et al. (2026) name the individual skill of "interpreting AI outputs, knowing when to override"
   (C2 #2). Lyons and Stokes (2012) report that reliance "on the human aid decreased" when automation was
   present (abstract, C2 #4). That is the failure mode, observed and already named.
6. **What algorithmacy adds survives, but it is narrow.** No rival in the corpus or the pick-up places at the
   individual level the work of telling "whether a prompt reflects the system or the colleague". The gap
   check confirms this for OpenAlex and Consensus (C2 verdict). That claim is the contribution, and it needs
   an intermediary that evaluates. Hinds and von Krogh's blind-following sentence does not provide one.

**Single strongest objection.** Scherer and Neesham (2023) already give OT an individual capacity, maturity
with "reversibility of standpoints", as the antidote to "subordination of individuals' judgement to …
machines that 'know better'" (pp. 6, 17). Hinds and von Krogh's guidance scenario (p. 7) offers no
evaluating intermediary on which algorithmacy could show what its triadic content adds.

## C-D. Expertise and the division of labour

**Verdict: shoehorned.**

**Objections**

1. **Hsu and Bechky ask about domain expertise and locate its loss in career structure.** The expertise
   they want to keep is the expertise "to critically analyse, question and channel this powerful
   technology" (p. 12). Its loss comes through the apprenticeship model: writers' assistants in writers'
   rooms who become staff writers. "As veterans retire and new screenwriters are not experiencing writers'
   rooms, this loss of expertise … will have an impact" (p. 9). Their Figure 1 traces "changing skill
   distributions" into "occupational narrowing" (p. 11). That is the asker's own answer. A relational
   ability to work through an intermediary would not restore a screenwriter's craft, so the proposed answer
   swaps in a different ability.
2. **Hinds and von Krogh already give the division of labour two shapes.** They ask whether organizations
   will see "hyper-specialization", where GenAI takes general tasks, or "hyper-generalization", where
   specialized tasks "are no longer available or visible to organizational members", or whether the two
   will "amalgamate" (pp. 10–11). "Task deskilling and rising relational demand" would be a third pattern
   at the person's level. The question, though, sits at the organization (OQ-hinds2024-16, record), and
   algorithmacy says nothing about how tasks are allocated across roles.
3. **The pick-up already names the new demand that the answer says no asker names.** Hubert: "la valeur ne
   réside plus dans une expertise substitutive, mais dans des fonctions d'orchestration" (p. 7, full text).
   S.G. and Lyytinen name "argumentation labor", the work "required to adjudicate machine-generated
   reasoning", and show experts who repair "the limits of machine reasoning" and "absorb asymmetric
   liability" (abstract only; the card warns to read the full text before citing). Dowsett finds GenAI lets
   "novices enter expert domains" (p. 168, partial read). The P2 convergence note puts it plainly: expertise
   "moves", as it does in the brief (`PICKUP_P2.md`, candidate 5). The shift is documented. Only the claim
   that the new demand is relational is new, and no rival setting supplies the triad.
4. **The settings lack the triad.** In Hsu and Bechky's thought experiment the system stands between studios
   and consumers as a recommender loop (p. 10), not between two people doing interdependent work whom it
   evaluates. Avnoon's review would be the place to look for a triadic arrangement of expertise, but its card
   rests on the abstract alone (being revised).
5. **The rival tradition the asker cites is older than all of these.** Hsu and Bechky list Barley (1986),
   "Technology as an occasion for structuring", in their references (p. 12). Stark and Vanden Broeck ask the
   professions question in field terms: "Under what conditions can we expect resistance by professionals to
   incursions by algorithmic practices? When are compromises likely?" (p. 9). Both place the division of
   labour in role structures and professions, which algorithmacy does not theorize.
6. **No gap search was run** for this candidate. The rivals above come from the corpus and the P2 cards.

**Single strongest objection.** Hsu and Bechky want to keep the domain expertise needed to "critically
analyse, question and channel" the technology, and they locate its loss in shrinking apprenticeships
(pp. 9, 12). Algorithmacy offers a different, relational ability that would not restore that expertise,
while Hubert's "fonctions d'orchestration" (p. 7) and S.G. and Lyytinen's "argumentation labor" (abstract)
already name the new demand the answer says no one names.

## C-E. Organization and communication as one field

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** The paper should claim the participant's capacity within a field that
others conjoin, and cite the CCO school as the field-level answer. It should set the relation-form criterion
explicitly against Emigh (2024) and Gloy (2025). And it should state the level change openly: Stark and
Vanden Broeck say "we operate at the organizational level" (p. 2, record).

**Objections**

1. **The conjoining Stark and Vanden Broeck call for is the CCO school's founding claim, and the corpus
   carries it.** Dion et al. (2025) argue that "organizationality, counterintuitively, is a property of
   communication, that transfers into organizations" (p. 11, record). Following Taylor and Van Every, they
   hold that "to organize is to generate Thirdness through communication" (p. 8). Schoeneborn et al. (2026)
   build organizationality on attribution in communication and apply it to "platform-based" forms (pp. 2–3,
   record). Stark and Vanden Broeck do not cite that literature. Their only CCO-adjacent hit in the
   reference list is unrelated (searched for Cooren, Kuhn, Schoeneborn, Taylor). A reviewer from the CCO
   school will say the field is already conjoined and the call already answered at its level.
2. **The CCO rival is itself triadic, which weakens the novelty of the relation-form criterion.** Dion et al.
   write that "binary relations are meaningless without a third term that provides interpretive grounding"
   (p. 15) and quote Smith's "holding two things together in a relationship through a third being" (p. 8).
   Algorithmacy's sequence (direct dyad, moderated dyad, triad) sorts capacities by what stands between two
   actors. The CCO school already sorts communication by the presence of a third. The paper must say how an
   evaluating, binding intermediary differs from Peircean Thirdness, which Dion et al. call "a pervasive
   mode of being" (p. 8).
3. **The genre half of the call has a corpus rival, and Stark's own model is genre analysis.** Stark and
   Vanden Broeck's agenda "considers genre forms, for example, the genre forms of ratings and rankings"
   (p. 16), after Yates's study of the report (p. 15). Orlikowski and Scott (2023) already analyse ratings
   and rankings as a valuation apparatus that displaces accreditation (pp. 9–10). Algorithmacy offers no
   analysis of genre. Yates studied the report, not the clerk's literacy.
4. **The three-step sequence has precedents outside the corpus.** The gap check finds Emigh (2024)
   extending orality and literacy to "digitality" in the *Annual Review of Sociology* (abstract only). It
   finds Gloy (2025), *From orality via literacy to artificial intelligence*, known only through a review
   (unread, flagged for a hand check). Heyd (2021) proposes "tertiary orality" (p. 136, full text). Jones
   (2019) lists "Algorithm as audience" (abstract). All of these sort by medium. The relation-form criterion
   is the only claim left standing, and the gap check says as much (`GAP_CHECK.md`, C1 verdict).
5. **Pick-up: the call is barely taken up, and the likeliest engagement is unread.** Gulbrandsen and Just
   find "people's ability to participate is constrained by the platforms' ability to organise publics"
   (p. 162, full text), which is the conjoining seen from the public sphere. Hall and Kuhn's 15-page review
   on "communicatively theorizing corporations" cites Stark and Vanden Broeck, but its card is at depth
   "none". Kuhn's name puts it in the CCO school. It is the work most likely to answer the call as CCO would,
   and nobody has read it.
6. **What algorithmacy adds.** It supplies a person's capacity indexed to the platform form, which neither
   the CCO school nor Orlikowski and Scott provide. That contribution is real, but it sits a level below the
   call.

**Single strongest objection.** The conjoining Stark and Vanden Broeck call for is the founding claim of the
CCO school the corpus already carries. Dion et al. hold that organizationality "is a property of
communication" (p. 11) and ground it in a Thirdness where "binary relations are meaningless without a third
term" (p. 15). Algorithmacy's relation-form criterion therefore has an OT rival that is itself triadic, and
the paper's own addition shrinks to the person's capacity, one level below the call.

## C-F. The co-opted person's side

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** The paper should drop Hinds and von Krogh's OQ-21, which asks for
psychological responses. It should enter through Justesen and Plesner, presenting the three parts as a
specification of compensating work in triadic settings. And it should place itself against Bucher et al.'s
"pacifying the algorithm", which the asker already cites.

**Objections**

1. **The asker's own text already holds the triadic case, under a name the brief borrows.** Justesen and
   Plesner cite Bucher et al. (2021): employees on platforms with "algorithmic recommendation systems" must
   "swallow their negative emotions both during interactions with clients, but also in replying to negative
   reviews". They spend "cognitive, social and emotional effort … aimed … at pacifying the algorithm"
   (p. 15). That is a worker, a client and an evaluating algorithm, with the unintended task named and
   filed as invisible work. The algorithmacy brief uses "pacifying the algorithm" as its first failure mode
   (brief, "Two failure modes"). The rival's construct is already in algorithmacy's own vocabulary.
2. **The asker's typology already has a slot for the three parts.** Compensating work is "activities that
   compensate for errors, limitations, or other shortcomings related to digital technologies that promise
   more than they can deliver" (Justesen & Plesner, p. 12). Interpreting an opaque verdict, phrasing intent
   to survive the intermediary and tracking a changing rule all compensate for a system that promises a
   neutral channel. The paper can specify that category. It cannot present its parts as unnamed.
3. **OT already has a co-opted-person account.** Jaser et al. (2024) theorize "performative consent", "those
   consensual behaviours that have the appearance of voluntariness" (p. 4). Their worker is "cyberized" and
   "thespianized" (p. 16), and they set this against Bucher et al.'s "anticipatory compliance" (p. 2).
   Scherer and Neesham (2023) define "organized immaturity" as "this erosion of human capacity for the
   public use of reason, under the pressures of socio-technological systems" (p. 2). Both describe the
   co-opted person's side. The first does it as consent, the second as an eroded capacity, the same genus
   algorithmacy uses.
4. **The pick-up answers both askers in their own genus.** For Hinds and von Krogh's psychological
   responses, Aleksić finds chatbot use goes with "higher levels of autonomy, competence, and relatedness"
   (p. 236, partial read). Rikala et al. find "Meta-work was found to increase burdens and worsen
   technostress" (p. 1, full text). For Justesen and Plesner's unintended tasks, Choroszewicz shows users
   positioned as "testers, AI outputs' quality verifiers and interpreters", with vigilance as "continuous
   cognitive and emotional labour" (p. 9, full text). S.G. and Lyytinen's "argumentation labor" is a
   "layered, additive form of invisible labor" (abstract only). The P2 batch states the genus mismatch
   directly: "All three works describe responses or burdens" (`PICKUP_P2.md`, candidate 4).
5. **Even the "more turns, more ability" claim has a measured rival.** Rikala et al. sort respondents into
   four technostress subgroups "that differ by skill, age and organization" (card, "What the work says").
   That is differential capacity in the same formal position, measured. Algorithmacy states it without
   measuring it.
6. **"Comes before" changes the question.** Hinds and von Krogh ask for "psychological responses" and list
   outcomes: "stress, creativity, sense of control, psychological safety, turnover intentions" (p. 11). An
   answer that puts capacity first is an antecedent, not a response. Unless the paper predicts which
   responses vary with the ability, it answers a neighbouring question.
7. **What algorithmacy adds.** Every rival's object is the machine (its output, its demands, its
   surveillance), except Bucher et al., where the client appears only as a source of reviews. Algorithmacy
   adds the counterpart as someone to be reached through the system. That point survives all the rivals
   above, and the paper should lead with it.

**Single strongest objection.** The asker's own text already names the co-opted person's unintended task in
the triad: Justesen and Plesner cite Bucher et al.'s "pacifying the algorithm", the "cognitive, social and
emotional effort" spent on clients and reviews (p. 15). Algorithmacy's brief uses that very phrase for its
first failure mode, so the answer must show what its three parts add to a construct the asker has already
filed under invisible digi-work.

## Across the six

- **Rivals in the askers' own texts.** In four of six candidates the strongest rival appears in the asking
  article or in a work it cites: Stark's triangle and Curchod et al. (C-A), Patriotta's competent agency
  (C-B), Hsu and Bechky's apprenticeships (C-D), and Justesen and Plesner's citation of Bucher et al. (C-F).
  A paper that cites the asker without these rivals will be read as not having read the asker.
- **The corpus rivals cluster in three places.** CCO's triadic Thirdness (Dion et al. 2025; Schoeneborn et
  al. 2026) bears on C-A and C-E. Scherer and Neesham's maturity bears on C-C and C-F. Orlikowski and
  Scott's ratings apparatus bears on C-B and C-E. None of the three names a capacity exercised toward a
  human counterpart through an evaluating intermediary.
- **The one claim no rival takes.** An individual ability aimed at a counterpart reached through a system
  that evaluates the person. It holds cleanly only where the asker's passage contains such a system: Stark
  p. 14, and Hinds and von Krogh p. 9 rather than p. 7. The other passages lack one.
- **Reads still owed before any rival sentence goes into the paper.** Curchod et al. (2020) and S.G. and
  Lyytinen (2026), both read from the abstract only; Hall and Kuhn (2025), not read at all; Gloy (2025),
  known only through a review; and the four P1 cards now being revised (avnoon2026, bujold2026, decius2026,
  phillips2026).
