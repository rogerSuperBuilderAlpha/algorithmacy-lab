## C-A. Partner or medium: technology between people

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** Answer Stark & Vanden Broeck alone. Drop Whyte et al. Recast the
answer as a *restriction* of Stark's partner thesis: algorithmacy covers only the cases where a human
counterpart stands behind the machine partner, and it must say what it does with the many cases Stark
names in which none does.

**Objections**

1. **The asker's question is about the machine as the partner, and algorithmacy denies that premise.**
   Stark & Vanden Broeck draw the line between technology as "merely the means of communication among
   parties" and technology that has become a communication partner itself (p. 9, "Object of
   management"), and they ask about "communicating not by means of technology but with technology"
   (p. 16, "Out of Bounds"). Their examples carry no human counterpart at all: "learning a language on
   Duolingo, managing stress on Happify, or 'chatting with Jesus Christ' on textwith.me", and "making
   purchases or booking travel arrangements" (p. 16). Algorithmacy's first move is to say the system is
   *not* the person's counterpart (brief, "Not a dyad"). Algorithmacy therefore answers a narrower
   question than the one posed, and calls the asker's central case a failure mode. A hostile reviewer will
   say the entry point refutes its own host. *New objection.*
2. **Whyte et al. ask about the moderated dyad, which the brief excludes by definition.** Their
   "represented people as well as other co-located people" (p. 14, "Concluding Remarks") are colleagues
   on a video screen and colleagues in the room. The video conference is the medium; it judges no one.
   Whyte et al.'s only third party, the "large software providers, who own the software that become the
   means of communication used" (p. 14), owns the channel and gets the data but evaluates and binds no one.
   The record's hand-check reaches the same reading (`records/whyte2023.md`, Hand-check, "Fit
   adjudications"). Pairing Whyte with Stark invites the reviewer to say the paper cannot tell its triad
   from a channel. *New objection; worsens obj. 6*, because the one place where the brief's "neither
   counterpart nor channel" distinction should bite, it bites against the cited asker.
3. **Stark's own triangle is lopsided, so citing Stark strengthens obj. 1.** Stark & Vanden Broeck place
   "the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners" (p. 14,
   "Accountability"), but the user in their example is an evaluator, not one of two bound parties: "When a
   user rates a provider on eBay, for example, she is not acting as a subordinate to whom eBay has
   delegated authority" (p. 14). The buyer rates; the seller is rated. From the seller's seat that is a
   triad, and from the rating buyer's seat it is a channel for her verdict. This is panel objection 1
   restated in the asker's own example. *Worsens obj. 1.*
4. **Stark's platform is a *tertius gaudens*, not an arbitrator.** "The platform benefits from her agency
   without her acting as its agent" and "The platform operator leverages rather than delegates" (p. 14).
   That is the third who profits from the two, not the third whose verdict both accept. The brief rests
   the triad on Simmel's arbitrator (brief, "The structure it answers to"). Citing Stark hands the
   reviewer the counter-reading in Stark's words. *Worsens obj. 3.*
5. **The asker already supplies an older name for the phenomenon.** Stark & Vanden Broeck build the
   question on Esposito (2022) (pp. 9, 16), and they date technology as a partner back to Suchman's copy
   machines (p. 16). A reviewer will ask why "artificial communication" with an evaluating machine needs a
   new literacy word. *Worsens obj. 4.*
6. **Accountability in Stark is "distributed, deflected, and denied" (p. 14)**, which supports the
   brief's claim that the intermediary does not answer. "Deflected" is not "unanswerable", though: the
   platform deflects accountability, so someone still asks for it. *Defuses obj. 5 in part only.*
7. **The algorithm cannot see a person, so there is nothing to address intent to.** Stark & Vanden
   Broeck write that "the user—as nothing more than an ensemble of data points—has no personhood from the
   algorithm's perspective" and that the user is a "multiplicity of profiles" that may differ "tomorrow
   morning from what they were this afternoon" (p. 9). Algorithmacy's second part has the person specify
   intent so that it survives the intermediary and reaches the counterpart. On Stark's account the
   intermediary keeps no stable addressee. The reframing must answer this. *New objection.*

**Strongest objection.** Stark & Vanden Broeck ask about a machine *as* the communication partner, often
with no human behind it (p. 16), and algorithmacy answers by denying that the machine is the partner, so
it answers a narrower question and labels the asker's main case a failure mode.

## C-B. Legitimacy tests co-produced by humans and technology

**Verdict: shoehorned.** The answer changes the level, the participant and the counterpart, and the
platform in the call neither evaluates nor binds. Under the brief's scoring rule the entry needs a
different definition.

**Objections**

1. **Level and genus change.** Algorithmacy is "the ability to do interdependent work with another
   person" (brief, "The concept in one sentence") and "an individual's capacity" (brief, "Level of
   analysis and limits"). Patriotta et al.'s focal actor is the organization, and its counterpart is
   "its evaluating audiences", the public arena (p. 2, "Introduction"). The proposed answer says "The
   organization must interpret outcomes…", which makes the organization the participant. The brief's own
   rule caps such an answer ("If an answer needs a different genus, level or scope, record the change and
   cap the score at 3"). The record's hand-check put the asker's level at "the organization and its field"
   (`records/patriotta2025.md`, Hand-check). *New objection.*
2. **The platforms in the call curate; they do not evaluate or bind.** Patriotta et al. write that
   "algorithmic systems and AI-driven platforms play a growing role in curating and disseminating
   information, indirectly influencing which justifications gain visibility" (p. 19, "Future research
   directions"). "Indirectly" and "visibility" describe a channel that filters, the moderated dyad the
   brief excludes. The evaluator in a legitimacy test is the audience, judging against orders of worth
   (p. 2; p. 16). *Worsens obj. 6*: once the platform is a filter, nothing in the case needs the word
   "algorithm" beyond a newspaper editor's role.
3. **Most lopsided triad in the set.** The organization may feel bound by the platform's curation; the
   audiences do not experience the platform as anything that binds them. A many-headed public is not a
   counterpart who submits to the same third. *Worsens obj. 1.*
4. **The third is Simmel's distant third.** A platform that "indirectly" shapes visibility for "global
   audiences" (pp. 18–19) stands too far off to form a unity with the organization and its publics. That
   is the very third the panel says Simmel excludes. *Worsens obj. 3.*
5. **The asker already owns the ability.** Patriotta et al. define "competent agency" as "the ability of
   actors to navigate plural value systems, adapt to evolving controversies and strategically deploy
   justifications that resonate with key audiences" (p. 17, "Competent agency and the settlement of
   legitimacy tests"), after Giddens (1984) and Patriotta et al. (2011) (p. 8). The proposed answer
   presents algorithmacy as the ability that competent agency "needs", but "adapt to evolving
   controversies" already covers the third part and "deploy justifications that resonate" covers the
   second. *Worsens obj. 4.*
6. **The answer does not explain co-production.** The asker asks how human and technological actors
   co-produce the *test* (p. 19). The proposed answer describes what the organization must be able to do
   inside a test that the platform shapes. That is a response to co-production, not an account of it.
   *New objection.*
7. **Patriotta et al.'s one system-in-the-middle case cuts against "unanswerable".** In the Post Office
   Horizon scandal, the organization defended its own IT system ("Horizon is an extremely robust system",
   p. 11), and public pressure ended with "the Post Office being forced to undertake a third-party forensic
   accounting investigation of its IT system" (p. 12). The system was the counterpart's own instrument,
   not a third chosen by neither party, and its verdicts were in the end called to account. *Worsens obj.
   3 and obj. 5.*
8. **Patriotta et al. move away from dyads toward society, not toward triads.** They fault crisis
   research for staying with "cognitive and dyadic relational processes" (p. 16) and answer with orders of
   worth, a societal grammar. A triadic reading of their test therefore offers them a structure they did
   not ask for. *New objection.*

**Strongest objection.** The answer turns an individual's capacity to work with a person into an
organization's capacity to win a public, and the platform in the call only "indirectly" shapes visibility
(p. 19). That changes the definition, so the brief's own rule fails the entry.

## C-C. Blind following and emergent interdependence

**Verdict: shoehorned.** As posed, the proposed answer is circular. The AI in Hinds & von Krogh's
question guides team members and binds no one, and their mechanism, progressive encapsulation, says
that tracking the rule becomes impossible. The entry could hold only if moved to Hinds & von Krogh's
evaluation example (p. 4), which is not the question asked.

**Objections**

1. **The answer is circular.** "Emergent interdependence is disrupted where members lack the ability and
   kept where they hold it" (CANDIDATES, C-C) defines the ability by the outcome it is said to explain. A
   reviewer will ask what observation could show that a member held algorithmacy and interdependence was
   still disrupted. *New objection.*
2. **Hinds & von Krogh's AI does not bind, so the triad does not form.** In their illustration, GenAI
   would synthesize "specialists' needs … into simple statements that prompt each participant to reflect
   on their work" (p. 7, "Team coordination"). A prompt that members may "blindly follow" is guidance, not
   a verdict. The team in Mayo (2022) is a medical team in a hospital (p. 7), whose members can speak to
   each other without the system. Neither "binding" nor "neither participant chose it" holds. *Worsens
   obj. 1*: the colleague, the second party, may not be inside any triad.
3. **The asker's mechanism denies the third part.** Hinds & von Krogh argue that, as GenAI learns, "the
   distinction between human organizing and AI organizing may become blurred and, over time, …
   inaccessible to humans" (p. 6, "Generative AI and Progressive Encapsulation"), and that researchers can
   observe only "input and … output, but the process for getting there is opaque" (p. 6). Algorithmacy's
   third part says a person can keep track of a changing rule. Hinds & von Krogh predict that keeping track
   fails progressively. The entry point puts algorithmacy in the paper that argues its third part away.
   *New objection; worsens obj. 5*, because the asker's theory makes the rule not merely unanswerable but
   untrackable, which is a stronger claim than algorithmacy can survive.
4. **Hinds & von Krogh pose two questions, and the answer takes one.** The next sentence asks: "Or might
   emergent interdependence be less necessary as the AI reliably identifies more nuanced requirements for
   coordination inside and outside the team?" (pp. 7–8). Algorithmacy assumes the interdependence stays
   and the person must keep the colleague in view. It says nothing about the branch where the colleague
   matters less. *New objection.*
5. **"Blind following" already has a name in the corpus.** Hsu & Bechky cite Chen & Chan (2023) for
   "anchoring when humans use generative AI as a ghostwriter, as users do not deviate much from the AI's
   initial outputs" (Hsu & Bechky, p. 7). Hinds & von Krogh cite Cameron (2021) on drivers who "play games
   with the algorithm" (p. 4). Calling anchoring or deference "collapsing the triad" relabels it. *Worsens
   obj. 4.*
6. **Partial defusal of obj. 2.** Hinds & von Krogh's team case does involve shared understanding of
   interdependence, built through members who "anticipated and adapted their own work to the needs of
   those specialists" (p. 7), and Weick & Roberts's heedful interrelating (p. 8). The Okhuysen & Bechky
   condition "common understanding" sits closer to its source meaning here than in a gig setting.
   *Defuses obj. 2 in part.*

**Strongest objection.** Hinds & von Krogh's own mechanism says the AI's learning becomes "inaccessible
to humans" (p. 6), so algorithmacy's third part (keeping track of the changing rule) is the very thing the
asker predicts fails, and the answer "disrupted where members lack the ability" is circular.

## C-D. Expertise and the division of labour

**Verdict: shoehorned.** Both askers pose the question at the level of the occupation or the
organization. One of them predicts that relations between people will thin, and the other names the
ability that algorithmacy claims no one names.

**Objections**

1. **Hinds & von Krogh predict falling relational demand.** They conjecture that GenAI "might absorb
   entirely the function of informal coordination and minimize the relations between team members"
   (p. 7), with costs of "weaker social ties and a reduced sense of team identity" (p. 9) and "less
   satisfying social interactions, fleeting team identity" (p. 10, "Discussion"). The proposed answer
   claims that "task deskilling and rising relational demand go together". The asker's text predicts the
   reverse. *New objection.*
2. **Hinds & von Krogh's question is about the allocation of tasks across the organization.** Their two
   options are "hyper-specialization", in which general tasks such as "translation, accounting, auditing"
   go to GenAI, and "hyper-generalization", in which specialized tasks do (pp. 10–11). Algorithmacy picks
   neither and moves to an individual capacity. *New objection (level).*
3. **Hsu & Bechky name the ability: domain expertise.** The expertise "needed to critically analyse,
   question and channel this powerful technology" (p. 12, "The Deeper Role that Technology Plays in
   Social Categorization") is craft knowledge passed on through the apprenticeship of writers' rooms:
   "writers' assistants take extensive notes in writers' rooms … eventually gaining writing and production
   expertise" (p. 9). Their worry is that "as veterans retire and new screenwriters are not experiencing
   writers' rooms, this loss of expertise … will have an impact on the creative output of the industry"
   (p. 9). The proposed answer's claim that the arrangement "calls for an ability none of the askers
   names" is false for Hsu & Bechky. Its concession that tasks are deskilled concedes the loss Hsu &
   Bechky call "formidable" rather than answering it. *Worsens obj. 4.*
4. **Hsu & Bechky's settings have no binding third.** A screenwriter who drafts with ChatGPT (p. 8)
   works with a tool, which is a dyad. The recommender loop runs between studio and consumer (pp. 9–10).
   Neither puts an intermediary that binds two people at the centre. The definition needs no algorithm,
   and the cases offer no triad, so the name has nothing to stand on. *Worsens obj. 6.*
5. **The causal claim lacks a mechanism.** "As a system takes over tasks, what a person must be able to
   do shifts toward working with a counterpart through that system" (CANDIDATES, C-D) is asserted, not
   derived. Hsu & Bechky expect "the number of creatives needed throughout the production process" to
   "substantially decrease" (p. 9). Fewer people means fewer counterparts. *New objection.*

**Strongest objection.** The askers predict that GenAI will "minimize the relations between team
members" (Hinds & von Krogh, p. 7) and will erode a named expertise (Hsu & Bechky, pp. 9, 12), so
"rising relational demand" and "an ability none of the askers names" contradict the texts they claim to
answer.

## C-E. Organization and communication as one field

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** Claim less. Algorithmacy can supply one participant-level element of
the conjoined field, namely what the person must do with the rating genre, and nothing more. The answer
must drop the claim that the oracy, literacy, algorithmacy sequence *is* the communication field Stark &
Vanden Broeck ask for, and it must square that sequence with Stark's own lineage of Lazarsfeld and Yates.

**Objections**

1. **Level mismatch.** Stark & Vanden Broeck's "field of communication" is the counterpart of Lazarsfeld's
   "mass communication", which Lazarsfeld studied as a research domain alongside Merton's bureaucracy (p.
   15, "Out of Bounds"). They "operate at the organizational level" (p. 2, "Introduction"). Their content
   for the field is "genre forms … of ratings and rankings" and "technologies … of artificial intelligence"
   (p. 16), and their claim is that "organizational design is inseparable from design of the digital
   interface" (p. 15). A typology of individual capacities is a different object. *New objection.*
2. **The literacy row fails against Stark's own lineage.** The brief's sequence assigns literacy to "dyads
   moderated by a text" (brief, "A sequence of forms"). Stark & Vanden Broeck's historical analogue for
   text is Yates's written communication in bureaucracy: "a medium for communicating orders from top to
   bottom or for reports issued by subordinates to superiors" (p. 16), which is hierarchy, not a dyad. Their
   analogue for the earlier communication field is mass communication, which runs one to many (p. 15).
   Neither matches "a dyad moderated by a text". If the middle term of the analogy does not hold in the
   asker's own history, the analogy that names the concept does not hold either. *Worsens obj. 6.*
3. **Stark's ratings sort positions.** In Stark's example the user rates and the provider is rated (p.
   14). The genre of ratings and rankings that Stark & Vanden Broeck put at the centre of the field (p. 16)
   makes one party an evaluator and the other the evaluated. A typology that files the arrangement as a
   "triad" files it from the provider's seat. *Worsens obj. 1.*
4. **The platform in Stark's field profits; it does not arbitrate.** Twisted accountability "makes it
   possible for the platform operator to deflect accountability" (p. 14). Here Stark & Vanden Broeck
   describe a *tertius gaudens*. *Worsens obj. 3.*
5. **"Technology as organization" leaves no separate third.** Stark & Vanden Broeck propose that "we
   should begin considering technology as organization" and ask whether technology is "synonymous with
   organizational structure" (p. 16). If the intermediary *is* the organization, the triad's third is the
   arrangement itself, and Simmel's figure of a third party standing between two others no longer fits.
   *New objection.*
6. **Partial defusal of obj. 2.** "Form of the relation" as a sorting criterion does not need Okhuysen &
   Bechky at all. If the answer leans on relational form rather than on the three coordination
   conditions, it sidesteps the four-features-to-three-conditions fit. *Defuses obj. 2 in part*, but only
   by dropping the claim in the brief's parts table ("Restores (GDOC, after Okhuysen & Bechky 2009)").

**Strongest objection.** Stark & Vanden Broeck ask for an organizational-level field of communication
built on genres of ratings and rankings (pp. 15–16), and their own historical analogue for text is
hierarchical written communication and one-to-many mass communication, not a "dyad moderated by a text",
so the sequence that is meant to answer them fails at its middle term.

## C-F. The co-opted person's side

**Verdict: holds with reframing.** The individual level fits both askers. The answer must (a) say what
algorithmacy predicts for the outcomes Hinds & von Krogh list, rather than claim to come "before" them;
(b) take a position on Justesen & Plesner's reading of the same practices as devalued, invisible work;
and (c) answer Stark & Vanden Broeck's point that use is the mechanism of co-optation.

**Objections**

1. **"Comes before" sidesteps the question.** Hinds & von Krogh ask about "individuals' psychological
   responses" and list outcomes: "stress, creativity, sense of control, psychological safety, turnover
   intentions" (p. 11, "Discussion"). The proposed answer declines to predict any of them. A reviewer
   will read that as declining the question. *New objection.*
2. **Justesen & Plesner read the same practice the other way.** They file Bucher et al.'s effort "aimed
   through direct and indirect practices at pacifying the algorithm" under invisible cleaning work and
   emotional labour (p. 15, "Far from the core: Moderating content and moderating oneself"). The brief
   names "pacifying the algorithm" as algorithmacy's first failure mode (brief, "Two failure modes"). The
   record's hand-check flags the clash (`records/justesen2024.md`, Hand-check, "Note for synthesis"). The
   asker treats the practice as labour that the organization depends on, while the entry point treats it
   as incompetence. *New objection.*
3. **Competence framing turns devalued labour into an asset.** Justesen & Plesner's concept is "thick":
   invisible work is "unrecognized, undervalued and often underpaid work" (p. 3, introduction). Calling the
   same "unintended tasks" an ability that some "hold more of" turns a cost that the organization offloads
   onto the person into a credit for the person. A feminist-STS reviewer will call this the devaluation
   mechanism itself. *New objection.*
4. **The parts already have names in the asker's text.** "Compensating work", work that makes up for
   "shortcomings related to digital technologies that promise more than they can deliver" (p. 12,
   "Compensating"), and "connecting work", "the maintenance and repair of digitally afforded connections"
   (p. 10, "Connecting"), cover interpreting failures and keeping a connection to the counterpart alive.
   *Worsens obj. 4*, with a locator inside the very article the entry point cites.
5. **Repair is documented, so "unanswerable" weakens.** Connecting work is, by definition, *repair*
   (p. 10). Justesen & Plesner also report microworkers who "often struggle to identify who to contact"
   when an account is blocked (p. 11), which supports algorithmacy. Both readings sit in one article: the
   system may be hard to answer, but people do repair work on it. *Worsens obj. 5.*
6. **The ability is supplied by others, not held by the person.** In Justesen & Plesner's public-sector
   case, citizens lacking "digital skills" are carried by case workers who act as "supporters, teachers
   and IT problem solvers" (p. 13, citing Pors, 2015), and smart-grid technicians act as "middlemen" who
   train users (p. 11). The capacity is distributed across hidden helpers. "People with more turns at it
   hold more of the ability" makes individual what the asker shows to be collective. *New objection.*
7. **Co-optation through use: more turns means more co-opted.** Stark & Vanden Broeck write that "it is in
   using the algorithm that one is folded into algorithmic management" (p. 9) and that the platform
   "benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). On that account, a person with more
   turns at the cycle is more fully enrolled and more useful to the platform. The claim to give "the
   co-opted person's side" (brief, "What the paper claims") then reads as the platform's side seen from
   inside. *Worsens obj. 3*: the platform as *tertius gaudens* profits most from the most able person.
8. **Partial defusal of obj. 1.** Hinds & von Krogh's and Justesen & Plesner's questions are posed from
   one seat, the person bearing the cost. Since both askers want a positional account, a typology that
   sorts positions fits their question better than it fits the paper's claim about arrangements. *Defuses
   obj. 1 in part*, but only if the paper accepts that its unit is the position.

**Strongest objection.** Justesen & Plesner read the effort of "pacifying the algorithm" as devalued
invisible labour that organizations depend on (p. 15), while the brief reads it as a failure of the
person's ability. Taking their call as an entry point puts algorithmacy on the side of the devaluation
their concept exists to expose.

## Note on the deliberate weak candidate

On the merits, C-B is the weakest: it changes the participant, the counterpart and the level, and its
platform neither evaluates nor binds. C-D is a close second, because both of its askers' texts contradict
the proposed answer. I judged each candidate on its own text and not on that guess.
