## 2. The four owed reads

### 2.1 Haase, Schuessler, Schmiel, Ortmann, Suchanek & Schoeneborn (2025)

*The past, present and future of the corporate actor: Ontological, epistemological and theoretical
considerations.* *Schmalenbach Journal of Business Research* 77, 643–680.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s41471-025-00213-w. The record matches Schoeneborn et al.'s list (p. 17). OA (CC BY).
**Read in full.**

**What it says.** Haase et al. is a curated article of four essays. The passage that Schoeneborn et al. (2026, p. 13) relay comes from
Schoeneborn's own essay, §5 "The Strategic (De-)Construction of Corporate Actorhood":
- "there is a countervailing trend of deliberately downplaying organizational actorhood (Vergne and Wry
  2014), usually in an attempt to avoid responsibility attributions for certain corporate actions" (p. 668).
- The three drivers he names are networked organizing, open organizing, and organizing outside formal
  organization (pp. 668–669). **Platforms are not among them.**
- Platforms appear only in the Table 1 summary: "Accountability of corporate actors is weakened if they
  succeed in downplaying their actorhood status (e.g. digital platforms' self-portrayal as tech
  infrastructure rather than content provider)" (Table 1, p. 650). "Pre-empt regulation" also appears only in
  Table 1: "in an attempt to evade responsibility attributions and/or to pre-empt regulation" (p. 650).
- The editors' overview adds: "corporate actors downplay their power and deny their responsibility for the
  assessed consequences of their actions" in "AI and digitalization" (p. 651).

**Support for Schoeneborn et al.'s use.** The source supports the claim *as a claim by the same author*, and
the platform example is an illustration in a summary table, with no evidence behind it. The p. 13 remark thus
cites Schoeneborn's own earlier essay, which in turn cites Vergne & Wry.

**Bearing on the introduction's question.** Weak. Haase et al. place accountability with society, regulators
and science, and they treat the corporate actor's responsibility for "wrongdoing", not where users or
workers locate responsibility. One usable line is the editors' question "Who can be held socially and
ethically responsible and legally accountable, and for what?" (p. 651). It is Ortmann's question, framed at
the level of law and society.

### 2.2 Vergne & Wry (2014)

*Categorizing categorization research: Review, integration, and future directions.* *Journal of
Management Studies* 51(1), 56–94. https://doi.org/10.1111/joms.12044. Schoeneborn's list gives "51, 56–94", which
matches. Closed access. **Not accessible; abstract only.**

**What the abstract says.** It is "a systematic review of the literature on organizational categories and
categorization published in the last 14 years (1999–2012)" and proposes "integrative definitions of its
core constructs" (abstract). The abstract mentions neither platforms, actorhood, accountability nor regulation.

**Support.** I cannot verify it from the abstract. A review of categorization research is an indirect source for
"platforms … downplay their collective actorhood status to … preempt regulation". If it supports the claim at all,
it would be through a passage on firms avoiding or straddling categories. Someone with access needs to find
the page. Until then, treat the p. 13 remark as resting on Haase et al. (2025) alone.

**Bearing on the introduction's question.** None visible.

### 2.3 van Houwelingen & Stoelhorst (2023)

*Digital is different: Digitalization undermines stakeholder relations because it impedes firm
anthropomorphization.* *Academy of Management Discoveries* 9(3), 297–319.
https://doi.org/10.5465/amd.2021.0245. The record matches Schoeneborn's list (p. 19); it was online 2022. OA via
UvA-DARE (published version). **Read in full** (abstract, Studies 1–4 results, discussion).

**What it says.** Four vignette experiments (total n = 819). "people attribute less experience (but not
less agency) to more digitalized firms and this reduces their willingness to affiliate with, trust, or
enact prosocial behavior toward such firms" (abstract, p. 297). In Study 1, for agency, "the highly digital
firm … and the minimally digital firm … were seen as about equally agentic" (p. 302). The discussion says
digitalization "only affects the attribution of experience, and not the attribution of agency" (p. 313).
The firms were conventional ones, such as an insurance broker (pp. 307, 309). None was a platform or a new
organizational form.

**Support for Schoeneborn et al.'s use.** Partial. The paper does show that digitalization impedes
anthropomorphization in one dimension (experience), with costs for "trustful stakeholder relations", which
matches Schoeneborn's last clause. It does **not** show that digitalization lowers attributions of the
dimension closest to actorhood, agency ("the ability to intend and act on intentions", p. 298). It also does
not study "new organizational forms". Schoeneborn et al.'s "and thus also attributions of collective
actorhood" goes beyond the source.

**Bearing on the introduction's question.** It bears directly, and it cuts against Complication X2–X3. Users
of a digitalized firm still see it as able to intend and act, so they still see it as an actor that decides.
The paper offers no support for "the party that decides may be the party least likely to be charged with
deciding". It does support a narrower claim: digitalized firms are seen as less able to *feel*, and people
trust them less. The author should decide whether to keep X2 as a report of Schoeneborn et al. with this
caveat, or to cut the inference.

### 2.4 Roulet & Pichler (2020)

*Blame game theory: Scapegoating, whistleblowing and discursive struggles following accusations of
organizational misconduct.* *Organization Theory* 1(4). https://doi.org/10.1177/2631787720975192. Schoeneborn's
list gives "1(4), 1–30"; Crossref gives article number 2631787720975192, with no page range. OA (CC BY-NC), but
**not accessible from this session**: SAGE served a bot challenge, the Cambridge repository returned 503, and
Consensus holds no OA copy. **Abstract only.**

**What the abstract says.** "when organizations are accused of misconduct, the resulting ambiguity also
opens opportunities for organizations and their members to generate discourses aimed at deflecting blame"
and "the sequence of discursive strategies employed by an organization and its members to strategically
shift blame by attributing responsibility to others or denying misconduct" (abstract).

**Support for Schoeneborn et al.'s use.** The abstract supports their p. 11 use: scapegoating moves blame for
corporate wrongdoing onto individuals. I could not check the block quotation that Schoeneborn et al. reproduce
on p. 11 and attribute to Roulet and Pichler's p. 9 ("For an organization, the objective of this strategy is
to deflect an accusation of misconduct…"). It contains a nested quotation of Gangloff et al. (2016, p. 1617).
Read it when the publisher or repository is reachable.

**Bearing on the introduction's question.** Indirect. The blame moves *inside* the organization, from
organization to member, after a public accusation. Users and workers do not locate it. Roulet and Pichler
are closest to Elish (C9): both show blame landing on an individual. The introduction does not cite Roulet and
Pichler, and has no need to.
