## 1. Verdict

**I found no work that treats a platform participant's outcome-by-outcome sorting of responsibility among platform, algorithm and counterpart as charging or discharging the platform's actorhood, and none that treats that sorting as a capacity a person needs in order to work with a counterpart.** Sources: Consensus (9 searches), OpenAlex (15 searches, one rejected for a wildcard, and 6 citation snowballs), Crossref (3 searches, one failed), Semantic Scholar (citation lookups only; its search returned HTTP 429 or nothing), all run on 23 November 2026. Scholar Gateway refused every call (ACCESS_DENIED), so one of the two named sources is missing from this search.

The narrow claim the introduction makes holds. Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) have no citing works in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar, so no follow-up has yet taken their p. 14 question to platform participants.

The verdict covers less than it may seem to. Its parts are occupied. Three literatures already study the people a platform stands between as they decide whom to hold to account:

1. **Service-failure attribution in marketing** asks, by experiment, whether customers blame the platform or the provider after a failure (Fu, Bai & Gu, 2025; Ketron & Mai, 2020; Zheng & Zhu, 2026; Davidson, Meng & Gleim, 2026). This is the everyday case, seen from the customer's side and measured one failure at a time. It uses attribution theory, not actorhood.
2. **Platform-labour ethnography and HCI** show the counterpart absorbing blame for what the platform's data or dispatch produced (Meng, Shi, Liu & Chen, 2026; Curchod et al., 2020; Chan, 2022; Wang, Zhang & Chen, 2024).
3. **The CCO actorhood group itself** has tested how framing a platform as "platform" or "app" lowers individuals' attributions of collective actorhood (Buhmann, Maltseva, Schoeneborn & Haack, 2021), and one of the 2026 authors, writing with Schüßler and Maric, says that platforms "do not openly assume this actorhood" (Schüßler, Maric & Dobusch, 2026, pp. 3–4).

What remains open is the join: nobody puts the participant's attribution between platform and counterpart in Schoeneborn et al.'s terms of charging and discharging, and nobody asks what a person must be able to do to make that attribution well enough to keep working with the other person. The introduction can claim that join and no more. It must cite (1)–(3) to claim it.

## 3. Rivals the introduction must cite

1. **Fu, Bai & Gu (2025)**, with Zheng & Zhu (2026) as support. These are the everyday case, measured. Without them a reviewer from marketing or service research will say the "person who must decide, outcome by outcome, whom to hold to account" is already a studied phenomenon. The distinction: they measure the customer's attribution as a reaction and stop there. The paper asks what the attributor must be able to do, and what the attribution does to the platform's actorhood.
2. **Buhmann, Maltseva, Schoeneborn & Haack (2021).** This is the only work found that measures individuals' attribution of collective actorhood to platforms. It shares an author with the anchor paper, so a reviewer from that circle will expect it to be cited. Distinguish: portrayals, not outcomes; no counterpart.
3. **Meng et al. (2026).** It supplies the `[NEEDS]` case (see §4). Cite it as a preprint.
4. **Rahman, Karunakaran & Cameron (2024).** It is the *Annals* review of platform accountability, and it already puts providers and customers in the accountability picture. Distinguish: system design, not participants' attribution.
5. **Schüßler, Maric & Dobusch (2026).** Same claim as Schoeneborn et al. p. 13, from the same circle, with the same Haase et al. (2025) source. It is worth citing beside p. 13, and it makes Haase et al. (2025) a read owed twice over.

Scolari et al. (2026) should be cited if the paper's competence claim is put against "platform work literacies". Leonardi (2025) should be cited if attribution is called a skill.

## 4. Sentences of the introduction the search bears on

1. **"Deflected responsibility does not disappear. It lands on the people the platform stands between."** (Concern, first [C:] sentences.) The search partly contradicts this as a general claim. Fu et al. (2025), Ketron and Mai (2020) and Zheng and Zhu (2026) find customers putting responsibility *on the platform*, even when they know the provider caused the failure. Meng et al. (2026) and Eom et al. (2026) find it landing on people. The direction depends on who attributes (customer or worker) and how the platform is framed. As written the sentence is stated without a source and is half supported. A hedge or a citation is needed; the wording is the author's decision.
2. **`[NEEDS: one published case of a counterpart blamed for a platform's verdict …]`.** Meng et al. (2026) supply one: users read a stalled dot or a missed algorithmic ETA as courier negligence, and couriers absorb blame for dispatch and kitchen delays (pp. 8, 9, 15). It is an arXiv preprint (v1, 30 Sep 2026), and the case is a counterpart blamed for platform *data and dispatch*, not for a scored verdict. Whether that is close enough is the author's call.
3. **"a high degree of digitalization can impede anthropomorphization and thus also attributions of collective actorhood" (Schoeneborn et al., p. 10), used to say the deciding party "may be the party least likely to be charged with deciding".** Buhmann et al. (2021) support the p. 10 report for portrayals. Zheng and Zhu (2026) point the other way for outcomes: algorithm-directed framing raised responsibility attributed to the platform. Responsibility attribution is not attribution of collective actorhood, so this is no contradiction, but a reviewer may raise it. The sentence's "may" is warranted. It should not be strengthened.
4. **"The everyday case … is not among the cases they name."** This is confirmed as a claim about Schoeneborn et al. p. 14. If it is widened to "is not studied", rows 3–8 contradict it.
5. **"neither follows a decision to the people on either side of it"** (Complication). This is true of the two OT papers as the run read them. Chan (2022), Curchod et al. (2020) and Meng et al. (2026) do follow decisions to both sides, so the sentence must stay scoped to "both conversations".
