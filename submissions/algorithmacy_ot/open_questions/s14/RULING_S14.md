## Answer to the author

The introduction can sell the question, and after S14 it has better grounds for doing so than the draft used. Three things must change first.

1. **The gap is a join, not an empty field.** Service researchers already measure whom a customer blames after a platform failure, and students of platform work already record the blamed worker. What I found no work doing is to treat that placing of responsibility as something the two people must be able to do, or as bearing on the platform's actorhood. The introduction may claim that and no more, and only with the rivals named in it.
2. **One citation in your text is wrong.** Karunakaran et al. (2022) study social media commentary on hotels and emergency services, not automated systems. I propose a replacement citation that fits your sentence as written; the decision is yours (§2, row C2).
3. **The concern block answers the question before asking it, and half the answer is wrong.** "It lands on the people the platform stands between" holds for the sanction and fails for whom people hold responsible. Customers often place responsibility on the platform. Stated by condition, the concern is stronger, because the direction turns on what each person can see, and what each can see is set in the platform's interface (§3; the second clause is my inference from three sources).

Two finds at source improve the opening. Your own cited study, Curchod et al. (2020), reports sellers in dispute with a buyer being told "we're only a platform" (accepted manuscript, p. 21). Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024) write that "the platform owner can claim to merely be a conduit for a service" (preprint p. 6). The opening no longer has to rest on Schoeneborn et al.'s unevidenced remark alone.

## 1. The gap claim, as it may now be stated

### 1.1 What the search supports

S14a ran 9 Consensus searches, 15 OpenAlex searches (one rejected), 6 citation snowballs and 3 Crossref searches (one failed) on 23 November 2026; I recounted the log and the figures match. Scholar Gateway refused every call and Semantic Scholar's search returned nothing usable. No work in the table occupies the gap. Of the 25 nearest works, nine are now read in full and sixteen rest on abstracts. S14a's "sixteen" was nineteen when written; rows 8, 9 and 21 moved to full text in S14b–d.

The claim has two halves, and they differ in strength.

- **The actorhood half is true and cheap.** Schoeneborn et al. went online on 19 September 2026 and have no citing work, so nobody could yet have applied "charging" and "discharging" to platform participants. A reviewer will not credit that as a gap. The claim should therefore name the older idea, attribution of actorhood to an organization, where Buhmann et al. (2021) already stand.
- **The capacity half carries the weight.** I found no work that treats the placing of responsibility as something a person must be able to do in order to go on working with a counterpart. The nearest statements are a moderator and a list: "workers with greater technological knowledge may better distinguish between system inherent flaws and organizational choices" (Bujold et al., 2026, p. 15), and Scolari et al.'s "platform work literacies", which handle customers and the platform in separate dimensions (accepted manuscript, p. 21; S14a's read).

One find narrows the claim further. The participant's side of a platform that disclaims actorhood is already observed. Curchod et al.'s sellers say eBay staff "withdrew behind" the claim of neutrality whenever a seller had a problem with a buyer, and the authors record what "sellers expected from a platform owner—an actor taking responsibility for transactions and helping resolve disputes between buyers and sellers" (AAM p. 21). Curchod et al. file this under power asymmetry ("eBay's indifference and distance", Figure 1, AAM p. 53). The introduction cannot present the participant's side as unseen.

Without (c) the paper carries (a) and (b), which state the same thing by naming who did what. I recommend (a) and (b) in the introduction and (c) held for the methods note or dropped.

### 1.3 What the introduction may not claim

1. That the everyday case is unstudied. Fu et al., Zheng and Zhu, Ketron and Mai, Lee et al., Chan, Muralidhar et al. and Meng et al. all study it.
2. That nobody follows a decision to both sides. Lee et al. interviewed 21 drivers and 12 passengers (pp. 1603–1605) and Meng et al. 23 users and 17 couriers (p. 5).
3. That deflected responsibility lands on people as a rule (§3).
4. That participants' readings discharge the platform. Schoeneborn et al. leave the direction open (p. 14), and row K7 already says so.
5. That nobody has studied individuals' attribution of collective actorhood to platforms. Buhmann et al. (2021) have.
6. That the paper supplies an attribution practice. Row K6 already declines it.

### 3.2 How the concern block must be stated so that it holds

1. **Say that the platform leaves responsibility to be placed, not where it lands** (row C1).
2. **Keep the sanction apart from the attribution.** The sanction's direction is set by design and is well evidenced. The attribution's direction varies.
3. **State the direction by condition, with both directions cited.** The counterpart is held to account where the platform's part cannot be seen or rated apart. The platform is held to account where it intermediates heavily or is shown assigning the work. S14c's phrase "made visible" fits Zheng and Zhu and not Fu et al., whose conditions are intermediation, severity and encounter failures (S14d, correction 8).
4. **Say nothing about what these attributions do to the platform's actorhood.** Row K7 already leaves it open, and no source read says.
5. **Keep cause and responsibility apart.** The draft slides from one to the other: the seller settles "whether the drop came from the platform" (cause, row C4), the everyday case is "whom to hold to account" (responsibility, row C8). Fu et al. name their finding the "locus-responsibility shift" because the two come apart. `[NEEDS: your decision whether interpreting, the first of the three parts, is about what caused an outcome or about who answers for it. The closest rival's finding is that knowing the first does not settle the second.]`

### 3.3 Stronger or weaker

**Stronger, on five counts.**

1. The premise now has an observed case and an *Annals* review behind it (Curchod AAM p. 21; Rahman et al. preprint p. 6).
2. The phenomenon is measured: 24,183 complaints and four experiments in Fu et al., four experiments with 839 participants in Zheng and Zhu. A reader cannot call the question hypothetical.
3. The variation is what makes room for the question's first half. If responsibility always fell one way, a person would have nothing to be able to do. It falls differently by what each person can see, and the two people on either side of one outcome can place it differently: Meng et al.'s customers read a stalled dot as negligence (p. 8) while the couriers call it "a system problem" (p. 11).
4. What each can see is set in the platform's interface. Passengers cannot rate the system apart from the driver "by design" (Rosenblat & Stark, p. 3772); fees are "selectively hidden from the customers" (Muralidhar et al., AAM p. 21); on the tracking interface restaurant backlog and signal loss "are only weakly represented" (Meng et al., p. 15). A platform that declines responsibility still shapes where others place it. That last sentence is my inference, and it claims no intent: Meng et al. write that their data "cannot determine" whether the interface results from "deliberate design choices" (p. 15). Whether the introduction says it is your decision.
5. Schoeneborn et al.'s fourth question, whether attribution practices "vary depending on the particular context" (p. 14), now has evidence that they do.

**Weaker, on four counts.**

1. "The platform escapes and people pay" is gone as a general claim. Fu et al. title their paper "Why platforms become scapegoats".
2. Service research has answered part of "where does the responsibility come to rest" for customers. The paper's own ground is the first half of the question and the join.
3. The evidence that a counterpart is blamed, in the blamer's own words, is one preprint. The peer-reviewed cases are the blamed party's reports, and Lee et al.'s passengers describe themselves as lenient raters (p. 1608).
4. The direction argument leans on two abstracts.

The net effect is a stronger question and a narrower claim.
