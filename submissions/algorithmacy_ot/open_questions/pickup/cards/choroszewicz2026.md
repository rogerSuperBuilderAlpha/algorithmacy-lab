## What the work says

From 2.5 years of ethnography in two Finnish public organizations testing three LLM-based tools, Choroszewicz finds five mechanisms that make users "moral crumple zones", among them taken-for-granted human oversight, trial-and-error learning offloaded to users and "persuasive fallibility", and shows the resulting verification, interpretation and vigilance as invisible, emotionally costly work the tools depend on. She adds "interpretative" and "operational" opacity to the familiar epistemic opacity (p. 9) and calls for accountability pathways beyond the user.

## Caution

The interpretation she describes is of AI output against rules and sources, not of a colleague through the system; the one colleague in the data is the person a worker turns to when the bot's confidence clashes with her own view, which is a human check on the tool, not the triad. "Interpretative opacity" is the nearest term to algorithmacy's first part and should be cited as a neighbour, not as the same idea. Her account of overreliance is a worry voiced by users, not observed blind following.
