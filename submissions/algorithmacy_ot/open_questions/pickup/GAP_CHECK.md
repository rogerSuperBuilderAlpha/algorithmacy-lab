## Candidate 1. A communicative capacity classified by the form of the relation (oracy → literacy → an algorithmic form)

**The answer checked.** A capacity that extends the sequence oracy → literacy to a third member, where the members are told apart by the form of the relation (direct dyad; dyad moderated by a text; triad with an evaluating, binding intermediary), not by medium or technology.

### Closest prior works, ranked

1. **Emigh, R. J. (2024). Whither digitality? The relationship between orality, literacy, and digitality, past and present: From spoken traditions to digital media. *Annual Review of Sociology*, 50(1), 715–736. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-soc-033022-035644** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "Orality, literacy, and digitality are forms of knowledge and communication based on speech, reading and writing, and electronic technologies using binary formats, respectively." (abstract)
   - Does: extends orality and literacy to a third term in a top sociology venue, and reviews how the three relate historically.
   - Does not: classify the three by the form of the relation. Emigh's criterion is the technology (speech; reading and writing; binary electronic formats). Digitality is a form of knowledge and communication at the level of societies and histories, not an individual's capacity, and nothing in the abstract places an evaluating third between two people.
   - Bearing: the nearest sequence in the literature and the most likely reviewer comparison. The paper can cite it as the medium-based extension against which its relation-based criterion is set.

2. **Gloy, K. (2025). *From orality via literacy to artificial intelligence*. Würzburg: Könighausen & Neumann (publisher as spelled in the review record).** Known to me only through a review: **Ludewig, A. (2026). Vom Mythos der Schrift als Trägerin der Kultur. *Deutsche Zeitschrift für Philosophie*, 74(1), 117–122. https://doi.org/10.1515/dzph-2026-0009** (review abstract only; I retrieved no record for the book itself)
   - Quotation (review abstract): "the three stages of civilization—orality, literacy, and artificial intelligence—are outlined and related to one another before ultimately suggesting a transhuman development"
   - Does: names the same three-step shape, ending in AI, in a 2025 book.
   - Does not: on the review's account, the stages are stages of civilization and culture, not capacities of a person in an interaction, and the reviewer reports no criterion based on the form of the relation. I could not read the book. **The author should check the book before submission**, since its title is the closest match to the sequence in the brief's abstract sentence.

3. **Heyd, T. (2021). Tertiary orality? New approaches to spoken CMC. *Anglistik*, 32(2), 131–147. https://doi.org/10.33675/angl/2021/2/10** (full text read)
   - Quotation: "these dynamics of convergence are so substantial as to warrant a new distinct stage in the evolution of literate societies – we might speak of the stage of tertiary orality." (p. 136)
   - Does: proposes a stage beyond Ong's secondary orality, and lets algorithms into it: on YouTube, "oral discourse created by humans, but optimized and structured toward being read and recommended by a machine" (p. 142).
   - Does not: the stage is defined by convergence of modes (spoken/written) and of human and non-human voices (p. 139), that is, by medium and agency, not by the form of the relation between two people. It is a linguistic stage, not a capacity, and the algorithm ranks content; it does not bind a counterpart.
   - Neighbours in the same line, all medium-based and none algorithmic-triadic: Lee, D.-H. (2010). Tertiary orality: The presence of the word in the age of "new new media". *Journal of Communication Research*, 47(1), 43–76. https://doi.org/10.22174/jcr.2010.47.1.43 (abstract only; the English abstract in the Consensus record lists "the I-you relationship" among the features examined, but the object is Web 2.0 media, not an evaluating intermediary).

4. **Odukale, B. (2024). The rise of AI literacy: Redefining human communication competence in the algorithmic age. *International Journal of Multidisciplinary Evolutionary Research*, 5(1), 76–90. https://doi.org/10.54660/ijmer.2024.5.1.76-90** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "This paper introduces AI literacy as an expanded form of digital literacy that encompasses awareness of automation, data bias, and ethical reasoning in communicative contexts." (abstract)
   - Does: frames an AI-era capacity as a redefinition of communication competence.
   - Does not: the capacity is an expansion of digital literacy (person–system knowledge and ethics). No sequence by relational form, no counterpart, no triad.

5. **Jones, R. H. (2019/2021). The text is reading you: Teaching language in the age of the algorithm. *Linguistics and Education*, 62, 100750. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.linged.2019.100750** (abstract only, via the Consensus record)
   - Quotation: "the algorithms that lie behind texts that monitor the actions of readers and writers and alter the form and content of the texts they are exposed to." (abstract)
   - Does: moves literacy from the text to the algorithm that reads the reader, and lists folk metaphors including "Algorithm as authority" and "Algorithm as audience".
   - Does not: the relation stays reader–algorithm; no second human party, no sequence of forms.

The wider algorithm-literacy and algorithmic-skill literatures returned by every query (for example Hong, Cheng & Liu 2026, below under C3) treat a person's knowledge of, and dealings with, one system. None I read sets that capacity in a sequence with oracy and literacy by the form of the relation.

### Verdict, candidate 1

I found no work that classifies communicative capacities by the form of the relation and extends oracy and literacy to an algorithmic capacity for triads with an evaluating, binding intermediary in OpenAlex (search API, 22 November 2026) or Consensus (22 November 2026); Scholar Gateway was unavailable (access denied, 22 November 2026).

Two flags for the author. Emigh (2024) and Gloy (2025) already extend orality and literacy to a third, digital or AI term; the paper's novelty rests on the *criterion* (form of the relation) and the *level* (an individual's capacity), not on the three-step shape. Gloy's book is unread; it needs a hand check.

## Candidate 2. An individual capacity that keeps the AI-mediated colleague in view, as the remedy for blind following

**The answer checked.** Blind following of AI guidance is a collapse of the triad into a person-and-machine dyad; the remedy is an individual's capacity to keep the colleague in view (interpret whether a prompt reflects the system or the colleague, specify intent so it reaches the colleague, track guidance that changes).

### Closest prior works, ranked

1. **Yuh, C., Cho, C.-H., & Lee, Y.-M. (2026). The AI colleague: Reimagining interprofessional collaboration and education in medicine. *JMIR Medical Education*, 12, e94034. https://doi.org/10.2196/94034** (abstract and three full-text excerpts returned by Consensus; JMIR has no page numbers and the excerpts carry no section heading, so locators are "abstract" or "body text")
   - Quotations: "learners must be trained to coordinate human-AI-human collaboration in realistic clinical settings." (abstract). "The pedagogical aim is calibrated, critical engagement: teams that verify, question, and, when warranted, override AI contributions rather than defer to them." (abstract). "Both failure modes are social-cognitive challenges that demand collaborative—not merely individual—educational responses." (body text)
   - Does: names automation bias in teams, frames the setting as human–AI–human, and proposes a trainable competence as the remedy. This is the closest prior answer I found.
   - Does not: (i) the competence is placed at the team, explicitly "not merely individual"; (ii) its content is disciplined scrutiny of the AI's output ("the same disciplined scrutiny that well-functioning teams apply to any consequential claim", body text), not keeping the human counterpart in view through the system; (iii) the AI systems are "consequential participants in team cognition" that supply "recommendations, uncertainty, and constraints" (abstract), which is the partner picture the brief rejects; it does not evaluate or bind the clinicians; (iv) none of the three parts appears.
   - Bearing: the paper must cite and distinguish it. The distinction is sharp (level, object of attention, and the intermediary's role) but a reviewer in medicine or teams research will raise it.

2. **González, C., Donahue, K., Goldstein, D. G., Heidari, H., Jalali, M. S., Schelble, B. G., et al. (2026). Toward a science of human–AI teaming for decision making: A complementarity framework. *PNAS Nexus*, 5(3), pgag030. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag030** (abstract and Consensus full-text excerpt; body text)
   - Quotation: "Humans need practice interpreting AI outputs, knowing when to override, and coordinating under time pressure" (body text)
   - Does: names individual skills against over- and under-trust, including interpreting AI outputs.
   - Does not: the object is the AI's output and the human–AI pair; the human colleague as counterpart reached through the system is absent.

3. **Riedl, C., Savage, S., & Zvelebilova, J. (2026). Cognitive spillover in human–AI teams. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, 33(3), 1–33. https://doi.org/10.1145/3805039** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "AI exposure produces causal spillover into human–human interaction—affecting shared language, collective attention, shared mental models, and social cohesion." (abstract)
   - Does: shows experimentally that AI reshapes the relation between colleagues, which supports the premise of Hinds and von Krogh's question.
   - Does not: propose any capacity; the remedy offered is a design shift.

4. **Lyons, J. B., & Stokes, C. K. (2011/2012). Human–human reliance in the context of automation. *Human Factors*, 54(1), 112–121. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720811427034** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "when individuals are provided information from both a human aid and automation,their reliance on the human aid decreased during high-risk decisions." (abstract; the missing space is in the source)
   - Does: documents the colleague dropping from view when automation is present: the failure the brief names.
   - Does not: offer a capacity or a remedy; the automation is a parallel source, not an intermediary between the two people.

5. **Schmutz, J. B., Outland, N. B., Kerstan, S., Georganta, E., & Ulfert, A.-S. (2024). AI-teaming: Redefining collaboration in the digital era. *Current Opinion in Psychology*, 58, 101837. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2024.101837** (abstract only)

6. **Spektor, F., Fox, S., Min, S., Sarfo, G., Stringam, B. B., & Riordan, C. A. (2025). Working together: Algorithmic management and peer relationships in the hospitality industry. *Proceedings of the 2025 ACM Designing Interactive Systems Conference*, 3221–3234. https://doi.org/10.1145/3715336.3735704** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "the tool's implementation still challenges traditional communication and coordination." (abstract)
   - Does: an empirical case of an algorithmic platform mediating work between peers. Does not: name a capacity; the remedy offered is design.

### Verdict, candidate 2

I found no work that proposes an individual's capacity to keep an AI- or platform-mediated colleague in view as the remedy for blind following or automation over-reliance in teams in OpenAlex (search API, 22 November 2026) or Consensus (22 November 2026); Scholar Gateway was unavailable (access denied, 22 November 2026).

Flag for the author: Yuh, Cho & Lee (2026) come closest, with a team-level competence for "human-AI-human collaboration" aimed at automation bias. Their competence is collective and aims at scrutinizing the AI's output, and their AI is a participant, not an evaluating intermediary. The paper should cite them and draw that line.

## Candidate 3. A third position between "technology as partner" and "technology as medium"

**The answer checked.** The system is neither the person's counterpart nor a channel between two people, but an intermediary that evaluates and binds both; the competence that position requires is algorithmacy.

### Closest prior works, ranked

1. **Pfadenhauer, M., & Kirschner, H. (2017). From dyad to triad: Mediatization and emerging risks for professional autonomy. *Professions and Professionalism*, 7(1), e1646. https://doi.org/10.7577/pp.1646** (full text read)
   - Quotation: "the ongoing commercially driven entanglement between business models (e.g., app development and user data-based platforms) and their clients bypasses the traditional dyadic healthcare service encounter in a twofold manner." (p. 7)
   - Does: names the move from a practitioner–client dyad to a triad with a platform as the third, calls rating platforms "commercially driven platforms of re-evaluation" (p. 2), and asks what practitioners must do: "practitioners have to develop specific risk-management strategies according to these challenges" (pp. 7–8).
   - Does not: the platform evaluates the practitioner only (clients rate doctors); it does not bind both parties. The third is a business model, not set against the partner and medium pictures. The response is professional risk management at the level of the profession, without the three parts.
   - Bearing: the closest structural precedent. It shows the dyad-to-triad move is not new, which the brief already concedes ("It does not claim the triadic structure is new").

2. **Curchod, C., Patriotta, G., Cohen, L., & Neysen, N. (2019/2020). Working for an algorithm: Power asymmetries and agency in online work settings. *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 65(3), 644–676. https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839219867024** (abstract only; repository PDF not reachable)
   - Quotation: "an implicit coalition between buyers and the platform owner, who join together in the evaluation procedures. Second, by mediating and objectifying relations, algorithms reproduce power asymmetries among the different categories of actors" (abstract)
   - Does: places the algorithm between seller and buyer, evaluating the seller, and finds sellers "exploit their practical knowledge of the algorithm to increase their agency" (abstract).
   - Does not: treat the algorithm as binding both parties (it sides with buyers); frame the position against partner and medium; name a competence.

3. **Snyder, C. (2025). *On worker behavior in the AI-enabled service triad* [Doctoral dissertation, University of Michigan]. Deep Blue. https://doi.org/10.7302/25733** (abstract only, from the OpenAlex record; the PDF and landing page refused automated access)
   - Quotation: "the interdependent relationships between human workers, human customers, and algorithms inside AI augmented service systems. Although each pairwise interaction in the triad is well-understood, the three-way interaction has remained relatively unstudied." (abstract)
   - Does: names a worker–customer–algorithm triad and treats the three-way interaction as the gap.
   - Does not: the algorithm is decision support for the worker ("workers' decisions to either default to or deliberate on algorithmic advice", abstract); it neither evaluates nor binds the customer. The outcomes are reliance, productivity and fairness, not a competence.

4. **Afgiansyah, A., Arlusi, R. D., Sakaril, G., & Fasta, F. (2026). AI-mediated communication beyond human-AI dyads: A systematic review of chatbot and agent interactions. *Journal of Communication, Language and Culture*, 6(1), 40ff. https://doi.org/10.33093/jclc.2026.6.1.3** (full text read)
   - Quotation: "communication infrastructures now embed multiple non-human actors whose coordinated operations cannot be captured by dyadic models alone." (p. 49)
   - Does: moves from the Human–Computer–Human (neutral channel) model to a Human–AI–Human model, i.e., away from the medium picture.
   - Does not: arrive at a third position; it lands on the partner picture, calling for "platforms that recognise AI as a co-communicator rather than a simple tool" (p. 50). No evaluating or binding, no competence.

5. **Edwards, A. P., Edwards, C. C., & Abendschein, B. (2026). Social robots and the dialectics of presence and absence. *Social Media + Society*, 12(3). https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261479988** (abstract only)
   - Quotation: "we frame social robots within a plural communication ontology—operating both as communicative partners and as structuring media that regulate relational expectations." (abstract)
   - Does: refuses the choice between partner and medium. Does not: take a third position; it holds both at once, for a human–robot encounter, with no second human party and no competence.

6. Statements of the two pictures the brief opposes (useful as sources for the dilemma, not prior answers):
   - Partner: **Esposito, E. (2026). Answer engines and other communication partners. *Communication Theory*, 36(2), 86–94. https://doi.org/10.1093/ct/qtaf036** (abstract only): LLMs "function themselves as communication partners capable of responding autonomously to user queries in a personalized manner." **Peter, J., & Kühne, R. (2018). The new frontier in communication research: Why we should study social robots. *Media and Communication*, 6(3), 73–76. https://doi.org/10.17645/mac.v6i3.1596** (Consensus full-text excerpt): "social robots challenge our notions of medium and media" and "our understanding of the communication partner."
   - Medium / agent of one party: **Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. E. C. (2020). AI-mediated communication: Definition, research agenda, and ethical considerations. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, 25(1), 89–100. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmz022** (abstract only): AI-MC is "interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages".

7. Platform-triangle accounts without the competence (abstract only unless noted): **Rahman, H. A. (2021). The invisible cage. *ASQ*, 66(4), 945–988. https://doi.org/10.1177/00018392211010118**, "an opaque third-party evaluation algorithm" that evaluates freelancers, with reactivity, not competence, as the response; **Cruz, S. A., & Gameiro, A. (2023). Digital work platform. *Frontiers in Sociology*, 7, 1075808. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2022.1075808**, which calls for analysis of "these triangular relations" of platforms, workers and clients; **Schüßler, E., Attwood-Charles, W., Kirchner, S., & Schor, J. B. (2021). *Socio-Economic Review*, 19(4), 1217–1243. https://doi.org/10.1093/ser/mwab038**, platforms as "three canonical social relationships" (mutuality, autonomy, domination), which are relations, not positions of the technology; **Hong, X., Cheng, X., & Liu, D. (2026). Working the algorithm. *Big Data & Society*, 13(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517261449362**, gig workers' "algorithmic skills" in three dimensions (awareness; learning and comprehension; utilization and mastery), all directed at the algorithm with the customer absent.

### Verdict, candidate 3

I found no work that sets technology as an evaluating, binding intermediary between two people, as a third position against both the partner and the medium pictures, and names the competence that position requires, in OpenAlex (search API, 22 November 2026) or Consensus (22 November 2026); Scholar Gateway was unavailable (access denied, 22 November 2026).

Flag for the author: the triad itself has precedents (Pfadenhauer & Kirschner 2017; Curchod et al. 2019/2020; Snyder 2025), and so does the refusal of the partner/medium choice (Edwards et al. 2026, who hold both). Each of these lacks at least two of: evaluation and binding of *both* parties, the explicit third position, and a named individual competence. The paper's claim must rest on that combination.
