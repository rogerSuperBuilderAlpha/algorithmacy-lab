# Derek Robinson, T., & Atanasova, A. (2025). The Organizational Ontology of Person-Institutions.

## What it argues
A commentary on Nigam et al. (2025). The authors accept that some people may become institutions but argue that, before that, figures like Taylor Swift are person-brands, so person-institutions are an outcome of branding processes. Drawing on marketing theory (Fournier & Eckhardt's two-bodied brand: body natural, body politic, body corporate), they argue that a person-brand's ontology is composite and contested, and that mortality, hubris, unpredictability and social embeddedness impede the taken-for-grantedness institutionalization needs. Swift's feuds, re-recordings, collaborator tensions, political silence and image shifts show a person with "institutional potential" not fully realized. They reverse Nigam et al.'s Oprah comparison, propose four stages (person, person-brand, proto-person-institution, full person-institution) and close with a list of research questions. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
1. (reader's inference) The Morrissey case suggests fans can institutionalize a persona apart from the person: "In the case of Morrissey the institutionalizing potential endures through fans institutionalizing a fictive version of the artist that simply does not exist." (p. 7, "Non-transcendence due to mortality"). How a fictive version is institutionalized, and by whom, is left open.
2. (reader's inference) The authors locate person-brands' power in "cultural arbitrations" (p. 8, "Conclusion") without specifying who the parties to that arbitration are or how hosting dissonance works in practice.
3. (reader's inference) Practitioner networks are said to include "a menagerie of back-office stakeholders with other values than hers" (p. 6, "Contention in values and meaning-making due to unpredictability and social embedding"); how these back-office actors take part in or block institutionalization is not developed.
