# Hsu, G., & Bechky, B. A. (2024). Exploring the digital undertow: How generative AI impacts social categorizations in creative work.

## What it argues
Hsu and Bechky answer Phillips, Kalvapalle and Kennedy by placing generative AI in a long tradition of technologies (clocks, timetables, review platforms) that restructure social categories. Using Orlikowski and Scott's "digital undertow", they argue that the main effects on categories will be indirect and gradual. Three streams of category research (how categories develop, how categories shape evaluation, how categories drive conformity) each already show technology at work. Early experimental studies find that generative AI raises speed and average quality, helps novices more than experts, and homogenizes outputs. A thought experiment on screenwriters, studio executives and critics in television and film traces how those proximate effects could cascade: fewer writers' rooms and apprenticeships, eroded expertise, narrower and more repetitive content, a recommender loop that rewards similarity, weaker critics, and new category segmentation into AI-made versus "authentic" human-made work. They ask category scholars to treat technology as an actor in its own right. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
1. The authors expect generative AI to be "dampening the impact of intermediaries" (pdf p. 8, "How these issues connect to social categorization") while recommender systems take over curation. The paper leaves open whether the AI system then becomes the new intermediary between producers and audiences, a question the paper raises only for critics. (reader's inference)
2. The scenario predicts "category segmentation" into AI-made and human-made work (pdf p. 19). The paper does not say who would certify that a production is "all-human", which suggests an opening for new evaluators or labels. (reader's inference)
3. The thought experiment derives field-level change from individual-level experiments (novices gain more than experts; outputs converge, pdf p. 13). How the aggregation from individual output to occupational and category change works is asserted, not modeled. (reader's inference)
