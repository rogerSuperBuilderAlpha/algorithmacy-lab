# Hawbaker, A., & Hallett, T. (2025). Call it What You Want, Just Not an Institution.

## What it argues
A reply to Nigam, Amis and Logue (2025). The authors accept that Taylor Swift is a focal point of roles and interactions but deny that any individual can be an institution. Drawing on inhabited institutionalism and microsociology (Goffman), they argue that institutions, like interactions, are supra-individual, and that calling a person an institution produces tautology and costs definitional clarity. What became institutionalized is an "institutional Swiftology", an institutional mythology co-created by Swift, her crew and her fans, which could outlast her (the Harry Potter fandom's break with J.K. Rowling is the comparison). Using Nigam et al.'s own examples (Table 1) as evidence of interaction, they offer three alternative ways to theorize influential persons: as Selznickian institutional leaders, Durkheimian/Collins totems, and Griswoldian cultural creators. (reader's paraphrase)

## The conversation it joins
- Positions against / extends: directly against the lead piece: "However, Nigam et al. go further to argue that “people, as individuals rather than as representatives of an office, can become institutions” (Nigam et al., 2025, abstract). We disagree." (p. 2, opening section, untitled). Against hero narratives: "Centering an institution within a person fits the tendency within some older types of institutional and management theory to valorize individuals and create hero narratives that sanitize the collective work of people in acting together to (re)create institutions" (p. 9, "Discussion")
- Named interlocutors: Nigam, Amis & Logue (2025); Selznick (1948, 1949, 1957); Goffman (1956, 1967, 1983); Meyer & Rowan (1977); Barley (2008, 2019); Becker (1986); Collins (2005); Griswold (2013); Jepperson & Meyer (2011).

- OT articles it cites: Nigam, A., Amis, J., & Logue, D. (2025), "People as institutions or why Taylor Swift rules the world," *Organization Theory*, 6. (Its Ocasio 2023 entry is the Cambridge University Press book, not the OT article.)

## Open questions
### OQ-hawbaker2025-01
- Quote: "We welcome further theorizing about the impact of key institutional interactants, and the contexts and social dimensions that allow some people to leverage interactions to co-create mythologies." (p. 9, "Discussion")
- Form: invitation
- Scope: influential individuals as key interactants in institutional mythologies; enabling contexts
- Addressee: institutional theorists, especially inhabited-institutionalist and interactionist scholars
- Level of analysis: interaction
- Paraphrase: The authors invite theory on how key individuals, under which contexts and social conditions, use interaction to co-create institutional mythologies. (reader's paraphrase)

### OQ-hawbaker2025-02
- Quote: "Race, gender, class, and geography are heavily implicated in the creation of institutional mythologies." (p. 9, "Discussion")
- Form: boundary_condition

- Addressee: institutional theorists; empirical researchers

## Implicit openings
1. (reader's inference) The Harry Potter case shows inhabitants taking a mythology away from its creator: "Disgusted by Rowling’s politics, the fans took control, prioritizing the art over the artist." (p. 7, "Leaders, Totems, and Cultural Creators"). The conditions under which inhabitants "take the crown" are not specified.
2. (reader's inference) The reply treats the Eras Tour as an organization that became an institution ("Those concerts are over, but the institution endures.", p. 3, "Definitional Clarity") alongside Swiftology as a mythology. How a temporary organization's institutionalization relates to the broader mythology is left open.
3. (reader's inference) The closing describes Swiftology as "coupled differently depending on setting and interactants" (p. 9, "Discussion"). Variable coupling across settings is asserted rather than examined.
