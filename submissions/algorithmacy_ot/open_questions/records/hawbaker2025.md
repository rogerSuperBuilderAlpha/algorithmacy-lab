# Hawbaker, A., & Hallett, T. (2025). Call it What You Want, Just Not an Institution.

## What it argues
A reply to Nigam, Amis and Logue (2025). The authors accept that Taylor Swift is a focal point of roles and interactions but deny that any individual can be an institution. Drawing on inhabited institutionalism and microsociology (Goffman), they argue that institutions, like interactions, are supra-individual, and that calling a person an institution produces tautology and costs definitional clarity. What became institutionalized is an "institutional Swiftology", an institutional mythology co-created by Swift, her crew and her fans, which could outlast her (the Harry Potter fandom's break with J.K. Rowling is the comparison). Using Nigam et al.'s own examples (Table 1) as evidence of interaction, they offer three alternative ways to theorize influential persons: as Selznickian institutional leaders, Durkheimian/Collins totems, and Griswoldian cultural creators. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
1. (reader's inference) The Harry Potter case shows inhabitants taking a mythology away from its creator: "Disgusted by Rowling’s politics, the fans took control, prioritizing the art over the artist." (p. 7, "Leaders, Totems, and Cultural Creators"). The conditions under which inhabitants take the crown ("they took the crown", p. 7, quoting the song) are not specified.
2. (reader's inference) The reply treats the Eras Tour as an organization that became an institution ("Those concerts are over, but the institution endures.", p. 3, "Definitional Clarity") alongside Swiftology as a mythology. How a temporary organization's institutionalization relates to the broader mythology is left open.
3. (reader's inference) The closing describes Swiftology as "coupled differently depending on setting and interactants" (p. 9, "Discussion"). Variable coupling across settings is asserted rather than examined.
