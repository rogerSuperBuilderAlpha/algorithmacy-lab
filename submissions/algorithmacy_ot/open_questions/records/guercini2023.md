# Guercini, S., & Lechner, C. (2023). Competence Attrition: A linguistic theory of the effects of external competence acquisition for organizations.

## What it argues
The authors ask what happens to an organization's existing competences when it acquires new ones. Absorptive capacity theory treats learning as cumulative and one-directional: prior knowledge helps new knowledge in. Reasoning by analogy from linguistic research on first-language attrition in bilinguals, the authors propose competence attrition (CA): acquiring and using a new competence (C2) can partly erode an older one (C1), through bidirectional "cross-competence influence" and an activation threshold that unused competences fall below. Four propositions follow: more interaction between competences makes attrition more likely; greater similarity raises initial acquisition but lowers effective acquisition; effective acquisition benefits from distance because interaction effects fall while deliberate learning effort rises; and recently acquired competences are most exposed to attrition. Three stylized vignettes from textiles, fashion and footwear illustrate the claims. CA is presented as a concept separate from, and broader than, absorptive capacity. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
- The authors leave two framings side by side: CA "can be incorporated into absorptive capacity theory as a useful moderator" or is "a concept that is separate from absorptive capacity" (p. 16, "Theoretical implications"); they prefer the second, but which framing better fits evidence is not settled. (reader's inference)
- The cross-level transfer of the analogy rests on the claim that "language is thus similarly both an individual and organizational competence" (p. 8, "Competence attrition theory based on analogical reasoning"); whether attrition mechanisms observed in individual bilinguals hold for organizational routines is assumed, not shown. (reader's inference)
- All three vignettes come from one sector ("three stylized vignettes from the same industry", p. 10, "From language attrition to competence attrition"); whether the propositions hold outside textiles, fashion and footwear is open. (reader's inference)
