# Sasaki, I., & Baba, S. (2024). Shades of Cultural Marginalization: Cultural Survival and Autonomy Processes.

## What it argues
The authors say organization and management theory lacks a systematic account of cultural marginalization, the exclusion of groups because of their values, beliefs and practices. They build a typology of four dynamics (cultural extermination, isolation, exiting and distancing), placed on a continuum by the level of structural imposition and the agency the group can exercise. They then extend the culture-as-toolkit perspective (Swidler, 1986) with Bourdieusian and related theories of power to explain how marginalized groups use embedded, discursive and material cultural elements, first for cultural survival (defensive, restorative, situational) and, as constraints ease, for cultural autonomy (restorative plus creative). Groups can also slide back from autonomy to survival when the institutional context turns hostile. The paper is conceptual and uses published cases as illustrations. (reader's paraphrase)

## The conversation it joins
- Positions against / extends: "the current literature in organization and management theory (OMT) lacks substantial theoretical development, particularly in understanding the diverse forms of cultural marginalization" (pdf p. 4, "Introduction"); "the current application of the CaT perspective in organizational theory does not sufficiently account for the different social positions of marginalized groups" (pdf p. 6, "Introduction").
- Named interlocutors: Swidler (1986) on culture as toolkit; Bourdieu (1977, 1990); Ortner (2006); Emirbayer (1997) and Emirbayer & Johnson (2008); Sewell (1992); Lounsbury & Glynn (2019); Creed, DeJordy & Lok (2010); Martí & Fernández (2013); Baba, Sasaki & Vaara (2021); Alkhaled & Sasaki (2022).
- OT articles it cites: Cornelissen, Höllerer & Seidl (2021), "What theory is and can be: Forms of theorizing in organizational scholarship"; Eisenman & Frenkel (2021), "Remembering materiality: A material-relational approach to organizational memory".
- Move: typology (four dynamics) plus process model (survival to autonomy and back); extension of an existing perspective (culture as toolkit) with power theory.

|---|---|---|

| Cultural isolation | "refers to situations where specific groups are rejected from mainstream society and culture" (pdf p. 16, "The Continuum of Cultural Marginalization") | Proposed here |
| Cultural exiting | "situations where groups who perceive themselves as marginalized within a society deliberately detach themselves from the dominant culture" (pdf p. 18, "The Continuum of Cultural Marginalization") | Proposed here |
| Cultural distancing | "pertains to situations where cultural marginalization and loss arise from the active response of marginalized groups who feel alienated from the dominant culture" (pdf p. 20, "The Continuum of Cultural Marginalization") | Proposed here |
| Cultural survival | "the process through which individuals, organizations, or communities who lack power, status, or resources strive to safeguard essential elements of their cultural values and practices in the face of external pressures imposed on them by dominant groups and institutions in society" (pdf p. 26, "Cultural Survival and Cultural Autonomy Processes") | Proposed here |
| Cultural autonomy | "the independent shaping or modification of their own cultural values and practices by individuals, organizations, or communities without significant power, status, or resources" (pdf p. 26, "Cultural Survival and Cultural Autonomy Processes") | Proposed here |

## Implicit openings
- The authors say that enacting counter-narratives openly "may have the opposite effect of accelerating the phenomenon from which the marginalized groups seek to extricate themselves" (pdf p. 35, "Cultural autonomy toolkit: Discursive cultural elements"); they offer it as "somewhat counter-intuitive" and do not study when it happens (reader's inference).
- The four dynamics are "ideal-type representations to which actual instances of cultural marginalization can be compared" (pdf p. 13, "The Continuum of Cultural Marginalization"); how actual cases map onto the types, and who judges, is left to later work (reader's inference).
- The paper says formal organizations "contribute to it either directly or indirectly, through their silence and the voices they amplify" (pdf p. 44, "Discussion"); how organizational communication and media shape it is not worked out (reader's inference).
