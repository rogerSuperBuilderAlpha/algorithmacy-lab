# Fotaki, M. (2023). Why Do We Desire and Fear Care: Toward developing a holistic political approach.

## What it argues
Fotaki asks why care is socially valued yet systematically undervalued in organizations and society. Her answer joins feminist political care theory (Tronto, Federici) with feminist psychoanalysis (Butler, Benjamin, Ettinger). Care rests on embodied vulnerability, our dependence on others. Because vulnerability triggers fear of annihilation, people disavow it, and neoliberal ideology exploits the disavowal by offering the fantasy of an invulnerable, rational individual. She proposes "holistic political care", an intersubjective, intercorporeal and political conception that treats vulnerability as a source of strength and extends care to distant others and non-human life. She draws implications for organizational research, policy, and management education. The paper is conceptual. (reader's paraphrase)

## The conversation it joins
- Positions against / extends: organizational ethics-of-care work "neither theorize why care is systematically undervalued in organizations and society nor focus sufficiently on the political underpinnings of care" (p. 2-3, "Introduction"); the article also says psychoanalytic feminists' work on the unconscious "has not been applied to theorizing care in organizations and organizing" (p. 13, "Discussion").
- Named interlocutors: Tronto; Federici; Fraser; Butler; Benjamin; Ettinger; Gilligan and Noddings; Lawrence & Maitlis (2012); Liedtka (1996); the Care Collective (Chatzidakis et al., 2020); Johansson & Wickström (2022).
- OT articles it cites: none found in the reference list (I scanned the list for "Organization Theory"; the only hits are the running header and the journal's own masthead). Johansson & Wickström (2022) is cited, and I did not check its journal.
- Move: new framework / re-theorization (concept of holistic political care) combining two literatures; critique of depoliticized care ethics in organization studies.

## Implicit openings
- The article states that "there are no explanations linking the evasion of this reality with the actual politics of undermining care" (p. 13, "Discussion"). The empirical study of how that link operates in particular organizations is not undertaken (reader's inference).
- The framework is said to need an unresolved balance between a universal principle of care and justice-based limits on whose needs are met (p. 16, "Discussion": "determining which and whose needs we should meet must be guided by the principles of justice") (reader's inference).
