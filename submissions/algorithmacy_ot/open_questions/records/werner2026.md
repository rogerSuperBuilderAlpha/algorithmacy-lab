# Werner, M. D., Cornelissen, J., & Turkenburg, K. (2026). Moral Selves: Organizational character and the pursuit of purpose.

## What it argues
Starting from firms' divergent responses to the second Trump administration's attack on ESG and DEI commitments (Boeing retreating, Starbucks doubling down), the authors ask why some organizations stay the course. They argue that CSR and business-ethics theory casts organizations as "actors" in institutionally prescribed roles, which yields a thin, procedural morality and functional explanations. Drawing on Charles Taylor and Bernard Williams, and reviving Selznick and Basu and Palazzo, they conceive organizations as moral "subjects" whose character is formed through moral reasoning, aspirations and commitments. They offer a typology of three ideal-typical characters (role performer, institutional idealist, moral steward; Table 1), which differ in moral scope, reasoning, aspirations, commitments and pathologies, and a process model (Figure 1) of triggers and enablers (accountability pressures, pledging, individuation, "accounting for oneself", narration or perspective-taking) through which an organization may move between characters and change its purpose. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
1. The process model is said to be enterable from any character: "analytically the process could be entered from the perspective of the other character types as well" (p. 20, "Organizational Character Pathways"). Only regressions are illustrated (Fairphone, Unilever); the triggers for moving from steward or idealist back toward role performer are not modelled. (reader's inference)
2. Character is built from talk: "organizations consist of ongoing instances of talk and conversations through which members of the organization collectively articulate to themselves and others who they are" (p. 5, "Organizational Character and the Moral Background"). The model leaves open how external voices (NGOs, media, governments) enter that talk, beyond being "triggers". (reader's inference)
3. The authors state that they centered "our theorizing on the processes rather than the contents of their moral deliberation" (p. 21, "Theoretical and research implications"); how one would measure or classify an organization's character empirically is not specified. (reader's inference)
