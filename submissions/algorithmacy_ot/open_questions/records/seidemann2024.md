# Seidemann (2024). Blinded by the Light: A critique on the universality, normativity, and hegemony of paradox theory and research.

## What it argues
The author intervenes in paradox theory through three provocations. Universality: the standard definition (contradiction, interrelation, persistence) labels too many tensions as paradoxes and misses subtler ones such as outcome irony, because the markers are not tied to any ontology. Normativity: the theory assumes paradoxes hold potential for good, which burdens individual leaders with a "paradox mindset" and favours proactive over "defensive" responses, including resistance. Hegemony: the both/and response is too simple for grand challenges and puts harmony ahead of conflict. For each, the author sketches a way forward: read the markers through ontological positions, build normative approaches into paradox management, and study paradox responses at finer granularity over time and space. The stated spirit is emancipatory theorizing from within the field. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
- After noting that Fredberg finds seven "paradoxes", the author writes "the legitimate question of the value of such revelations arises" (p. 6, "Universality"). No test is offered for when a multiplicity of found paradoxes (for example Fredberg's seven) adds knowledge. (reader's inference)
- The paper deliberately sets aside "dialectics" and "systems theory" accounts of paradox (p. 2, "Introduction"). How the three provocations apply there is not tested. (reader's inference)
- The author calls for "a distinct alternative to the dominant mode of using the literature" (pp. 16-17, quoting Alvesson & Sandberg) but gives no procedure for choosing among the normative approaches it lists (discourse ethics, intersectional-feminist theory, intra-action). (reader's inference)
