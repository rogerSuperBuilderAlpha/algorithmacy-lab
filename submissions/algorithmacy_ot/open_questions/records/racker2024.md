# Räcker, T., Geiger, D., & Seidl, D. (2024). From coordinating in space to coordinating through space: A spatial perspective on coordinating.

## What it argues
Coordination research has treated physical space as context. Drawing on the spatial turn and Lefebvre's theory of spatial production, the authors argue that coordination practices always produce space, which in turn affects coordination outcomes, and that actors can produce space on purpose to integrate interdependent work. They cross three Lefebvrian modes of spatial production (designing, enacting, shaping the experience of space) with three dimensions of space (distance, boundaries, movement) to form a framework. A configuration of spatial production yields intermediate effects (connection, differentiation, visibility, familiarity, sequencing, adaptability) that create the integrating conditions of accountability, predictability and common understanding. Elements can reinforce or offset each other, illustrated with an open-plan office redesign. Non-space-focused practices such as rules and roles also produce space inadvertently. The paper adds spatial production to the repertoire of coordinating mechanisms and sets out a four-area empirical research programme. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
- (reader's inference) The authors propose that "effective coordinating might depend on reflecting on and being knowledgeable about the effects of different spatial configurations of intended and inadvertent spatial production" (p. 11, "Configurational effects of spatial production"). What this knowledge consists of and how practitioners acquire it is not developed.
- (reader's inference) The open-plan illustration is described as "(partly fictional)" (p. 11, "Configurational effects of spatial production"); the configurational dynamics it shows await empirical cases.
- (reader's inference) The paper notes that "virtual settings involve physical space as well" (p. 12, "Space-focused coordinating in practice") but does not theorize digitally mediated space; how designing, enacting and experiencing apply to technology-mediated collaboration is left open.
