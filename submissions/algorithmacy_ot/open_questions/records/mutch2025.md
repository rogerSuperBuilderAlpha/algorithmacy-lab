# Mutch (2025). Law as Logic.

## What it argues
Mutch argues that law is a distinct societal institutional logic and that organization theory has neglected it. He draws on Ferguson and Latour for the ontological centrality of law, and on Friedland for the idea that a logic is a substance (for law, justice) carried by practices. He then contrasts common and civil law systems, with a table of indicative differences in sources, procedure, contracts, argument and decisions. He cites studies of law firms, trust and contracts, and of German and English factories, to show that the legal tradition shapes organizations and interacts with politics, economy, religion and education. He says law's place challenges the Thornton, Ocasio and Lounsbury list of logics, especially "professionalism", and that organizational scholars should study how practices transfer between the legal and organizational domains. (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
- Mutch finds that professionalism "should therefore be seen as a cross-cutting organizing logic" (p. 12, "Implications") and questions its place in the Thornton et al. list, but he does not say how other listed logics should be re-examined. (reader's inference)
- He notes that Smets et al. (2012) did not study "whether such changes in organizational practice led to subsequent change in the respective legal systems" (p. 10, "Systems and Practices Compared"). The link from organizational practice to legal-system change is left open. (reader's inference)
- He reads the Church of Scotland's accounting and education practices as moving into secular domains (p. 13); whether law-to-organization transfer follows the same path is described as a "potential" only. (reader's inference)
