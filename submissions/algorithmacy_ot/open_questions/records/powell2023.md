# Powell, W. W., & DiMaggio, P. J. (2023). The Iron Cage Redux: Looking Back and Forward.

## What it argues
Forty years after "The Iron Cage Revisited", the two authors recall the Yale setting, colleagues and fieldwork (publishing, arts nonprofits, public television) from which the isomorphism argument came, trace its slow initial reception and later spread across disciplines, and review four lines of institutional work they have followed: world society, micro-foundations, heterogeneous diffusion and institutional change. They say the paper's field-level, relational argument was eclipsed by attention to the three mechanisms, and that the paper evaded questions of what diffuses, through which channels and with what effect. They propose research on thresholds, partitioning, optimal distinctiveness, layering/blending/intercalation and "poisedness" across macro, meso and micro levels. They close by arguing that organization studies has become insular "suburbs" and urge scholars to engage unfamiliar communities. (reader's paraphrase)

## Key constructs
| Term | Definition (verbatim, with locator) | Coined or borrowed |
|---|---|---|
| Field (organizational) | "a field is a community of organizations who interact regularly with one another and develop a shared, mutual understanding of the enterprise they are engaged in" (p. 11, "Garnering Attention") | coined (in the 1983 paper; restated) |
| Mimetic isomorphism | "When organizations face uncertain environments, or when their goals are ambiguous, they often turn to templates or models." (p. 7, "Yale in the Early 1980s") | coined (1983), drawing on Auerbach and Cyert & March |
| Heterogeneous diffusion | "the varied ways in which different types of organizations are more or less receptive to changes in the external environment" (p. 14, "Heterogeneous diffusion") | borrowed / developed in later work |
| Contested diffusion | "a case where the spread of a practice provokes counter-movements and/or criticisms that what is being adopted is ineffectual" (p. 14, "Heterogeneous diffusion") | coined here (as label) |
| Intercalation | "Traditions can meet and even transform one another, but they do not lose their separate character." (p. 17, "Institutional change") | borrowed (Galison, 1997) |
| Poisedness | "the availability or vulnerability of a social and historical context to the reception of and reconfiguration by an innovation" (p. 18, "Institutional change") | borrowed (Padgett & Powell, 2012) |

## Implicit openings
- The authors note that institutional entrepreneurship studies rely on short temporal frames and successful cases ("This oversight usually derives from the short temporal frame covered by studies of institutional entrepreneurship", p. 16, "Institutional change"); this points to longer-horizon studies that include failed projects. (reader's inference)
- Symbolic and contested diffusion are named as alternatives "Neither ... considered in our 1983 paper" (p. 14, "Heterogeneous diffusion"), which leaves open how isomorphism theory incorporates counter-movements against diffusing practices. (reader's inference)
- The world society section says "We did not anticipate this macro turn. We wrote with a US-centered lens" (p. 12, "World society"), leaving open how field concepts built on national sectors handle transnational fields. (reader's inference)
