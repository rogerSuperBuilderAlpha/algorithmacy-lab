# du Gay, P., & Lopdrup Hjorth, T. (2024). Organizing States: The continuing relevance of formal organization within political administration.

## What it argues
The authors argue that formal organization, understood as the official structure of offices into which the activities needed to realize a purpose are divided, is the core concept and object of organization theory. Drawing on Stinchcombe, Weber, Barnard, Brown and Wolter, they show that the state is a formal organization realized through a structure of offices, with ethics specific to each office and a system of objective authority that depends on unbroken, authenticated lines of communication. Against de-formalization in popular management, alternative-organizing scholarship, network governance and public-sector reform, they argue that formality can serve substance, that the question is which formalization suits a given purpose, and that populist "bureaucratic backsliding" threatens liberal-democratic government. They call for organization theory to return to a practical, design-oriented "classical stance". (reader's paraphrase)

## Implicit openings
- The authors note that critics of formality "rarely provide detailed descriptions of the (daily) consequences of the organizing practices they implicitly or explicitly commend" (p. 4, "Formality and Formal Organization"); this leaves open detailed empirical study of the daily consequences of informal or "structureless" organizing. (reader's inference)
- The claim that "Recent forms of 'bureaucratic backsliding', we suggest, have implications not only for representative democratic government but for the very survival of the 'person of the state'" (p. 14, "Implications for political administration research and practice") is stated as a suggestion, leaving its empirical test open. (reader's inference)
- Stinchcombe's criteria—formalities that are "cognitively and practically adequate to the task at hand, are communicable ... and are correctable and improvable" (p. 5, "Formality and Formal Organization")—are offered as assessment standards but not operationalized, leaving open how to assess specific formalities against them. (reader's inference)
