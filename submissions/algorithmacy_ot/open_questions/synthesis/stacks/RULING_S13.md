## 1. Contested points, checked at source

**1.1 What Schoeneborn et al. require of an attribution practice (p. 9).** The definition requires communication and recurrence: "We conceptualize attribution practices here as recurrent instances of communication that ascribe characteristics or activities to a social address." (p. 9, "Charging and Discharging Actorhood Via Specific Attribution Practices"). It does not require uptake. The sentence the critic cites for uptake states when an address stabilizes: it "will fail to gain stability over time unless attributions of collective actorhood are also made by external observers, including the media" (p. 9, "Charging With Actorhood Versus Discharging of Actorhood"). The same paragraph counts attributions that "can also be made by individuals who are considered to belong to that collective", and names "other external stakeholders" among the observers (p. 9).

*Ruling.* The critic is right that an unvoiced reading of an outcome is no attribution practice, because it is not communication. The critic's "They also require uptake beyond insiders" (CRITIC_S13 §2, C1) and MAP_1's reviewer line "circulates and is taken up" (MAP_1 §3, Framing A) set a bar the text does not set. The text asks for further attributions from outside observers, and asks for them as a condition of the address's stability. Co-opted users are not members, so they may count among the external stakeholders. The text does not say.

**1.2 Whether blaming an algorithm discharges the platform (p. 11).** The text permits that reading and does not assert it. The authors ground organizationality in "practices that attribute actorhood either to a collective social address, thereby “charging” it with actorhood, or to individual (human or other-than-human) social addresses, thereby “discharging” the collective of actorhood" (p. 11, "Discussion"). Both discharge practices the authors describe run to humans: scapegoated members and a heroized chief executive (p. 11). The word "algorithm" occurs nowhere in the article (search of the text layer: 0 hits).

*Ruling.* The critic is right against both mappers. "The competent participant charges it back" (MAP_1 §3, Framing A) and "A person who exercises that part charges the platform with actorhood" (MAP_2 §3, Framing 1) do not follow from the text, which leaves the direction open. The critic missed two passages that turn the objection into part of the askers' own agenda. Their fourth question asks whether practices "always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary depending on the particular context" (p. 14); the record's quotation of OQ-schoeneborn2026-07 stops before the last clause. They also report that "a high degree of digitalization can impede anthropomorphization and thus also attributions of collective actorhood" (p. 10). The direction of an attribution to "the system" is therefore an open question the askers pose, and digital forms are a case they already find hard.

**1.3 The platform remark (p. 13).** The wording is as both mappers quote it: studying attribution practices makes it possible to explain "how new organizational forms, such as digital platforms, rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation" (p. 13, "Dynamizing Organizationality"). The critic is right that this is a claim and not a question, and that it rests on two citations (Haase et al., 2025; Vergne & Wry, 2014) the run has not read. One point none of the three files makes: in this clause the platform does the downplaying. The disattribution the askers name is the platform's own act, and the paper's subject stands at its receiving end.

**1.4 Whether ASM asserts that interpreting is attribution.** It does: "the attribution of agency to a collective or to individuals (Schoeneborn et al., 2026) is what a participant does when she interprets a result" (ASM, Discussion, "Placing the Abilities on One Criterion, From Dyads to Triads", ¶4). No argument follows. The next sentence places the askers' concept under the paper's criterion, "I thus propose one criterion, the form of the relation, with these and other concepts placed in relation to it" (same ¶), in the frame of their own "We thus propose one single clear axis of organizationality with other practices positioned in relation to it" (p. 12). The critic is right on both counts. By 1.1 the asserted identity does not hold as stated: an attribution practice is communication, and ASM itself calls unvoiced interpreting "a private diagnosis" (ASM, "The Co-opted Person's Side of Platform Organization", ¶1).

**1.6 Mena et al.'s republican answer (pp. 10–11).** The republican lens "focuses on how managerial authority and hierarchical structures create forms of domination that undermine workers’ freedom and autonomy, even when authority is not actively exercised" (pp. 10–11). Its remedy is control and voice: "the need for democratic control by workers (e.g. decision-making rights, seats on the board) and the institutionalization of worker voice" (p. 11). The DAO is a case where "organizational members usually have equal control (Li & Chen, 2024)" (p. 11), and the follow-on question asks about "domination and authority from a class of workers other than managers (e.g. professions)" (p. 11). The critic is right. **1.7 Whether ASM says the ability keeps an unfair arrangement running.** It does: studying the parts explains "how their growing ability keeps an unfair arrangement running, since people who absorb quietly what would otherwise show lower the pressure to change it" (ASM, "The Co-opted Person's Side of Platform Organization", ¶2). GDOC says the same in its own words: "their adaptive competence inadvertently preserves the platform’s opaque authority" (GDOC, "The Co-opted Person’s Side of Platform Organization Rewrite", last ¶).

**1.8 Hinds and von Krogh, p. 6.** The passage reads as the critic quotes it: "Over time as managers and applicants interact with the system, the evaluation algorithm may become increasingly refined and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). The critic draws three things from it that the text does not carry.

- It is not "the one Hinds passage that meets the brief's scope" (CRITIC_S13 §2, C4). On p. 4 managers use GenAI "to augment their ability to motivate and evaluate employees, such as determining job assignments or pay increases", relations "cascade between managers, GenAI, and employees", and the system "may ultimately obscure managers’ and employees’ visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). That passage has two people who work together, decisions, learning and opacity for both. My S7 hand-check already listed pp. 4, 6 and 9 (record `hinds2024`, Fit, OQ-04, point b).
- P. 6 does not show an intermediary that is "binding" between "two people" in the brief's sense. It shows a system that evaluates applicants and grows opaque to the manager. Nothing says its result binds the manager, and a manager and an applicant do no interdependent work. P. 4 comes closer, and it too shows evaluation of one party.
- It does not "avoid the scope change" on OQ-hinds2024-04. That question is asked of guidance in a team: "might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members blindly follow guidance from the AI?" (p. 7). An answer anchored on p. 4 or p. 6 narrows the question to GenAI that decides. An answer that keeps the question's own case widens "binding" to guidance. `REPORT.md` (Entry 2) already states that the paper pays one of the two.

**1.9 Points on which I confirm the critic without change.** Stark and Vanden Broeck define the new sociality by the partner premise, "algorithmic practices in which the technologies are themselves our communication partners (Esposito, 2022)" (p. 16), and their cases have no counterpart; their triangle is on p. 14. Patriotta et al.'s platforms act by "indirectly influencing which justifications gain visibility" (p. 19), so MAP_2's "A curating platform does evaluate both contenders" (MAP_2 §1, R09) is not in the text, and "competent agency" (p. 17) already names the organization-level skill. David Seidl "was a Senior Editor of Organization Studies and an Associate Editor of Organization Theory" (`schoeneborn2026`, p. 19) and is corresponding author of Räcker et al. (p. 1).
