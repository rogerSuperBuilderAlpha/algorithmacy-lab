## 2. The candidates

### C1. R08: who charges the platform with actorhood

**What the askers argue, and whether they would accept algorithmacy.** Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl locate organizationality in communication, not cognition: "We conceptualize attribution practices here as recurrent instances of communication that ascribe characteristics or activities to a social address." (p. 9). Their examples are public and circulating: metaphors in the media, legal personhood, accusations that are "Ultimately aimed at holding a collective social address accountable, such attributions are typically voiced in public" (p. 10). They also require uptake beyond insiders: a collective address "will fail to gain stability over time unless attributions of collective actorhood are also made by external observers, including the media." (p. 9). Measured against that, algorithmacy's first part (an individual interpreting an outcome to find its cause) is not an attribution practice. It is at most the private judgement that precedes one. The askers would accept algorithmacy only as a *site*: a population of participants whose voiced attributions might charge or discharge a platform. They would say that what is missing is the communication itself and any evidence that it recurs and is taken up.

The platform remark the framing leans on is one clause in a sentence about formal organizations, supported by two citations: "how new organizational forms, such as digital platforms, rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation (Haase et al., 2025; Vergne & Wry, 2014)." (p. 13). The authors do not pose it as a question. OQ-schoeneborn2026-07 asks for "other attribution or disattribution practices" (p. 14), and S6 scored it 0 from both seats.

**An objection neither mapper saw, and it cuts against the framing's mechanism.** Both maps assume that a participant who locates the cause in "the system" charges the platform with actorhood, and that one who blames the counterpart discharges it (MAP_1 §3 A, "The competent participant charges it back"; MAP_2 §3 Framing 1). Schoeneborn et al. allow discharge in two directions: "or to individual (human or other-than-human) social addresses, thereby “discharging” the collective of actorhood." (p. 11). "The algorithm did it" can be read as an attribution to an other-than-human individual address, the algorithm, and so as a discharge of the platform as a collective, just as "Tesla is Elon Musk" discharges Tesla (p. 11). The text does not settle which reading applies. The mappers' mechanism therefore has no determinate sign. Unless the paper argues which way an attribution to the system cuts, it cannot claim that algorithmacy charges anything.

**Change to the definition.** Level: from "an individual's capacity, exercised in an interaction" (brief, "Level of analysis") to a communicative practice that recurs and is taken up. Both mappers name this change. The brief's rule allows it, capped at 3. The question score stays at 1 under the definition as written. My reasons: the definition contains no communicative act, the platform line is not a question, and the direction of the effect is indeterminate (p. 11).

**Strongest objection, and whether the paper can meet it without new data.** The objection runs: "Actorhood is charged by communication that recurs and is taken up by observers (pp. 9–10). You describe a cognition and call it an attribution practice, and you cannot even say which way it charges (p. 11)." The paper can meet the level half of this objection conceptually if it stops claiming that algorithmacy *is* an attribution practice. It would instead claim that algorithmacy is the capacity a co-opted participant needs before any attribution to the platform is possible, because the platform presents itself as a channel. The sign problem is also conceptual: the paper must argue whether an attribution to the system names the platform (charging it) or a technical individual (discharging it). Evidence that platform workers voice such attributions in forums or complaints would have to come from published studies. I have not checked whether the studies ASM cites (Rahman, 2021; Sutherland et al., 2020; Cameron, 2024) report voiced attributions of this kind, so this is unverified.

**The ASM question: continuity or liability?** ASM is a liability for this entry as it stands, for three reasons the files show.

1. *ASM already makes the claim the framing needs, and makes it by assertion.* "the attribution of agency to a collective or to individuals (Schoeneborn et al., 2026) is what a participant does when she interprets a result" (ASM, Discussion, "Placing the Abilities on One Criterion", ¶4). That is the level conflation the objection targets, and the paragraph gives no argument for it. The same paragraph then places organizationality *under* algorithmacy's criterion: "I thus propose one criterion, the form of the relation, with these and other concepts placed in relation to it" (same ¶). Schoeneborn et al. make that move for their own axis: "We thus propose one single clear axis of organizationality with other practices positioned in relation to it" (p. 12). A reader from the exchange will see their integrative move turned back on them, with their construct as the subsumed item.
2. *The template is visible.* ASM was "Written sentence by sentence against Schoeneborn, Dobusch & Seidl (2026)" (ASM header comment), and the skeleton shows through. The title "Toward an Account of Algorithmacy: A Triadic View of Working Through Algorithms" answers "Toward a Theory of Organizationality: A Gradual View on Collective Actorhood" (schoeneborn2026 record, `apa`). The "dyad dilemma" stands where the "openness-specificity dilemma" stood. "And while other criteria could have been chosen, I have argued that this one has three advantages" (ASM, Discussion ¶2) follows "we have argued that it offers several advantages over other approaches" (p. 12). And "The account opens a research agenda by raising four questions. First, it is worth asking:" (ASM, Conclusion ¶2) follows "opens up a new research agenda for organization studies by raising four important questions. First, it is worth asking:" (pp. 13–14). A paper whose skeleton is recognisably Schoeneborn et al.'s, and which then names Schoeneborn et al.'s open question as its point of entry, invites the reading that the anchor was chosen to match the model. The run cannot rebut that reading from its own record: ASM was built on 8 October 2026 (ASM header) and the run's search began on 22 November (BASELINES header). The model came first and the question was found later.
3. *The reviewer pool overlaps.* The schoeneborn2026 record notes that David Seidl's biography (p. 19) states he served as an Associate Editor of *Organization Theory*. He is also corresponding author of Räcker et al. (racker2024, p. 1), the anchor of C4. I do not claim he would handle the paper. The point is that the two framings with the cleanest theoretical lineage both route through one author who would recognise the template.

Two version notes the author should rule on. ASM's header calls its text "Claude's wording of the author's argument; not yet read or ruled on by the author", so the mirroring is not yet the author's choice. PAPER says the algorithmic intermediaries operate with gradual organizationality, "functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood" (PAPER, Introduction ¶3 and §2 ¶1). GDOC and ASM, following p. 13, say platforms *downplay* that actorhood. On the very point R08 turns on, the versions disagree.

What ASM gives in continuity is real but small. It already quotes the p. 13 line and already names the participant as the person to whom accountability is deflected (ASM, "The Co-opted Person's Side", ¶2). The continuity holds for the claim that a platform downplays its own actorhood. It does not hold for the claim that interpreting is attributing, and that second claim is the one the R08 entry would need.

**Verdict: holds with reframing, narrowly.** The reframing: algorithmacy is offered to organizationality research as the participant-side condition for any attribution to a platform that presents itself as a channel, together with a stated position on the p. 11 sign problem. It is not offered as an attribution practice, and it does not answer -07. On that footing R08 is a legitimate audience, but its recent anchor reaches 1, not 2. If the paper keeps ASM's ¶4 claim and form while entering through -07, the entry is shoehorned and visibly so.

### C2. R06 (with R05): authority moves into the intermediary

**What the askers argue, and whether they would accept algorithmacy.** Mena, Mair and Bruder's republican lens defines the problem as domination: it "focuses on how managerial authority and hierarchical structures create forms of domination that undermine workers’ freedom and autonomy, even when authority is not actively exercised." (pp. 10–11). They answer it with control, not with competence. With Breen they point to "the need for democratic control by workers (e.g. decision-making rights, seats on the board) and the institutionalization of worker voice" (p. 11). Their DAO case is one in which "organizational members usually have equal control (Li & Chen, 2024)" (p. 11). It is not a case of an opaque evaluator. A republican asker would reject algorithmacy as an answer. An ability to work under an arbitrary, opaque rule is the condition of the dominated, not freedom from domination. ASM concedes as much: people's "growing ability keeps an unfair arrangement running" (ASM, "The Co-opted Person's Side", ¶2). The follow-on question MAP_2 relies on asks about "domination and authority from a class of workers other than managers (e.g. professions)" (p. 11). That is authority *over others*. Algorithmacy's "more turns at the cycle" gives some participants more ability, but no authority over anyone.

OQ-mena2026-12 is weaker still. It sits in the *outside-in* deliberative focus, whose lead question is "Does public sphere deliberation significantly influence the organization and, if so, how?" (p. 14). The "within organizations" half that MAP_2 scores is the tail of a question about the public sphere.

**Change to the definition.** Three changes. Genus: from an ability to a basis of power, so that a class holds authority. Scope: the intermediary need not evaluate (a DAO protocol binds), and deliberation becomes "interdependent work". Level: from the interaction to the governance structure. The brief's rule allows each change, capped at 3, but with three changes nothing of the definition is left doing the work.

**Strongest objection.** "Non-domination requires control; competence under an uncontrolled rule is adaptation to domination (pp. 10–11). DAO governance is not opaque by Mena et al.'s description (p. 11)." The paper cannot meet this without a theory of domination it does not have. That is a gap in theory, not in data.

**Verdict: shoehorned.** OQ-mena2026-05 and -12 stay at 1. At most, R06 is a place to cite ASM's own admission that the ability sustains the arrangement.

### C3. R11: the algorithm is the third, not the partner

**What the askers argue, and whether they would accept algorithmacy.** Stark and Vanden Broeck draw the structure algorithmacy needs: "they observe a triangular structure (Vallas & Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners." (p. 14). They place the co-opted rater inside it: "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). For their triangle, algorithmacy supplies what the brief claims, "the co-opted person's side" (ASM, Introduction, last paragraph; the brief cites it as ¶8). The question MAP_1 and MAP_2 score highest, OQ-stark2024-07, rests on the opposite premise. The authors write that "we are interested, instead, in algorithmic practices in which the technologies are themselves our communication partners (Esposito, 2022)." (p. 16), and their cases are dyads: "whether learning a language on Duolingo, managing stress on Happify, or “chatting with Jesus Christ” on textwith.me." (p. 16). Stark and Vanden Broeck would accept algorithmacy as an account of the person at a corner of their p. 14 triangle. On p. 16 they would call it a refusal of their question, and they would say it is missing an account of the partner-with-no-counterpart cases they list. The S6 final score of 2 for -07 is right, and both mappers' 3 is inflated. The stronger 2024 contact is OQ-stark2024-06 (S6 final 3), on the communication field joined to the organizational form, which both maps scored at 2–3.

The bridge to 2025–26 fails on Patriotta et al.'s own text. Their platforms are curators acting "indirectly": "algorithmic systems and AI-driven platforms play a growing role in curating and disseminating information, indirectly influencing which justifications gain visibility." (p. 19). The verdict in a legitimacy test belongs to audiences, so the platform neither evaluates nor binds the contenders, contrary to MAP_2 (§1, R09). Patriotta et al. already have a construct for the skill involved, at the organizational level: "Competent agency (Giddens, 1984; Patriotta et al., 2011) refers to the ability of actors to navigate plural value systems, adapt to evolving controversies and strategically deploy justifications that resonate with key audiences." (p. 17). A reviewer would say algorithmacy duplicates competent agency at the wrong level.

**Change to the definition.** For Stark and Vanden Broeck, none: the answer contests the premise of -07 and keeps the definition. For the Patriotta bridge, level (the organization becomes the participant) and scope (audiences take the counterpart's place, and the intermediary does not bind). That puts OQ-patriotta2025-10 at 1, as S6 scored it.

**Strongest objection.** It is the editor's, and MAP_1 states it well: R11 went from 123 questions to 8, technology "went from object to condition", and no 2025–26 piece takes technology as its main question (STACKS, R11). The paper cannot meet this with any argument, because it concerns the journal's agenda, not the construct. The premise conflict with p. 16 can be met conceptually: concede the dyadic cases and argue from p. 14 that the triangle is the authors' own.

**Verdict: holds with reframing, as backstory, not as home.** The reframing: enter on OQ-stark2024-06 (the communicative capacity of the platform form), not on -07. Present the triad as Stark and Vanden Broeck's own p. 14 structure and say plainly that the p. 16 partner-sociality is outside scope. Drop the Patriotta bridge.

### C4. R05: integrating conditions under an algorithmic mechanism

**What the askers argue, and whether they would accept algorithmacy.** Räcker, Geiger and Seidl define the integrating conditions as products of mechanisms: "Coordinating achieves integration by enacting so-called ‘coordinating mechanisms’, such as rules, roles or routines, that are meant to create three integrating conditions (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009)" (p. 2). The objection MAP_1 anticipates, that conditions belong to systems and not to individuals, is weaker than MAP_1 thinks. Räcker et al. are practice theorists: mechanisms "are not understood as reified ‘things’ that arise prior to coordinating but are constituted in and through coordinating practices as ongoing activity" (p. 3). A participant's interpreting, specifying and tracking can be read as coordinating practice that reconstitutes conditions the intermediary withholds. The residual problem is plurality. Accountability exists "such that the involved actors know who is responsible for what activities" (p. 2), and one person's ability restores it only on one side. Räcker et al. would accept algorithmacy as a candidate practice. They would say it is missing the question they actually ask, how mechanisms *interact* (OQ-racker2024-10, p. 13).

For Hinds and von Krogh the bindingness problem is real in the team passage, where GenAI guides: "specialists’ needs could be synthesized into simple statements that prompt each participant to reflect on their work as it relates to the activities of other participants." (p. 7). Neither mapper cites the one Hinds passage that meets the brief's scope: "If a manager is using GenAI for hiring, for example, the AI would likely continually improve on its ability to select promising candidates." (p. 6). There, "the evaluation algorithm may become increasingly refined and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). That gives an evaluating, binding, adaptive and opaque intermediary between two people. An answer that restricts itself to evaluating GenAI can use p. 6 and avoid the scope change that the hand-check attached to OQ-hinds2024-04.

**Change to the definition.** None, if the claim is limited to intermediaries that evaluate, as at p. 6. Scope, if team-guidance GenAI (p. 7) is included; the brief's rule caps that at 3, as the hand-check ruled (HC_H1).

**Strongest objection.** "Every anchor is from 2024. The 2025–26 askers in R05 ask about tie history and timing, and STACKS lists coordinating mechanisms as such among the agendas with no 2025–26 asker." The paper cannot meet this by argument. It can meet the theoretical objections (plurality; bindingness via p. 6) without new data.

**Verdict: holds with reframing at the level of the concern, on 2023–24 evidence only.** The reframing: algorithmacy as coordinating practice in Räcker et al.'s p. 3 sense, restricted to evaluating intermediaries (Hinds p. 6), with the one-sidedness of restored conditions stated. It is not an entry into a live conversation.

## 3. Are the prevailing concerns (R01, R02, R03) out of reach?
