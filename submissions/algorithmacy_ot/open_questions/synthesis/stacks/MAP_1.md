**The yardstick, as I hold it.** Algorithmacy is "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive" (brief, quoting ASM Introduction ¶7). Its object is a triad in which the intermediary "evaluates and binds" both people. It is an individual's capacity, exercised in an interaction, under an organizational form. Its three parts restore accountability, shared understanding and predictability, after Okhuysen and Bechky (2009). Two conditions do most of the scoring work: the intermediary must hold a verdict over both people (bindingness), and the counterpart must be a human (otherwise the relation is a dyad).

**Two notes on the rules.** The task caps concern fit at 2 when an answer needs a change of genus, level or scope. The brief's scoring rule caps question fit at 3 under the same condition. I apply each cap at its own level. Where I name a change, I state it.

**R01 Institutions and logics: 0.** The 2025–26 askers test whether a person, a body of law or a hype can be an institution, and what counts as a logic. Algorithmacy has no claim about institutions, logics or their units. Nigam et al.'s remark that "alternative digital forms of connection" may help persons become institutions (OQ-nigam2025-10, p. 9) names a setting, not a mechanism algorithmacy supplies.

**R02 Theory and theorizing: 1.** The 2026 editorial welcomes proposals that "introduce new conceptual vocabularies" (OQ-hollerer2026-01, p. 5). Algorithmacy is a new term, so it qualifies as any new concept would. That is vocabulary fit. The 2024 question about machine theorizing (OQ-cornelissen2024-04) concerns the scholar and a language model, which is a dyad, and has no asker since.

**R03 Moral standard and responsibility: 1.** The core of the stack (Werner et al., Kalkman, Pavlisa et al., Friedrich) asks by what moral standard organizations should act. Algorithmacy offers no standard. One question is the exception: Schoeneborn et al. ask what charging and discharging actorhood means for individual and collective responsibility (OQ-schoeneborn2026-06). I score it under R08, where it also sits. Algorithmacy's accountability is a coordination condition (who caused this outcome), not a moral one, and turning it into a moral criterion would change its scope.

**R04 Productive tension: 0.** The askers ask when dissent, paradox and contradiction help. Algorithmacy has nothing to say about it. Stark and Vanden Broeck's "What contradictions are arising in [...] such a system?" (OQ-stark2024-05, p. 15) is the nearest contact, and algorithmacy does not name a contradiction.

**R05 Ties, coordination and capability: 2.** This is algorithmacy's home construct by its own lineage. Räcker, Geiger and Seidl define coordinating as enacting mechanisms "that are meant to create three integrating conditions (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009)": accountability, predictability and common understanding (`racker2024`, p. 2). These are the three conditions the brief says algorithmacy's parts restore. Algorithmacy therefore adds a mechanism the 2023–24 askers lacked: what a person must be able to do when the coordinating mechanism is an opaque, binding, adaptive intermediary. It could not answer the 2025–26 version of the concern. Brennecke et al. and Garud et al. ask how the history of a tie and the timing of an arrangement shape joint work. Algorithmacy has no account of tie history, and its third part (tracking a rule that changes without notice) touches time without theorizing it. Hence 2: a usable case and a mechanism, aimed at a version of the concern that has no 2025–26 asker.

**R06 Democracy and participation: 1.** Mena et al. ask how social media and AI shape deliberation (OQ-mena2026-12) and whether DAO-like structures prevent managerial authority (OQ-mena2026-05). Algorithmacy would describe the person deliberating or working under a ranking or governing intermediary. It says nothing about democracy, the demos or non-domination. Scherer and Neesham's call to organize "the communicative conditions needed for mature citizens" (OQ-scherer2023-13, p. 21) echoes algorithmacy's place in a sequence after oracy and literacy. Linking the two would move the construct from work to citizenship, a change of scope.

**R07 Sustainability and ecological dependence: 0.** Corver and Filler's models are tools an organization uses on itself, and Friedrich's attunement concerns more-than-human others. Neither involves a third that binds two people. Corver and Filler's question about the capacities of "a good 'tool user'" (OQ-corver2026-09) is the dyad that algorithmacy defines itself against.

**R08 Organization as actor: 2.** The 2026 askers explain organizationality by "attribution practices" that "charge" or "discharge" a collective social address with actorhood (`schoeneborn2026` record, Key constructs; pp. 7, 9). They name the platform as a case: "new organizational forms, such as digital platforms, rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation" (p. 13, "Dynamizing Organizationality"). Algorithmacy's second failure mode, treating the platform as a neutral channel, is the participant's side of that discharge. Its first part, locating an outcome's cause in the system, the counterpart or both, is a situation in which participants attribute to a collective address. So algorithmacy offers a usable case and a candidate attribution practice. It cannot say what the participant's attribution does to the platform's actorhood. That is the stack's own question, and answering it would take algorithmacy from an individual capacity to a communicative practice. Hence 2, not 3.

**R09 Legitimacy and public contestation: 1.** The askers ask how legitimacy tests form, escalate and settle. Patriotta et al. note that "algorithmic systems and AI-driven platforms" curate "which justifications gain visibility" (p. 19). That curator is an intermediary between an organization and its audiences. Algorithmacy's second part, specifying intent so it survives the intermediary, would apply only if the organization were the participant, which is a change of level. Algorithmacy says nothing about escalation or settlement.

**R10 Futures and imagination: 0.** Nothing in the questions about imagining, future-making or utopia touches a triad or an intermediary.

**R11 Digital technology: 3.** The concern asks what digital technologies do to organizing and whether technology is part of organization. Algorithmacy contributes a construct the askers lack at a level they work at: the capacity of the person inside a platform triad, which Stark and Vanden Broeck leave open when they describe a "triangular structure (Vallas & Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners" (`stark2024`, p. 14, "Accountability"). It also reframes Hinds and von Krogh's "progressive encapsulation" (opacity plus learning) from a property of the system to a demand on the person. It is not a 4, for three reasons. It answers only the person's side, not what technology does to organizing. Several askers' cases fail its bindingness condition: GenAI that guides a team does not hold a verdict over its members. And the 2025–26 version of the concern asks what legitimacy, democracy or innovation become once technology is present, which algorithmacy reaches only through the platform case.

**R12 Inequality and exclusion: 1.** The brief says people "with more turns at it hold more of the ability than others in the same formal position". That is a possible mechanism of inequality within a position. The one 2026 core piece asks how firms contribute to economic inequality through stakeholder governance (OQ-pavlisa2026-06, -05), and the other recent questions name race, class and gender as moderators. Algorithmacy addresses neither.

## 3. Entry framings

No 2025–26 question reaches question fit 3. Three reach 2: OQ-schoeneborn2026-07, OQ-schoeneborn2026-06 and OQ-patriotta2025-10. Every 3 comes from Stark and Vanden Broeck in 2024. The framings below are ranked by how recent their anchors are, and I say for each one what holds only at the level of the concern.

### Framing A (first choice): R08 Organization as actor. "Who charges the platform?"

- **Stack.** R08, persistent and rising: 7 questions in 2023–24, 24 in 2025–26, last ask 19 September 2026.
- **Recent anchors.** OQ-schoeneborn2026-07 (2); OQ-schoeneborn2026-06 (2). Schoeneborn et al.'s own remark that platforms "strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13) carries the framing and should be quoted as such.
- **Context showing persistence.** OQ-stark2024-08 (2) and OQ-phillips2024-09 (2), both filed in R08. OQ-stark2024-04 (2) supplies the accountability problem ("distributed, deflected, and denied", p. 14).
- **How the paper would answer.** The platform discharges its own actorhood by presenting itself as a channel between users. Its participants then meet outcomes whose cause could lie with the counterpart, the platform or both. Algorithmacy names the capacity to locate that cause. Its first part is, in Schoeneborn et al.'s terms, a recurrent site of attribution among three social addresses. The participant who collapses the triad, or treats the platform as neutral, ratifies the platform's discharge. The competent participant charges it back, at least situationally. The paper would offer this as one of the "other attribution or disattribution practices" that -07 asks for, located among the co-opted rather than among regulators, media or courts. It would answer -06 in part: when a platform is discharged, responsibility for an outcome drifts to the human counterpart, and algorithmacy is what keeps the participant from misplacing it.
- **What the paper would have to add or give up.** It would have to show that the capacity is exercised in communication, as when people say "the app did it" in a complaint, a forum post or a dispute. Without that, it does not touch an attribution practice. That is a move from an individual capacity to a communicative practice, and the brief's level line ("an individual's capacity, exercised in an interaction") just allows it. It would have to give up any claim to explain organizationality as such. It would also have to keep "Thirdness" out of the argument, or say plainly that Dion et al.'s Thirdness is a different notion.
- **Strongest objection.** A CCO or organizationality reviewer: "Actorhood is charged by communication that circulates and is taken up. A gig worker's private reading of why a rating fell charges nothing. You have described a cognitive skill and called it an attribution practice." The reply has to show the interpretive part producing public, recurrent attributions that others take up.

### Framing B: R11 Digital technology, entered through legitimacy. "The co-opted person's side"

- **Stack.** R11, fading in effect: 123 questions in 2023–24, 8 in 2025–26, one core piece.
- **Recent anchors.** OQ-patriotta2025-10 (2), the only recent R11 question at 2. OQ-mena2026-12 (1) and OQ-garud2025-08 (1) show the setting is still named, but neither is addressed.
- **Context showing persistence.** OQ-stark2024-07 (3), OQ-stark2024-06 (3), OQ-stark2024-04 (2), OQ-stark2024-01 (2), OQ-hinds2024-04 (2), OQ-hinds2024-22 (2), OQ-hsu2024-08 (2), OQ-justesen2024-16 (2).
- **How the paper would answer.** Stark and Vanden Broeck draw the platform as a triangle and ask about the "new sociality" with an algorithm. The paper would answer that the sociality is triadic and would name what the co-opted person must be able to do in it: interpret, specify and track. This is the brief's own stated contribution ("the co-opted person's side", ASM Introduction ¶8). The paper would reach 2025–26 through Patriotta et al.: when algorithmic platforms curate "which justifications gain visibility" (p. 19), anyone who must justify through them faces the same three demands.
- **What the paper would have to add or give up.** It would have to accept that its strongest interlocutor is a 2024 conversation with no 2025–26 asker. The Patriotta link needs a level move, from the person to the organization as a justifying actor. That move is a change of level and caps the link at 2. The Hinds questions need a stated bindingness condition, or they fall out of scope.
- **Strongest objection.** An editor's or reviewer's: "This answers a 2024 question the journal has stopped asking. Since 2025 our authors treat technology as a condition of legitimacy, democracy and innovation. They do not treat it as an object." A Stark-side reviewer would add that, after Esposito, they meant the algorithm as the partner, and that the triad sidesteps their claim rather than answering it.

### Framing C (concern-level only): R05 Coordination. "Integrating conditions under an algorithmic mechanism"

- **Stack.** R05, persistent: 46 questions in 2023–24 and 31 in 2025–26, but the recent object moved to tie history and time.
- **Recent anchors.** None at question fit 2. The best are OQ-mena2026-05 (1) and OQ-corver2026-11 (1). I say this plainly: the framing holds at the level of the concern, on 2023–24 evidence.
- **Context showing persistence.** OQ-racker2024-10 (2), OQ-hinds2024-04 (2), OQ-hinds2024-25 (2), OQ-hsu2024-08 (2).
- **How the paper would answer.** Coordinating mechanisms create accountability, predictability and common understanding (Räcker et al., p. 2, after Okhuysen and Bechky). When the mechanism is an opaque, binding, adaptive intermediary, it withholds all three from the people it coordinates. Algorithmacy is the capacity by which they rebuild each one: interpreting restores accountability, specifying restores shared understanding, tracking restores predictability. This is the cleanest fit of the three framings at the level of the concern, since the construct is built on the coordination literature's own terms.
- **What the paper would have to add or give up.** To reach 2025–26 it would need an account of relational history under an intermediary: what a platform's record of past outcomes does to a tie (Brennecke et al.). Algorithmacy does not contain one, and adding it would change its scope. Without that, the paper joins a conversation whose recent askers have moved on.
- **Strongest objection.** A coordination reviewer: "Integrating conditions are properties of the coordinating system, not abilities of individuals. If one participant restores accountability alone, you have shown a workaround, not coordination." Brennecke et al.'s readers would add that nothing here concerns how ties persist.

## 4. R11 or a live non-technology stack?

R11 is the better home for fit and the worse home for the audience. Its concern fit is the highest of the twelve (3). Its 2023–24 questions are the only ones algorithmacy answers at 3, and the brief names Stark and Vanden Broeck as the conversation it joins. But the stack holds 8 recent questions against 123, one core piece, and no 2025–26 piece that takes technology as its main question (STACKS, R11 "Now"). A paper housed there answers a conversation that has gone quiet.

A live non-technology stack is the better entry, provided the paper keeps the R11 material as its evidence of persistence. R08 is the live stack with the strongest recent contact: two 2026 questions at 2, a rising count, and an asker who names platforms that discharge actorhood (p. 13). The cost is a level move. The interpretive part has to be shown working as communication, and the concern fit stays at 2. R05 fits algorithmacy's own terms better, but in 2025–26 it asks about history, which algorithmacy does not theorize.
