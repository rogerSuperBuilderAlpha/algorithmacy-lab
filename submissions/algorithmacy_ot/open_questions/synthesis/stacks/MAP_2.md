**The yardstick in one line.** Algorithmacy is an individual's ability "to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive" (brief, quoting ASM Introduction ¶7). The structure is a triad in which the intermediary "evaluates and binds" both people. The ability has three parts: interpreting an outcome to find its cause, specifying intent so it survives the intermediary, and keeping track of a rule that changes without notice. These restore Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability, shared understanding and predictability. Below, every score rests on that definition unchanged. Where an answer needs a change, I name it and cap the score at 2.

Against the 2023–24 posing — Hinds and von Krogh on what GenAI does to team coordination, Räcker et al. on how coordinating mechanisms interact — it would supply a construct the askers lack, a 3. **R06 Democracy and participation — 2.** Two of Mena et al.'s 23 questions ask what happens to democratic organizing when governance or deliberation passes through technology: whether DAO-like structures prevent managerial authority or breed a new dominating class (OQ-mena2026-05, p. 11), and how social media and AI shape deliberation (OQ-mena2026-12, p. 14). Algorithmacy gives a usable case for both: an intermediary that holds the verdict relocates authority rather than removing it, and members with more turns at the cycle hold more of the ability than others in the same formal position. It says nothing about who the demos is, backsliding, or civic engagement, which carry most of the stack. The DAO case also strains the triad, because a DAO protocol binds members but need not evaluate them.

**R07 Sustainability and ecological dependence — 0.** Corver and Filler's models are tools for inquiry, "tools for problem solving rather than ... a finished representational product" (record, Key constructs, p. 14); they neither evaluate nor bind the people who use them. Friedrich's "technological dependencies" (OQ-friedrich2026-05) name technology as one of many entanglements. Neither is a triad.

**R08 Organization as actor — 2.** Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl define organizationality by collective actorhood, which "attribution practices" charge or discharge (record, Key constructs). On p. 13 they say that "new organizational forms, such as digital platforms, rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation". Algorithmacy's first part — interpreting an outcome to find whether the system, the counterpart or both caused it — is the person-level act through which a platform's actorhood is charged or let go, and its second failure mode (treating the platform as a neutral channel) is the discharge. That is a usable mechanism for the platform case. It stays at 2 because the move needs a change of level: Schoeneborn et al.'s attributions are recurrent communicative practices addressed to a collective, and algorithmacy is an individual's capacity; the paper would have to argue that the interpretation, once voiced, is such a practice. Dion, Cooren and Dupuis's "Thirdness" (OQ-dion2025-11) is Taylor and Van Every's recurrence or habit (record, Key constructs, p. 8), not a third party, so the shared word is vocabulary only.

**R09 Legitimacy and public contestation — 1.** Patriotta et al. ask how "human and technological actors" co-produce legitimacy tests (OQ-patriotta2025-10, p. 19); the record's scope reads "algorithmic systems and AI-driven platforms curating which justifications gain visibility". A curating platform does evaluate both contenders. But the parties to a legitimacy test are an organization and its audiences, not two people doing interdependent work, so using algorithmacy here changes its scope. Patriotta et al.'s own construct, "competent agency" — "the ability of actors to navigate plural value systems" (record, Key constructs, p. 17) — is the nearer analogue, at the organizational level. I score OQ-patriotta2025-10 under R11, where it also sits.

## 3. Entry framings

None of the three reaches question fit 3 on a recent anchor. Each holds at concern level 2.

### Framing 1 (preferred). R08 Organization as actor: who charges the platform with actorhood

- **Stack.** R08, persistent and rising: 7 questions before 2025, 24 since; last ask 19 September 2026 (STACKS.md).
- **Recent anchors.** OQ-schoeneborn2026-07 (2) and OQ-schoeneborn2026-06 (2), with the claim on p. 13 that digital platforms "strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation".
- **Persistence.** OQ-stark2024-08 (2), technology as organizational structure; OQ-stark2024-04 (2), accountability for algorithmic decisions; OQ-phillips2024-09 (1), what organizations are once artificial persons join them. Earlier R08 context is thin (one core piece, STACKS.md).
- **How the paper would answer.** Schoeneborn et al. explain organizationality by who attributes agency to a collective, and they name platforms as the case where the collective works to shed it. Algorithmacy locates one site of that attribution: the participant in a platform triad who interprets an outcome and decides whether the system, the counterpart or both produced it. A person who exercises that part charges the platform with actorhood; a person who treats the platform as a neutral channel discharges it and blames the counterpart, which is the scapegoating pattern the authors describe. The paper would argue that the distribution of algorithmacy among a platform's users therefore co-determines the platform's organizationality, and that Stark and Vanden Broeck's "twisted accountability" persists where users lack it.
- **What it would have to add or give up.** It must argue that a voiced interpretation (a complaint, a forum post, a dispute) is a recurrent communicative attribution practice in Schoeneborn et al.'s sense. That is a change of level, from an individual's capacity to a practice, and it is why the framing stays at 2. It gives up foregrounding the person's ability for its own sake; the ability becomes the explanans of an organization-level outcome.
- **Strongest objection.** A reviewer from the organizationality exchange would say that attribution practices are communicative events addressed to a social address, and that an individual competence is the wrong kind of thing to enter that theory; Dion et al. would add that the paper smuggles in an entitative view of the platform (OQ-dion2025-13 asks authors to state their ontological postures).

### Framing 2. R06 Democracy and participation, with R05: authority moves into the intermediary

- **Stack.** R06, persistent; all 29 recent questions from 2026, 23 of them from one paper (STACKS.md).
- **Recent anchors.** OQ-mena2026-05 (2), with its follow-on question about "forms of domination and authority from a class of workers other than managers" (p. 11); OQ-mena2026-12 (2).
- **Persistence.** OQ-scherer2023-13 (2), communicative conditions for mature citizens; in R05, OQ-hinds2024-16 (3) and OQ-hinds2024-25 (3) on coordination once GenAI absorbs it. No 2023–24 question in R06 itself asks about technology and governance above fit 1 (OQ-jaser2024-06 is a call for democratic oversight of surveillance).
- **How the paper would answer.** Mena et al. ask, from a republican view, whether new governance structures remove managerial domination. Algorithmacy answers that where an intermediary evaluates and binds members, domination does not disappear. It moves to the intermediary, which holds the verdict, and to the members with more turns at the interpret–specify–track cycle, a class marked by ability rather than office. For deliberation the same structure means that the better argument must also be specified to survive the system that ranks it, so that deliberative equality depends on an unequally held ability.
- **What it would have to add or give up.** It must show that DAOs and AI deliberation tools are triadic intermediaries; a DAO protocol binds but does not plainly evaluate, so the paper either widens the intermediary's definition (a change it must state, capping fit at 2) or restricts itself to systems that rank members. It gives up the gig-work home setting and must engage republican and deliberative theory on their own terms.
- **Strongest objection.** A democracy-and-organization reviewer would say that opacity is a design choice — DAO code is open — and that unequal competence is an argument for transparency and training, not a new theory of domination. They would also note that the concern rests on one paper.

### Framing 3. R11 with R05: coordination when technology is the third party

- **Stack.** R11, fading in effect (8 recent questions against 123), entered through R05's persistent coordination concern.
- **Recent anchors.** OQ-mena2026-12 (2); OQ-patriotta2025-10 (2, after a scope change); OQ-garud2025-08 (1) as a foil, since it frames Gen AI as a "human-AI ensemble" (p. 16).

- **How the paper would answer.** When technology became a condition of legitimacy, democracy and innovation in 2025–26, the field stopped asking what the technology demands of the people who work through it. Algorithmacy answers that question at the level of the interaction: an intermediary that evaluates and binds both parties strips away accountability, shared understanding and predictability, and the person restores them by interpreting outcomes, specifying intent and tracking a moving rule. It explains why task deskilling and rising relational demand arrive together (Hinds's division-of-labour question) and why following the AI disrupts interdependence (Hinds's emergent-interdependence question).
- **What it would have to add or give up.** It must carry the argument beyond teams and gig work into the conditions the 2025–26 askers name — deliberation, legitimacy tests — where the scope changes. It gives up the claim to address a concern the journal is now asking.
- **Strongest objection.** A reviewer would say that the paper answers 2024's questions in 2027, and that Hinds and von Krogh's GenAI absorbs coordination rather than evaluating anyone, so the triad is the paper's assumption, not their phenomenon.

## 4. Is R11 a better or worse home than a live non-technology stack?
