## Key question 2 — Is every use in the manuscript supported by the source? (quotations, pages, generality)

### Takeaway
Of roughly 50 citing clauses, most are supported. Five need wording changes: Traeger et al. (the robot did not express "uncertainty", and talk became more equal only against a *silent* robot, not a neutral one); Yates (the clause that firms "came to assume that their people could read and write" is the author's inference); Wing (cited for a claim about appeals that its abstract does not carry); Simmel (the mediator does not have "aims of its own"; the arbitrator passage is in 1902 Part II, not only 1950); and Stark & Vanden Broeck as proponents of "a triadic view" (they write "triangular structure" and credit it onward). **Selznick (1949)** — one use, "The Form of the Relation as a Defining Criterion": "Selznick (1949) describes co-optation as an organization giving a critic a seat on one of its boards, in contrast to a platform, which enrolls people on its own terms and gives them none."
- Selznick's definition (p. 13): "cooptation is the process of absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence"; formal cooptation: "Appointments to official posts are made, contracts are signed, new organizations are established — all signifying participation in the process of decision and administration"; p. 14: "what is shared is the responsibility for power rather than power itself", with examples of "normally disfranchised groups … temporarily given representation in the councils of government" and "enlisted personnel representation in the courts-martial machinery" — [local OCR, lines 800–880](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/dissertation/research/library/pdfs/selznick1949.txt).
- Verdict: **partly supported.** The substance (outside elements absorbed into decision structures to avert threats; responsibility shared, power not) is Selznick's. "A critic" and "a seat on one of its boards" are a textbook compression, not his words; his examples are groups and interests, not an individual critic, and "board" is not in the definitional passage. The contrast with a platform is the author's and is marked as such. Read depth: definitional chapter pp. 13–14, full text this session (OCR).

**Simmel (1902)** — two uses: Introduction, "Building on Simmel's (1902, 1950) account of the third party"; and "The Form of the Relation": "I follow Georg Simmel's (1902, 1950) argument that the number of parties to a relation determines its form. As Simmel argued, a relation of three is a different kind of thing from a relation of two, because a third party can mediate between the other two, profit from their disagreement, or set them against each other, and in each case their relation passes through the third, who has aims of its own."
- Part I (AJS 8(1)), pp. 45–46: the dyad is "distinguished by the fact that both phenomena are lacking: both the strengthened attachment through a third … and also the interruption and distraction of pure and immediate reciprocation"; in combinations of three "each individual element operates as a court of appeal between the two others … It operates both in combining and separating" — [archive.org scan text](https://archive.org/download/jstor-2762037/2762037_djvu.txt).
- Part II (AJS 8(2)), p. 166: "the tri-unity as such appears to me to produce three sorts of typical group-forms, which on the one hand are not possible with two elements, on the other hand, in case of a number greater than three, are either likewise excluded, or are merely extended quantitatively without changing their form-type." The three forms are headed "1. The unpartisan and the mediator" (p. 166), "2. The tertius gaudens" (p. 174) and "3. Divide et impera" (p. 182) — [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/jstor-2761932/2761932.pdf) (text layer empty; extracted locally).
- On the mediator's aims, p. 174: "The mediator and the arbitrator alike wish to preserve the group unity against the danger of disruption. The nonpartisan, however, may use his relatively superior status in a purely egoistic interest. While in the former cases he acted as a means to the ends of the group…" — same source.
- Verdict: "the number of parties … determines its form" and the three roles — **supported** (full text this session, both parts). "In each case … the third … has aims of its own" — **partly**: true of the tertius gaudens and divide et impera; for the nonpartisan mediator Simmel says the opposite (he "seeks to eliminate himself", p. 167; acts "as a means to the ends of the group", p. 174).
