## Key question 7 — Desk assessment

### Takeaway
On the evidence, an editor would most likely not desk-reject on scope (the topic is wanted and the form is right) but could on contribution and completeness: a construct whose theoretical derivation is five sentences of Simmel, a reference list below the journal's floor with the sister journal absent, an undrawn figure, and, if disclosed as the policy requires, a sentence-by-sentence AI build on a template the editors have just published. The same facts, handled before submission, are the manuscript's case for review.

**Mechanism-to-feature mapping as written.** The manuscript ties interpreting to opacity, specifying intent to bindingness, keeping track to adaptivity, and separately ties the loss of accountability, predictability and common understanding (Okhuysen & Bechky) to the intermediary; it then says "Interpreting thereby restores accountability" but states no corresponding restoration for predictability or common understanding — [ASSEMBLED.md](/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/manuscript/sbs_schoeneborn2026/ASSEMBLED.md), "Moderators Versus Third Parties" and "Practising the three parts". ### Inferences

**Most likely reasons for a desk rejection, in order of weight:**
1. **Contribution not yet earned in the journal's terms.** The novelty is a name (algorithmacy) and a sorting criterion (form of the relation); the derivation of the three parts occupies three paragraphs of about 100 words each, and the theory it borrows gets five sentences. Against a population where every 2026 article expounds its tradition in 4–24 paragraphs and the launch editorial's rejection ground is a contribution that "falls short", an editor can reasonably read the manuscript as a well-organised proposal rather than a developed theory. The question an editor will ask is what algorithmacy explains that Rahman's reactivity, Zhou's competency and Sutherland's gig literacies do not, and the manuscript's answer (a criterion that keeps the second person in view) is stated rather than shown.
2. **Reference list below the floor, sister journal absent.** 65 against 94–222, zero *OS*, one *AMR*, no engagement with the journal's own editorials on theorizing, and the technology-theory lineage the journal published (sociomaterial, relational) cited as cases rather than as rivals. An editor reads the reference list first.
3. **Incompleteness.** No drawn figure; a title, abstract and keywords the author has not ruled on; the oracy/literacy premise resting on unread sources. These are desk-visible.
4. **AI build and template.** Under SAGE's tiers the drafting must be disclosed as generative use. Disclosed, it is not a rejection ground; but a disclosure that says the text was written sentence by sentence against a published article, read by an editor who then recognises that article's scaffold in the Introduction, is a combination no other submission to the journal is likely to present, and the launch editorial's "in-depth critique and reflection" standard is not met by form alone. Undisclosed, it is a rejection ground "at any point".
5. **Unhedged necessity claims without boundary conditions.** Not a desk-rejection ground on its own, but it sharpens 1: a theory stated without the "contingent" and "can be presumed" that the model uses invites the editor to test it against the obvious counter-case (a disclosed, appealable rule), which the manuscript names only as a future "course of action".

**Most in its favour:**
1. **Scope and timing.** No 2025–2026 article is about algorithms, platforms or AI; the three that were (2023–2024) are among the journal's most cited; the manuscript builds on all three with located quotations; and the journal has just opened a section for "emerging topics… at the margins". A cover letter can say this in two sentences.
2. **Form.** On every measured count but references and hedging the manuscript is inside the journal's range, and on propositions, cases, limitations, agenda and practical implications it matches the majority practice. Its sentence and paragraph lengths match the venue; its abstract equals the model's length.
3. **A single criterion and a named dilemma** are an architecture the editors accepted in the same issue; the oracy/literacy/algorithmacy sequence is memorable and gives the journal a bridge to communication scholarship it does not currently have.
4. **The construct is evaluable.** Three parts, three features, three published cases per part, and a cycle: a reviewer can say exactly where they disagree, which is what the journal's "bold thought experiments through in-depth critique" asks for.
5. **Two thousand words of headroom** under the 11,000-word limit, enough for a Simmel section, thirty references and a drawn figure without cutting anything.

**What a first-round reviewer would most likely ask for:**
- A proper exposition of Simmel's triad (mediator, tertius gaudens, divide et impera) with pages, and an argument for why an algorithm, rather than the operator who deploys it, is the third *party*; the "fourth party" citation invites the objection that the arrangement is a tetrad and the construct mislocated.
- Engagement with the sociomaterial reading of the same cases (Orlikowski & Scott, whose framework denies that the algorithm is a separable party) and with the relational perspective (Bailey et al. 2022; Faraj & Pachidi 2021) as rivals to the triadic view, not as illustrations.
- A boundary table or paragraph separating algorithmacy from algorithmic competency, reactivity, gig literacies, folk theories and AI literacy on stated dimensions (as Stark & Vanden Broeck hold one comparison across five dimensions), and a statement of when the triad does not hold (disclosed rules; appealable results; a human intermediary).
- The oracy-to-literacy premise sourced from Ong, Goody & Watt, Havelock and Olson read in full, or dropped to a single sentence.
- Completion of the Okhuysen & Bechky mapping: if interpreting restores accountability, what restores predictability and common understanding, and are three parts exhaustive.
- Thirty-odd references from *OS*, *AMR*, *JMS* and the algorithmic-management literature; the drawn figure; hedged mechanism claims with boundary conditions; a research agenda that includes theoretical, not only measurement, questions (the journal does not publish empirical tests).
- An AI-use disclosure and, if the reviewer knows the model, a rewrite of the Introduction's last four paragraphs, the three-reason run and the recap sentences in the author's own frames.
