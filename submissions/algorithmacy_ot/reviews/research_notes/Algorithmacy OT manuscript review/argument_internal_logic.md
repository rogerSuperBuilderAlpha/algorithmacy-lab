# Internal soundness of the argument in ASSEMBLED.md (Organization Theory manuscript)

Source read in full: `/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/manuscript/sbs_schoeneborn2026/ASSEMBLED.md` (cited below as MS, by section heading). Also read: `LEDGER.md` (same folder; the drafter's own list of inferences the source draft does not state), `OUR_BRIEF.md`, the earlier workshop version at `/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/lima_pdw/archive/2026-09-10_PAPER_submitted.md` (cited as Lima), and Suddaby (2010) pp. 346–353 from the local PDF. Cornelissen (2017), Whetten (1989) and Podsakoff et al. (2016) could not be reached in full (see Gaps at the end); where I lean on them I say so and mark the point as unverified.

Verdict in one paragraph. The manuscript's organizing idea is good and should be kept: an intermediary that evaluates and binds two people is a party, not a channel, and the participant's position is equivocal (one signal, two possible sources) rather than uncertain. The argument built on that idea has three faults that reach the central claim. First, the definition of algorithmacy does not require an algorithm, and the form it names (an opaque, binding, non-chosen third between two interdependent people) is old: a court, a blind referee, an examination board. The Conclusion itself lists "peer review with hidden scoring" as a site. Either the ability is not new, and the oracy→literacy→algorithmacy sequence and "one does not grow into the next" fall, or the manuscript has not named what is new. Second, the stated criterion, "the form of the relation between the people involved," is not the criterion the framework uses; the framework sorts by features of the intermediary, and the manuscript concedes this in a parenthesis. The first rival placement (person and chatbot) has no second person and cannot be placed by the stated criterion at all. Third, the three-from-three derivation is not one-to-one: opacity enters all three parts, three further features the text relies on (authority not conferred, change unannounced, no channel to ask) are uncounted, and the promised pairing of parts with Okhuysen and Bechky's three conditions is stated for one part only and misaligned for that one. Below each of these, the empirical support for the three parts is drawn almost entirely from settings the manuscript itself classifies as dyads, and two of the four cases lack a binding third.

## 1. Reconstruction: premises and conclusions, and their status

### Takeaway
Of fourteen load-bearing steps, four are argued, six are cited, and four are asserted. The two steps that carry the thesis (that the three features are the features, and that each demands exactly one part) are asserted.

### Cited Findings
Status key: ARGUED (reasons given in the text), CITED (rests on a source), ASSERTED (stated without reasons or source).

- P1 (CITED). Existing accounts place the system either as the one a person deals with or as a channel between two people. — MS, Introduction ¶1–2; The Triadic View ¶3–4.
- P2 (ASSERTED for the first half, CITED for the second). The first placement loses the other person; the second leaves the system "with nothing to decide." — MS, Introduction ¶3: "an account which keeps the other person in view treats the system as a channel, a helper, or an obstacle that decides nothing, while an account which treats the system as hidden and powerful reduces the other person to a source of ratings (Rahman, 2021; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025)." The LEDGER (S-010) records that "the citations support the second half only."
- C1 (ASSERTED). The "dyad dilemma": no account keeps both. — MS, Introduction ¶3.
- P3 (CITED). The triadic view: platform and two parties form a relation of three. — MS, Introduction ¶4; The Triadic View ¶1. LEDGER S-011 notes the three papers "do not call themselves 'a triadic view'."
- P4 (ASSERTED, with one quotation as witness). The triadic view "has not said what a person inside the arrangement must be able to do." — MS, Introduction ¶4. The Stelmaszak et al. quotation shows one account placing capability in the organization; it does not show that no account names the person's part.
- P5 (CITED, paraphrased). Simmel: the number of parties determines the form of a relation; a third can mediate, profit, or divide. — MS, The Form of the Relation ¶2. LEDGER S-060–S-061: no quotation from Simmel; "the draft does not state it in those words."
- P6 (CITED). Organization theory distinguishes coordination forms by the position each gives the participant. — MS, The Form of the Relation ¶5.
- P7 (CITED). Coordination rests on accountability, predictability, common understanding. — MS, Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶1, citing Okhuysen & Bechky (2009).
- C2 = Proposal 1 (ARGUED, three reasons: simplicity, agreement with OT, reach). Abilities are distinguished by the form of the relation: oracy for direct and chained dyads, literacy for text-moderated dyads, algorithmacy for triads. — MS, Introduction ¶5; The Form of the Relation ¶4–6. LEDGER S-068–S-069 marks the simplicity reason as an inference the source draft does not make.
- P8 (CITED to Olson, then ASSERTED). A text "judges no one, decides nothing, and does not change once it is written." — MS, Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶1.
- P9 (ASSERTED, illustrated with Hinds & von Krogh quotations). An algorithm that evaluates two people "acts," and so is a third party, not a moderator. — MS, Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2.
- P10 (ASSERTED). Therefore the intermediary is opaque, binding, and adaptive. — MS, The Form of the Relation ¶8: "Accordingly, the thing in the middle of a triad is not a moderator but a party. In turn, this means that a person in a triad deals with an intermediary that is opaque … binding … and adaptive." Nothing in "is a party" entails these three; "this means" is doing the work of an argument that is not given.
- P11 (ASSERTED; "I assume"). The two people lose the three conditions, one per feature. — MS, Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2: "I assume that two people in this position lose the conditions on which their coordination rested."
- P12 (CITED). Equivocality versus uncertainty (Daft & Lengel). — MS, Three Features ¶1.
- C3 = Proposal 2 (ARGUED from P10–P12, one paragraph per part). Interpreting from opacity, specifying intent from bindingness, keeping track from adaptivity. — MS, Introduction ¶6; Three Features ¶2–4.
- C4 (ASSERTED). The parts form a cycle; more turns, more ability. — MS, Introduction ¶6; Three Features ¶1.
- C5 (ASSERTED, by analogy). "One does not grow into the next: a good speaker cannot thereby read, and a good reader cannot thereby work through an algorithm that judges her." — MS, Discussion, Placing the Abilities ¶3.
- C6 (ILLUSTRATED). Failure = treating the triad as a direct or a moderated dyad. — MS, Practising the Three Parts, "Failing" ¶1–2.
- C7 (ASSERTED with citations that do not carry it; see §9). The framework explains why organizations fail, why identical workers diverge, and how ability keeps an unfair arrangement running. — MS, Discussion, The Co-opted Person's Side ¶1–2.

### Inferences
- The chain C1 → P4 → C2 → C3 is valid in form, but C3 is only as strong as P10 and P11, both of which are asserted. The manuscript's thesis therefore rests on two unargued premises placed one sentence apart (The Form of the Relation ¶8; Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2).
- The LEDGER's own list of "inferences the draft does not state" (S-018, S-064–S-066, S-068–S-069, S-183, S-192) coincides with the reasons given for Proposal 1 (that the criterion "keeps" the rivals, that it is "simpler," that a list owes an account of its items). The argument for Proposal 1 is, by the drafter's own record, largely drafter-supplied.

### Gaps
- None for reconstruction; the manuscript is short enough to map in full.

## 2. Definitions: are the terms defined once and used consistently?

### Takeaway
Four key terms shift meaning inside the manuscript ("direct dyad," "counterpart," "acts" as the third-party criterion, and "the criterion" itself); "ability" is never defined; "sensibility" and "coordinative co-optation" do not appear. The moderator/third-party line is principled only when stated as "evaluates and binds," and the text states it that way only some of the time.

### Cited Findings
- "Direct dyad" is defined twice with different members. Introduction ¶1: "the arrangement is a direct dyad, with the person as one member and the system as the other." The Form of the Relation ¶6: "oracy … belongs to the direct dyad, in which I speak to you and you answer." The first is person–machine, the second person–person. — MS, both locations.
- The criterion is "the form of the relation between the people involved" (Introduction ¶5) and "sorting by the relation among the people" (The Form of the Relation ¶4), yet the manuscript also says of the first placement that "a basis in the person and the system shows no second person" (The Form of the Relation ¶2). A relation with one person has no "relation among the people" to sort by. — MS.
- The criterion is then restated as something else: "the relation itself (or, to be more precise, what the thing in the middle does to the two people) indicates what a person must be able to do" (The Form of the Relation ¶5). — MS. The parenthesis replaces the number-and-form criterion with a feature-of-the-intermediary criterion.
- "Counterpart" names the system in the review ("a counterpart in a system of work (Anthony et al., 2023)," Introduction ¶1; "the worker's only counterpart," The Triadic View ¶5; "not as a tool, a medium, or a counterpart," Conclusion ¶3) and the other person in the framework ("the counterpart, who is the other person she works with," Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶1). — MS.
- "Moderator" is defined as "something that stands between two people and shapes what passes between them without being one of them" (Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶1). A third party also stands between two people and is not one of them; the clause does not separate the two. — MS.
- The third-party criterion is given as action: "a text … takes no action of its own. A third party, in turn, acts" (Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2). But the chained dyad's relay also acts: "a message passes through a person, who can change it, forget part of it, or add to it" (same section ¶1), and this is classed as a chain of dyads, not a triad. — MS.
- Where the criterion is stated precisely, it is "evaluates and binds": "an intermediary … that evaluates and binds them is a party" (Algorithmacy as the Ability ¶1); "wherever a system evaluates and binds two people who depend on each other" (Conclusion ¶3). — MS.
- "Intermediary" is sometimes the genus ("like a text, an intermediary stands between two people," Algorithmacy as the Ability ¶1) and sometimes the species ("the intermediary, which is the system between them," Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶1). — MS.
- "Opaque," "binding," "adaptive" are defined once (The Form of the Relation ¶8) and used consistently thereafter. — MS.
- "Ability" is never defined. The nearest statement: "I treat each part here as something a person does and learns by doing, and not as a procedure that can be written down and taught (Jablin & Sias, 2001; Sandberg, 2000)" (Practising ¶1). — MS. The Lima version had a subsection "Competency, Not Skill or Capability" and called the construct a "sensibility"; both are gone. — Lima, headings list.
- "Sensibility" and "coordinative co-optation" do not occur in the manuscript. LEDGER S-028: "The compound 'coordinative co-optation' is not attributed to them." — MS; LEDGER.
- Suddaby's second rule for a definition: avoid building antecedent or outcome variables into it, e.g. "cognitive ability" as "a capability that enables people to learn more effectively in contexts that are dynamic or complex" (Suddaby 2010, p. 347). The manuscript's definition has the same shape: "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive" (Introduction ¶6). The three features are scope conditions packed into the definiens. — Suddaby 2010 PDF p. 347; MS.

### Inferences
- The "it acts" criterion is ad hoc as written; "it evaluates and binds both" is the principled one, and the text should say only that. On the "acts" criterion the chained dyad is a triad and the manuscript's own three-form scheme has four forms.
- Test cases on the "evaluates and binds both, with a rule the parties cannot see, that changes" criterion:
  - Human editor: shapes what passes, acts for one side, binds at most the writer. Moderated dyad (Hancock's "on behalf of"). Literacy.
  - Clerk: relays and may alter. The manuscript's own John-and-Sally case: chained dyad. Oracy.
  - Bureaucratic form: a text that constrains what can be said; the office behind it decides by a published rule. Moderated dyad with the office as counterpart; Yates (1989) is cited exactly for this as literacy. Shows that binding can occur without opacity, which the manuscript's "in turn, this means" treats as impossible.
  - Rating agency: evaluates the issuer, informs the investor, binds both through regulatory thresholds, methodology partly published, revised over time. A triad with reduced opacity; the framework has no graded version of its features and cannot say what ability it calls for.
  - Referee (blind review): evaluates the author, binds through the editor, rule hidden, authority not conferred by the author. A triad on the manuscript's criterion. The Conclusion concedes this: "peer review with hidden scoring."
  - Auctioneer: binds buyer and seller by a public rule. Triad, not opaque.
  - Court: binds both parties, deliberation hidden, law changes, authority not conferred by the parties. Satisfies all three features.
- Because the court and the referee satisfy the definition, the manuscript's construct as defined is not specific to algorithms. See §3 and §10.

### Gaps
- Podsakoff et al. (2016) was not reachable; their checklist on necessary-and-sufficient attributes would sharpen the "packed definition" point but I could not verify its wording.

## 3. Is "triad" doing the work claimed? Why a text is not a third party

### Takeaway
The triad does less work than claimed because (a) Simmel's third has "aims of its own," and the manuscript never settles whether the party is the algorithm or its operator; (b) the triad-with-binding-third is old, so form alone cannot ground a new ability.

### Cited Findings
- Simmel is invoked for a third "who has aims of its own" (The Form of the Relation ¶2). The manuscript's own quotation of Stark and Vanden Broeck gives the aims to the platform ("The platform benefits from her agency," The Form of the Relation ¶7), and its citation of Gibbs et al. says "the machine also carries the rules of whoever deployed it" (The Triadic View ¶3). — MS.
- The manuscript says the third "decides for the other two and presents itself as not a party" (The Form of the Relation ¶8). — MS. This is a property of an operator, not of a rule.
- The arbitrator contrast: "Unlike the arbitrator Simmel (1950) describes, however, an algorithmic third party was not given its power by the two people, and all they can do is leave" (Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2). — MS. This is the one feature that separates the construct from arbitration, and it is not among the three features from which the parts are derived.
- Suddaby, citing Walker and Cohen: "Finding a single exception is often fatal to a construct because it implies that any proposition associated with the construct is false" (Suddaby 2010, p. 349). — Suddaby PDF.

### Inferences
- If the party is the operator acting through a rule, then the manager-with-GenAI case (operator = the manager's own organization, user = the manager) and the planning-simulation case (operator = the planners themselves) are not triads of the kind Stark and Vanden Broeck describe; the third's "aims" are one of the two parties' aims. The manuscript needs a statement of who the party is.
- A court is an opaque (deliberation hidden), binding, adaptive (law and precedent move), non-conferred third between two interdependent parties. Litigants have needed the ability to interpret verdicts, address the bench and the opponent in one filing, and track a changing body of rules for as long as there have been courts. The manuscript must either (i) add a feature that courts lack (no forum of appeal outside the system; the rule is never published even in principle; change is continuous and unannounced; the third denies being a party) and derive something from it, or (ii) accept that algorithmacy is the litigant's ability under a new name, which would cost it "one ability does not grow into the next" and the oracy→literacy→algorithmacy sequence. The text has the materials for (i) scattered (no appeal: Conclusion ¶2; denial: The Form of the Relation ¶8; unannounced: Three Features ¶4) and counts none of them.

### Gaps
- Whether Simmel's "number determines form" thesis (1902) is stated in a way that admits a non-human member is not checked against Simmel; the manuscript gives no quotation (LEDGER S-061).

## 4. The derivation of three parts from three features

### Takeaway
The mapping is neither one-to-one nor exhaustive: opacity is needed for all three parts, specifying intent needs the system to read the participant's words (a channel property) rather than bindingness, and the "restores one condition" pairing is stated for one part only and misaligned for that one.

### Cited Findings
- Specifying intent is derived from bindingness: "under a binding intermediary whatever the participant says to the counterpart is also evidence that will be scored. This is because the place where she addresses the counterpart is the place where both of them are judged" (Three Features ¶3). — MS. The premise is that the system reads what she writes to the counterpart. That is a property of the system as channel-plus-evaluator, not of bindingness; a binding system that scores only the counterpart's rating (the marketplace case) does not read her words at all.
- The same paragraph makes the demand turn on opacity: she must write "in a form that survives the processing" (Practising ¶3). She cannot know what survives unless she knows the rule; this is opacity, not bindingness. — MS.
- Keeping track adds a feature not in the list: "a change in the rule is not announced, and the participant detects it only from its effects" (Three Features ¶4). — MS. Unannounced change is opacity over time. Under adaptivity with announcement there is nothing to detect.
- Interpreting is derived from opacity, but the stakes that make interpreting necessary come from bindingness: without a binding result there is nothing to trace. — MS, Three Features ¶2 (inference from the text).
- The "lost conditions" are mapped to causes that do not match the three features: "accountability, because the intermediary decides and denies deciding; predictability, because the rule changes without notice; and common understanding, because neither can see how the other's part was weighed" (Moderators Versus Third Parties ¶2). — MS. Denial of deciding is not one of the three features; "without notice" adds unannounced; "cannot see how weighed" is opacity.
- The "restores" pairing is stated once: "Interpreting thereby restores accountability, because it settles who or what was responsible" (Practising ¶2). — MS. No sentence says what specifying intent or keeping track restores.
- The Conclusion concedes the list may not be closed: "is deciding whether the system or the other person caused a change a fourth part of its own? … one study indicates that it is a task in itself (Rahman, 2021)" (Conclusion ¶2). — MS.
- Lima stated what the derivation claims more carefully: "That mapping is what the phrase 'derived from the structure of the triad' means in this paper, and it is the whole of what the phrase claims. Whether the three exhaust the deficits … is what RQ4 tests." — Lima, line 167. The MS drops the qualification and keeps the claim.

### Inferences
- Alignment check of the two triples. Features → parts: opaque→interpreting, binding→specifying, adaptive→keeping track. Features → lost conditions (from the text's own causes): opaque→common understanding, adaptive→predictability, [denial]→accountability. If each part restores the condition its feature removed, interpreting (opacity) should restore common understanding, not accountability. The one stated pairing contradicts the only consistent alignment. The "neat pairing" is not a pairing at all once written out.
- Alternative cuts the manuscript does not rule out. Two features: a hidden rule with binding stakes, taken at one time (interpreting now; addressing two readers now) and over time (tracking). Four features: add non-conferred authority / no forum, from which "appealing to the system that produced it" (Conclusion ¶2) would yield a fourth part. The manuscript asserts three without saying why not two or four.
- Double counting: opacity is a necessary condition of each part. A reader who grants only opacity and bindingness can derive all three parts (tracking being interpretation repeated under a rule that may have moved), which makes adaptivity a condition on repetition, not a source of a distinct part.

### Gaps
- Cornelissen (2017) on typological theorizing (dimensions must be exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and the theorist must explain why the types differ) is the relevant standard; the PDF link returned 404, so the wording is unverified. From memory, the expectation is as stated.

## 5. "One ability does not grow into the next": analogy, typology, or history?

### Takeaway
The claim is asserted by analogy and is empirical; the manuscript runs an analogy (speaker/reader), a typology (forms of relation) and a historical sequence (Yates) together without marking which it is making.

### Cited Findings
- The claim: "one does not grow into the next: a good speaker cannot thereby read, and a good reader cannot thereby work through an algorithm that judges her" (Discussion, Placing the Abilities ¶3). — MS. No source, no mechanism.
- Historical register: "this is in line with the history of American firms, which between about 1850 and 1920 came to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files" (The Form of the Relation ¶6); "the abilities that came before algorithmacy" (same ¶; Discussion ¶2). — MS.
- Typological register: "oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads" (Introduction ¶5). — MS.
- Evidence that bears on the claim exists and was in the earlier version: Zhou et al. report that general digital competence correlates r = .37 with algorithmic competency. — Lima, line 117. Not in MS.
- The manuscript's own evidence for the parts is drawn from dyads (see §8), which is inconsistent with the claim that the dyadic abilities do not carry over.

### Inferences
- What would count as evidence: people high on literacy or CMC competence (Spitzberg) doing no better than others in a documented triad after exposure is controlled. The manuscript names no such test; the Conclusion's third question ("Is algorithmacy different in practice from general digital skill…?") admits it is open while the Discussion states it as settled.
- "Oracy" is a 1965 coinage for an ancient ability; the sequence oracy→literacy→algorithmacy is a sequence of names, and the manuscript treats it as a sequence of abilities arriving in history. If the court case in §3 holds, the ability the manuscript names is also ancient, and the historical register fails.

### Gaps
- No source consulted on whether literacy skills transfer to CMC competence; the claim remains untested here as well.

## 6. The dyad dilemma: real or a strawman?

### Takeaway
The dilemma is overstated. At least three accounts the manuscript cites already keep both the other person and the system's power (Sutherland et al.'s gig literacies, Rahman's split locus of evaluation, Gibbs et al.'s actor–artifact–institution triad), and one of them could be extended without a new construct.

### Cited Findings
- The dilemma: "an account which keeps the other person in view treats the system as a channel … that decides nothing, while an account which treats the system as hidden and powerful reduces the other person to a source of ratings" (Introduction ¶3). — MS.
- Sutherland et al. are placed on the second horn, with the evidence that workers reach the person "by leaving the system" (The Triadic View ¶5). — MS. In Lima, gig literacies include "relationship-building" with clients as a sub-dimension (Lima, line 177). A construct with a client-relationship dimension does not reduce the client to a source of ratings; the "leaving the system" finding is about where the relationship is built, not about the construct's reach.
- Rahman's cage includes "an indeterminate locus of evaluative authority divided between the platform and the individual client" (Lima, line 125, quoting Rahman p. 976). — Lima. The MS cites Rahman for the second horn and, in the Conclusion, for the very attribution task (system or person?) that the dilemma says such accounts cannot see.
- Gibbs et al. (2021) "treats the person and the machine as an isolated pair when the machine also carries the rules of whoever deployed it" (The Triadic View ¶3) — the MS cites them as critics; Lima records that "their triad links actor, artifact, and institution" (Lima, line 71). A three-party account inside the first literature.
- Hancock et al.'s definition, "operates on behalf of a communicator," was in Lima the axis of difference ("their agent optimizes for a designated communicator's goals, the intermediary here for institutional metrics external to both," Lima line 79). — Lima. The MS keeps the quotation and drops the argument.

### Inferences
- Which reviewed account could be extended: Hancock's AI-mediated communication with one parameter changed (the optimization goal from a communicator's to an external one) yields the triad. Spitzberg's competence model could take "evaluative intermediary" as a context variable. Zhou et al.'s "Remediating" dimension plus Sutherland's "relationship-building" together cover both horns. The manuscript must say why these extensions fail; as written it says only that each account "turns the relation of three into a relation of two" (The Triadic View ¶2).
- The dilemma is a fair description of the two purest programmes (Guzman's HMC; Long and Magerko's AI literacy; Walther's SIP) and a strawman of the platform-work studies, which are the nearest rivals.

### Gaps
- I did not re-read Rahman (2021) or Sutherland et al. (2020); the claims about their contents rest on the Lima version's audited summaries.

## 7. Level of analysis and construct status

### Takeaway
Algorithmacy is an individual practical ability acquired by doing, but the manuscript never says so in one place, contradicts itself on whether it can be taught, never confronts sensemaking, and makes its one growth claim close to tautological.

### Cited Findings
- Individual level: "a person's algorithmacy grows through repeated turns of the cycle" (Three Features ¶1); "people who have had more turns at it hold more of the ability than others in the same formal position" (Introduction ¶6). — MS.
- Not teachable: "not as a procedure that can be written down and taught … instruction cannot replace this learning" (Practising ¶1). Teachable: keeping track "is learned by taking part and is sometimes taught" (Practising ¶4). — MS. Direct contradiction within one section.
- Non sequitur: "Because the operator does not disclose the rule, instruction cannot replace this learning, which is why scales that test what a course taught about AI do not measure it (Laupichler et al., 2023)" (Practising ¶1). — MS. That an AI-literacy scale does not measure algorithmacy follows from the constructs differing, not from non-disclosure.
- Sensemaking is absent. The manuscript builds interpreting on equivocality (Daft & Lengel) and on attributing a result to "the rule, in the people, or in both" (Three Features ¶2), which is the sensemaking problem under a new label; neither Weick nor "sensemaking" is cited. — MS, References.
- Political skill, impression management, and audience design (addressing two readers at once is "audience design" in Clark's sense) are absent; Clark and Brennan are cited only for grounding. — MS.
- Discriminant claim, asserted: "it is, because neither of those involves reading and addressing two parties through one hidden rule" (Conclusion ¶2). — MS.
- Suddaby requires, beyond a definition, scope conditions, semantic relations to neighbours, and coherence (Suddaby 2010, p. 347, 349–351). Lima had an explicit scope paragraph and a nomological network with four predicted correlations (Lima, lines 155, 177); the MS has neither.

### Inferences
- Falsifiability. "More turns, more ability" is unfalsifiable as stated unless the ability is measured independently of turns; the Lima version made it falsifiable through a predicted null with Zhou's "Embracing" and an HTMT threshold. The MS retains the claim and drops every instrument.
- The construct is distinguishable in principle from AI literacy (knowledge about systems) and from CMC competence (two readers who reply). It is not yet distinguished from sensemaking under algorithmic evaluation, which is the rival a theory-journal reviewer will raise first.

### Gaps
- Whetten (1989)'s "what/how/why/who-where-when" could not be verified in full text; from memory the "why" (the causal story) is the test the derivation in §4 fails.

## 8. The four cases: do they have all three features and interdependent work?

### Takeaway
Only the marketplace case is a documented platform triad, and it was the one the author's ledger flags as "close to" the cases ruled out. The manager/GenAI case is from a conceptual paper and bindingness on the manager is not shown; the hotel case has a crowd, not a counterpart, and no binding on guests; the planning simulation neither evaluates nor binds the two people. The manuscript's empirical support for the three parts comes almost wholly from settings it classifies as dyads.

### Cited Findings
- The four: manager/employee under GenAI (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024); buyer/seller (Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024); hotel/guests (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023); planners/stakeholders (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). — MS, Practising ¶1.
- Hinds and von Krogh is a theory piece ("expect generative AI to," "likely to evolve," MS Practising ¶5 and Three Features ¶4). Opacity is quoted; adaptivity is quoted as expectation; bindingness on the manager is not stated anywhere. The manager "enters an assessment of an employee into a system that will fold it into an evaluation" (Practising ¶3): the manager is a co-evaluator, and by Hancock's definition the system "operates on behalf of" her, which is the manuscript's own moderated dyad. — MS.
- Marketplace: "a seller whose standing drops holds one buyer's rating, processed by a rule she cannot see" (Three Features ¶2). Binding on the buyer is not shown; interdependent "work" is a transaction. LEDGER item 8: "Marketplace sellers (S-126) as a case: not drivers or freelancers, and close to them." — MS; LEDGER.
- Hotel: "a hotelier can read what her guests wrote and cannot read how the platform weighed it" (Practising ¶2). The counterpart is anonymous guests in aggregate; the ranking binds the hotel and not the guest; there is no interdependent task. — MS.
- Planning: the simulation "in one organization became 'the primary arbiter of planning decisions'" (The Form of the Relation ¶7). The planners chose the model, chose how to present it ("called the model a tool," Practising ¶3), and stakeholders sued (seventeen lawsuit mentions). A party the two people can call a tool and litigate against does not bind them; the model forecasts, it does not evaluate either person. — MS.

## 12. Ranked list of findings by damage to the central claim

### Takeaway
Three findings reach the thesis; four weaken its support; the rest are repairable inconsistencies.

### Cited Findings (wrong vs unargued)
Reaches the thesis:
1. WRONG as a claim of novelty: the construct as defined is not specific to algorithms (court, referee, auction, rating agency); the Conclusion concedes peer review.
