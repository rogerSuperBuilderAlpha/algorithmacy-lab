# Reviewer 2 — *Organization Theory*, Theory Article

**Manuscript:** "Algorithmacy: Working With Another Person Through a Third That Judges Both" (REVISION_2026-10-09.md)
**Reviewer stance:** construct-clarity hawk (Suddaby, 2010; Podsakoff et al., 2016). I reviewed the manuscript cold, read it whole including the reference list, and checked every quotation I lean on below against the source before using it. Where I propose a prior-art rival I give a DOI I resolved through Crossref or OpenAlex on 9 October 2026. Where I could not verify something I say so.

---

## Verdict

**Reject.** The construct as defined does not pick out a new ability. Where the definition is distinctive (an intermediary that binds *both* parties by a rule fed by their own dealings), the paper has no evidence that any person has, lacks, or varies in the ability. Where the paper has evidence (lopsided triads: Rahman, Curchod et al., Bucher et al., Cameron), the definition is not met and the construct collapses into Jarrahi and Sutherland's algorithmic competency, which the paper itself calls "the closest." Each of the three parts has a named antecedent (Kelley's covariation attribution; the multiple-audience problem and Clark's audience design; DeVito's adaptive folk theorization), and the paper offers no argument that the bundle is more than their sum. The mapping of the three parts to Okhuysen and Bechky's three integrating conditions rests on a reading of "accountability" that the source does not support.

**The single most important fix**, if the author resubmits as a new paper: define the ability by what the person does rather than by the situation she is in, and then show, against Kelley, Fleming and Darley, Clark, and DeVito, that the three parts form one ability rather than three borrowed ones. If that cannot be shown, drop the ability claim and write the paper that is actually here, which is a Simmelian re-description of the two-sided reputation triad as an arbitration nobody agreed to. That paper would be shorter, more defensible, and still publishable.

The author's standing rules, as I understand them, are plain statement, no filler, claims carrying scope conditions rather than modal padding, and no drivers or freelancers as illustrative examples. First person at 6.4 per 1,000 words, which sits in the range solo-authored exemplars in this genre run.

**Bar I hold it to.** The house style in `~/.claude/writing-style.md`, with the piece's own plainness winning where the two differ. I will not flag the quoted material or the bracketed AUTHOR flags. The prose is clean, and Part 2 is correspondingly short.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and argument structure

### 1.1 Abstract and title: the definition promises more than the paper delivers

The abstract commits the paper to a strong definitional claim: "Algorithmacy is the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both by a rule that is hidden, cannot be questioned, changes without notice, and takes their dealings with each other as its evidence." The title repeats it: "a Third That Judges Both." The paper then concedes, in the applicability section, that "An intermediary that binds both parties equally is less common in the published record than one that binds a single side. The usual case is the lopsided triad." A theory article whose defining feature holds in the minority of its own cases has a problem the abstract does not disclose. I return to this at 1.9.

The abstract also says "no account names the ability it asks of them or defines that ability over all three parties." The second half is defensible as a claim about definitions; the first half ("no account names the ability") is defeated by the paper's own text, which names Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019), Sutherland et al. (2020), Zhou et al. (2025), Hong et al. (2026), Klawitter and Hargittai (2018, not cited; see 1.12), and Meng (2026) as naming abilities in the same arrangement. What remains is "defines it over all three parties," and that is a claim about the *definition's scope*, not about a new ability.

### 1.2 Introduction: the two-pictures framing is a false dichotomy, and the gap it opens is narrower than stated

The Introduction sorts prior work into two pictures: "In the first, the system is what the person deals with... In the second picture, the system is a channel between two people." It then says "Each picture drops something the arrangements above contain. The first has no second person. The second has no system that decides." This is tidy, and it sets up the triad as the only thing that could fill the gap. But the paragraph that follows undercuts it: "Several accounts come closer than either," and then the author lists three that contain all three parties, including the one he calls "The closest."

I checked Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) against the authors' deposited copy. The definition is quoted accurately and sits on the page numbered 9 of that copy (in LNCS pagination that should be p. 586; the author's own flag covers this). Their Figure 1 does have exactly two nodes, "Gig workers" and "Platform algorithms," as the manuscript says. But the sentence that introduces the figure reads: "As shown in Fig. 1, algorithms shape workers and clients interactions on the platform, but are simultaneously shaped by worker activities." The client is in their sentence if not in their figure. And their data contain the paper's three parts already: Participant 3 "discovered that clients could leave hidden feedback after he had a bad interaction with a client and noticed that his job success score dropped afterwards" (that is interpreting: attributing a result to the counterpart's hidden act through the rule), and workers made client accounts "in order to" see the platform "from the client side" (that is reconstructing the counterpart's view, the prerequisite of specifying intent). So the manuscript's sharpest sentence, "The observations in this literature are triadic and the constructs are dyadic," is right as a description and wrong as a diagnosis. The remedy it implies is to add a node to a figure. That is a redrawing, not a new ability.

The claim "This paper asks a narrower question: what must a person be able to do to work with another person through a system of this kind?" is a good question. The paper's answer, as I argue below, is: attribute (Kelley), design for two audiences (Clark; Fleming and Darley), and revise a folk theory when the system changes (DeVito). Each of those is an ability already named. The paper's contribution is to say they co-occur under one arrangement. That is a scope claim about three constructs, not the discovery of a fourth.

### 1.3 The novelty-search statement is not adequate, and the term is findable

The paper reports: "I searched Consensus, OpenAlex, Crossref, and the 2022–2026 contents of *Administrative Science Quarterly*, *Organization Science*, *Organization Studies*, and *Organization Theory*, and found no work that names this ability or defines it in this way."

Four problems. (a) No search terms are reported, so the statement cannot be reproduced. (b) "Defines it in this way" is unfalsifiable; any two definitions differ in some way. The relevant test is whether a prior construct *covers the same cases and predicts the same differences*, and that test is never run. (c) The venue list omits the journals where the nearest rivals live: *New Media & Society* (Bishop 2019; Marwick & boyd 2011; Meng 2026, which the paper cites), *Information, Communication & Society* (Gillespie 2016; Hargittai et al. 2020, cited), *Social Studies of Science* (Ziewitz 2019), *International Journal of Communication* (Klawitter & Hargittai 2018; Rosenblat & Stark 2016), the CSCW and CHI proceedings (Eslami et al. 2015; DeVito 2021, cited), *Social Cognition* and *JPSP* (Fleming & Darley 1991; Fleming et al. 1990), and *Language* (Clark & Carlson 1982). Searching organization journals for a communication-and-attribution construct and reporting a null is not a gap search. (d) I ran the term through Crossref and OpenAlex myself (9 October 2026): Crossref returns nothing for "algorithmacy"; OpenAlex returns one irrelevant 1995 record. A web search, by contrast, returns a Substack series, a talk PDF, and a conference call for papers, all by one named author, all using the term in the sense the manuscript gives it. Two consequences. The statement "found no work that names this ability" is true only of the peer-reviewed record and should say so. And for a double-anonymized submission the term is a fingerprint; the author's own bracketed flag acknowledges this, and the editor will need to decide whether the paper can go to blind review at all under this title.

### 1.4 Simmel: the borrowing cuts against the paper

The Simmel section is the best-written part of the manuscript and the author's reading of the arbitrator/mediator line is sound. I checked the card for the AJS articles and Wolff's translation; the distinction between the third who reconciles and the third who decides, and the point that the second "requires a prior submission by both," is there. The author's twist, "An algorithmic system that scores two people decides as an arbitrator does, but they did not choose it, and their confidence in it was never asked for," is the paper's one genuinely sharp theoretical sentence.

But the author then admits the platform is not an arbitrator: "A platform that decides between two parties and profits from what they do combines positions that Simmel kept apart." And in the Research Agenda: "a relation in which only one party is bound may be closer to Simmel's third position, in which the third divides in order to rule, than to arbitration." If the usual case (lopsided) is *divide et impera*, then the two parties are not "working together through" a third; they are being set against each other by it. Curchod et al. (2020), which the paper cites, say exactly that: buyers and the platform "in effect allied against the seller." Under that reading the ability the bound party needs is not the ability to coordinate with a counterpart through a judge; it is the ability to survive an adversary who has recruited the counterpart. Those are different abilities, and the paper's definition ("interdependent work with another person") describes the first while its evidence describes the second. The paper's own closing admission that it does not know which Simmelian third the platform is means it has not settled the foundation on which the definition rests. A theory article may leave empirical questions open. It may not leave open which of two incompatible structures it is theorizing.

A second Simmelian problem. The paper demotes the human relay to a "conduit": "the relay is a person who can be asked what was said, and the relay holds no verdict over the people at either end. The relay is what Obstfeld et al. (2014) call a conduit." But Obstfeld et al. list three brokerage orientations, and the *tertius gaudens* broker, Simmel's own paradigm case, exploits the separation of the two and profits from it. Burt's broker holds no formal verdict and binds nobody by rule, yet controls access and, in practice, decides who gets what. The sorting criterion "does it evaluate them and bind them?" excludes the gatekeeping broker only by reading "bind" as "formal verdict." The paper does not defend that reading, and it needs to, because the chain is then not a dyad resting on oracy but a Simmelian triad that the paper has filed under the wrong heading. The three-rung ladder (oracy, literacy, algorithmacy) depends on the chain being a dyad.

### 1.5 The three forms: the ladder is category-crossing, and the analogy that licenses the name fails

"On this sorting, oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads in which the third evaluates and binds both."

Oracy and literacy are named for a *medium*: speech, letters. Algorithmacy as defined is named for a *role structure*: a third that evaluates and binds. The first two rungs sort by what passes between the parties; the third sorts by who holds the verdict. The author half-sees this ("I sort by what the thing in the middle does to the two people") but does not see that it breaks the analogy the name depends on. The paper's own defense of the name makes this explicit and, I think, fatal:

> "I name the ability for algorithmic systems for the reason that literacy is named for letters though it applies to any text: that is where the form has become general."

Literacy without letters is not literacy; letters are constitutive of the ability. Algorithmacy without an algorithm is, by the paper's own account, still algorithmacy: "A blind review with a hidden scoring scheme has most of the features, and I expect an author in that position to need the same ability," and "Algorithmacy is therefore not confined to software, and I do not define it by the technology." So the algorithm is not constitutive of the ability; it is the current prevalent instance. The literacy analogy therefore shows the opposite of what the author wants. The honest name for what is defined is something like "the ability to work under opaque third-party adjudication of one's own dealings," and the honest claim is about prevalence ("algorithmic systems have made a demanding version of that triad an ordinary condition of work"), which the paper also makes. A prevalence claim does not warrant a new "-acy."

The appraisal-form passage is good as far as it goes: "An appraisal form presents demands, but the manager fills it in, signs it, can be asked why, and answers for the rating. The form shapes the judgment and a person makes it. Replace the form with a system that computes the rating from what both of them entered, and the verdict has moved to a third." But run the manager case one step further (see test case 7 below). A manager applying a forced-distribution calibration decided by a committee the employee never meets, on a rubric HR will not disclose, revised yearly, using peer feedback the employee and her coworker gave about each other: the manager "can be asked why" and will answer "calibration." That is the paper's four features without a line of code. The line "who holds the verdict" does not separate the form from the system; it separates a manager with discretion from a manager without it, and the second kind long predates algorithms.

### 1.6 Test cases run against the definition

The definition (from the Algorithmacy section): "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both by a rule that is hidden, unanswerable, and changing, and that takes their dealings with each other as its evidence." I ran the eight cases the editor asked for. Two kinds of failure matter: a case that meets the definition but plainly needs no new ability, and a case that needs the ability but fails the definition.

**1. A court.** The paper says a court removes the four features "by design." Three of the four, agreed: the rule is published, reasons are required, appeal exists. But the fourth feature is mis-stated. "A court hears each party in a forum set apart from their dealings. A platform scores the reviews and ratings that are those dealings." In contract, family, and employment litigation the *evidence is precisely the parties' dealings with each other*: the emails, the performance, the conduct. What differs is the *forum* (set apart, after the fact) and the *timing* (the dealings were not conducted under contemporaneous scoring). The feature the author needs is not "dealings as evidence" but "dealings scored as they happen, by a scorer the parties know is watching," which is reactivity (Espeland & Sauder, 2007), a construct the paper cites and then sets aside as "not say[ing] what they are able to do." Also, platform reviews are not "the dealings"; they are each party's *testimony about* the other after the transaction, which is exactly what a court hears. The fourth feature, as written, does not distinguish the platform from the court.

**2. An arbitrator.** Commercial and securities arbitration routinely issues *unreasoned* awards (FINRA awards historically carried no reasons unless both parties requested them; AAA commercial rules make reasons optional). There is no appeal on the merits. The arbitrator's private weighting of the evidence is hidden. The evidence is the parties' dealings. Only "changing" fails, and the paper's own scope condition then says: "Where the rule is stable, keeping track is finished once the rule is learned." So arbitration without reasons calls, on the paper's own terms, for two of the three parts. Nobody has proposed an "-acy" for parties to arbitration. The paper's reply is that "societies made a profession of it" and the litigant has counsel. That reply concedes the point: what distinguishes the platform participant is that *no counsel is supplied*, which is an institutional fact about the arrangement, not a property of an ability. An ability does not become new because the people who need it are now unrepresented.

**3. Blind peer review.** The author's own example: "A blind review with a hidden scoring scheme has most of the features, and I expect an author in that position to need the same ability." But with whom does the author do "interdependent work"? Not the reviewer; they do not work together and the reviewer is not bound by the editor's verdict on the manuscript. The author's own example fails the first clause of the author's own definition. That clause ("interdependent work with another person") is therefore doing no work in the author's hands, and the construct is really about *being judged by an opaque third on a record one co-produced*, counterpart or no counterpart.

**4. An auction / ad auction.** Google's ad auction binds advertiser and (via placement) the user; the Quality Score rule is partially hidden, unanswerable in practice, changes without notice, and takes the advertiser's dealings with users (click-through) as evidence. Interdependent work with "another person" is weak here, which the definition's first clause would exclude. Yet the practical craft of writing one text for a reader and a ranking algorithm at once, and of diagnosing whether a traffic drop came from an algorithm update or from readers, is the SEO trade, twenty years old, and it has an academic literature: Dick (2011) on SEO in UK newsrooms (doi:10.1080/17512786.2010.551020), Gillespie (2016) on being "algorithmically recognizable" (doi:10.1080/1369118x.2016.1199721), Ziewitz (2019) on the ethical work of optimization in web search (doi:10.1177/0306312719865607), and Petre, Duffy and Hund (2019) on platforms policing the line between optimization and "gaming" (doi:10.1177/2056305119879995). None is cited. All describe parts 2 and 3 of the ability.

**5. A credit-rating agency / credit score.** Borrower and lender; a bureau's score that is hidden, unanswerable (adverse-action "key factors" aside), changing, and built from the borrower's dealings with lenders; the lender bound by regulation and by its own models to use it. This satisfies the definition more fully than any case in the paper, and Fourcade and Healy (2013, doi:10.1016/j.aos.2013.11.002; 2017, doi:10.1093/ser/mww033) and Kiviat (2019, doi:10.1177/0003122419884917) describe the scored party's position in nearly the paper's words: a credit identity as "a dynamic project to be managed" against a standard the party chases but does not set, scored "in a manner opaque to them." Nobody in that literature proposes a new ability; the person's side is "financial literacy," and a credit-repair industry supplies the counsel. The definition includes this case and the paper does not mention it.

**6. A school exam board.** Published syllabus and mark scheme, appeal by re-mark, changes with notice: the features mostly fail, as the paper would predict. But the *ability* the paper calls "specifying intent" (write for the counterpart's understanding and for the hidden rubric at once) is "teaching to the test," a century old and the canonical case of Campbell's law. The paper says "two of the three have been named before" and credits a 2026 CHI paper on dating apps. The naming is older than the author allows by about ninety years.

**7. A human manager applying a secret rubric.** Covered at 1.5. Forced-distribution calibration with peer input satisfies all four features with no software. The author would say "I do not define it by the technology," and that is the problem: a construct named for algorithms that is satisfied by a 1990s calibration committee is misnamed, and the ability needed in that committee's shadow has been studied for decades as impression management toward multiple audiences and as organizational politics.

**8. A two-sided marketplace (Airbnb-style).** Fits the definition. But the two studies the paper cites for it show something awkward. Bolton et al. (2013) and Fradkin et al. (2021) show that a *one-line change in the operator's posting rule* (simultaneous reveal) removes the distortion that the paper says costs the parties their common understanding. If a design parameter the operator controls removes the need for the ability, then the "ability" is a workaround for a design defect, not a competence the form requires. Literacy does not disappear when a text is well written. The paper's own "Three Courses" section concedes this ("It can disclose the rule and give reasons, which should reduce the need for interpreting and for keeping track") without noticing that the concession dissolves the construct's claim to be a standing ability of the form.

**Summary of the test.** Cases 2, 5, and 7 satisfy the definition and are handled in their literatures without a new ability. Case 3, the author's own, needs the ability (on the author's say-so) and fails the definition. Case 1 shows the fourth feature is mis-stated. Case 8 shows the operator can switch the need off. By Suddaby's (2010) standard, which the paper invokes ("a single clear exception counts heavily against one"; the source says "Finding a single exception is often fatal to a construct," p. 349, and I checked), the construct does not survive its own test.

### 1.7 Is algorithmacy distinguishable from sensemaking plus audience design plus learning?

No, not as argued. The paper itself supplies the reduction.

**Part 1, Interpreting.** "Interpreting is working out whether a result came from the rule, from the counterpart, or from both... A participant interprets by setting the result beside what the counterpart visibly did, and by comparing across counterparts and across time." That is Kelley's covariation model of causal attribution (Kelley, 1973, doi:10.1037/h0034225): consistency over time, distinctiveness across entities, consensus across persons. The paper describes the model in its own words without the name. The paper also says, of the participant's position, "Weick et al. (2005) would call her situation one for sensemaking," and the only thing it adds is "a specific source of equivocality." A specific source of equivocality is a scope condition on sensemaking, not a new ability. The empirical antecedent is also older and closer than the paper allows: Eslami et al. (2015, doi:10.1145/2702123.2702556) found Facebook users blaming friends for stories the feed algorithm had hidden, which is the paper's "attributing every result to the counterpart" failure mode, documented in 2015 and not cited. Rosenblat and Stark (2016) report the mirror image from the counterpart's seat: passengers "cannot rate the Uber system separately from the driver," so blame for the system lands on the person. Not cited.

**Part 2, Specifying intent.** "Specifying intent is making one's meaning reach the counterpart and the intermediary at once... The participant addresses two readers who want different things." This is the *multiple audience problem* (Fleming & Darley, 1991, doi:10.1521/soco.1991.9.1.25; Fleming, Darley, Hilton & Kojetin, 1990, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.58.4.593), a named construct with thirty-five years of experiments on how speakers craft one message for audiences with different knowledge and different stakes. It is also Clark's audience design (Clark & Carlson, 1982, doi:10.2307/414102; Bell, 1984, doi:10.1017/s004740450001037x), in which a speaker designs an utterance for addressees *and* for side-participants and overhearers; the algorithmic intermediary is an overhearer whose uptake binds. And on platforms specifically it is context collapse and the imagined audience (Marwick & boyd, 2011, doi:10.1177/1461444810365313), which the paper reaches only through Litt (2012), a review of that very literature. The paper cites Clark and Brennan (1991) on grounding but not Clark and Carlson (1982) on overhearers, which is the paper that already contains the paper's second part.

**Part 3, Keeping track.** The author concedes this one: "Research on social media has described much of this," and "Meng's (2026) algorithmic adaptability is keeping track seen from another [setting]." DeVito (2021) is the direct antecedent and is cited. What the author leaves "open," whether "telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart is a fourth part," Bishop (2019, doi:10.1177/1461444819854731) documented in 2019 as a collective practice: vloggers "take the temperature" in closed groups ("are you having a terrible week too?") precisely to diagnose whether a visibility drop is personal or platform-wide. Not cited.

The paper's answer to the reduction, in "How the Parts Fit Together," is that the parts depend on one another: "Without specifying intent, interpreting is a private diagnosis that changes nothing. Without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting. Without keeping track, the other two run on an understanding that has expired." These are assertions, and they are true of *any* bundle of perception, action, and updating; the same three sentences hold for a chess player, a salesman, or a litigant. Mutual dependence of a general kind does not make a bundle a construct. The construct-clarity test is whether the bundle predicts something the parts do not. The paper does not propose such a prediction.

**Versus algorithmic competency, gig literacies, folk theorization, reactivity.** The Discussion sorts rivals on "how many parties the construct covers, and whether it describes an ability or a response." On that table algorithmic competency is an ability over two parties and algorithmacy an ability over three. But in the lopsided case the paper itself collapses the third party into evidence: "the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence." That is exactly how Jarrahi and Sutherland, Cameron and Rahman (2022), and Zhou et al. treat the client. So in the case where the evidence is, algorithmacy and algorithmic competency cover the same two effective parties and the same practices (sensemaking, circumventing, manipulating). Gig literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020), which the paper says "come nearest," already include "building relationships (as the foundation of longer-term engagements with clients)" (I checked; p. 471 of the article as paginated). The paper never says what algorithmacy adds to gig literacies beyond the word "triad."

### 1.8 The three-parts-to-three-conditions mapping is asserted, and the first row misreads its source

"Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) define coordination as 'the process of interaction that integrates a collective set of interdependent tasks' (p. 463) and identify three integrating conditions: accountability, predictability, and common understanding. Each feature of the intermediary undoes one. Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all."

I checked Okhuysen and Bechky. The p. 463 pinpoint is correct for the definition of coordination. But their *accountability* is not answerability. It "addresses the question of who is responsible for specific elements of the task," and they are explicit that it can be achieved "through informal and emergent action as well as by formal means," by the coordinating parties themselves, in meetings, status reports, and display practices. It is responsibility-allocation among the parties, not a judge giving reasons. The manuscript's sentence imports the Bovens-style forum sense (a party that must give an account) and attaches it to a source that defines the term otherwise. This is a conceptual substitution at the hinge of Table 1. If accountability in O&B's sense is responsibility-allocation, then a platform that scores both parties does not remove it; it *supplies* it, brutally and without appeal, by telling each party exactly what she is answerable for. And "interpreting" does not recover responsibility-allocation; it recovers something closer to O&B's predictability or common understanding. The first row of Table 1 is wrong as attributed, and once it moves the other two rows have no reason to stay where they are.

Two further problems with the mapping. First, the paper has four features and three parts and patches the surplus: "The hidden rule is a condition of all three parts and does not distinguish among them." But "unanswerable" is a feature that plainly calls for its own practice, and the paper's own evidence shows it: the Zhou et al. (2025) item quoted for unanswerability reads "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)," which is a *measured competency at appeal*. A validated instrument already includes a part the three-part structure omits. Second, Okhuysen and Bechky themselves say the three conditions interact and substitute for one another, so that a strict separation is "somewhat artificial." A one-to-one table built on conditions whose own authors say they substitute needs a sentence on why the substitution does not run here. There is none.

### 1.9 The scope conditions restate the definition; the "lopsided triad" clause is an escape hatch

"Because the features vary independently, the need for the ability varies with them, and the account can be wrong. Where the rule is published and results come with reasons, I expect interpreting to reduce to ordinary reading. Where the intermediary does not read the two people's dealings, specifying intent reduces to ordinary writing. Where the rule is stable, keeping track is finished once the rule is learned."

Each of these is the definition's corresponding feature negated. They are not predictions about when the ability is needed; they are statements that the ability is defined over situations having the features. Nothing observable could refute "where the rule is hidden, people need to work out what it did," because the need follows from the words. The one claim in the paper that could be wrong is empirical and untested: "I therefore expect people in the same formal position to differ in algorithmacy... This is an expectation. No one has measured it." That is the paper's only falsifiable content, and it is deferred.

The lopsided clause is the escape hatch: "Where the intermediary binds only one of the two, the triad is lopsided: the bound person needs the ability, and the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence." This lets every one-sided system, which the paper admits is "the usual case," count as an instance. But once the counterpart is "part of the intermediary's evidence," the arrangement is, from the participant's seat, a dyad with a machine that reads her dealings, which is the first picture the Introduction said drops the second person. The scope condition that saves the evidence base returns the construct to the picture it was built to escape.

### 1.10 Does any evidence come from a triad of the defined kind?

No. I went through every empirical citation.

- **Both-bound triads (definition met):** Fradkin et al. (2021), Airbnb, two-sided review; the paper concedes it "reports nothing about what hosts or guests are able to do." Bolton et al. (2013), lab and eBay feedback; reports distortion and its cure by design, nothing about an ability. So: two studies, zero evidence on the ability.
- **Lopsided triads (definition not met):** Rahman (2021), Curchod et al. (2020), Bucher et al. (2021), Cameron (2022, 2024), Cameron and Rahman (2022), Bellesia et al. (2023), Möhlmann et al. (2021), Rahman and Valentine (2021), Jiang and Sinchaisri (2025), Lee et al. (2015), Yao et al. (2021), Heiland (2025), Orlikowski and Scott (2014, 2023), Karunakaran et al. (2022). All the behavior the paper organizes comes from here, and here the construct is coextensive with algorithmic competency.
- **Dyads with a machine:** Sundar and Nass (2000), Sundar and Kim (2019), Hohenstein and Jung (2020), Lebovitz et al. (2022), Tong et al. (2021), Lin et al. (2026). The paper concedes "it shows it in dyads and not in triads."
- **Social-media audiences (no counterpart):** Bucher (2017), Cotter (2019), DeVito et al. (2018), DeVito (2021), Meng (2026). The counterpart is a diffuse audience, not a party with whom one does interdependent work.
- **Non-qualifying by the paper's own ruling:** Leonardi and Leavell (2026), Waardenburg et al. (2022), Christin (2017).
- **Thought experiments:** Hinds and von Krogh (2024), and the author's own two-employee case.

The evidence base therefore has the shape of a pincer. Where the definition holds, nothing bears on the ability. Where something bears on the ability, the definition does not hold. A theory article may argue without data, but it may not cite data for a claim the data do not reach, and that is what every sentence of the form "Rahman (2021) found... I expect the same tendency in a triad" does.

### 1.11 The invented two-employee case

"To illustrate with an invented case: an employee revises her self-assessment with a language model until an automated scorer rates it highly, and it now tells her manager nothing he can use. Another employee writes her manager a candid note about a problem on the team, and the system files it as a complaint against her. The first wrote for the intermediary alone and the second for the counterpart alone."

Invented vignettes are acceptable in this journal when labeled, and this one is labeled. Three problems nonetheless. (a) The Introduction promises "one thought experiment, and I say which is which." There are two: Hinds and von Krogh's, which the paper borrows, and this one. (b) The first employee's case is not a triad under the definition: the manager reads the polished self-assessment and is not bound by the scorer's rating; the paper itself says, of the manager in the generative-AI case, "Whether the manager is bound is doubtful." So the only employee example in the paper fails the definition. (c) The second employee's "failure" is the system's defect: a candid note to one's manager filed as a complaint against its author is a mis-designed system, and calling the note a failure "to specify intent" lands responsibility for the system's error on the person. Two pages later the paper warns, citing Elish (2019), that "Algorithmacy may work as a private repair for a public defect." The vignette enacts the move the paper warns against. If the author keeps the case, the second employee should be the paper's illustration of the Elish risk, not of a failure of ability.

The case also makes a prediction the author should not want: an employee who writes candidly to her manager is *less* algorithmate than one who launders her self-assessment through a language model until a scorer approves it. If that is what the ability selects for, the Discussion's worry is not a "possibility that follows from the account"; it is the account.

### 1.12 Prior-art trap: nearest rivals and whether the novelty claim survives

Ranked by how much each narrows or kills the claim, with identifiers resolved on 9 October 2026.

1. **Kelley (1973), "The processes of causal attribution," *American Psychologist* 28(2): 107–128, doi:10.1037/h0034225.** The covariation model is the paper's "Interpreting" part stated forty years earlier with the names of the three comparisons the paper describes. Kills the claim that part 1 is new; what remains is a scope condition (attribution between a person and an opaque rule).
2. **Fleming & Darley (1991), "Mixed messages: The multiple audience problem and strategic communication," *Social Cognition* 9(1): 25–46, doi:10.1521/soco.1991.9.1.25; Fleming, Darley, Hilton & Kojetin (1990), *JPSP* 58(4): 593–609, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.58.4.593.** The multiple audience problem *is* "specifying intent for two readers at once." Kills the claim that part 2 is new.
3. **Clark & Carlson (1982), "Hearers and speech acts," *Language* 58(2): 332–373, doi:10.2307/414102; Bell (1984), "Language style as audience design," *Language in Society* 13(2): 145–204, doi:10.1017/s004740450001037x.** Audience design with addressees, side-participants, and overhearers. The algorithm is an overhearer whose uptake binds. Narrows part 2 to a special case.
4. **Marwick & boyd (2011), "I tweet honestly, I tweet passionately," *New Media & Society* 13(1): 114–133, doi:10.1177/1461444810365313.** Context collapse and the imagined audience on platforms. The paper reaches this only via Litt (2012).
5. **Eslami et al. (2015), CHI '15, pp. 153–162, doi:10.1145/2702123.2702556.** Users attribute the algorithm's omissions to their friends. Documents the paper's first failure mode of interpreting a decade before the paper names it.
6. **Bishop (2019), "Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip," *New Media & Society* 21(11–12): 2589–2606, doi:10.1177/1461444819854731.** Collective "temperature-taking" to tell a platform change from a personal one. Answers the "fourth part" question the paper leaves open, and shows the ability is social, not individual.
7. **Klawitter & Hargittai (2018), "It's like learning a whole other language," *International Journal of Communication* 12: 3490–3510 (IJoC assigns no DOIs).** "Algorithmic skills" among marketplace sellers with real buyers and real income. A seller-buyer-ranker triad studied as a skill in 2018.
8. **Gillespie (2016), doi:10.1080/1369118x.2016.1199721; Ziewitz (2019), doi:10.1177/0306312719865607; Dick (2011), doi:10.1080/17512786.2010.551020; Petre, Duffy & Hund (2019), doi:10.1177/2056305119879995.** The SEO and optimization literature: writing for a reader and a ranker at once, and diagnosing algorithm updates. Parts 2 and 3 as a trade.
9. **Fourcade & Healy (2013, doi:10.1016/j.aos.2013.11.002; 2017, doi:10.1093/ser/mww033); Kiviat (2019, doi:10.1177/0003122419884917).** Credit scoring as the scored party's "dynamic project to be managed" under opacity. A case that satisfies the definition better than any in the paper and is handled without a new ability.
10. **Rosenblat & Stark (2016), *International Journal of Communication* 10: 3758–3784.** The counterpart cannot separate the system from the person: the attribution problem from the other seat.
11. **Stark & Pais (2020), cited.** Their characterization of algorithmic management ("rules but not bureaucratic," unstable and non-negotiable; "triangular geometry"; accountability "deflected") already contains three of the paper's four features and the triad. The paper's features are a restatement of Stark and Pais's form description, which means the paper's novelty cannot lie in the features and must lie in the ability, and the ability is where items 1–3 cut.

**Does the novelty claim survive?** It dies as a claim about a new ability. It narrows to: "the three named abilities (covariation attribution, multiple-audience design, adaptive folk theorization) are jointly demanded of unrepresented persons under opaque third-party scoring of their own dealings, and this arrangement has become common." That narrower claim is true, useful, and not a construct.

### 1.13 Load-bearing weak points, as a hostile referee would attack them, in order

1. **The definition is by situation, not by what the person does.** "The ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that..." defines an ability by the circumstances in which it is exercised. Suddaby asks for the essential properties of the thing. The essential properties here are supplied later as three parts, each of which is a named construct elsewhere. Does not survive as written.
2. **The lopsided admission.** "The usual case is the lopsided triad." The defining feature of the construct ("binds them both") holds in the minority of cases; the evidence is from the majority; and in the majority the construct is coextensive with algorithmic competency. Does not survive.
3. **Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability.** Misattributed sense. Removes the derivation of the first row of Table 1 and with it the claim that the mapping is derived rather than arranged. Repairable only by abandoning the attribution and arguing the mapping on other grounds.
4. **The literacy analogy.** The name is licensed by an analogy that the paper's own "not confined to software" clause breaks. Repairable only by renaming, which the author's public use of the term may make costly.
5. **The novelty statement.** No terms, wrong venues, unfalsifiable wording, and a term findable online under one author's name. Repairable, but the honest version of the statement weakens the paper.

### 1.14 Smaller points, section by section

- *Introduction, last paragraph.* "The parts of the ability are not all new, since two of the three have been named before." The third (interpreting) has been named too (Kelley; Eslami). The honest count is three of three.
- *Simmel section.* "Pyyhtinen (2018) has since asked what becomes of the triad when the third is not human." The paper raises the question and does not answer it; it stipulates that "the intermediary is what stands between them, which is the rule as applied, with its operator behind it." Simmel's third has interests and is aware of the other two; the "rule as applied" has neither. A theory article that borrows Simmel's triad owes a paragraph on why a rule can occupy a member's position.
- *Three Forms.* The Yates inference is flagged as the author's; good. But "organizations first relied on people who could talk, then also on people who could handle text, and are now beginning to rely on people who can work in a triad" is a historical claim with no source and no scope condition; it also ignores numeracy, which is the "-acy" that reading a score, a rank, or a star rating actually requires.
- *What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart.* Katsh and Rifkin's "fourth party" is used for contrast and then dropped; it is the closer antecedent for the whole paper (technology seated in an adjudication) and deserves more than a sentence.
- *Keeping Track.* Rahman's finding that "workers who faced criteria that shifted unpredictably either experimented, at risk to their standing, or cut back their activity" is cited as evidence of a *second* thing that can change. Rahman's workers did not distinguish rule-change from client-change; the paper supplies the distinction and attributes the finding to it.
- *How the Parts Fit Together.* "Jiang and Sinchaisri (2025) found a learning curve across more than a million orders on one delivery platform." A learning curve on a delivery platform is evidence of learning *the algorithm*, in a setting with no meaningful counterpart; it is dyadic evidence used for a triadic claim.
- *Where the Account Applies.* The Leonardi and Leavell figures (83% vs 9%, 17 lawsuits) and quotations check against the article. The paragraph's purpose is unclear until the Three Courses section; move the broker point there and cut the numbers here.
- *Discussion, "The Intermediary as a Party."* The reply to Faraj and Pachidi ("From where she stands...") concedes that the construct is phenomenological: the intermediary is a party *as experienced*. Then "binds" is also only "as experienced," and the definition should say so.
- *Research Agenda.* Krakowski et al. (2023) on chess ("capabilities at playing with a machine were 'unrelated, or even negatively related, to traditional capabilities'") is cited as "an indirect reason" that algorithmacy differs from general skill. Centaur chess is a dyad with a machine and an opponent who is not scored by it. The citation does not bear on the claim.
- *Conclusion.* "The ability has three parts because the intermediary takes three things away." The intermediary has four features and the paper says one of them is "a condition of all three parts." Four features, three removals, three parts: the arithmetic is arranged, not derived.

## Part 2 — AI-slop audit (register-aware)

The prose is plain, agentive, and mostly free of the tells. Numbers for the body (about 7,100 words, excluding references): em-dashes 4 (0.56 per 1,000; low, and in this register deliberately so); agentless "has/have been + participle" 2 ("have been named," "has been tried"); first person 45 ("I"), all marking authorial moves, none carrying a claim; no emphasis markers; no inflation words outside quoted material ("navigate" occurs once, inside the Zhou et al. quotation, which is off-limits). The register is the author's own and I am not going to convert it.

What I do flag:

**Landing-line habit.** Six of forty-nine paragraphs close on a sentence of nine words or fewer, and several more close on an epigram of ten to twelve: "In each case one corner of the triad stands in for the whole." / "The same practice has passed from one form to the other." / "The buyer and the reviewer do not." / "This is an expectation. No one has measured it." / "Installing a system is none of these." Each is good alone. Together they are a drumbeat. The house guidance is to convert a closing epigram to a forward hook naming what the next stretch walks; the author does this well in some places ("The next section says what separates these two cases") and should do it in two or three of the others. See Part 3.

**Performed limitation.** "The claim is limited in three ways. The paper reports no data; its cases come from published studies and from one thought experiment, and I say which is which." The sentence narrates the paper's candor about its method. State the limits; do not announce that you are stating them. See Part 3.

**Recycled formula.** The "two pictures" formula recurs four times: Introduction (twice), "How the Parts Fit Together" ("The two ways of failing at the ability correspond to the two pictures in the Introduction"), and the scope conditions ("the accounts built on the first picture cover it"). The fourth is fine; the third repeats the first. Keep the Introduction's statement and the closing callback; cut the middle one.

**Antithesis machine.** "The form shapes the judgment and a person makes it." / "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (quoted; off-limits). / "Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat." / "which leaves the rule as it is and moves the cost of its opacity onto them." Four contrast-pair closers in the span of two sections. Two can stay.

**One agentless passive worth fixing.** "two of the three have been named before" hides who named them (and, per Part 1, undercounts). Name the namers.

**Nothing else.** No nominalization stacks, no "it is important to note," no "crucially." The sentences are the author's. The problems in this manuscript are in the ideas.

## Part 3 — Line-level revisions (ranked by value)

Each is a replacement in the author's register. None is more nominalized or more passive than the original.

**1. Abstract, the novelty sentence.** *Current:* "Organization theorists have described the arrangement itself as a relation of three, and field studies have recorded what people do inside it, but no account names the ability it asks of them or defines that ability over all three parties."
*Replace with:* "Organization theorists have described the arrangement as a relation of three, and field studies have recorded what people do inside it. The abilities those studies name are defined over the person and the system. None is defined over all three parties."
(Drops the false "no account names the ability"; keeps the defensible scope claim.)

**2. Introduction, limits paragraph.** *Current:* "The claim is limited in three ways. The paper reports no data; its cases come from published studies and from one thought experiment, and I say which is which. The parts of the ability are not all new, since two of the three have been named before; what I add is the definition over the triad and the tie between each part and a feature of the intermediary."
*Replace with:* "The paper reports no data. Its cases come from published studies and from two thought experiments, one borrowed from Hinds and von Krogh (2024) and one my own. Kelley (1973) named the attribution that interpreting requires, Fleming and Darley (1991) named the problem of writing for two audiences, and DeVito (2021) named the revising of a folk theory when a platform changes. What I add is the claim that one arrangement demands all three of a person at once, and the tie between each and a feature of the intermediary."
(States the limits without narrating candor; counts three of three; names the namers.)

**3. The definition.** *Current:* "I define algorithmacy as the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both by a rule that is hidden, unanswerable, and changing, and that takes their dealings with each other as its evidence."
*Replace with:* "I define algorithmacy as three things a person can reliably do when she works with another person under an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both: tell the rule's share in a result from the counterpart's, address the counterpart and the rule in one act, and notice when the rule has moved. The definition holds where the rule is hidden, unanswerable, and changing, and where it takes the two people's dealings with each other as its evidence."
(Defines by what the person does; moves the situation to a stated scope. This is the minimum repair; it does not by itself answer Part 1's reduction.)

**4. The fourth feature.** *Current:* "Fourth, it *takes the two people's dealings with each other as its evidence*. A court hears each party in a forum set apart from their dealings. A platform scores the reviews and ratings that are those dealings."
*Replace with:* "Fourth, it *scores the two people's dealings as they happen*. A court hears the parties' dealings afterward, in a forum set apart from them, and the dealings were not conducted under its eye. A platform scores each transaction as it closes, and both people know it, so what each says to the other is said partly to the scorer."
(Repairs the court comparison; states the feature that actually distinguishes the platform, which is contemporaneous scoring with reactivity.)

**5. Table 1, first row, and the sentence that introduces it.** *Current:* "Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all."
*Replace with:* "Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability is knowing who is responsible for which part of the task, and the two people can build it between themselves. The intermediary does not remove it; it overrides it. Each person is now answerable to a third for a share of the result that neither of them can see, and the two cannot settle between themselves what that share was."
(Uses the source's own sense; states the loss correctly. Note that the recovery column then changes: what interpreting recovers is the two people's *own* allocation of responsibility, which is closer to common understanding than to accountability, and Table 1 must be re-derived.)

**6. The two-employee case, second employee.** *Current:* "Another employee writes her manager a candid note about a problem on the team, and the system files it as a complaint against her. The first wrote for the intermediary alone and the second for the counterpart alone."
*Replace with:* "Another employee writes her manager a candid note about a problem on the team, and the system files it as a complaint against her. The first wrote for the intermediary alone. The second wrote for her manager alone, and the system punished her for it; whether that is her failure or the system's is the question I take up under Elish (2019) below."
(Stops the vignette from enacting the crumple-zone move the paper later warns against.)

**7. "How the Parts Fit Together," closing paragraph.** *Current:* "The two ways of failing at the ability correspond to the two pictures in the Introduction. A participant who treats the triad as a dyad with the system explains every result by the rule and treats the counterpart as noise. A participant who treats it as a moderated dyad reads the intermediary's verdict as the counterpart's opinion. In each case one corner of the triad stands in for the whole."
*Replace with:* "A participant who treats the triad as a dyad with the system explains every result by the rule and treats the counterpart as noise. One who treats it as a moderated dyad reads the intermediary's verdict as the counterpart's opinion. Which arrangements actually put a person in the full triad, and which only look as if they do, is the next question."
(Cuts the recycled "two pictures" callback and the epigram; ends on a hook into "Where the Account Applies.")

**8. "Where the Account Applies," last paragraph.** *Current:* "The upshot is a limit and a prediction. An intermediary that binds both parties equally is less common in the published record than one that binds a single side. The usual case is the lopsided triad, and in it the demand for algorithmacy falls on one side of the relation."
*Replace with:* "An intermediary that binds both parties equally is rarer in the published record than one that binds a single side. In the lopsided case the demand falls on the bound party alone, and there my account and Jarrahi and Sutherland's (2019) cover the same person doing the same things. The two-sided case is where the account says something theirs does not, and it is the case for which there is no evidence about ability. That is the gap the research agenda has to fill first."
(Says the pincer out loud. A hostile referee will say it if the author does not.)

## Findings ranked most-damaging first

1. **Definition by situation; the three parts are three named constructs** (Algorithmacy section; Interpreting; Specifying Intent; Keeping Track). "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that..." / "comparing across counterparts and across time" (= Kelley 1973) / "addresses two readers who want different things" (= Fleming & Darley 1991; Clark & Carlson 1982) / "Research on social media has described much of this" (= DeVito 2021, conceded). **Fatal as written.**
2. **The lopsided admission dissolves the construct's defining feature and makes it coextensive with algorithmic competency where the evidence is** (Where the Account Applies; Scope conditions). "The usual case is the lopsided triad" / "the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence." **Fatal as written.**
3. **Okhuysen and Bechky's "accountability" is used in a sense the source does not hold** (The Participant's Position; Table 1). "Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons." O&B: "who is responsible for specific elements of the task," achievable "through informal and emergent action." **Repairable, but the repair re-derives Table 1.**
4. **The literacy analogy that licenses the name fails on the paper's own "not confined to software" clause** (What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart). "I name the ability for algorithmic systems for the reason that literacy is named for letters though it applies to any text." **Repairable only by renaming.**
5. **No evidence from a both-bound triad bears on the ability; all ability evidence is from triads that fail the definition** (Where the Account Applies; every "I expect the same tendency in a triad"). **Fatal for a paper that cites the evidence it cites; repairable by withdrawing the evidence claims and arguing purely conceptually.**
6. **The fourth feature does not distinguish a platform from a court** (What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart). "A court hears each party in a forum set apart from their dealings." Courts adjudicate on the parties' dealings; the distinguishing feature is contemporaneous scoring. **Repairable (Part 3, item 4).**
7. **The novelty statement is unreproducible, searches the wrong venues, and the term is findable online under one name** (Introduction). **Repairable; the honest version weakens the paper and raises an anonymity question for the editor.**
8. **The invented case enacts the Elish move the paper warns against, and its first employee is not in a triad under the definition** (Specifying Intent). **Repairable (Part 3, item 6).**
9. **Simmel: the paper does not settle whether the third is an arbitrator or a divider, and the definition assumes the first while the evidence describes the second** (Simmel; Research Agenda). **Repairable only by choosing.**
10. **Scope conditions restate the definition's features in the negative; the sole falsifiable claim is deferred** (What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart; How the Parts Fit Together). **Repairable by converting at least one to a prediction with a comparison group.**

## Closing note

**The piece's biggest genuine strength.** The Simmel section, and specifically the sentence "An algorithmic system that scores two people decides as an arbitrator does, but they did not choose it, and their confidence in it was never asked for." That is a real theoretical observation, stated in nine words fewer than most people would need, and it is the seed of a paper I would want to read: not a paper about an ability, but a paper about *arbitration without consent* as a coordination form, with the court comparison in "What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart" (publish the rule, require reasons, allow appeal, supply counsel) as its spine. The author has written that paper's best two pages and then attached them to a construct they do not support. The prose, throughout, is clean, agentive, and his own; I found nothing in it that reads as manufactured.

**The one thing only the author can supply.** A case, from the author's own observation, of a person in a *two-sided* triad (host and guest, buyer and seller on a platform that scores both, two employees under a system that files on both) doing the three things the paper names, and doing them visibly better or worse than a comparable person. The paper's entire evidence base is borrowed from lopsided settings. One worked case from the defining setting, even a single interview or a single documented exchange, would do more for the construct than the forty-odd citations that currently stand in for it. Without it, the claim that "organizations... are relying on an ability that they have neither named nor checked that their people have" is itself unchecked.
