## Verdict line

**Major revisions.** The single most important fix: decide what the triad's defining criterion is. As written, the construct is defined over an intermediary that "evaluates and binds them both," the paper then concedes that the usual case binds one side, and the scope paragraph re-describes the unbound counterpart as "part of the intermediary's evidence," which collapses the lopsided triad into the dyad-with-a-machine the paper set out to replace. The research agenda lists "whether the lopsided triad and the two-sided triad are one form or two" as a question for later. It cannot be. It is the construct's boundary, and a theory article has to draw it.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and argument structure

### 1.1 The contribution, and whether it is large enough for this journal

The paper asks "what must a person be able to do to work with another person through a system of this kind?" and answers with a named ability, a sorting of three forms of working together, three parts of the ability, and a tie from each part to a feature of the intermediary and to one of Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) integrating conditions. That is a construct paper with a form-theoretic wrapper.

The wrapper is where the claim to this journal lives. *Organization Theory* has published the triadic view of platforms (Stark & Vanden Broeck 2024), the relational view of emerging technology (Hinds & von Krogh 2024; Faraj & Pachidi 2021), the biography of an algorithm (Glaser et al. 2021), and the sociomaterial account of valuation (Orlikowski & Scott 2023). All of these are cited, and the manuscript positions itself as filling what they leave open: they describe the arrangement; nobody describes the person's part. That positioning is accurate and the gap is real. I checked it against what I know of the field, and against the paper's nearest rivals (below). The question for the editor is not whether the gap exists but whether the paper fills it with something a reader of this journal can use.

My answer is: partly. The strongest material is the derivation. If each part of the ability follows from a feature of the intermediary and recovers a condition of coordination, then the construct is not a list of things workers were seen doing (the charge the paper rightly lays against Jarrahi and Sutherland's inventory) but a structure. The paper says this is what it adds: "the definition over the triad and the tie between each part and a feature of the intermediary." So the contribution stands or falls on whether the tie holds. In section 1.4 I argue that one of the three ties is mis-specified against the source and a second is loose, which means the thing that makes this a theory contribution rather than a competency inventory is currently the weakest part of the paper. That is fixable, and fixing it is what the revision should be about.

The oracy/literacy/algorithmacy sorting is the paper's most ambitious move and its least developed. The historical sequence rests on an inference from Yates (1989) that the author marks as "mine and not Yates's," and the literacy half of the analogy invites a literature the paper does not touch. The sorting earns its place as a device for locating the construct (two parties and a text that records versus three parties and a third that decides), and it should be kept for that. It should not be allowed to look like a periodization of organizational history, because it cannot carry one.

### 1.2 Introduction

The opening paragraph works. Three arrangements (host–guest, seller–buyer, manager–employee with GenAI), the field's literature named by scholar, and a narrow question. The second paragraph's two pictures (person-and-machine; two-people-and-a-channel) are the paper's best framing move and they recur usefully at the end of "How the Parts Fit Together." The third paragraph lands the gap in one sentence: "The observations in this literature are triadic and the constructs are dyadic." That sentence is earned.

Two problems. First, the fourth paragraph's handling of Kellogg, Valentine and Christin (2020). The paper cites it once, in a bundle, as having shown "how such systems direct, evaluate, and discipline." For a reader from my seat that is the review that defines the field's vocabulary for exactly the features the paper later derives from scratch. Kellogg et al. characterise algorithmic control as comprehensive, instantaneous, interactive and opaque, and they sort mechanisms into six (restricting, recommending, recording, rating, replacing, rewarding) under directing, evaluating and disciplining. The paper's four features (hidden, unanswerable, changing, fed by the two people's dealings) overlap with "opaque," "interactive" and "instantaneous," and its "evaluates" is their "rating." The paper does not have to adopt their vocabulary, but it has to say how its features relate to theirs, because the first reviewer at this journal who works on algorithmic management will ask. The same goes for their catalogue of worker responses (gaming, decoding, solidarity), which is the field's existing answer to "what do people do inside it."

Second, the limitation paragraph says "I searched Consensus, OpenAlex, Crossref, and the 2022–2026 contents of [four journals], and found no work that names this ability or defines it in this way." The `[AUTHOR:]` flag on this sentence must be resolved before submission; a reviewer cannot evaluate an anonymized paper's claim to a term the author has used in public without knowing how the author intends to handle it. I will not rule on that; I note that the sentence as written is a documented gap search, which is the right form.

### 1.3 Simmel on the Third Party

This section is the paper's spine and it is well handled. The mediator/arbitrator line ("who holds the decision") is the right line to borrow, and the observation that a platform decides as an arbitrator without having been chosen, and profits as a *tertius gaudens* while deciding, "combines positions that Simmel kept apart," is a genuine analytic result. I verified the Stark and Vanden Broeck quotation ("The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent") against the published PDF; it is at p. 14 as cited.

Three things are missing, and all three come from inside organization theory rather than from Simmel.

(a) **Caplow.** The paper's "lopsided triad" is, in Curchod et al.'s (2020) description, buyers and the platform "in effect allied against the seller." That is a coalition in a triad, and the theory of coalitions in triads is Caplow's (*Two Against One*, 1968). Simmel gives the paper the positions a third can take; Caplow gives it the question the paper leaves open in its agenda, namely under what distribution of power two members of a triad combine against the third. The lopsided triad is a Caplow coalition between the intermediary and the unbound party. Citing Caplow would let the paper state the lopsided case as a form rather than defer it.

(b) **Burt.** *Tertius gaudens* entered organization theory through Burt (1992), and the paper's claim that the platform profits from the two parties' separation is Burt's structural-hole argument applied to an operator that owns the hole. Obstfeld et al. (2014) are cited, and they descend from Burt; the ancestor should be named.

(c) **Who the party is.** The paragraph that settles on participant / counterpart / intermediary, with the intermediary as "the rule as applied, with its operator behind it," is good and should be kept. But the paper then says, of the employer case, that "one corner of the triad is closer to one party than to the other, and I return to that case below." It returns to it twice (in "Where the Account Applies" and in the agenda) and both times leaves it open. See 1.6.

The verification note for this section: the Simmel page pinpoints (1902b, pp. 167, 172–173, 174) are not in the lab's local sources and I could not check them. I do not assert an error; I report that I could not verify them.

### 1.4 Three Forms of Working Together

The sorting question ("does it evaluate them and bind them?") is clean. The moderated dyad is the clever part: a text between two people "records what was said and leaves open how it was meant," and the reader "cannot question the page," which is why literacy is a pair of abilities. The paragraph on Cooren, Kuhn, and Lehtinen and Pälli pre-empts the obvious CCO objection and answers it with a precise criterion: an appraisal form presents demands, but a manager fills it in and answers for it; "replace the form with a system that computes the rating from what both of them entered, and the verdict has moved to a third." That is the best single paragraph in the paper.

Two weaknesses. First, the "chain" (I tell John and John tells Sally) is in the sorting because Obstfeld et al.'s conduit needs a home, but nothing later uses it; it is a fourth form in a sorting of three and it should either do work or go. Second, the claim that "organizations first relied on people who could talk, then also on people who could handle text, and are now beginning to rely on people who can work in a triad" is where the paper is most exposed to prior art. Zuboff (1988) argued that computer-mediated work turns work into an electronic text and demands "intellective skill" from the people who work through it. That is the nearest rival to the paper's literacy-to-algorithmacy step, and it is not cited. The novelty survives, because Zuboff's intellective skill is the worker's relation to the text and contains no second person, but the paper has to say so, in the same place it says why Jarrahi and Sutherland's competency is dyadic.

### 1.5 What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart

The court comparison is the right comparison and the paper uses it well: a court removes the four features by design (publishes the rule, gives reasons, allows appeal, supplies counsel), which both shows that the ability is real (societies made a profession of it) and shows what organizations do when they do not expect ordinary people to have it. The Katsh and Rifkin "fourth party" is a nice find.

The four features are the paper's load-bearing claim and three of them need more care.

**Hidden and unanswerable.** These are listed as two features but behave as one. The paper says, in "The Participant's Position," that "the hidden rule is a condition of all three parts and does not distinguish among them," yet Table 1 assigns "hidden and unanswerable" jointly to Interpreting. If hidden does not distinguish, the table should not list it as what calls for Interpreting. And the evidence offered for "unanswerable" cuts the other way: the Zhou et al. (2025) item quoted, "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)," is a scale item about a worker's ability to use an appeal function that exists. The paper's gloss, "a complaint goes back into the system that produced the result," is a fair description of what such a function usually does, but the quotation does not show it. Rahman (2021) does show it: his freelancers describe a "lack of recourse," and his "invisible cage" is explicit that "who influences the evaluation ... (i.e., client vs. platform) ... is unspecified" (I verified this passage in the typeset PDF). That is the citation for "unanswerable," and it also supports the paper's own definition of the triad better than the Zhou item does. I would also expect Burrell (2016) here, because "hidden" has three forms in her account (secrecy, technical illiteracy, and the opacity of machine learning itself) and the paper's "hidden" is all three at once without saying so.

**Changes without notice.** Glaser et al. (2021) trace an algorithm changing as it moves across settings, which is a different thing from a rule changing under a participant who stays put. Bailey et al. (2022) and Hinds and von Krogh (2024) say emerging technologies are "always changing" in general. The best evidence for the feature as the paper means it is Rahman (2021), whose freelancers faced "criteria that shifted unpredictably," and the paper cites him for it only in "Keeping Track." Move him up. Faraj, Pachidi and Sayegh (2018) on the learning algorithm is the standard citation for a rule that changes because it learns from the people under it, and it would do double duty for the fourth feature.

**Dealings as evidence.** This is the feature that does the most work in the paper and the one with the least support. The paper states it ("A platform scores the reviews and ratings that are those dealings") and then supports it, in "The Participant's Position," with Bolton et al. (2013) and Fradkin et al. (2021), both of which are about reciprocity in two-sided ratings. Those are good citations for one consequence (what each says about the other is strategic) but they do not establish the feature. Kornberger et al. (2017), cited in the Introduction, establish it directly: on eBay, "with each new user, eBay recruits an additional auditor (in disguise)," the users' evaluating and policing *is* the control. Cameron and Rahman (2022), also cited in the Introduction, show the mirror: workers' dealings with customers are a route to the algorithm. Both belong under this feature. More important, this feature is, I will argue below, the real criterion for the triad, and the paper should consider promoting it.

**The fifth fact** (authority not conferred, operator declines the role) is well sourced. I verified Schoeneborn et al. (2026, p. 13) and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 14; "distributed, deflected, and denied") against the PDFs; both are as cited. The Selznick quotations at pp. 13 and 14 are verbatim (checked against the full text). "Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat" is a good line and it correctly names a mechanism, so it stays.

**The scope paragraph** is where the construct's trouble shows. "Where the intermediary binds only one of the two, the triad is lopsided: the bound person needs the ability, and the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence." Read that against the Introduction's complaint about the first picture: "The first has no second person." If the unbound counterpart becomes "part of the intermediary's evidence," then from the participant's position the arrangement has no second person either; it has a machine and some inputs. The paper's own fourth feature says the two people's dealings are evidence. In the lopsided case the counterpart does not stop being a person she must work with; the buyer still has to be understood, addressed and dealt with. So the sentence misdescribes the paper's own case, and the misdescription matters because the paper then says the lopsided case is "the usual case."

### 1.6 Algorithmacy: definition, position, and the Okhuysen and Bechky mapping

**The definition** is clear and the "ability" gloss (what a person can reliably do in a kind of situation, learned mainly by doing it) is well anchored in Sandberg (2000) and Jablin and Sias (2001). Separating it from a trait, an organizational capability (Stelmaszak et al. 2026, verified at p. 346) and knowledge about a technology (Long & Magerko 2020) is the right triangulation.

**The Participant's Position** is sound. Daft and Lengel's uncertainty/equivocality distinction is exactly right here and the paper uses it precisely: "More data cures the first and does not cure the second." The addition that the triad supplies "a specific source of equivocality ... and the absence of anyone to ask" is the paper's best use of a borrowed construct.

**The mapping to Okhuysen and Bechky** is where I have to be most direct, because it is the move that turns the paper from an inventory into a theory, and one third of it does not match the source.

Okhuysen and Bechky define accountability as the condition that "addresses the question of who is responsible for specific elements of the task"; by creating it, "organization members make clear where the responsibilities of interdependent parties lie," and they stress that it can be achieved "through informal and emergent action as well as by formal means" (their pp. 483–490; I checked the lab's reading card, which was built from the source text, and it carries the same warning the paper has not heeded). Their accountability is the allocation of responsibility among the people doing the work, made visible. It is not answerability to a forum, and it is not the giving of reasons by an evaluator.

The manuscript says: "Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all." That is Bovens's accountability (an actor who owes an account to a forum), not Okhuysen and Bechky's. A reader who opens the source will find assemblers constructing accountability among themselves through dialogue and will ask why the seller and the buyer cannot.

The repair is available and it strengthens the paper. The paper's *Interpreting* is "working out whether a result came from the rule, from the counterpart, or from both." That is responsibility allocation. What the hidden rule removes is precisely the participant's ability to see "where the responsibilities of interdependent parties lie," because one of the parties to the result is a rule she cannot read. Stated that way, the loss is Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability and Interpreting is its partial recovery, exactly as the paper wants. The sentence that has to change is the one that explains the loss by "gives no reasons," which belongs to the "unanswerable" feature, not to this condition.

*Predictability* is the clean match. Okhuysen and Bechky's predictability is the ability to "anticipate subsequent task related activity by knowing what the elements of the task are and when they happen," and a rule that changes without notice removes it. Keep.

*Common understanding* is loose. Their common understanding is a shared conception of the activities and of how each party's part fits the whole. The manuscript says it goes "because the two people's dealings are also evidence, so neither can take what the other says at face value," and supports that with Bolton et al. and Fradkin et al. on retaliation and reciprocation in ratings. Strategic rating is a loss of candour, which is closer to a trust problem than to a loss of shared conception of the task. The paper can get to common understanding, but by a different route: when what you say to the counterpart is also an input to the rule, the two of you no longer share one conception of what your exchange *is* (a message about the work, or a move in the scoring), and *Specifying intent* rebuilds that by addressing both readers in one act. That is the paper's argument in "Specifying Intent"; it just is not the argument in "The Participant's Position."

Two further things from the source. Okhuysen and Bechky say the three conditions interact and can substitute for one another, and call a strict separation "somewhat artificial." The paper's one-to-one table (one feature, one part, one condition) should say in one sentence why the substitution does not run here, or concede that the correspondence is an idealization. And the pinpoint p. 463 is right for the definition of coordination but the conditions are defined at pp. 483–490; a reader who wants them should be sent there.

### 1.7 The three parts

**Interpreting.** The seller example from Curchod et al. is well chosen and the two readings ("change how she deals with buyers" versus "wasted effort and may make things worse") show why attribution matters. The support paragraph is honest that the attribution studies are dyadic (Sundar & Nass; Hohenstein & Jung; Sundar & Kim) and that "no study I know of tests it there." That honesty is a strength and also an exposure: the construct's first part has no triadic evidence.

**Specifying intent.** This is the part that most needs a case from inside the paper's own scope. The two failures are illustrated with an invented employee who tunes a self-assessment to an automated scorer and another who writes a candid note that the system files as a complaint. Both are the manager–employee–GenAI arrangement that the paper later calls "a case that may arrive," where "whether the manager is bound is doubtful" and the operator "is closer to one party than to the other." The paper's clearest illustration of a part is drawn from the case the paper says may not qualify. Curchod's sellers and Fradkin's hosts are in scope and would illustrate both failures; use them.

Two citations the part needs. Petre, Duffy and Hund (2019) show platforms drawing a line between legitimate optimisation and "gaming," which is the line a participant who writes for the intermediary has to find, and it gives the first failure ("wrote for the intermediary alone") a mechanism: the system punishes writing that is too obviously for it. Marwick and boyd (2011) on the imagined audience and context collapse is the standard citation behind Litt (2012) and Viduchinsky's dual-audience dilemma.

**Keeping track.** Well supported from social-media research (Bucher; Cotter; DeVito; Meng), and the organizational turn to Orlikowski and Scott (2014) is right. The sentence "What the triad adds is a second thing that can change" is the part's real claim and it comes in the second paragraph; lead with it.

**A missing part, or a missing half of one.** The construct is motivated by the absence of the second person in the competency literature. Yet all three parts, as written, are about the participant's relation to the rule: interpret the rule's share, write so the rule does not misread you, notice when the rule moves. The counterpart appears in each part as the thing to be separated from the rule. What the construct does not contain is the work a participant does *with* the counterpart under the rule: the host who messages the guest before reviews post, the seller who pre-empts a bad rating by writing to the buyer, the contractor and client who use the platform's tools to repair a misaligned expectation. Rahman and Valentine (2021) is cited for exactly this ("collaborative repair") and is used as one item in a list. Cameron and Rahman (2022) is cited for workers influencing the algorithm through their dealings with customers. These are the triadic evidence the paper says it lacks, and they describe an activity (managing the counterpart as a route to the rule, and the rule as a constraint on the counterpart) that the three parts do not name. Either it is a fourth part or it is the counterpart-facing half of Specifying intent. The paper has to say which, for the same reason it has to decide the "telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart" question it raises twice and leaves open.

**How the Parts Fit Together.** The dependence argument ("Without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting") is tight. The acquisition paragraph is good: Jiang and Sinchaisri's learning curve, Rahman's dependence and setbacks, Lee et al. and Yao et al. on the limits of forums. "This is an expectation. No one has measured it." is the right register. The closing paragraph, which maps the two failure modes onto the two pictures from the Introduction, is the paper's best structural move and should stay exactly where it is.

### 1.8 Where the Account Applies

The cases are used honestly, and I want to say that plainly because it is rare. Fradkin et al. "establishes the arrangement and not the ability." Curchod et al. and Orlikowski and Scott (2023) are lopsided and the paper says so. Hinds and von Krogh is a thought experiment and the paper "use[s] their case as they do." Leonardi and Leavell is set aside as not qualifying and then used for what it does show. I verified the Leonardi and Leavell figures and quotations (83% versus 9% absolute statements, "just one tool of many," "17 mentions of unique lawsuits," and the "not an inherent property of AI tools" sentence) against the published PDF; they are at pp. 526, 535, 537 and 538 as cited. I verified the Hinds and von Krogh quotations at pp. 4 and 6 and the Orlikowski and Scott "proprietary algorithms" quotation at p. 3.

The honesty exposes the construct. Read the section as a hostile referee would: the best-fit case shows no ability; the usual case binds one side; the symmetric employer case has not arrived. There is no instance in the paper of a person exercising algorithmacy in a two-sided triad. For a theory article that is permissible, but the paper then needs at least one participant walked through all three parts in one setting, so a reader can see the construct as a sequence of things a person does and not only as a table. The Curchod seller is the obvious candidate and the paper already has her in "Interpreting."

The "upshot" paragraph states the problem of 1.5 as a prediction: "The usual case is the lopsided triad, and in it the demand for algorithmacy falls on one side of the relation." If that is the usual case, then "binds both" is not the criterion that picks out the arrangements the paper is about. What picks them out is the fourth feature: the intermediary evaluates the participant using her dealings with a second person as its evidence. That holds in Curchod's marketplace, in Orlikowski and Scott's hotel rankings, and in Fradkin's two-sided reviews. "Binds both" then becomes a dimension of the triad (symmetric or lopsided) rather than its definition, and the paper's own Simmel apparatus accommodates that: a symmetric triad is arbitration without appointment, a lopsided one is a coalition of the intermediary with the unbound party. I would urge this redefinition. It keeps every case the paper has, it keeps the second person in the lopsided case as a person, and it turns the research-agenda question into an answer. The title would need to change with it; "a Third That Judges Both" overclaims for a paper whose usual case judges one.

One factual correction, verified: the Orlikowski and Scott (2023) quotation "may or may not have experienced ... in person" is on p. 8, not p. 9. The running heads in the published PDF place the sentence ("specific hotels and restaurants (which they may or may not have experienced in person)") on the page headed "8 Organization Theory." The paper's rendering of the parenthetical as being about "the hotel" is a fair gloss, since the source says "hotels and restaurants."

### 1.9 Discussion

**What the Account Adds.** The two-dimension comparison (how many parties; ability or response) is a good way to place the rivals, and the concession that "two of the three parts have names already" (Viduchinsky; Meng) is right. The paragraph is a stacked list of findings and needs grouping (see Part 2). The Zhou et al. pinpoints (p. 2 and, earlier, p. 8) are to the lab's working-paper PDF, not to the *APJHR* version of record (article e70004), and must be re-pinned before submission; the quotations themselves match the working paper.

**The Intermediary as a Party.** The sociomaterial objection is answered at the level of a reply and not at the level of integration. The reply is perspectival: "From where she stands, a result arrives that she cannot trace ... Calling that source a party describes what she faces. It makes no claim that the technology exists apart from the people and practices that produce it." That is defensible, and Newlands (2021) is a fair ally. But the paper has a stronger answer in its own fourth feature and does not use it. If the intermediary's evidence is the two people's dealings, then the verdict is co-produced by the participant; the rule is enacted through her own conduct, which is the sociomaterial point. The triad is a sociomaterial accomplishment in which one party contributes to a verdict she cannot read. Said that way, Orlikowski and Scott, Faraj and Pachidi and Glaser et al. become support for the construct rather than an objection to it, and the paper's definition of the intermediary as "the rule as applied, with its operator behind it" already says as much. I could not verify the Faraj and Pachidi (2021, p. 1) quotation locally and do not assert an error.

**The moral-crumple-zone paragraph** is correct about Elish (2019) and is the right place for the risk that algorithmacy "may work as a private repair for a public defect." The hedge ("a possibility that follows from the account and not as a finding") is flat and proper.

**Three Courses.** Clear and practical, and the Christin (2017) closer (adopted on paper, worked around in practice) is apt. The brokers in Waardenburg et al. and the planners in Leonardi and Leavell are the right pair. One addition from my seat: the paper's own fourth feature implies a fourth course, which is to stop taking the two people's dealings as evidence (hide reviews until both are in, as Fradkin's treatment did; score the work and not the exchange). The paper has the citation and the mechanism and does not draw the course.

### 1.10 Research Agenda and Conclusion

The agenda's first two questions are not research questions; they are definitional. "Whether the lopsided triad and the two-sided triad are one form or two" and "the conditions under which an employer's system counts as a party" decide what the construct covers. The paper has the materials to answer both (1.8 above for the first; the employer case is a triad when the system's verdict is one the manager cannot set aside, which Lin et al. (2026) suggests is often not the case, and a hierarchy instrument otherwise). Answer them and move the empirical questions up.

The empirical questions are good, and the Krakowski et al. (2023) chess finding is an apt indirect reason to expect algorithmacy to differ from general skill (I could not verify the p. 1425 quotation locally).

The last paragraph closes on "they are relying on an ability that they have neither named nor checked that their people have." That is a fair conclusion, correctly scoped, and not cute. Keep.

### 1.11 Table 1 and Figure 1

**Table 1.** Row 1 lists "hidden and unanswerable" as the feature calling for Interpreting, against the text's statement that the hidden rule "does not distinguish among" the parts. Row 3's failure column gives only one direction ("acting on an understanding of the rule that no longer holds"), whereas rows 1 and 2 each give two; the missing direction is attributing a change in the counterparts to the rule, which the text names. Add it.

**Figure 1.** The three black arrows run Intermediary → Participant (1, Interpreting), Participant → Counterpart (2, Specifying intent), Counterpart → Intermediary (3, Keeping track). Arrows 1 and 2 are defensible. Arrow 3 is not: keeping track is something the participant does, and it is drawn as a flow from the counterpart to the intermediary. Specifying intent is also mis-drawn: the text says it is "one act addressed to two readers," and the arrow goes to one. If the cycle is meant to be the participant's cycle (interpret → act → observe), all three arrows should originate at or return to the participant. The grey arrangement (verdict, verdict, their dealings taken as evidence) is correct and is actually the more informative half of the figure.

### 1.12 Prior-art trap

The novelty claim is "no account names the ability or defines it over all three parties." Nearest rivals, in order of threat:

1. **Jarrahi & Sutherland (2019), "algorithmic competency."** The paper engages it and the engagement is right: their figure has two nodes. The claim narrows to "defined over three parties" and survives. The p. 9 pinpoint carries an `[AUTHOR:]` flag and I could not verify the quotation; the chapter's full text is not in the lab's sources.
2. **Sutherland et al. (2020), "gig literacies."** The paper concedes this "comes nearest" because it includes building relationships with clients. I verified that the source lists "building relationships" among the literacies. The claim survives because gig literacies is an inventory, not a derivation.
3. **Zuboff (1988), "intellective skill."** Not cited. A named ability demanded by a new textual medium of work, which is the paper's literacy-to-algorithmacy step. Dyadic (worker and electronic text). The claim survives narrowed, but only if the paper says so.
4. **Rahman & Valentine (2021), "collaborative repair."** Cited in passing. Triadic (manager, worker, platform tools) and about an activity. It is not a competence construct, so the claim survives, but it is the paper's best triadic evidence and is under-used (1.7).
5. **Kellogg et al. (2020), "algoactivism."** The field's catalogue of responses. A response, not an ability; the paper's "ability or response" axis handles it, but only if Kellogg et al. are placed on it, which they currently are not.

Verdict on novelty: survives, narrowed to "a definition over three parties with each part derived from a feature of the intermediary," which is what the paper claims. The derivation therefore has to be sound, and 1.6 says where it is not.

## Part 2 — AI-slop audit (register-aware)

The prose is plain, the agents are named, and the passive count is near zero (four `has been / have been` hits in 7,160 words, all of them with a named or obvious agent: "two of the three have been named before," "AI feedback has been tried," "surveillance ... has been understood"). The first-person moves are authorial ("I define," "I sort," "I searched," "I take three points") and not claim-bearing. Em-dashes: four, two of them in one sentence pair; nothing to cut. By the lexical measures this manuscript is clean, and I did not find a single inflation word (no tapestry, delve, crucial, foster, landscape, navigate as filler; "navigate" appears once, inside a Zhou quotation, which is off-limits).

What remains is at the paragraph layer, and it is the author's own rule ("nothing cute or aphoristic") that the manuscript bends.

**The landing-line drumbeat.** Sections and paragraphs close on a polished antithesis or chiasmus often enough that the pattern shows:

- "The observations in this literature are triadic and the constructs are dyadic." (Introduction, ¶3)
- "A platform that decides between two parties and profits from what they do combines positions that Simmel kept apart." (Simmel, ¶4)
- "The form shapes the judgment and a person makes it." (Three Forms, ¶5)
- "Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat." (Sets Apart, ¶3)
- "Under an algorithmic intermediary all four hold at once, for people with no counsel, as an everyday condition of their work." (Sets Apart, ¶4)
- "Without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting." (Fit Together, ¶1)
- "In each case one corner of the triad stands in for the whole." (Fit Together, ¶3)
- "Calling that source a party describes what she faces." (Intermediary as Party, ¶1)
- "Installing a system is none of these." (Three Courses)
- "Where they do not, they are relying on an ability that they have neither named nor checked that their people have." (Conclusion)

Each of these, alone, is earned; several name a mechanism that the paragraph has established, and I would keep the Introduction's, the Simmel section's, the "formatting" line, and the conclusion's. The problem is frequency. Ten section- or paragraph-closers of the same shape in 7,000 words is the uniformity the house style names, and it is at odds with the author's "nothing aphoristic" rule. The cure is not to blunt them but to vary the exits: let some paragraphs end on a forward hook ("The next section says what separates these two cases" is the model, and it is the one place the manuscript does it), and let some simply stop. Candidates to convert to hooks or plain stops: "The form shapes the judgment and a person makes it" (already followed by a hook-like sentence; cut the epigram and keep the hook), "Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat" (keep), "In each case one corner of the triad stands in for the whole" (keep; it is the structural point), "Calling that source a party describes what she faces" (convert; see Part 3).

**Performed rigor.** One instance: "A construct needs stated limits, and a single clear exception counts heavily against one (Suddaby, 2010)." This is the paper narrating the virtue of the paragraph that follows. Suddaby does say that a single exception is often fatal to a construct (verified, his p. 353 region); the content is fine, the frame is self-certification. Cut the sentence and open on "Because the features vary independently, the need for the ability varies with them." If Suddaby must stay, put him in the limitation paragraph of the Introduction, where the paper is already stating scope.

**Ownership-flagging as a tic.** "The sorting is mine." / "The inference is mine and not Yates's." / "I use their case as they do." / "I say which is which." Each is honest and each is short; four of them in one paper is a pattern, and the second one in particular reads as the paper pre-empting a referee rather than stating a claim. Keep "The sorting is mine" (it is doing work against Thompson) and fold the Yates one into the sentence it qualifies.

**Stacked evidence lists** (house-style mechanism 8). Three paragraphs emit one finding per sentence with a parallel verb:

- Interpreting ¶2: "Sundar and Nass (2000) found ... Hohenstein and Jung (2020) found ... Sundar and Kim (2019) found ..." Three "found" sentences, then a fourth with Heiland. Group under the point they share (people assign credit and blame between person and machine in ways content alone does not explain), which the paragraph states only afterwards.
- What the Account Adds ¶1: six constructs in six sentences, each with its own citation parenthesis at the end. The two dimensions the paragraph announces (parties; ability or response) are the grouping; use them as the sentence subjects.
- Field studies ¶2 (Discussion): "Workers craft ... (Cameron, 2022), manage ... (Bellesia et al., 2023), and comply ... (Bucher et al., 2021). Clients and contractors repair ... (Rahman & Valentine, 2021), and workers learn ... (Möhlmann et al., 2021)." This one is already subordinated into two sentences and is acceptable.

**Antithesis.** Eight ", not X / rather than" constructions in 7,160 words is well under the venue's rate and none is decorative. No flag.

**Rhythm.** Mostly good. Short sentences land after dense ones ("I define it." in the abstract; "It does not name one ability or define it over the three parties."; "More data cures the first and does not cure the second."). The one stretch that runs uniform is "What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart" ¶2, where First / Second / Third / Fourth each take one long sentence of the same build. The structure is doing work; vary the length inside it.

## Part 3 — Line-level revisions, ranked by value

Each rewrite is in the author's register. Where I add a citation the author must verify it before it enters the draft.

**1. The scope sentence on the lopsided triad** (Sets Apart, ¶5). Current:

> Where the intermediary binds only one of the two, the triad is lopsided: the bound person needs the ability, and the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence.

Replace with:

> Where the intermediary binds only one of the two, the triad is lopsided. The bound person needs the ability. The other person is still someone she has to work with, and what passes between them is still the intermediary's evidence; what the unbound person lacks is any verdict to interpret, and so any need to keep track.

**2. The accountability loss** (Participant's Position, ¶2). Current:

> Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all.

Replace with:

> Accountability, in Okhuysen and Bechky's sense, is knowing where the responsibilities of interdependent parties lie. It goes because one of the parties to every result is a rule the participant cannot read, so she cannot tell which part of the result is the counterpart's and which is the rule's.

(And move "gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all" to the "unanswerable" feature, where it belongs.)

**3. The common-understanding loss** (same paragraph). Current:

> Common understanding goes because the two people's dealings are also evidence, so neither can take what the other says at face value.

Replace with:

> Common understanding goes because the two people's dealings are also evidence, so they no longer share one conception of what their exchange is: a message about the work, or a move in the scoring. Each writes with an eye to the rule, and each knows the other does.

Then the Bolton and Fradkin sentences follow as support for "each knows the other does."

**4. The sociomaterial reply** (Intermediary as a Party, ¶1). Current closing:

> Calling that source a party describes what she faces. It makes no claim that the technology exists apart from the people and practices that produce it.

Replace with:

> Calling that source a party describes what she faces, and it concedes the point: the verdict is made partly from her own dealings, so she is one of the practices that produce it. The triad is enacted, and she helps enact a rule she cannot read. That is the position the ability answers to.

**5. The Suddaby frame** (Sets Apart, ¶5). Current opening:

> A construct needs stated limits, and a single clear exception counts heavily against one (Suddaby, 2010). Because the features vary independently, the need for the ability varies with them, and the account can be wrong.

Replace with:

> Because the features vary independently, the need for the ability varies with them, and the account can be wrong in four places.

**6. The "Keeping track" lead** (Keeping Track, ¶1–2). Move the second paragraph's first sentence to open the part:

> Keeping track is noticing that the rule has changed and telling that change apart from a change in the counterpart. What the triad adds to the social-media case is a second thing that can change. ...

**7. The Interpreting evidence list** (Interpreting, ¶2). Current: three "found" sentences. Replace with:

> People assign credit, blame and trust between a person and a machine in ways the content alone does not explain. Sundar and Nass (2000) had people rate the same content differently by the source they were told it came from; Hohenstein and Jung (2020) found suggested replies raising trust in a chat partner and, when a conversation went badly, moving blame from the partner to the system; and Sundar and Kim (2019) found people more willing to give a credit-card number to an agent they were told was a machine. Heiland (2025) reports workers forming false theories of an algorithm they could not see. All of this is in dyads. I expect the same tendency in a triad, where the stakes are higher and the machine's share is harder to see, and no study I know of tests it there.

**8. Kellogg et al. placement** (Sets Apart, ¶2, new opening sentence before "First, its rule is *hidden*"). Draft, author to verify the characterisation against the source:

> Kellogg et al. (2020) characterise algorithmic control as comprehensive, instantaneous, interactive and opaque. The four features below overlap with the last three and add one their review does not separate out: what the system takes as its evidence.

**9. Table 1, row 1, column 2.** Replace "The rule is hidden and unanswerable" with "The rule is unanswerable (hidden is a condition of all three parts)". Row 3, column 4: "Acting on an understanding of the rule that no longer holds, or reading a change in the counterparts as a change in the rule."

**10. Title.** If the redefinition in 1.8 is adopted: "Algorithmacy: Working With Another Person Through a Third That Judges From Their Dealings." If not, the title must still lose "Both," because the paper's usual case judges one.

## Recommendation to the editor

**Major revisions.** The paper is in the right journal, its gap is real, and its Simmel apparatus and its handling of the CCO and sociomaterial objections are better than most construct papers manage. What keeps it from a minor-revisions verdict is that the move which makes it a theory contribution, the derivation of three parts from four features via three integrating conditions, has a definitional hole (the "both" criterion) and a source mismatch (Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability), and the paper's own cases, used with unusual honesty, show that the criterion as written excludes the usual case. Each of these is repairable within the word limit (about 1,300 words of room), and the repairs I have proposed make the construct sharper rather than smaller. I would want to see the revised version. I would not want to see it with the definitional question still deferred to "future research."

## Closing note

**Biggest genuine strength.** The paper does not oversell its cases. "The study concerns platform design and reports nothing about what hosts or guests are able to do, so it establishes the arrangement and not the ability" is a sentence most authors would not write about their best-fit case, and the same candour runs through the GenAI and Leonardi and Leavell paragraphs. Combined with the mediator/arbitrator line from Simmel and the verdict criterion that separates an appraisal form from a scoring system, it gives the paper a spine that the current definitional trouble does not break. The reader trusts the author, which is why the author can afford to make the harder definitional call this review asks for.

**The one thing only the author can supply.** A participant walked through the whole cycle in one real setting, with the detail that shows interpreting, specifying and keeping track as things a person did in sequence, not as cells in a table. The published studies give the arrangement; none of them gives the person's turn through it. If the author has one such case, from fieldwork, from a seller's or host's own record, or from the author's own position inside a system of this kind, it belongs in "Algorithmacy" between the definition and the parts, and no reviewer can write it for them.
