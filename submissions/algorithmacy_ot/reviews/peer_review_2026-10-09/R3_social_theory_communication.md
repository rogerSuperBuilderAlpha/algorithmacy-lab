## Verdict

**Major revisions.** The single most important fix: re-read Simmel's *tertius gaudens* pages (1902b, pp. 174–177) and rebuild the "Simmel on the Third Party" section around what they say. The manuscript claims the platform "combines positions that Simmel kept apart." Simmel did not keep them apart. He derived the *tertius gaudens* from the non-partisan who turns the position to his own ends, contrasted it with the arbitrator in so many words, and described its most comprehensive form as a third who "can set his own conditions for the decision" and whose conditions the other parties "must even seek to anticipate... to guess the unspoken or unconscious wishes" of. That is the paper's intermediary, in Simmel, in 1902. The paper's novelty does not die, but it moves: it is not that the platform occupies a position Simmel lacked, it is that Simmel's *tertius gaudens* has become the ordinary organizer of interdependent work and that nobody has asked what the two courted parties must be able to do. Everything downstream (the three-form typology, the "who holds the verdict" line, the terminology) needs to be re-set against that corrected base.

## Step 0 — Register and bar

**Register identified.** A first-person-singular theory article in deliberately plain words: short declaratives, no modal padding, scope conditions stated rather than hedged, no cute lines, no illustrative drivers or freelancers. The "I" marks the author's own moves (I sort, I define, I searched, I infer) and does not carry the claims. Quoted material and the bracketed `[AUTHOR: …]` flags are off-limits for prose flags.

**Bar I will hold it to.** The house style at `~/.claude/writing-style.md`: named agents and active voice, claim first, concrete anchors, verified citations, dense sentences punctured by short ones, cohesion across sentences (tail-head linkage, end-focus, forward hooks). My job is to make the piece better at being this plain, first-person theory article, not to convert it into standard journal prose. If any rewrite I propose is more nominalized or more passive than the original, it is wrong and the author should ignore it. I state that constraint to myself before Part 2.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and argument structure

### Abstract

The abstract does its five moves and reads cleanly. Two notes. First, it runs to 256 words; the author should check the journal's limit. Second, the sentence "Building on Simmel's account of the third party, I distinguish three forms in which people work together" promises more Simmel than the paper delivers, since the three-form sorting is the author's own and Simmel is used for one line within it (mediate/arbitrate). That is fine if the Simmel section is rebuilt as I propose below; as it stands, the abstract advertises a Simmelian foundation that the body treats as a borrowed distinction plus a disclaimer.

### Introduction

The two-pictures framing (one person and a machine; two people and a channel) is the paper's best structural idea and it is set out well. The sentence "The observations in this literature are triadic and the constructs are dyadic" is the paper's thesis in nine words and it is earned by the paragraph before it.

Three points from my seat.

**(1) The HMC picture is not quite "one person and one machine."** Guzman and Lewis (2020, p. 71) do define HMC by "technologies designed as communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects"; I verified the quotation and page. But the paper then says this picture "contains one person and one machine," which is true of the CASA lineage (Nass & Moon; Reeves & Nass) and not of HMC's research agenda, which already asks about machines positioned between people and about who is the "source" behind a message (Sundar & Nass 2000 is itself a three-position study: user, machine, programmer/networker). The author's own evidence in "Interpreting" comes from that work. The cleaner statement is that the first picture has one person facing a machine *and asks whom the machine speaks for*, which is already the beginning of the paper's question. Say that and the Introduction stops arguing against a straw CASA.

**(2) The second picture is not left "nothing to decide."** Hancock et al. (2020, p. 89 abstract, repeated p. 90) define AI-MC as an agent that "operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages." The verification is fine. But an agent that modifies and generates messages decides something; what it does not do is evaluate and bind. The author's own criterion is "evaluates and binds," so the Introduction should say "leaves it nothing to *judge*" rather than "nothing to decide." Small, but it matters because the whole paper turns on which verb separates the pictures.

**(3) The Jarrahi and Sutherland quotation is unverified.** The author has already flagged the pagination. From the lab's card (publisher abstract retrieved), the chapter's own words are "developing a literacy for understanding and working with algorithms" and "work around or manipulate them." The definition quoted in the manuscript may well be in the chapter; nobody on this side has read it. Either obtain the chapter or quote the abstract.

### Simmel on the Third Party

This is the section my seat exists for, and it is where the paper is weakest. I read Small's translation of Part II (pp. 166–182) against each claim.

**What checks out.** "Seeks to eliminate himself" is on p. 167 (the sentence runs "in which instance the mediator seeks to eliminate himself, and only to bring to pass that the two disunited or ununited parties may unite directly"). The block quotation ("So long as the third party works as a real mediator… out of their own hands") is verbatim. "The voluntary appeal to an arbitrator, to whom the parties subordinate themselves a priori, presupposes a greater subjective confidence in the objectivity of the judgment than any other form of decision" is verbatim and does straddle pp. 172–173. The three positions (non-partisan who mediates or arbitrates; *tertius gaudens*; *divide et impera*) are Simmel's numbered headings at pp. 166, 174 and 182.

**What does not.**

*(a) The block quotation sits entirely on p. 172, not pp. 172–173.* Both sentences are on p. 172 in the scan; the page break falls after "presupposes a greater subjective confidence" in the following sentence. Cite "(Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)."

*(b) "As a means to the ends of the group" (p. 174) is said of the mediator and the arbitrator together, not of the mediator alone.* The passage reads: "The mediator and the arbitrator alike wish to preserve the group unity against the danger of disruption. The nonpartisan, however, may use his relatively superior status in a purely egoistic interest. While in the former cases he acted as a means to the ends of the group, in this case, on the contrary, he makes the reciprocal occurrences between the parties and between himself and the parties a means for his own ends" (pp. 174–175). The manuscript attaches the phrase to the mediator and then, two paragraphs later, says "The mediator has no stake. A platform does." In Simmel neither the mediator nor the arbitrator has a stake; the stake belongs to the *tertius gaudens*. The author's point survives and gets stronger once stated right: the platform decides as an arbitrator does and profits as a *tertius* does.

*(c) "A platform that decides between two parties and profits from what they do combines positions that Simmel kept apart" is wrong as a reading of Simmel.* Three passages, all in the section the manuscript cites:

- p. 174: the *tertius gaudens* is introduced as the non-partisan who "may use his relatively superior status in a purely egoistic interest." Simmel does not keep the deciding third and the profiting third apart; he derives the second from the first.
- p. 176: "The formations of this type which are more essential at this point, result when the third party, for reasons of prudence respecting his own interests, adopts an attitude of practical support toward the one party (that is, not merely by way of intellectual decision, as in the case of the arbitrator) and from this attitude derives his mediate or immediate gain." Simmel contrasts the *tertius* with the arbitrator himself, and the contrast is not decide-versus-profit but decide-disinterestedly-versus-decide-for-gain.
- p. 176: "In both cases the advantage of nonpartisanship, with which the tertius originally stood in antithesis with the other two, consists in the fact that he can set his own conditions for the decision." And p. 177, on the purchasing public as "the most comprehensive illustration of the tertius gaudens": "the producers must even seek to anticipate these conditions, to guess the unspoken or unconscious wishes of the consumer, to suggest to him conditions that are not present, or to accustom him to desirable conditions."

That last passage is the manuscript's "hidden rule" and its "specifying intent for two readers at once," in Simmel's own words, about a third who sets undisclosed conditions and whom both parties must court. The paper is not describing a combination Simmel did not foresee; it is describing the *tertius gaudens* installed as the standing organizer of two parties' joint work. The correct claim, and a better one, is: Simmel described this third, and described it as an occasional and parasitic form that arises "ad hoc" (p. 175) from a conflict; algorithmic systems have made it permanent, non-conflictual, and the condition under which ordinary cooperation happens, and nobody in the triad literature has asked what the courted parties must be able to do. Novelty survives, narrowed and relocated.

*(d) Simmel's arbitrator ends a conflict between the two; the platform's score is addressed to others.* Simmel's arbitrator "finally takes a decided position on one side" (p. 169) and the parties have "projected their purpose of conciliation beyond themselves" (p. 172). A host and a guest who review each other are not in conflict and the platform does not decide between them; it rates each one for the benefit of future hosts and guests, who are the actual audience of the verdict. The manuscript's own best case (Fradkin et al.) is therefore not an arbitration triad but a reputation structure in which the third speaks to a fourth. Simmel flags exactly this at p. 175 ("the reciprocity within the tetrad"). The paper should say openly that it borrows from Simmel the *form* of the arbitrator (the decision has left the two parties' hands) and not the *function* (ending a dispute), and that "binds" in its definition is often realized through a fourth party's choices rather than through the third's own act. Otherwise a Simmel-literate referee will say the two-sided reputation case is a tetrad and the paper's unit is wrong.

*(e) Simmel already contains "specifying intent for two readers."* At p. 169: "each must also express himself more objectively than in the case of immediate confronting of the contestant. For now it is an object to each party to win over the mediator also to its standpoint." Speaking in the presence of a third changes how each party addresses the other; the dual audience is a property of every Simmelian triad, not of the algorithmic one. This cuts two ways. It supports the paper's claim that the triad differs in kind from the dyad. It also means "specifying intent" cannot by itself distinguish algorithmacy from what any party before a mediator has to do; what distinguishes it is that the second reader's rule is hidden and that it reads the dealings themselves. The paper's Table 1 already ties the part to that feature; the Simmel section should take the credit for it.

*(f) Simmel says the mediator/arbitrator line blurs in practice.* p. 173: "very many intermediate grades are produced… a status in which the mediatorial and the arbitrative element cannot always be separated." The manuscript draws its moderated-dyad/triad boundary "at the same place." That is legitimate for an ideal type (Simmel says so himself in the geometry analogy that follows), but the paper should acknowledge that Simmel treats it as a line between types, not between cases, and that the "lopsided triad" of the later sections is one of his intermediate grades.

*(g) The relay.* The manuscript says a relay "can change a message, forget part of it, or add to it." Simmel's own account of the relay, on the page before the one the manuscript quotes, is different and more useful: what the intermediate person strips is affect. "Recommendations and testimonials which must first pass several intermediate persons are so often impotent, even if their objective content comes with no real diminution to the person who is to give the final decision. In the transfer affective imponderabilities are lost" (p. 168). This is a chained dyad producing an evaluation (a testimonial) that passes through a conduit to a decider; it is the paper's chain, in Simmel, with a verdict at the end of it. It would strengthen the "Three Forms" section and it costs one sentence.

*(h) Pyyhtinen (2018).* The manuscript says Pyyhtinen "has since asked what becomes of the triad when the third is not human." I could not confirm that from the 2018 book. Cantó-Milà's published review of it (*Digithum*, 2018) reports the book's argument on the triad as: the dyad is already a triad, because the relation between the two is itself a third, and Simmel erred in assuming the third must be a person (p. 91 of the book). The more-than-human argument is Pyyhtinen's 2015 book (*More-Than-Human Sociology*). Either cite 2015 for the non-human third, or, better, use what 2018 actually argues, which helps the paper more: if the relation is already a third, then the thing that stands between two people is a candidate third on Simmel's own terms, and the question is only whether it evaluates and binds. Verify against the book before either.

*(i) "Whose interests the intermediary carries."* The paragraph that introduces participant/counterpart/intermediary is good and the distinction between the operator (who has interests) and the rule as applied (what the participant meets) is the right one. Two things are missing. First, Burt's *tertius gaudens* (1992) is the brokerage literature's rendering of the same Simmel pages and is the version an OT reader knows; the paper cites Obstfeld et al. (2014) for the three orientations, which is correct, but not Burt, which will look like an omission. Second, the paper never says what the operator's interest *is* in the triad: on a marketplace it is transaction volume and retention, which is why the rule reads the dealings (they are the product). Stark and Vanden Broeck's line "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14, verified) says this; the paper should draw out that the operator's interest explains feature four, not just that an interest exists.

### Three Forms of Working Together

**The typology does not survive the communication literature as stated, but it can be restated to survive.**

*Goffman is dismissed in one sentence and he is the paper's best friend.* The manuscript: "Goffman (1981) showed that even face-to-face talk involves more than a speaker and a hearer… Neither point touches a claim about two people who depend on each other to get something done." The second sentence is wrong. Goffman's participation framework (ratified participants, bystanders, overhearers) is precisely a claim that the dyad is never a dyad, and his production format (animator, author, principal) is precisely the tool the paper's "Interpreting" part needs. When a verdict arrives, the participant's question is Goffman's: who animated this (the platform), who authored it (partly the counterpart, partly the rule), and who is the principal whose position it commits (the operator)? "Working out whether a result came from the rule, from the counterpart, or from both" is a production-format decomposition. And a third that "takes the two people's dealings with each other as its evidence" is a ratified overhearer with power, a position Goffman catalogued without the power. The paper should bring Goffman in rather than wave him off: the dyad cell of the typology is an idealization Goffman denies exists in pure form, and the honest claim is that in the direct dyad none of the additional positions evaluates and binds.

*Ong, Olson and the orality–literacy tradition.* The use of Ong (1975) is accurate (abstract verified) and the Olson point ("records what was said and leaves open how it was meant") matches the lab's card. But the paper is invoking the orality–literacy tradition (oracy versus literacy as competences keyed to media) while citing none of its core texts (Goody & Watt 1963; Havelock; Ong 1982, *Orality and Literacy*; Goody 1986, *The Logic of Writing and the Organization of Society*). The omission matters for two reasons. First, the "great divide" between oral and literate competence is contested (Street 1984; Finnegan), and a sorting that puts oracy in one cell and literacy in another will be read as endorsing it unless the author says the cells are about *what the thing in the middle does*, not about cognitive consequences of media; the paper half-says this ("not a theory of speech or of writing") and should say it fully. Second, Goody 1986 is the standard argument that writing is constitutive of bureaucracy, the state and organized religion, which bears directly on the historical claim I take up next.

*The historical claim about what organizations "relied on" is unsupported.* "The claim is that organizations first relied on people who could talk, then also on people who could handle text, and are now beginning to rely on people who can work in a triad." As history this is not supported by Yates (whose window is 1850–1920 and whose subject is American firms adopting *systematic* internal communication, not firms adopting literacy) and it is contradicted by the tradition the paper draws on: Goody's whole case is that organizations of the relevant kind do not exist before writing, and Weber's bureaucracy rests on "the files." There is no pre-literate stage of organization in Yates's sense for the first clause to describe. The third clause is also weaker than it looks: Simmel's testimonials (p. 168), letters of reference, credit bureaus (nineteenth century), guild examinations and the merit-rating systems of the 1910s all put a judging third with an undisclosed rule between two working parties. The paper says, rightly, that the triad is older than software. It should drop the sequence and keep the claim it can defend: that the demanding version (all four features at once, no counsel) has become ordinary. The author's own disclaimer two sentences earlier ("Nor do I claim that one form replaced another or that the words arrived in this order") shows the author already suspects the sequence cannot bear weight.

*"Oracy in its coordinating use" is not clean.* Simmel at p. 169 (quoted above) says that speaking before a third changes how each party speaks. So the competence of talking-to-coordinate is already different in the triad, and "oracy belongs to the dyad" needs the qualifier "and is reshaped, not replaced, in the other two forms." The paper's own claim that literacy and algorithmacy *add* to oracy supports this; say it.

*The Thompson comparison.* Thompson (1995) sorts interaction on four criteria (space-time constitution; range of symbolic cues; action orientation toward specific others or an indefinite range; dialogical or monological) and Thompson (2020, verified) adds mediated online interaction. The manuscript's summary ("by the medium and by whether the audience is specific") compresses this to two; acceptable, but a Thompson reader will notice the missing dialogical/monological criterion, which is the one closest to the paper's own ("the reader cannot question the page"). Name it and the comparison lands.

*The nearest antecedent is not Thompson; it is Latour, and the paper's terms collide with his.* See the terminology point below; it belongs here because this is the paragraph that defines the terms.

### Who holds the verdict: does the line separate a text from a third party?

Not as drawn. Three problems, in rising order.

*(1) "Hidden" and "unanswerable" are already properties of the text.* The paper says of the moderated dyad: "the reader cannot question the page" and the text "leaves open how it was meant." Those are features one and two of the algorithmic intermediary ("its rule is hidden"; "it is unanswerable: it gives no reasons, there is no one to ask"). So two of the four features that are supposed to set the algorithmic third apart from a court also fail to set it apart from a text. Only features three (changes without notice) and four (takes the dealings as evidence) are distinctive against the moderated dyad. The paper should say this, because it is the real content of the typology: a text is hidden and unanswerable but fixed and deaf; the algorithmic third is hidden, unanswerable, moving, and listening.

*(2) The CCO objection is answered by changing the subject.* The paper concedes Cooren (2004, p. 373, verified in abstract) and Kuhn (2008) and then says "The line I draw is about who holds the verdict. An appraisal form presents demands, but the manager fills it in, signs it, can be asked why, and answers for the rating." That is a line about whether the *author* of the text is reachable and answerable. It is a property of the relation between participant and author, not of the thing in the middle. A contract that neither party can set aside alone, a statute, a safety code, a standardized test scored by a key the test-taker never sees, Kuhn's "authoritative text" that defines what counts as a contribution to the firm: each is a text that evaluates and binds two parties, with an author who is distant, unreachable, and in Kuhn's case the organization itself. From a CCO point of view the moderated dyad is already a triad, with the organization as the third speaking through the text; that is what "text" means in Taylor and Van Every. The paper can hold its line only by renaming it: the question is not "text or third" but "is the author of the verdict reachable, and does the verdict change in response to what the two parties do to each other." On that criterion the standardized test and the blind review fall on the algorithmic side (the paper already admits the blind review does), the appraisal form falls on the dyadic side, and the typology is honest.

*(3) The platform's verdict arrives as a text.* A score, a ranking, a visibility setting: the participant meets a text, as the paper says ("She meets the rule as it is applied to her"). So the participant in the triad is also a reader, and "interpreting" is reading a text whose author will not answer. That is why Olson helps the triad more than the dyad. The paper should use him there.

### What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart

The court comparison is the paper's second-best idea and the four features are well chosen. Katsh and Rifkin's "fourth party" is correctly reported (card verified: disclosed, seated beside a human neutral, design-oriented). Three notes.

*Selznick.* The p. 13 definition is verified. The p. 14 phrase "the responsibility for power rather than power itself" is not verified in the form quoted; the OCR I have of the book reads "a sharing of the burdens of and responsibility for power, rather than of power itself" in the summary of findings. The copyeditor should check the exact wording at p. 14 against a page scan.

*"Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat."* Good line, and it correctly inverts formal co-optation. But the lab's own Selznick card notes that Selznick's *informal* co-optation (unpublicized, no seat conferred) is closer to platform enrollment than the formal branch the paper contrasts against; a Selznick-literate referee will raise it. One clause.

*"Algorithmacy is therefore not confined to software."* Right, and consistent. But then "I name the ability for algorithmic systems for the reason that literacy is named for letters though it applies to any text" is an analogy that undercuts the construct: literacy is named for its *medium*; algorithmacy is being named for a *technology* that the author has just said is not definitional. The better analogy is numeracy, named for what is handled (numbers) not for what handles them. The author should either accept the inconsistency plainly ("I name it for where the form became general") and drop the literacy analogy, or find the medium-level word.

### Algorithmacy — the definition and the participant's position

*Sandberg and Jablin & Sias.* Used accurately; situated competence is the right lineage and the three-way separation (trait, organizational capability, knowledge about a technology) is clean. Stelmaszak et al. (p. 346, verified) say what the paper says they say.

*Daft and Lengel.* Accurate: uncertainty is absence of information, equivocality is conflicting readings, and more data cures only the first. One missing attribution: "equivocality" is Weick's (1979, *The Social Psychology of Organizing*) and Daft and Lengel took it from him. Since the next sentence is Weick, the paper should say "Daft and Lengel (1986), drawing on Weick, separated…" and the two sentences will join.

*Sensemaking.* The Weick et al. quotation is verified (p. 409). The use is thin. The paper takes from sensemaking only "a situation to be made sense of." It leaves on the table the tenet that would do the most work for it: enactment. Figure 1 is an enactment loop (the participant acts on her interpretation, the act becomes the intermediary's evidence, the next result tests the interpretation) and the "How the Parts Fit Together" section describes exactly that, without the word. Weick's plausibility-over-accuracy tenet also explains why participants act confidently on folk theories they know may be wrong (Heiland 2025 is cited for this later). Two sentences would convert a decorative citation into a load-bearing one. The paper also uses "sensemaking" for a situation and "interpreting" for the act; in Jarrahi and Sutherland's lineage (per the lab card, via Zhou et al. 2025) "sensemaking" is the name of the first *competency*. The paper should say that its "interpreting" is their "sensemaking," renamed because it is defined over three parties, or a referee will ask why the term was changed.

*Okhuysen and Bechky.* The coordination definition at p. 463 is verified. The three integrating conditions are theirs. But the paper misuses "accountability." Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability is responsibility-allocation ("who is responsible for specific elements of the task"), achievable "through informal and emergent action as well as by formal means," among the coordinating parties themselves (lab card, direct read at pp. 483–490). The paper states its loss in the answerability sense: "Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all." That is Bovens's accountability (a forum with standing to demand justification), not theirs. The mismatch is repairable without changing the architecture, and the repair makes Table 1 tighter: what the triad destroys is the participant's ability to tell *whose contribution produced the result*, which is exactly Okhuysen and Bechky's condition, and "interpreting" recovers exactly that. Restate the sentence and the mapping holds; leave it and a referee who opens the Annals article will find, on the same page, assemblers constructing accountability by dialogue and will ask why the participant cannot. The answer (the party who would have to answer is unreachable) is the paper's point; it should be the paper's sentence.

*The Bolton and Fradkin evidence.* Both verified from abstracts and the Fradkin card (reciprocation and retaliation fell, ratings fell, review volume rose; "precisely estimated null" on demand). The paper's use is accurate and modest. One addition the author will want: Fradkin's null on adverse selection means the "binding" in the two-sided case did not visibly re-sort the market, which is relevant to how hard the verdict actually binds; a sentence in "Where the Account Applies."

### The three parts

*Interpreting.* The dyadic-evidence problem is admitted ("though it shows it in dyads and not in triads"), which is the right thing to do, but the admission comes after the evidence has been stacked and the sentence that follows it ("People in these studies assigned credit, blame, and trust between a person and a machine in patterned ways") resumes as if the caveat had not been made. Two further points. Hohenstein and Jung (2020) is not a dyad: it is two humans and a smart-reply system, which is the Introduction's *second* picture (AI-MC). The paper's typology says that picture has no judging third; so what Hohenstein and Jung show is that attribution shifts toward the system even when the system does not judge, which is a stronger premise for the paper's expectation than the dyadic CASA studies, and it should be set apart from them. And the Sundar and Nass finding is not "rated the same content differently according to the source they were told it came from" simpliciter: the lab card (version of record) records that the Computer label was rated friendlier, more playful and more effective than the Programmer/Networker label, with one reversal (greater excitement with the human tutor). The paper's summary is defensible as a gloss; it should not imply a uniform "more human, more positive" pattern.

*Specifying intent.* Viduchinsky's "dual-audience dilemma" (p. 1) could not be verified from Crossref (no abstract). The Ong/Litt pairing is good. Bucher et al.'s "curtailing their outreach to clients" is verified (abstract and p. 10). The invented employee cases are fine as thought experiments and are labeled as such. What is missing is the Simmel p. 169 passage noted above: the dual audience is a general property of speaking before a third, and what algorithmacy adds is that the second audience's rule is hidden and reads the dealings. The part is already tied to feature four in Table 1; the text should say why that feature, and not the mere presence of a second reader, makes this a distinct ability.

*Keeping track.* The folk-theory literature is used accurately (DeVito 2021's adaptive folk theorization is the right cite for "revising could be taught"; Meng 2026's "algorithmic adaptability" is verified at p. 1171). The Orlikowski and Scott (2014) sentence is correct but does not obviously belong under "keeping track," since what they found was valuation practices reconfiguring hotel practices, not hotels detecting changes in a rule. Either say what in that study is about *detecting change* or move it.

*How the parts fit together.* The interdependence paragraph is well made. "Whether telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart is a fourth part… is a question I leave open" is flat and right. The acquisition paragraph is the paper's most empirical and the "No one has measured it" close is earned.

### Where the Account Applies

The ordering by fit is a good device and the honesty about Fradkin ("establishes the arrangement and not the ability") is exactly the right register. The Orlikowski and Scott 2023 quotations are verified (p. 3 for "proprietary algorithms," p. 9 for "may or may not have experienced"). The Hinds and von Krogh quotations are verified verbatim at p. 4 ("may cascade between managers, GenAI, and employees") and p. 6 ("an illustration as well as a thought experiment"; "the process for getting there is opaque"). The Leonardi and Leavell figures check: 83% and 9% at p. 535, "just one tool of many" at p. 526, "17 mentions of unique lawsuits" at p. 537, "not an inherent property of AI tools" at p. 538.

One structural point. The section's upshot ("The usual case is the lopsided triad") undercuts the Simmel section's choice of the arbitrator as the model. A third that binds one party and leaves the other "nearly free" is not an arbitrator between them; it is, as the Research Agenda admits two pages later, closer to *divide et impera* or to the *tertius gaudens* courting one party. The paper raises this in the agenda as an open question. From my seat it is not open: the lopsided triad is Simmel's *tertius gaudens* with the purchaser in the seat (p. 177), and the author should say so in the Simmel section and treat the two-sided case as the limiting form, not the paradigm.

### Discussion

*What the account adds.* The comparison on two dimensions (parties covered; ability versus response) is clear and fair. Zhou et al.'s definition is verified in the working paper, but the manuscript's page numbers (p. 2, p. 8) are working-paper pages; the published *APJHR* article will differ. Check before submission.

*The intermediary as a party.* This is the right objection to anticipate and the answer ("Calling that source a party describes what she faces") is the right kind of answer. It is, however, exactly the place where the paper must address Latour, and it does not. Faraj and Pachidi, Orlikowski and Scott, and Glaser et al. are all in the ANT-adjacent tradition the paper is answering, and in that tradition "intermediary" and "mediator" have fixed, opposite meanings: an intermediary "transports meaning or force without transformation," a mediator "transforms, translates, distorts, and modifies" (Latour 2005, p. 39; the 1994 essay in the lab's library states the same contrast). The paper calls the thing that evaluates and binds "the intermediary" and the thing that only passes messages on "a conduit." In Latour's vocabulary the paper's intermediary is a mediator and its conduit is an intermediary. A referee from that tradition will stop reading at the terminology paragraph. The fix is one paragraph, which I draft in Part 3.

The Newlands quotation (p. 719) could not be verified from the abstract, which has "surveillance with an algorithmic, as opposed to human, observer"; the quoted words "the observer and decision-maker can be a non-human agent" may be in the body on that page. Check.

*The risk paragraph.* Elish's words are "bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilit**ies**" (p. 40, abstract, and again in the body); the manuscript has the singular. Fix. The paragraph's substance (algorithmacy as private repair for a public defect) is one of the paper's best and should not be hedged further than it is.

*Three courses.* Waardenburg et al. and Christin are used accurately. The "broker" here is the human interpreter standing between system and judged; the paper should note that this is Obstfeld's *tertius iungens*, joining the parties, and that the paper's own intermediary is a *tertius gaudens*; the brokerage vocabulary the paper introduced in the Simmel section pays off here and is left unused.

### Research Agenda and Conclusion

The two theory questions are the right ones, and the first (one form or two) I have argued above is answerable from Simmel. The second (operator as employer; instrument of hierarchy rather than third) is genuinely open and well posed. The Krakowski et al. quotation is verified (p. 1425, abstract). The last paragraph is clean.

### Terminology (cross-cutting)

The paper uses four terms that each collide with an established usage, and it addresses only one collision (the statistical sense of "moderated").

| Paper's term | Paper's meaning | Collides with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| intermediary | the thing that evaluates and binds | Latour: an intermediary transports *without* transforming; the paper's intermediary is a Latourian mediator |
| moderated (dyad) | tempered by a text | platform studies: "moderation" is what the platform does to users (Gillespie 2018, Roberts 2019); in the paper the moderated form is the one *without* a platform |
| mediated (avoided) | — | Thompson's "mediated interaction" is the paper's moderated dyad; Simmel's "mediator" is the third who does *not* hold the verdict; by avoiding the word the paper loses both anchors |
| counterpart | the other person | negotiation research's standard term; no collision, but it imports an adversarial frame the paper does not want for a host and a guest |

The paper needs one paragraph, at the point where the terms are introduced, that names the collisions and says which usage it keeps. I draft one in Part 3. "Counterpart" can stay; "intermediary" should either be defended against Latour explicitly or replaced (candidates: "the third," which is Simmel's word and the paper's title; "the judging third"). "Moderated" should go; "text-mediated dyad" is what Thompson and everyone else calls it, and the paper's stated reason for avoiding "mediated" (statistical sense) applies to "moderated" at least as much.

### Load-bearing weak points (what a hostile referee attacks first)

1. **The Simmel reading** (c) above. A referee who opens *AJS* 8(2) at p. 176 finds the paper's intermediary described as the *tertius gaudens* and the "combines positions that Simmel kept apart" sentence falls. As written, it does not survive. Rebuilt as I propose, it survives and is stronger.
2. **"Who holds the verdict"** as the text/third line. A CCO referee produces Kuhn's authoritative text and the line falls. Restated as "is the author reachable, and does the verdict change with the dealings," it survives.
3. **The historical "relied on" sequence.** Unsupported and contradicted by Goody; drop the sequence, keep the claim that the demanding version is now ordinary.
4. **Dyadic evidence for triadic claims.** Admitted; survives because admitted, but the admission should come before the evidence, and Hohenstein and Jung should be separated from the CASA studies as a second-picture (three-position) result.

### Prior-art trap

The novelty claim is carefully scoped ("no work that names this ability or defines it in this way") and the search is disclosed, which is the right practice. From my seat the nearest rivals are: (a) Simmel's *tertius gaudens* and Burt's rendering of it, which supply the *position* but not the competence; (b) Katsh and Rifkin's fourth party, which supplies a non-human judging third but disclosed, in a dispute, with counsel; (c) Jarrahi and Sutherland's algorithmic competencies, which supply the ability but over two parties; (d) Goffman's production format, which supplies the decomposition "interpreting" performs but not as a competence; (e) Viduchinsky's dual-audience dilemma and Meng's algorithmic adaptability, which the paper already names as two of its three parts under other names. The claim "defines the ability over all three parties" survives all five. The claim "builds on Simmel's account of the third party" survives only if the account is read correctly, which currently it is not.
