# Sixty-five real references carry an overreaching argument

All 65 references in `ASSEMBLED.md` exist and match their database records in author, year, title, venue, volume and pages; none is fabricated or misattributed. The problems sit in how the manuscript uses them and in what it claims. Eight citations attach to sentences their sources do not make — Orlikowski & Scott (2023) for a hotelier's interpretive problem, Elish (2019) for workers "absorbing quietly", Hinds & von Krogh (2024) for an employee's weighing of manager against system, Rahman (2021) as a name for the ability, Hargittai (2002) for "help from peers", Wing (2016) for appeals, Yates (1989) for literate staff, Traeger et al. (2020) for a robot's "uncertainty" — and three more drop a source's hedge. The argument has a deeper fault: the definition of algorithmacy (interdependent work through an intermediary that is opaque, binding and adaptive) does not require an algorithm, so a court or a blind referee satisfies it, and the Conclusion concedes "peer review with hidden scoring"; the stated criterion (the form of the relation among the people) is not the criterion the framework uses; the three-parts-from-three-features derivation is asserted, not shown; and none of the four cases has an intermediary that binds both parties. Against the literature, the strict novelty claim survives — no published work names and defines this capability over the triad — but prior work (Jarrahi & Sutherland 2019, Cameron & Rahman 2022, Chan 2022) already describes the structure and what workers do in it, and the premise that "a text judges no one, decides nothing" collides with the CCO work the target journal publishes. *Organization Theory* applies a two-part test, coherent argument plus significant contribution, under an 11,000-word all-in cap, double-blind review and a SAGE policy that requires disclosure of generative AI use. The manuscript matches the journal's form on every measured count except two (65 references against a 2026 floor of 94; hedging at half the published minimum), but its theoretical exposition is five sentences of Simmel against a population that spends four to twenty-four paragraphs on its borrowed tradition, its figure is undrawn, its header says the author has not yet read it, and it reproduces the scaffold of Schoeneborn, Dobusch & Seidl (2026) closely enough that the model's authors would recognise it by page three. An editor who received it today would most likely desk-reject on contribution and completeness or, if sent out, return a reject; the same facts, fixed before submission, make a defensible case for review.

## The triad holds; the definition admits a court

The organizing idea survives: an intermediary that evaluates and binds two people is a party, not a channel, and the participant's position is equivocal (one signal, several sources that fit) rather than uncertain. The argument built on it does not survive as written, for three reasons that reach the thesis ([argument_internal_logic.md](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/reviews/research_notes/Algorithmacy%20OT%20manuscript%20review/argument_internal_logic.md)).

**The definition does not require an algorithm.** Algorithmacy is "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive" (Introduction ¶6). Run the test cases the manuscript does not: a court binds both parties, deliberates in private, moves with law and precedent, and holds authority the litigants did not confer; a blind referee evaluates the author, binds through the editor, and works by a hidden rule; an auctioneer binds buyer and seller by a public rule, so the framework has to say what ability a non-opaque triad calls for and cannot. The Conclusion concedes the point when it lists "peer review with hidden scoring" as a site. Litigants have needed to interpret verdicts, address bench and opponent in one filing, and track a moving body of rules for as long as there have been courts. The manuscript therefore has to do one of two things: add a feature courts lack and derive something from it, or accept that algorithmacy is the litigant's ability under a new name, which would cost it the oracy→literacy→algorithmacy sequence and "one does not grow into the next" (Discussion A ¶3). The materials for the first option are in the text and uncounted — "presents itself as not a party" (Form ¶8), "was not given its power by the two people" (Moderators ¶2), "a change in the rule is not announced" (Three Features ¶4), no forum of appeal outside the system (Conclusion ¶2). Suddaby's standard, read from the local PDF, is the one a reviewer will apply: "Finding a single exception is often fatal to a construct because it implies that any proposition associated with the construct is false" (Suddaby 2010, p. 349), and the same essay warns against packing scope conditions into the definiens (p. 347), which is the shape of the definition as written.

**The stated criterion is not the working criterion.** The manuscript says abilities are sorted by "the form of the relation between the people involved" (Introduction ¶5) and then, one section later, by "the relation itself (or, to be more precise, what the thing in the middle does to the two people)" (Form ¶5). Those are different criteria. The second is the one the framework uses, since opacity, bindingness and adaptivity are properties of the intermediary. The first cannot place the manuscript's own first rival: "a basis in the person and the system shows no second person" (Form ¶2), and a relation with one person has no relation among people to sort by. Related terms shift under the same pressure. "Direct dyad" is person–machine in Introduction ¶1 and person–person in Form ¶6. "Counterpart" is the system in Triadic ¶5 and the Conclusion and the other person in Moderators ¶1. The third-party criterion is given as "acts" (Moderators ¶2), but the relay in a chained dyad "can change it, forget part of it, or add to it" and is classed as a dyad; on the "acts" criterion the manuscript's three forms are four. Where the criterion is stated precisely, as "evaluates and binds" (Algorithmacy ¶1; Conclusion ¶3), it is principled, and the text should say only that.

**The derivation is asserted.** Of fourteen load-bearing steps, four are argued, six cited, four asserted, and the two that carry the thesis are asserted one sentence apart: "this means that a person in a triad deals with an intermediary that is opaque … binding … and adaptive" (Form ¶8), where nothing in "is a party" entails those three, and "I assume that two people in this position lose the conditions on which their coordination rested" (Moderators ¶2). The mapping of parts to features is then not one-to-one. Specifying intent is derived from bindingness, but its premise is that the system *reads* what she writes to the counterpart, a channel property a marketplace that scores only the buyer's rating lacks; and she must write "in a form that survives the processing", which she cannot know without the rule, so the demand turns on opacity. Keeping track adds unannounced change, which is opacity over time. The promised pairing with Okhuysen & Bechky's three conditions is stated once, "Interpreting thereby restores accountability" (Practising ¶2), and the text's own causes for the lost conditions align opacity with common understanding, not accountability; nothing says what the other two parts restore. The Conclusion asks whether attributing a change to system or person is "a fourth part of its own" and cites Rahman for it being "a task in itself", which is the limit of the derivation stated in the wrong place. The archived Lima paper had the careful version: "That mapping is what the phrase 'derived from the structure of the triad' means in this paper, and it is the whole of what the phrase claims" ([Lima archive, l. 167](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/lima_pdw/archive/2026-09-10_PAPER_submitted.md)); the manuscript dropped the qualification and kept the claim.

Below these three sit faults that weaken support. The evidence for the three parts comes from settings the manuscript classifies as dyads: smart replies (Hohenstein & Jung), a chat agent (Sundar & Kim), screen tutoring (Sundar & Nass), a feed algorithm (DeVito), a robot in a group (Traeger). A reviewer will hold that either the evidence is irrelevant or the thesis that dyadic abilities do not carry over is false. The one instance that fits all three parts, "she runs her explanation through a language model that tunes it for automated scoring and writes nothing a person can use" (Failing ¶2), is the Lima pilot's student at the gate, presented without a source as if general. "One ability does not grow into the next" is argued by analogy only, and the one number that bore on it (Zhou et al.'s r = .37 between general digital competence and algorithmic competency) was in the Lima version and is gone. The Discussion's three explanatory claims rest on Christin (newsroom and court tools with no bound counterpart), Hargittai and Elish, none of which carries them; the aggregation argument that made the third claim falsifiable ("identical friction rates would falsify the claim", Lima l. 193) was cut. Sensemaking is absent though interpreting an equivocal result under a hidden rule is sensemaking under a new label, and a theory-journal reviewer will raise it first; "ability" is never defined, and the Lima subsection that did so ("Competency, Not Skill or Capability") is gone. Several of the rhetorical moves a reviewer will test ("simpler than a list", "keeps what the rivals describe", "I thereby depart from earlier work", "a longer line of work") are flagged in the ledger as the drafter's inferences the author should confirm or cut (S-005, S-018, S-068–S-069).

## Prior work describes the structure, not the ability

The strict novelty claim stands. Across Consensus, OpenAlex, Crossref, the 2022–2026 tables of contents of *ASQ*, *Organization Science*, *Organization Studies* and *OT*, and the open web, **no published work names a capability and defines it as doing interdependent work with another person through an algorithm that is opaque, binding on both and adaptive** ([argument_against_literature.md §1, §7](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/reviews/research_notes/Algorithmacy%20OT%20manuscript%20review/argument_against_literature.md)). The word "algorithmacy" has no verified prior use in print: every open-web hit is the author's own (Substack posts of 22 February, 24 April and 3 June 2026; algorithmacy.org; the conference CFP), OpenAlex returns exactly one full-text hit, a paywalled 1995 Springer chapter by Oberschelp whose sense is unknown, and the Leese & Matzner 2016 talk the February post mentions has no findable record ([OpenAlex W1365843](https://openalex.org/W1365843); [Substack, 22 Feb 2026](https://rogerhuntphdcand.substack.com/p/algorithmacy-and-the-co-optation)). Two consequences: the author's online publication of the term, definition and conference should be disclosed under prior-dissemination rules, and the Substack sentence that the term "has never appeared in a published academic text" should not enter the manuscript until someone opens Oberschelp's chapter.

The loose claim does not stand. **Jarrahi & Sutherland (2019) coined "algorithmic competency"** for the same population and the same asymmetry — "workers' understanding of algorithms that assign and assess work conducted on gig platforms, and learning how to work with and around those algorithms … sensemaking, circumventing and manipulating" (p. 9) — and their data are triadic (a participant "discovered that clients could leave hidden feedback" and watched his score drop; another leaves milestones in the messenger "as evidence just in case of a disputes with a client") while their Figure 1 draws two nodes, worker and algorithm ([Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-15742-5_55)). That paper is both the closest prior use of the manuscript's noun and the exhibit the manuscript lacks for its claim that existing accounts reduce the triad to a dyad. Zhou et al. (2025), which the manuscript treats as the nearest rival, descends from it. Cameron & Rahman (2022) already theorise the triad in *Organization Science*: "customers rate workers, and algorithms tally and track these ratings"; "workers' everyday interactions with customers can influence and manipulate algorithms in ways that platforms cannot always observe" ([Org Sci](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2021.1557)). Chan (2022) names metrics that "mediate the trilateral relationship between platforms, workers, and customers"; Cameron (2022) describes the "relational game" around ratings; Bucher et al. (2021) describe "anticipatory compliance" that curtails outreach to clients; Waardenburg et al. (2022) describe "algorithmic brokers" who interpret for others, which is the manuscript's "an expert interprets for them" mechanism uncited. Two of the three parts have prior names: Viduchinsky (2026) calls specifying intent the "dual-audience dilemma where users perform for both humans and machines"; Meng (2024) calls keeping track "algorithmic adaptability", and DeVito (2021) already makes ongoing re-theorization a literacy requirement. The manuscript's novelty on the parts rests on their derivation from the features, which is what §"The triad holds" shows is asserted. The claim that no existing account "describes the ability" will read as false to anyone holding these papers; it survives as "no account names it, or defines it over the triad with a third that binds both".

Three premises are defeated outright. **"A text judges no one, decides nothing, and does not change once it is written"** (Moderators ¶1) collides with the communication-as-constitutive-of-organization literature, which the target journal publishes: Cooren (2004) classifies "the different types of action that texts can be said to be performing"; Taylor et al. (1996) describe the "double translation" between text and conversation; Lehtinen & Pälli (2020) show appraisal forms "present demands" in performance-appraisal interviews, a text-mediated evaluative triad; Cooren (2020) and Dion et al. (2025) are in *OT* itself ([Cooren 2004](https://doi.org/10.1177/1350508404041998); [Lehtinen & Pälli 2020](https://doi.org/10.1515/text-2019-0121)). The moderator/third-party cut cannot rest on texts being inert; it can rest on "evaluates and binds both". **"Oracy belongs to the direct dyad"** and **"literacy to the moderated dyad"** are the manuscript's constructions with no source behind them: Wilkinson's oracy is a general curriculum ability coined "by analogy to literacy and numeracy" ([Chartered College](https://my.chartered.college/?p=31170)); Goffman (1981) made even face-to-face talk multi-party with ratified and unratified hearers ([via Dynel 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-818x.2011.00286.x)); Ong (1975) made the reader a fiction the writer constructs ([PMLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/pmla/article/writers-audience-is-always-a-fiction/E4939B97B04B5F6073284D3A4497453D)); and Thompson (1995) already sorted interaction by relational form, separating the letter (dyadic, mediated) from the book (one-to-many), which makes him the antecedent the manuscript should cite for its own move ([LSE working paper](https://www.lse.ac.uk/media-and-communications/assets/documents/research/working-paper-series/EWP05.pdf)). The historical order of the "-acy" coinages is literacy → numeracy (Crowther 1959) → oracy and graphicacy (1965), so the sequence oracy→literacy is a sequence of names, not of abilities arriving in history. What survives from Olson is the warrant that writing "represents the locutionary act leaving illocutionary force underspecified" (1994, p. 93 per the NRFH review); "the reader's chance to ask" is nowhere in the project's record of the book.

On Simmel the manuscript is mostly right and wrong in one clause, as the audit table records; a reader of the 1950 Wolff translation will also want the manuscript to engage Pyyhtinen (2018), who argues "the dyad must already be a triad" and that a nonhuman third is "not the triad which Simmel had in mind", and to say whose purposes the algorithm carries. If the party is the operator acting through a rule, then the manager-with-GenAI case (operator: the manager's own firm) and the planning case (operator: the planners) are not triads of the kind Stark & Vanden Broeck describe. The organizational literature also already has the manuscript's two terms the other way round: Latour's *mediators* "transform, translate, distort and modify" while *intermediaries* transport without transformation, and Obstfeld, Borgatti & Davis (2014) call the third that only carries a "conduit" ([RSO](https://doi.org/10.1108/S0733-558X(2014)0000040007)). "Moderator" carries a statistical sense for *OT* readers. The worker–platform–customer triad is older than the three papers the manuscript credits (Fuller & Smith 1991; Lopez 2010; Havard et al. 2009), and Curchod et al. already frame it as "an implicit coalition between buyers and the platform owner".

"Binding on both" is the feature under most pressure, and it is the one on which the archived paper rested novelty ("Novelty survives on *mutual bindingness* and *interdependent joint work*", a defence the manuscript no longer states). The empirical record supports "opaque" and "adaptive" for platform scoring (Rahman 2021; Heiland 2023; Glaser et al. 2021) and shows "binding on both" holding only in some two-sided reputation systems — Airbnb's simultaneous reveal (Fradkin et al. 2021), ride-hail rider deactivation since 2019 — and failing on eBay (buyers rate sellers; sellers since 2008 may leave only positive ratings), on hotel platforms (anonymous raters "may or may not have experienced [the hotel] in person", Orlikowski & Scott 2023, p. 9), and in the manager–GenAI literature, where managers hold override rights (Lin et al. 2026) ([argument_against_literature.md §5](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/reviews/research_notes/Algorithmacy%20OT%20manuscript%20review/argument_against_literature.md)). One finding cuts against "more turns, more ability": Rahman reports that highly experienced freelancers "did not gain an advantage in working out the algorithm" and that dependence and prior setbacks sorted responses, which the manuscript's research agenda concedes (question 2) and its mechanism does not answer; Jiang et al. (2025) supply the learning curve the manuscript asserts, from over a million delivery orders, and Lee et al. (2015) and Yao et al. (2021) supply the limits of peer help it also asserts.

The uncited works a reviewer will most expect, in the lab's ranked order, begin with Cameron & Rahman (2022), Jarrahi & Sutherland (2019), Orlikowski & Scott (2014) with Scott & Orlikowski (2012), Cooren (2004), Thompson (1995), Waardenburg et al. (2022), Lebovitz et al. (2022), Rahman & Valentine (2021), Cameron (2022), Chan (2022), Glaser, Pollock & D'Adderio (2021, in *OT*), and Bailey et al. (2022), the relational perspective Hinds & von Krogh extend ([argument_against_literature.md §7(d)](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/reviews/research_notes/Algorithmacy%20OT%20manuscript%20review/argument_against_literature.md)). Thirty references the 8 October draft held, including Faraj & Pachidi (2021) in this journal and the folk-theory line (Bucher 2017; Cotter 2019; DeVito et al. 2018) from which "keeping track" descends, were cut in the last build; only Stark & Pais survived, and it is the orphan.

## *Organization Theory* tests coherence and magnitude, not propositions

The journal's standard is stated in three places that agree ([ot_journal_standards.md](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/reviews/research_notes/Algorithmacy%20OT%20manuscript%20review/ot_journal_standards.md)). The EGOS submission page sets the hallmark: "a theoretical argument is coherently developed and laid out"; "Meaningful new implications or insights for theory must be present in all articles published in the journal"; submissions must "clearly signal and communicate the nature of their theoretical contribution in relation to the existing literature" ([EGOS: Manuscript submission](https://egos.org/organization_theory/manuscript-submission)). The founding editorial adds the rejection ground: in-scope papers are turned away when they "fall short of our expectations towards a strong and significant theoretical contribution", and "Papers that are published in the first couple of issues of the journal already give a good sense of the magnitude of the contribution that we are looking for" (Cornelissen & Höllerer 2020, p. 3, [PDF](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/127903/Repub_127903_O-A.pdf)). The same editorial defines the journal against the proposition template of "most journals that currently publish theory papers" (p. 1), welcomes "philosophically informed essays, process theorizing, perspective pieces and critical essays" and "problem-driven theoretical work around contemporary problems and societal challenges, such as the automatization of labour, new forms of work" (p. 2), and asks each paper to "explicitly signal its theoretical aims and position and maintain congruity with this approach throughout" ([SAGE instructions, captured 7 October](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-algorithmacy-ot/submissions/algorithmacy_ot/JOURNAL_SPEC.md)). Scope excludes "papers that draw on empirical data to make data-driven theoretical claims" ([EGOS: Aims and Scope](https://egos.org/organization_theory/aims-and-scope)); the 2026 configurational call gives the current line on cases, "Limited empirical illustrations are welcome where they advance theory" ([EGOS CfP](https://www.egos.org/jart/prj3/egos/releases/de/upload/Uploads/CfP/CfP_Configurational%20Nature.pdf)). A construct-defining paper is "perfectly legitimate" but competes on coherence and magnitude, not on propositions, and "construct clarity" is a named plenary at the editors' own Winter Workshop, so it is a live reviewing criterion ([7th OT Winter Workshop CfP](https://www.egos.org/jart/prj3/egos/releases/de/upload/Uploads/CfP/7th%20OT%20Workshop%20CfP.pdf)).

The formal requirements, from the 7 October capture of the SAGE page (the live page returned HTTP 403 to every fetch on 9 October): **11,000 words including references**, an unstructured abstract of at most 300 words, 3–10 keywords, unnumbered three-level headings, APA references with every in-text citation in the list and vice versa, a fully anonymized manuscript and a separate title page carrying a six-item Statements and Declarations block, a cover letter saying why the manuscript suits the journal, Word or LaTeX, figures at 300 dpi, and a statement in the Acknowledgements and cover letter of "any writing or editing assistance from a third-party". The review process is double-blind, two independent reviews are required for a Revise or Accept, the founding aim was a first decision within two months and a final decision within two rounds, and "Manuscripts may be desk rejected without peer review" for being out of scope or otherwise unsuitable. No acceptance rate, desk-rejection rate or reviewer count is published anywhere reachable. The journal is open access with an APC of US$1,000 after the five-year founding waiver, with affiliation discounts up to 100% and possible EGOS support. The team in 2026: Co-Editors-in-Chief Rick Delbridge and Sigrid Quack, Consulting Editor Markus Höllerer, nine Associate Editors including Vern Glaser and Joel Gehman ([EGOS: Editors](https://egos.org/organization_theory/editors_and_editorial-board)). Höllerer facilitated Roundtable 4 at the Lima PDW on 6–7 October 2026, which listed the author's own paper LIMA2026-002, so the Consulting Editor has read the workshop version; blind review would treat a submission as new ([Lima programme PDF](https://www.udep.edu.pe/cceeee/wp-content/uploads/sites/99/2026/10/PDW_Lima_2026_Programme.pdf)).

The AI policy governs this manuscript directly. SAGE's page distinguishes assistive AI ("tools that make suggestions to improve or enhance your own work", no disclosure) from generative AI, which "must be disclosed upon submission so the editorial team can evaluate the content generated", in the methods or acknowledgements; it lists prohibited uses including "Generation of data or submission using a series of prompts", "Plagiarism or inappropriate attribution to prior sources" and "Fabricated references or falsified claims"; and it states that submissions "will not be rejected solely because of the disclosed use" but that undisclosed inappropriate use lets the editor "reject the submission at any time during the publishing process" ([SAGE: ChatGPT and Generative AI](https://www.sagepub.com/en-us/nam/chatgpt-and-generative-ai)). The journal's own LLM editorial (Cornelissen, Höllerer, Boxenbaum, Faraj & Gehman 2024) urges authors "to be careful and reflexive when they use LLMs to assist (parts of) their theorizing, and to transparently disclose this use", and closes with "a statement of Organization Theory's editorial policy on the use of LLMs" that no researcher could retrieve ([abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/26317877241239056)). A manuscript whose header reads "Claude's wording of the author's argument; not yet read or ruled on by the author. Title, abstract and keywords are Claude's proposals" is generative use under SAGE's tiers, and whether it is "assisted" or "generated … using a series of prompts" is a judgement SAGE leaves to editors; the defensible position depends on the author having read and ruled on every sentence, which the header says has not happened.

What an editor would most likely do. The journal would not desk-reject on scope: the topic is wanted, the form is right, and a cover letter can say in two sentences that this would be the first algorithm paper since October 2024 building on the journal's three most-cited technology pieces. My reading of the evidence: submitted today, the manuscript draws a desk rejection on contribution and completeness or, if it reaches reviewers, a reject in the first round, because the three thesis-level faults in §"The triad holds" are the questions a reviewer asks first and the manuscript's answers are a parenthesis, a concession in the Conclusion, and silence. ## Conclusion

The audit changes the picture in one way the author should weigh before anything else: the manuscript's weakest sentences and its strongest idea are the same sentences. The claims that outrun their sources (the hotelier, the employee weighing manager against system, workers absorbing quietly) are the claims that would illustrate the construct if a documented case carried them, and the one instance that fits all three parts is the author's own pilot student, uncited. The repair is therefore not a citation sweep. It is choosing cases in which an algorithm demonstrably evaluates and binds two interdependent people (two-sided reputation systems with simultaneous reveal; ride-hail after rider deactivation; peer review with hidden scoring, which the manuscript already names) and then counting the features that separate those cases from a court, which the text has scattered through its asides. Done that way, the court objection becomes the argument's spine rather than its defeat, "binding on both" stops being the weakest feature, and the derivation gains the fourth or fifth feature it already uses without admitting it.

The second shift is in what novelty can be claimed. Prior work has named the structure, catalogued what workers do in it, and coined "algorithmic competency" for the same population; what no one has done is define a single capability over the triad with a third that binds both and derive its parts from the intermediary's features. That narrower claim is both true and closer to what *Organization Theory* asks for, since the journal's test is a coherent argument that signals its contribution against the existing literature, and the existing literature here is larger than the manuscript's 65 references show. The headroom under the word limit is the author's to spend on that exposition. What the author decides about the template and the AI disclosure is a separate question, but it is one the journal will ask, and the header as it stands answers it in a way no editor can accept.
