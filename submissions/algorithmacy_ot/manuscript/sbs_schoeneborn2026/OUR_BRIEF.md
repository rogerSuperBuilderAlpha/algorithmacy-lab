## The research question

What must a person be able to do in order to work with another person when an algorithm stands between
them, evaluates both, and binds both?

Following the model, the paper does not put this as a question. It puts it as a dilemma between two
pictures of the system and carries it under one name, **the dyad dilemma**: an account that keeps the
other person in view makes the system a channel that decides nothing, and an account that makes the
system powerful loses the other person.

## The thesis, and its shape

Working with others takes a different ability for each form of relation. Oracy belongs to direct dyads
and chains of them. Literacy belongs to dyads moderated by a text. Algorithmacy belongs to triads, in
which an algorithm evaluates and binds two people. Algorithmacy has three parts, and each part answers
one feature of the algorithm: interpreting answers opacity, specifying intent answers bindingness, and
keeping track answers adaptivity.

The shape is the model's: **two stacked choices**. First choose one criterion out of several on offer —
the form of the relation. Then say what the ability is, inside the form that is new — three parts from
three features.

| The model | Ours |
| --- | --- |
| Two camps: narrow and broad views of organization | Two pictures: the system as the one a person deals with (a direct dyad), and the system as what passes between two people (a moderated dyad) |
| The openness-specificity dilemma | The dyad dilemma |
| An existing third way: organizationality | An existing third view: the triadic view of platforms (Stark & Vanden Broeck; Curchod et al.; Cameron) |
| Its gap: criteria fragmented, degrees unexplained | Its gap: it describes the arrangement from the platform's side and does not say what the person must be able to do |
| Proposal one: one criterion, collective actorhood | Proposal one: one criterion, the form of the relation |
| Departs from configurational and stage-based logics | Departs from accounts that define the ability by a list of what to know, or by how well a worker adapts |
| Proposal two: collective actorhood is gradual, charged and discharged by attribution practices | Proposal two: algorithmacy has three parts, each demanded by one feature of the intermediary |
| Consequence: organizationality rises and falls over time | Consequence: the parts form a cycle, and people who have had more turns hold more of the ability |

## Contribution moves, in the order the paper will make them

1. Introduction: one criterion that tells the three abilities apart; an account of how the third works.
2. Section 2: the existing work on the triad, and three ways existing accounts of ability place the
   system (as the other party; as a channel; as the worker's only counterpart).
3. Section 3: the choice of the form of relation as the criterion, with reasons.
4. Section 4: the framework — dyads and triads, the three features, the three parts with published cases,
   and how each part fails.
5. Discussion: two contributions, a subsection each — the abilities placed on one axis; the co-opted
   person's side of platform organization.
6. Conclusion: the model ends on a research agenda of four questions. **The draft has none.** It ends on
   three courses open to an organization. The author removed the research questions on 8 October as
   hedging. Whether to restore them for this section is the author's decision.

## Our register, where it differs from the model's

- **Key terms, held fixed:** algorithmacy; oracy; literacy; direct dyad; moderated dyad; triad;
  intermediary; counterpart; opaque, binding, adaptive; interpreting, specifying intent, keeping track.
- **"I", not "we".** One author.
- **No modal hedging on our own claims.** The model's "can", "may" and "can be expected to" are not
  carried over.
- **Plain verbs.** treat, define, propose, argue, depart, hold, explain, show, provide, leave out.
- **Nothing figurative.** The model's one sustained figure is its own construct. Ours has none.
