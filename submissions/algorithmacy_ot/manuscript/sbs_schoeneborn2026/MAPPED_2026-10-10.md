# Toward an Account of Algorithmacy: A Triadic View of Working Through Algorithms

## Abstract

⟦Abstract drafted by Claude from the author's introduction and rulings of 10 October.⟧ Organizations place people under algorithmic management who lack algorithmacy, which is the equivalent of giving books to people who lack literacy. Recent work in organization theory has moved past treating generative AI and platforms as tools, and it leaves open how someone coordinates with another person through a cooptive, opaque, adaptive intermediary. I argue that existing answers preserve a dyadic understanding: they set aside the third actor, treat the mediator as a passive channel, or count only a person and a system. I distinguish three sensibilities by what stands between two people. Oracy navigates the direct dyad, literacy the dyad moderated by a text, and algorithmacy the triad that an algorithm creates when it evaluates and binds people it did not ask. That triad is opaque in principle: knowing everything about the algorithm leaves a person no better off, while people who know nothing about it navigate it successfully. Algorithmacy operates through three competencies: interpreting outputs that look the same whatever produced them, specifying intent so that the people one is coordinated with can trace a result back, and keeping track of a rule that updates constantly by sensing its changes. Each gives back in part a condition of coordination that the intermediary takes away. Generative AI, on this account, is itself a platform, and it scales the arrangement up as the printing press scaled up the text.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy, algorithmic management, coordination, literacy, platforms

## Introduction

⟦How⟧ organizations adopt and understand the new platforms and services enabled by generative AI ⟦has attracted growing interest among organizational scholars in recent years⟧. While global leaders promote these technologies, quieter displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are what actually reconfigure how organizations perform (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023). Focusing on those quieter shifts allows us to recognize how generative AI sits inside the work and reshapes it, functioning more as—but not quite—a "counterpart" in a system ⟦of work that includes its design,⟧ implementation, and use (Anthony et al., 2023). Hoping to install a plug-and-play intelligence ignores that organizing now depends on relations among humans and algorithmic black boxes (Stelmaszak et al., 2026), which ⟦may⟧ reduce visibility and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024) while paradoxically manufacturing a false sense of epistemological security (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a cooptive, opaque, adaptive intermediary. Generative AI is itself a platform: it is the printing press of the platform, which scales up rather than changes the principle, and the scale-up itself introduces new issues.

Organizations have been doing the equivalent of giving books to people who lack literacy. We heap algorithmic management systems on people who lack algorithmacy, or the sensibility to navigate opaque triadic coordinative cooptation. As traditional firm boundaries dissolve, these algorithmic intermediaries co-opt (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), i.e., evaluate and bind ⟦, while downplaying their own collective actorhood⟧ (Schoeneborn et al., 2026). Evaluating and binding is platform co-optation; classical co-optation is Selznick's (1949). Building on the ⟦discussion in *Organization Theory* and *Organization Science*⟧ and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are trying to preserve a ⟦dyadic⟧ understanding ⟦, direct like oracy or moderated like literacy⟧. We do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or relying on a dyadic ontology that is blind to the emergent, binding power of the triad. ⟦In this paper, I therefore aim to specify how someone coordinates through such an intermediary;⟧ the how I propose also requires that we refactor the why.

Oracy is the sensibility to navigate direct ⟦and chained⟧ dyadic forms through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate ⟦dyads⟧: literacy is a kind of dyad, a moderated one, where oracy is a direct dyad. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. The algorithm creates an in-principle opaque, dynamic, mediated triad: I can tell you everything about the algorithm and you would not be any better off, while people who know nothing about it successfully navigate it. Drawing on recent views of imagination as a relational capacity enacted in the encounter between bodies, materials, and circumstances (Porsfelt et al., 2026), algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with the unseen. As a sensibility like literacy and oracy, it operates through the competencies to: 1) interpret idempotent outputs to determine the variable inputs; 2) specify intent so that those one is coordinated with have an easier time tracing back, which is distinct from prompting; and 3) keep track of rules that update constantly and unpredictably, which is less documenting them than sensing them as dynamic flows. We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

## The Triadic View of Platforms

{C2.001} The view that a platform sets up a relation of three, and not of two (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), has gained ground in organization theory in recent years (e.g., Cameron, 2022, 2024; Cameron & Rahman, 2022; Chan, 2022; Curchod et al., 2020; Kornberger et al., 2017; Rahman & Valentine, 2021; Vallas & Schor, 2020). {C2.002} Its figure is older than platforms and was drawn by sociologists of service work, who described a triangle of worker, manager, and customer (Fuller & Smith, 1991; Havard et al., 2009; Korczynski, 2013; Lopez, 2010). {C2.003} Following Stark and Pais (2020), Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe the people on a platform not as commanded, as in a hierarchy, nor as contracting, as in a market, nor as collaborating, as in a network, but as drawn in and organized by a platform that does not employ them (p. 5). {C2.004} Scholars have studied the relation in online marketplaces (Curchod et al., 2020), platform labor (Cameron, 2024), hotel ranking (Orlikowski & Scott, 2014, 2023), crowdwork (Bellesia et al., 2023), and reputation systems in which each party to a transaction reviews the other (Fradkin et al., 2021).

{C2.005} What the view still has to specify is the sensibility a person needs in order to work with another person inside such a relation. {C2.006} Existing accounts of that sensibility, however, are scattered across fields, and each counts fewer than three parties. {C2.007} In particular, different authors (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020; Spitzberg, 2006) give the sensibility different names. {C2.008} These names include AI literacy, algorithmic literacy, computer-mediated communication competence, algorithmic competency, gig literacies, and, for the responses of people under evaluation, reactivity. {C2.009} Different authors have also placed the system differently in relation to the people involved.

{C2.010} Scholars who place the system as the other party treat the arrangement as a pair made up of one person and one machine. {C2.011} For example, Guzman and Lewis (2020) propose to study AI systems as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). {C2.012} This placement fits a person who is using a chatbot or reading a tool's output. {C2.013} For instance, radiologists who use a diagnostic tool have to deal with its opacity (Lebovitz et al., 2022), whereas a person who works with other people through the system has those people to deal with as well. {C2.014} A similar placement is followed in work on AI literacy, whose best-known statement lists what a person should understand about AI, including the human role in it, and has no item on working with a second person through the system (Long & Magerko, 2020; see also Gagrčin et al., 2026; Hargittai et al., 2020; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). {C2.015} This placement is useful for studying how people respond to a machine socially, which is the finding it grew out of (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996).

{C2.016} Scholars who place the system as a channel treat the arrangement as two people with a technology between them. {C2.017} For example, building on work that showed people learning from each other's replies to carry personal meaning in plain text (Walther, 1992, 1996), Spitzberg (2006) describes communicating well through a computer as a matter between two people who judge each other. {C2.018} In this line the technology first carries what the two people write; it then modifies, augments, or generates their messages (Hancock et al., 2020); and in each case what is studied is what the two people come to think of each other. {C2.019} Accordingly, when suggested replies raise trust in a chat partner, or move blame from the partner to the system when a chat goes badly (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020), the finding is about the relation between the two people. {C2.020} Like the first placement, this one is useful within its range, which is two people who answer each other through a technology that issues no verdict on either.

{C2.021} Finally, scholars who study work on platforms place the system as the thing the worker has to understand and adapt to. {C2.022} Of particular relevance to the approach I develop in this paper, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) have proposed "algorithmic competency" for workers' understanding of the algorithms that assign and assess their work and their learning to work with and around them. {C2.023} On that basis, Zhou et al. (2025) have built and tested a questionnaire that measures workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 3 of the working paper [AUTHOR: replace with the page in the published article, which I could not open]; see also Hong et al., 2026). {C2.024} This includes even an item on putting right an unfair result. {C2.025} Compared to the first two placements, this one has the clear advantage of taking the system's power into account, since it treats the system as something that assigns and evaluates work. {C2.026} However, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) do not carry the client into the construct, although clients appear throughout their interviews. {C2.027} While their workers watch clients and keep records in case of a dispute, their figure has two nodes, the worker and the algorithm, and the construct covers the same two.

## What Stands Between as a Defining Criterion

{C3.001} The preceding section has shown that the triadic view still lacks an account of the sensibility it implies, and that existing accounts name that sensibility differently and place the system differently. {C3.002} To compare these accounts and to add to them, it is first necessary to settle what distinguishes one such sensibility from another. {C3.003} Only then can it be said what is new about working through an algorithm and what is not.

{C3.004} At present, the three placements summarized above each offer a different basis for the sensibility: what a person understands about the system, how two people communicate through it, and how well a worker adapts to it. {C3.005} A choice has to be made among these bases. {C3.006} Like any such choice, this one decides what researchers who adopt it will see—a basis in the person and the system shows no second person, and a basis in two people shows no system that gives a verdict. {C3.007} In making this point, I follow Georg Simmel's (1902a, 1902b; see also 1950) argument that the number of members conditions the form of a group. {C3.008} As Simmel argued, a third member changes more than the count, and what the third does to the other two decides what kind of relation they are in—a point he made by setting the mediator beside the arbitrator:

> So long as the third party works as a real mediator, the ending of the conflict rests finally in the hands of the parties themselves. By choice of the arbitrator they have put this ultimate decision out of their own hands. (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)

{C3.009} Over the following sections, I make the case for adopting what stands between two people, and what it does to them, as the criterion that distinguishes the sensibilities people need in order to work together. {C3.010} While aligning to a certain extent with Thompson (1995, 2020), who sorts interaction into face-to-face, mediated, and mediated quasi-interaction by the medium and by whether the audience is specific, my criterion differs in sorting by whether the thing in the middle evaluates and binds the two people, and it therefore reaches the case in which a system stands between them and settles matters for both. {C3.011} The criterion also keeps what each of the three placements describes. {C3.012} I treat what they describe as "secondary" in the sense that a system does address the person who uses it, does carry what passes between two people, and does hold power over a worker, and none of these by itself settles what form the person is working in. {C3.013} This allows the three bodies of work to be read together.

{C3.014} I give the reasons for this choice in turn. {C3.015} First, basing the account on one criterion makes it simpler than accounts that define the sensibility by a list. {C3.016} Both the list of things a person should understand about AI (Long & Magerko, 2020) and the questionnaire on algorithmic competency (Zhou et al., 2025) require not only a reason why these items belong and others do not—but also an account of how the items relate to each other. {C3.017} By contrast, a criterion that asks one question of the arrangement requires no list. {C3.018} Instead, what stands between the two people (or, to be more precise, whether it evaluates and binds them) indicates what a person must be able to do, since the conditions it takes from the two people are the ones the sensibility has to give back.

{C3.020} Second, although this criterion is one among others that could have been chosen, it has advantages beyond its simplicity. {C3.021} One advantage is that organization theory already distinguishes forms of coordination from one another (e.g., Powell, 1990). {C3.022} Similarly, Puranam et al. (2014) define a form of organizing as a set of solutions to problems that every organization has to solve. {C3.023} Building on the criterion therefore connects an account of individual sensibility to the field's accounts of forms. {C3.024} As Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 1) put it:

> Whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted.

{C3.025} Other scholars on whom the triadic view draws also describe an arrangement by the positions it gives people. {C3.026} Selznick (1949) defines classical co-optation as "absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization" (p. 13) and notes that what is shared is "the responsibility for power rather than power itself" (p. 14), whereas platform co-optation, as I use the term, is being evaluated and bound by an intermediary one did not choose. {C3.027} Kornberger et al. (2017) similarly show platforms coordinating people they do not employ through rating and ranking, while Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) define coordination as "the process of interaction that integrates a collective set of interdependent tasks" (p. 463) and identify the conditions that integration depends on.

{C3.028} In addition to being shared within organization theory, the criterion has the advantage of reaching the sensibilities that came before algorithmacy. {C3.029} For example, oracy, the sensibility to speak and to listen (Wilkinson, 1965), belongs to the direct dyad, in which I speak to you and you answer, while literacy belongs to the moderated dyad, in which a text stands between two people and records what was said without settling how it was meant (Olson, 1994). {C3.030} Yates (1989) describes American firms between about 1850 and 1920 coming to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files. {C3.031} And this is in line with the wider literature on texts in organizations, which has shown that documents do more than carry messages (e.g., Cooren, 2004; Kuhn, 2008).

{C3.032} Having proposed what stands between two people as the criterion, I turn to specifying the form that is new. {C3.033} A number of overlapping descriptions of the relation of three have been put forward in the literature on platforms (Chan, 2022; Curchod et al., 2020) as well as in the wider literature on evaluation at work (Fuller & Smith, 1991; Kornberger et al., 2017; Orlikowski & Scott, 2014). {C3.034} What these descriptions share is that the party in the middle evaluates the people on either side of it. {C3.035} According to Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024), for example, when a user rates a provider, "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). {C3.036} Similarly, Cameron (2024) draws an "Algorithmic Labor Triangle" (p. 466) in which the application stands between worker and customer. {C3.037} In an earlier study, Curchod et al. (2020) made a very similar point about an online marketplace, where the platform's evaluations of sellers drew on buyers' ratings and buyers and platform were in effect allied against the seller.

{C3.038} Accordingly, the thing in the middle of such a relation is not a carrier but a party. {C3.039} In turn, this means that a person in a triad deals with an intermediary whose verdict she cannot set aside by agreeing with the other person, and, in the arrangements this paper concerns, one whose rule is opaque in principle, changes constantly and without notice, and takes her dealings with the other person as its evidence: a person told everything about how the system is built is no better off, while people who know nothing about it work under it successfully, which is what separates this intermediary from a human third whose hidden rule could be disclosed. {C3.040} The same point bears on Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) observation that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13). {C3.041} To date, however, this line of research has been concerned with what the arrangement is and how it controls people rather than with what a person inside it must be able to do and how far the arrangement demands it, whether on a marketplace or under generative AI, which is itself a platform and scales the arrangement up as the printing press scaled up the text. {C3.042} Below I set out a framework that explains what that sensibility consists of and why each competency is needed.

## Algorithmacy as the Sensibility to Work in a Triad

{C4.001} Based on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) account of coordination, which integrates the field's work on how people get interdependent tasks done, I develop a framework to explain the three competencies through which the sensibility operates: to "interpret" results, to "specify intent," and to "keep track" of the rule. {C4.002} As shown in Figure 1, this framework rests on the distinction between a moderator and a third party and on the idea that a third party takes from two people something they need in order to coordinate. {C4.003} Building directly on debates about what algorithmic systems do to accountability at work (e.g., Karunakaran et al., 2022; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), I argue that what the intermediary takes explains what the person has to supply. {C4.004} In brief, the account rests on two closely related arguments. {C4.005} First, like a text, an intermediary stands between two people, which makes it something each of them has to read. {C4.006} Second, an intermediary that evaluates and binds is also a party: it issues a verdict on their joint work that neither of them wrote.

### Moderators Versus Third Parties

{C4.007} The framework proceeds from the premise that coordination rests on conditions that the people involved produce between them (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). {C4.008} As names for these conditions, I draw on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) "integrating conditions," a notion from the coordination literature, which work on people and AI has begun to extend (e.g., Lu & Yan, 2026). {C4.009} Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) identify three such conditions: accountability, which concerns who is responsible for which part of a task; predictability, which lets people anticipate when others will do their work; and common understanding, which is shared knowledge of the work, how it is to take place, and its goals, and they tie the three to mechanisms such as plans and rules, objects and representations, roles, routines, and proximity (p. 473). {C4.010} Accordingly, I understand these conditions as what two people stand to lose when the rule that coordinates them is one they cannot read. {C4.011} In line with Olson (1994), I hold that a written text records what was said and leaves open how it was meant. {C4.012} As moderators, texts come between people through what Cooren (2004) describes as their own contribution to organizing (i.e., reports, contracts, and work orders that "can be said to be performing something"; p. 373). {C4.013} It is through such texts that a manager and an employee meet over an appraisal form that goes on "presenting demands for the participants" (Lehtinen & Pälli, 2021, p. 47). {C4.014} Thus, a text between two people not only carries what one of them wrote but shapes what both can say, and the judgment still rests with a person who can be asked about it.

{C4.015} Central to the framework is the distinction between a moderator and a third party (Simmel, 1902b; see also Obstfeld et al., 2014). {C4.016} The two differ in who holds the verdict. {C4.017} For instance, an appraisal form presents demands, but the manager fills it in, signs it, and answers for the rating, with the assumption being that the employee can ask the manager why. {C4.018} A third party, in turn, holds the verdict itself, as an arbitrator does once the parties "have put this ultimate decision out of their own hands" (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172). {C4.019} Unlike an arbitrator, however, an algorithmic intermediary was not chosen by the two people, and its operator profits from what they do (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), which is the position of the third who benefits that Simmel (1902b) set apart from the arbitrator. {C4.020} For example, a seller on a marketplace is evaluated by a rule that draws on what buyers report (Curchod et al., 2020), in which case she did not choose the judge and cannot question it. {C4.021} Indeed, her remedy is exit with voice taken away (Hirschman, 1970), and rebuilding a reputation elsewhere would be too costly to count as a remedy (Curchod et al., 2020). {C4.022} In line with Okhuysen and Bechky (2009), or also Bolton et al. (2013), I assume that two people in this position lose in part the conditions on which their coordination rested, since a verdict that fuses what each of them did with what the rule did makes it hard to say who was responsible for a result, what will follow from the next act, and what the other person meant by what she reported. {C4.023} This is because the intermediary, and not either person, now settles what each one's act leads to for the other.

### What a Participant Has to Settle

{C4.024} The framework rests on the idea that the participant, who is the person whose position is in view, does not lack information. {C4.025} Here I draw on the notion of "equivocality" in the sense in which Daft and Lengel (1986) separate it from uncertainty: uncertainty is the absence of information, and equivocality is information that supports conflicting readings. {C4.026} In the framework, I use the notion to describe the participant's position—she has the result, what the counterpart said and did, and her own record, and she has several explanations that fit and no one to ask. {C4.027} This position is shown in Figure 1 as a cycle that runs from the intermediary's verdict to the participant, from her to the counterpart, and back to the intermediary. {C4.028} Consistent with the view that such a sensibility is acquired by doing the work (Sandberg, 2000), a person's algorithmacy grows through repeated turns of the cycle. {C4.029} Within this framework, the participant has three things to settle: (a) what caused a result; (b) how to make her meaning reach both the counterpart and the intermediary; and (c) whether the rule is still the one she has learned.

{C4.030} Regarding the first, the same output can follow from different inputs, so that the cause of a result can lie in the rule, in the counterpart, or in both (Rahman, 2021). {C4.031} For instance, a seller whose standing drops holds one buyer's rating, processed by a rule she cannot see (Curchod et al., 2020). {C4.032} Such a judgment can also be asked of an employee, as, for example, when her work is evaluated with generative AI, a kind of system whose inputs and outputs can be studied while "the process for getting there is opaque" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6; see also Tong et al., 2021). {C4.033} As Sundar and Nass (2000) have shown in an experiment, however, the attribution is not automatic: people who were given the same content rated it differently according to the source they were told it came from.

{C4.034} Second, regarding how her meaning reaches both, I posit that specifying intent is acting so that the people she is coordinated with can more easily trace a result back to what she meant, in a setting where whatever she says to the counterpart is also read and scored. {C4.035} This is because the place where she addresses the counterpart is the place where she is judged (Bucher et al., 2021). {C4.036} In Figure 1, this part of the cycle (see also Viduchinsky, 2026) is the arc from the participant to the counterpart, which the intermediary also reads, and done well it gives back some of the common understanding the two had lost.

{C4.037} Third, the participant keeps what she has worked out only as long as the rule stays the same. {C4.038} This is evident, for instance, in generative systems, whose functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4). {C4.039} However, the operator does not announce a change in the rule, and the rule updates constantly, in which case there are no updates to read and the participant detects a change only from its effects. {C4.040} In the case of hotels, for instance, valuations produced online by consumers differed in practice and in outcome from those of an older scheme—and their production came to reconfigure the everyday practices of the hotels being evaluated (see Orlikowski & Scott, 2014; Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). {C4.041} Telling a change in the rule from a change in the people (e.g., by comparing results across counterparts and across time) then becomes the work of keeping track, which is less documenting changes than sensing them in the flow of results, and which gives back some predictability (see DeVito, 2021).

### Practicing the Three Competencies, and Failing at Them

{C4.042} In this section, I describe how the three competencies are practiced in arrangements that organizational scholars have studied or anticipated, and how a person fails at them by mistaking the triad for a dyad. {C4.043} I treat each competency here as something a person does repeatedly and learns by doing. {C4.044} Following an understanding of competence as lying in how a person understands her work and not in a list of attributes (Sandberg, 2000), I further stress that the competencies are learned over time (see also Jablin & Sias, 2001). {C4.045} Below, I draw on published studies of marketplaces, two-sided review systems, hotel ranking, and evaluation with generative AI.

{C4.046} One of the most common occasions for interpreting is a result the participant did not expect, which she has to trace to the rule, to the counterpart, or to both (Bellesia et al., 2023; Chan, 2022). {C4.047} How people decide who or what is behind a message from a machine has long been studied in communication research (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996; Sundar & Nass, 2000). {C4.048} For example, Hohenstein and Jung (2020) studied chats between two people in which a system suggested replies (e.g., a short ready-made answer), and found that when a chat went badly people moved blame from their partner to the system. {C4.049} Conversely, for people who believe that machines are objective, research has shown the opposite lean: people told that a chat agent was a machine reported more willingness to give it a credit-card number than people told it was a human (Sundar & Kim, 2019).

{C4.050} Interpreting is sensemaking under a particular constraint (Weick et al., 2005). {C4.051} For instance, a seller can read what a buyer wrote and cannot read how the platform weighed it (Curchod et al., 2020), so that a fall in her standing follows from the rating, from the weighting, or from a change in the weighting. {C4.052} Two readings of a result lead to two courses of action, one toward the counterpart (in this case, the buyer) and one toward the rule (in this case, the platform's weighting) (Cameron & Rahman, 2022). {C4.053} If the counterpart judged the work poorly, the participant has reason to change the work or explain it better. {C4.054} Interpreting thereby restores a working answer to the question of who or what was responsible for a result, which is what accountability asks (in the sense of Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

{C4.055} Probably the most common occasion for specifying intent is writing something that the counterpart will read and the intermediary will process (Viduchinsky, 2026), as is the case when workers shape their dealings with customers with the rating in mind (Cameron, 2022; Cameron & Rahman, 2022). {C4.056} Like any writer, the participant has to address a reader she cannot see and has to imagine (Goffman, 1981; Litt, 2012; Ong, 1975)—here two readers who want different things, which is not prompting, since a prompt addresses the system alone, and which, like good writing, is easier to recognize than to define. {C4.057} Clark and Brennan (1991) describe how two people ordinarily check their understanding through small signals back and forth, and Suchman (2007) shows that a machine holds a thin record of what a person meant. {C4.058} Yet how far the second reader is the intermediary can vary. {C4.059} For example, while on the marketplace Curchod et al. (2020) studied the rule binds sellers and leaves buyers nearly free, the rental platform Fradkin et al. (2021) studied has host and guest each review the other and, in the experiment they report, held each review back until both were submitted. {C4.060} As this example illustrates, while a platform stands between two people in both arrangements, how far it binds both of them varies, as does the power each person holds over the other, and where it binds one the demand for the sensibility falls on that one.

{C4.061} In the same arrangements, keeping track is another common practice (Meng, 2026), often carried out by watching what counterparts do as well as one's own results (Cotter, 2019; DeVito et al., 2018). {C4.062} A way of working that used to succeed stops succeeding, for instance, when counterparts who used to be satisfied begin to object. {C4.063} Based on the expectation that "more and more functions are encapsulated as the GenAI learns and updates its model" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6), such change may leave an employee's understanding of an evaluation system out of date (see also Glaser et al., 2021). {C4.064} On one labor platform, for example, workers who faced criteria that shifted unpredictably either experimented, at risk to their standing, or cut back their activity to protect it (Rahman, 2021), and where the criteria can be learned, experience helps: across more than a million orders on a delivery platform, workers improved with experience and came to depart from the system's recommendations (Jiang & Sinchaisri, 2025).

{C4.065} Published studies also describe two ways of failing at these competencies. {C4.066} The first, which corresponds to both placements that pair a person with the system, is to treat the triad as that pair, so that she explains every result by the rule and treats what the other people do as noise. {C4.067} In Heiland's (2025) study of platform work, for example, the author reports what following the rule alone does to people who cannot see it:

> As a result, it becomes apparent how workers develop false theories about the algorithms' mechanisms due to their opacity, which effectively disciplines their actions. (p. 1)

{C4.068} A reading of this kind is what a participant falls back on, for example, when she curtails her outreach to clients in order to keep the algorithm satisfied (Bucher et al., 2021), as the counterpart then receives less of what the joint work needs.

{C4.069} Another way of failing is to treat the triad as a moderated dyad, in which what passes through the system is treated as passing only between the two people (Bolton et al., 2013). {C4.070} Such a reading becomes visible where each side of a transaction rates the other and expects a rating in return, so that what each reports is shaped by what the other may report back (see also Fradkin et al., 2021). {C4.071} Readings of this kind imply that a rating is a message to the other person and not also evidence. {C4.072} As Hinds and von Krogh (2024) put it of managers and employees working with generative AI, the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). {C4.073} Both failures can be presumed to cost the participant her footing inasmuch as one corner of the triad, the rule or the counterpart, stands in for the whole.

## Discussion

{C5.001} In this paper, I have sought to advance the triadic view of platforms as an approach that offers a way out of the dyadic understanding of this work. {C5.002} For this purpose, I first reviewed existing accounts of the sensibility to work through an algorithmic system. {C5.003} On that basis, I set out an account of algorithmacy.

{C5.004} The account rests on two proposals. {C5.005} In the first, I made the case for adopting what stands between two people as the criterion that distinguishes the sensibilities people need in order to work together. {C5.006} Second, I proposed that algorithmacy has three competencies, each of which gives back in part a condition that the intermediary takes: interpreting gives back accountability, specifying intent gives back common understanding, and keeping track gives back predictability. {C5.007} With these proposals, I contribute in two main ways to the literatures on platform organization and on algorithmic management, which I discuss in turn.

### Placing the Abilities on One Criterion, From Dyads to Triads

{C5.008} As is usual for a subject studied in several fields at once, the sensibility to work through algorithmic systems (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020; Spitzberg, 2006; Sutherland et al., 2020) has been given many names (e.g., as reviewed by Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). {C5.009} Although research on these sensibilities is growing, and although scholars agree that the systems are more than tools, they differ in where they place the system in relation to the people who work with it (e.g., Anthony et al., 2023; Gibbs et al., 2021; Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock et al., 2020). {C5.010} By going back through this work, I sought to bring out the placement that each account assumes. {C5.011} In particular, I identified three placements: the system as the other party, the system as a channel, and the system as the thing a worker adapts to. {C5.012} While none of these is wrong within its range, insofar as each describes an arrangement that exists, the review has shown what each leaves out when two people work through a system that gives a verdict on their joint work. {C5.013} Setting them side by side may help readers compare earlier accounts.

{C5.014} Building on these accounts (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), I developed my own proposal, which distinguishes sensibilities by what stands between two people. {C5.015} And while other criteria could have been chosen, I have argued that this one has advantages. {C5.016} First, it is simpler than accounts that define the sensibility by a list (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou et al., 2025). {C5.017} Second, it agrees with the way organization theory already distinguishes forms (Powell, 1990; Puranam et al., 2014). {C5.018} Third, it reaches the sensibilities that came before algorithmacy, as well.

{C5.019} Key to the proposal is the distinction between a moderator and a third party. {C5.020} This distinction rests on the premise that the verdict on two people's joint work is held either by one of them or by what stands between them. {C5.021} Depending on who holds it, the arrangement is a moderated dyad or a triad. {C5.022} The sensibilities can therefore be understood as belonging to the form in which they are used. {C5.023} In contrast to accounts that list what a person should know (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou et al., 2025), my account relies on one question asked of the arrangement. {C5.024} As a result, arrangements can be compared more readily because each is assessed by what the thing in the middle does to the two people. {C5.025} By contrast, a list gives less guidance on which of its items matter once a second person is involved. {C5.026} And unlike the construct proposed by Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019), which also takes the worker's dealings with an evaluating algorithm as its subject, my account includes the counterpart and so covers the triad.

{C5.027} At the same time, the account is compatible with these and other existing approaches. {C5.028} This is because several of their concepts, and particularly reactivity (Espeland & Sauder, 2007; Rahman, 2021) and the folk theories people form about algorithms (Bucher, 2017; DeVito, 2021), can be read as descriptions of what a participant in a triad does. {C5.029} I thus propose one criterion with these and other concepts placed in relation to it, that is, by asking what each says about a person who has to work with another person through a third party that gives a verdict on both.

{C5.030} The account further provides a way past the dyadic understanding identified at the outset. {C5.031} By treating the system as a party, in line with the triadic view (e.g., Curchod et al., 2020), I keep what accounts of platform work have established about its power (e.g., Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2021). {C5.032} At the same time, by defining the sensibility as work done with a counterpart, I keep the other person in view, as accounts of mediated communication do.

### The Co-opted Person's Side of Platform Organization

{C5.033} The framework also contributes an account of how the sensibility works over time (as the folk-theory literature has asked; DeVito, 2021). {C5.034} While Stelmaszak et al. (2026), drawing on Möhlmann et al. (2021), note that people who work under such a system learn to change how they behave (p. 350), this paper says what it is that they learn. {C5.035} More specifically, I explain the sensibility as a cycle in which an interpretation shapes the participant's next act and the next result shows whether the rule is still the same. {C5.036} Thereby I also show that the three competencies depend on one another and that none is finished once learned. {C5.037} The framework then raises follow-up questions, for instance, how people who have little of the sensibility get by. {C5.038} Earlier studies point to help from others, within limits: workers' forums pass on what experience can teach and fail where only the platform has the answer or where sharing means losing an advantage (see Lee et al., 2015; Yao et al., 2021). {C5.039} The framework specifies what such help would have to supply, and what a broker who stands between a system and its users supplies when interpreting for them (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026; Waardenburg et al., 2022). {C5.040} Also, the framework may help explain why systems that allow adjustment, such as the evaluation tools managers were more willing to use when they could change how a rating was computed (Lin et al., 2026), ask less of the people under them.

{C5.041} With this account of the participant's side, I also contribute to debates on algorithmic management (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2021; Cameron, 2024; Faraj & Pachidi, 2021; Kellogg et al., 2020; Newlands, 2021; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Vallas & Schor, 2020). {C5.042} While prior work has traced how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers—and how accountability under them is "distributed, deflected, and denied" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14)—it has not explained what a person must be able to do when that accountability is deflected onto her. {C5.043} The framework developed here (shown in Figure 1) addresses this by describing the three competencies of the sensibility and the condition each gives back, and it sits beside the labor-process argument that managerial control removes skill from the task (Braverman, 1974; see also Gandini, 2019): control through an intermediary adds a demand for skill to the relationship. {C5.044} By studying these competencies, therefore, it becomes possible to ask both how people whom a platform treats as identical come to different outcomes (see also Hargittai, 2002) and how their growing sensibility may keep a hidden rule in place, since a person placed in an automated system, in Elish's (2019) words, "may become simply a component—accidentally or intentionally—that bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilities when the overall system malfunctions" (p. 40).

## Conclusion and Research Agenda

{C6.001} In this paper, I have addressed the dyadic understanding in existing accounts of working through algorithmic systems by developing an account of algorithmacy based on what stands between two people. {C6.002} The account gets past that understanding and advances the triadic view by explaining what an intermediary that evaluates and binds takes from two people, and what a participant has to do to get it back in part. {C6.003} It treats the system as a party and keeps the other person in view.

{C6.004} The account opens a research agenda by raising four questions. {C6.005} First, it is worth asking: Are the three competencies distinct, and is telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart a fourth? {C6.006} Although I have placed that task within keeping track, users have been observed revising their theories after a platform changed (DeVito, 2021), and telling such a change from a change in one's audience may be a task in itself. {C6.007} The second question is whether algorithmacy explains who does well once dependence on the system and earlier setbacks are taken into account. {C6.008} This is relevant since Rahman (2021) found that these two things sorted how workers responded to an evaluation they could not read. {C6.009} Third, the account raises the question: Is the triad in which the intermediary binds one side the same form as the triad in which it binds both? {C6.010} Exploring this question would matter most on marketplaces and in rankings, where one side is bound and the other is nearly free (Curchod et al., 2020), as one would expect of an operator that sets its terms for each side so as to bring both on board (Rochet & Tirole, 2006). {C6.011} Finally, future research could examine whether the same sensibility is called for under a human third whose rule is hidden and whether algorithmacy differs in practice from general digital skill and from skill at communicating. {C6.012} For instance, related to current work on capabilities, future research could examine whether skill at working under an algorithmic intermediary is related to those older skills at all, given that in one study of chess the capabilities of people playing with a machine were "unrelated, or even negatively related, to traditional capabilities" (Krakowski et al., 2023, p. 1425).

{C6.013} In addition to these questions, the framework allows arrangements to be compared on one criterion and in that way connects organization theory to the study of communication, from which most accounts of the earlier sensibilities come (see also Thompson, 2020). {C6.014} By treating the algorithmic system as a party to a relation of three, and not as a tool, a medium, or the only thing a worker faces, the account widens the range of arrangements in which organizational scholars can study how people work together. {C6.015} As such, it invites researchers to look for algorithmacy wherever a system evaluates and binds people who depend on each other, including but not limited to marketplaces, two-sided review systems, rankings, and evaluations by generative AI (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). {C6.016} A task for future research will then be to show how far the sensibility explains what happens to people in these arrangements, and how far organizations that install such systems have checked that their people have it.
