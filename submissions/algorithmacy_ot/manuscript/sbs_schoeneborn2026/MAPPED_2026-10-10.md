# Toward an Account of Algorithmacy: A Triadic View of Working Through Algorithms

## Abstract

This paper addresses a dilemma in accounts of how people work together through algorithmic systems: how to treat the system as something that decides without losing sight of the other person. Existing accounts are split between those that treat the system as the one a person deals with and those that treat it as a channel between two people. While the first leaves the other person out, the second leaves the system with no verdict to give. To resolve this dilemma, I advance the triadic view of platforms and add to it the concept of algorithmacy—the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them by a rule that is hidden, gives no reasons, changes without notice, and takes their dealings with each other as its evidence. In particular, I distinguish the abilities that working together requires by what stands between the two people: oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads. The three parts of algorithmacy, in turn, depend on what the intermediary takes from the two people: interpreting restores a working sense of who was responsible for a result, specifying intent restores common understanding, and keeping track restores predictability. By taking what stands between two people as the criterion, I offer a framework that keeps what is known about the power of algorithmic systems while accounting for the person a participant still has to work with.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy, algorithmic management, coordination, literacy, platforms

## Introduction

{C1.001} The question of how algorithmic and generative AI systems change the way people work together has attracted growing interest among organizational scholars in recent years (e.g., Bailey et al., 2022; Hinds & von Krogh, 2024; Kellogg et al., 2020; Stelmaszak et al., 2026). {C1.002} Much of this work rests on one of two pictures of where such a system stands in relation to the people who work with it. {C1.003} On the one hand, some scholars treat the system as the one a person deals with: a communicative subject in its own right (Guzman & Lewis, 2020), a partner in a task that person and machine carry out together (Lu & Yan, 2026; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021), or an object the person has to understand (Long & Magerko, 2020). {C1.004} These scholars proceed from the finding that people respond to computers as they respond to other people (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), and on that premise the arrangement has two members, the person and the system.

{C1.005} On the other hand, a longer line of work treats the system as something that passes between two people, a medium through which they communicate (Spitzberg, 2006; Walther, 1992, 1996) or an agent that modifies or writes messages for one of them (Hancock et al., 2020). {C1.006} Proponents of this view base their accounts on the premise that the two people still judge each other and answer each other, and that the technology changes the conditions under which they do so and the words in which they do it. {C1.007} On this premise the arrangement is a dyad with something in the middle, in which the system stands between two people much as a written text stands between a writer and a reader. {C1.008} For example, Hancock et al. (2020) define AI-mediated communication as communication in which a system "operates on behalf of a communicator" (p. 89).

{C1.009} These two pictures leave organization theory with a dilemma that bears directly on how it understands work under algorithmic management. {C1.010} The dilemma is that an account which keeps the other person in view gives the system no verdict over the two people, while an account which gives the system a verdict tends to keep one person in view and to treat the other as a source of ratings (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Zhou et al., 2025).

{C1.011} As one way out of this dyad dilemma, organization theorists have recently described platforms as a relation of three, in which the platform and the two parties it brings together each stand in a relation to the other two (Cameron, 2024; Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). {C1.012} Scholars who take this triadic view hold that people on platforms are "co-opted" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 1), and that the platform rates and ranks the parties it brings together (Kornberger et al., 2017). {C1.013} Although this work has established what the arrangement is and how it controls the people in it, and although field studies have recorded much of what those people do in response (Cameron & Rahman, 2022; Chan, 2022), there remains a need to say what a person inside the arrangement must be able to do. {C1.014} The most recent relational account places the resulting capability in the organization and describes it as "not merely a human trait or skill" (Stelmaszak et al., 2026, p. 346), which leaves the person's own part without a name. {C1.015} In this paper, I therefore aim to advance the triadic view by specifying what a person must be able to do to work with another person through such a system and, correspondingly, the features of the system that make each part of that ability necessary. {C1.016} To this end, I make two proposals.

{C1.017} First, I propose to distinguish the abilities that working with others requires by one criterion only: what stands between the two people. {C1.018} By sorting on this criterion, so that oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads, I depart from earlier work that defines the ability either by a list of what a person should understand about the system (e.g., Long & Magerko, 2020), which leaves open how the items on the list relate, or by how well a worker understands and adapts to the system (e.g., Zhou et al., 2025), which leaves out the person on the other side.

{C1.019} Second, I propose that algorithmacy itself has three parts. {C1.020} Building on Simmel's (1902a, 1902b) account of the third party and on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) account of what coordination requires, I define algorithmacy as the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them. {C1.021} I argue that such an intermediary takes from the two people the conditions on which their coordination rested, and that interpreting its results, specifying intent to it and to the other person at once, and keeping track of its rule each give one of those conditions back in part. {C1.022} Consequently, people in the same formal position can be expected to differ in how well they work under the same system.

{C1.023} In sum, this paper sets out an account of algorithmacy that is based on what stands between two people who depend on each other. {C1.024} By taking this as the criterion, I not only provide one basis for distinguishing algorithmacy from oracy and literacy but also explain why existing accounts of digital skill, which count two parties, leave part of the arrangement out. {C1.025} More specifically, I develop a framework that explains how each of the three parts answers something the intermediary takes away and how the parts depend on one another. {C1.026} In this way, I provide organization theory with a research agenda for studying the co-opted person's side of the form that Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe.

## The Triadic View of Platforms

{C2.001} The view that a platform sets up a relation of three, and not of two (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), has gained ground in organization theory in recent years (e.g., Cameron, 2022, 2024; Cameron & Rahman, 2022; Chan, 2022; Curchod et al., 2020; Kornberger et al., 2017; Rahman & Valentine, 2021; Vallas & Schor, 2020). {C2.002} Its figure is older than platforms and was drawn by sociologists of service work, who described a triangle of worker, manager, and customer (Fuller & Smith, 1991; Havard et al., 2009; Korczynski, 2013; Lopez, 2010). {C2.003} Following Stark and Pais (2020), Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe the people on a platform not as commanded, as in a hierarchy, nor as contracting, as in a market, nor as collaborating, as in a network, but as drawn in and organized by a platform that does not employ them (p. 5). {C2.004} The relation has been studied in online marketplaces (Curchod et al., 2020), platform labor (Cameron, 2024), hotel ranking (Orlikowski & Scott, 2014, 2023), crowdwork (Bellesia et al., 2023), and reputation systems in which each party to a transaction reviews the other (Fradkin et al., 2021).

{C2.005} What the view still has to specify is the ability a person needs in order to work with another person inside such a relation. {C2.006} Existing accounts of that ability, however, are scattered across fields, and each counts fewer than three parties. {C2.007} In particular, different authors (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020; Spitzberg, 2006) give the ability different names. {C2.008} These names include AI literacy, algorithmic literacy, computer-mediated communication competence, algorithmic competency, gig literacies, and, for the responses of people under evaluation, reactivity. {C2.009} Different authors have also placed the system differently in relation to the people involved.

{C2.010} Scholars who place the system as the other party treat the arrangement as a pair made up of one person and one machine. {C2.011} For example, Guzman and Lewis (2020) propose to study AI systems as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). {C2.012} This placement fits a person who is using a chatbot or reading a tool's output. {C2.013} For instance, radiologists who use a diagnostic tool have to deal with its opacity (Lebovitz et al., 2022), whereas a person who works with other people through the system has those people to deal with as well. {C2.014} A similar placement is followed in work on AI literacy, whose best-known statement lists what a person should understand about AI, including the human role in it, and has no item on working with a second person through the system (Long & Magerko, 2020; see also Gagrčin et al., 2026; Hargittai et al., 2020; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). {C2.015} This placement is useful for studying how people respond to a machine socially, which is the finding it grew out of (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996).

{C2.016} Scholars who place the system as a channel treat the arrangement as two people with a technology between them. {C2.017} For example, building on work that showed people learning from each other's replies to carry personal meaning in plain text (Walther, 1992, 1996), Spitzberg (2006) describes communicating well through a computer as a matter between two people who judge each other. {C2.018} In this line the technology first carries what the two people write; it then modifies, augments, or generates their messages (Hancock et al., 2020); and in each case what is studied is what the two people come to think of each other. {C2.019} Accordingly, when suggested replies raise trust in a chat partner, or move blame from the partner to the system when a chat goes badly (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020), the finding is about the relation between the two people. {C2.020} Like the first placement, this one is useful within its range, which is two people who answer each other through a technology that issues no verdict on either.

{C2.021} Finally, scholars who study work on platforms place the system as the thing the worker has to understand and adapt to. {C2.022} Of particular relevance to the approach I develop in this paper, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) have proposed "algorithmic competency" for workers' understanding of the algorithms that assign and assess their work and their learning to work with and around them. {C2.023} On that basis, Zhou et al. (2025) have built and tested a questionnaire that measures workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 3 of the working paper [AUTHOR: replace with the page in the published article, which I could not open]; see also Hong et al., 2026). {C2.024} This includes even an item on putting right an unfair result. {C2.025} Compared to the first two placements, this one has the clear advantage of taking the system's power into account, since it treats the system as something that assigns and evaluates work. {C2.026} However, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) do not carry the client into the construct, although clients appear throughout their interviews. {C2.027} While their workers watch clients and keep records in case of a dispute, their figure has two nodes, the worker and the algorithm, and the construct covers the same two.

## What Stands Between as a Defining Criterion

{C3.001} The preceding section has shown that the triadic view still lacks an account of the ability it implies, and that existing accounts name that ability differently and place the system differently. {C3.002} To compare these accounts and to add to them, it is first necessary to settle what distinguishes one such ability from another. {C3.003} Only then can it be said what is new about working through an algorithm and what is not.

{C3.004} At present, the three placements summarized above each offer a different basis for the ability: what a person understands about the system, how two people communicate through it, and how well a worker adapts to it. {C3.005} A choice has to be made among these bases. {C3.006} Like any such choice, this one decides what researchers who adopt it will see—a basis in the person and the system shows no second person, and a basis in two people shows no system that gives a verdict. {C3.007} In making this point, I follow Georg Simmel's (1902a, 1902b; see also 1950) argument that the number of members conditions the form of a group. {C3.008} As Simmel argued, a third member changes more than the count, and what the third does to the other two decides what kind of relation they are in—a point he made by setting the mediator beside the arbitrator:

> So long as the third party works as a real mediator, the ending of the conflict rests finally in the hands of the parties themselves. By choice of the arbitrator they have put this ultimate decision out of their own hands. (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)

{C3.009} Over the following sections, I make the case for adopting what stands between two people, and what it does to them, as the criterion that distinguishes the abilities people need in order to work together. {C3.010} While aligning to a certain extent with Thompson (1995, 2020), who sorts interaction into face-to-face, mediated, and mediated quasi-interaction by the medium and by whether the audience is specific, my criterion differs in sorting by whether the thing in the middle evaluates and binds the two people, and it therefore reaches the case in which a system stands between them and settles matters for both. {C3.011} The criterion also keeps what each of the three placements describes. {C3.012} I treat what they describe as "secondary" in the sense that a system does address the person who uses it, does carry what passes between two people, and does hold power over a worker, and none of these by itself settles what form the person is working in. {C3.013} This allows the three bodies of work to be read together.

{C3.014} I give the reasons for this choice in turn. {C3.015} First, basing the account on one criterion makes it simpler than accounts that define the ability by a list. {C3.016} Both the list of things a person should understand about AI (Long & Magerko, 2020) and the questionnaire on algorithmic competency (Zhou et al., 2025) require not only a reason why these items belong and others do not—but also an account of how the items relate to each other. {C3.017} By contrast, a criterion that asks one question of the arrangement requires no list. {C3.018} Instead, what stands between the two people (or, to be more precise, whether it evaluates and binds them) indicates what a person must be able to do, since the conditions it takes from the two people are the ones the ability has to give back.

{C3.020} Second, although this criterion is one among others that could have been chosen, it has advantages beyond its simplicity. {C3.021} One advantage is that organization theory already distinguishes forms of coordination from one another (e.g., Powell, 1990). {C3.022} Similarly, Puranam et al. (2014) define a form of organizing as a set of solutions to problems that every organization has to solve. {C3.023} Building on the criterion therefore connects an account of individual ability to the field's accounts of forms. {C3.024} As Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 1) put it:

> Whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted.

{C3.025} Other scholars on whom the triadic view draws also describe an arrangement by the positions it gives people. {C3.026} Selznick (1949) defines co-optation as "absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization" (p. 13) and notes that what is shared is "the responsibility for power rather than power itself" (p. 14). {C3.027} Kornberger et al. (2017) similarly show platforms coordinating people they do not employ through rating and ranking, while Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) define coordination as "the process of interaction that integrates a collective set of interdependent tasks" (p. 463) and identify the conditions that integration depends on.

{C3.028} In addition to being shared within organization theory, the criterion has the advantage of reaching the abilities that came before algorithmacy. {C3.029} For example, oracy, the ability to speak and to listen (Wilkinson, 1965), belongs to the direct dyad, in which I speak to you and you answer, while literacy belongs to the moderated dyad, in which a text stands between two people and records what was said without settling how it was meant (Olson, 1994). {C3.030} Yates (1989) describes American firms between about 1850 and 1920 coming to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files. {C3.031} And this is in line with the wider literature on texts in organizations, which has shown that documents do more than carry messages (e.g., Cooren, 2004; Kuhn, 2008).

{C3.032} Having proposed what stands between two people as the criterion, I turn to specifying the form that is new. {C3.033} A number of overlapping descriptions of the relation of three have been put forward in the literature on platforms (Chan, 2022; Curchod et al., 2020) as well as in the wider literature on evaluation at work (Fuller & Smith, 1991; Kornberger et al., 2017; Orlikowski & Scott, 2014). {C3.034} What these descriptions share is that the party in the middle evaluates the people on either side of it. {C3.035} According to Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024), for example, when a user rates a provider, "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). {C3.036} Similarly, Cameron (2024) draws an "Algorithmic Labor Triangle" (p. 466) in which the application stands between worker and customer. {C3.037} In an earlier study, Curchod et al. (2020) made a very similar point about an online marketplace, where the platform's evaluations of sellers drew on buyers' ratings and buyers and platform were in effect allied against the seller.

{C3.038} Accordingly, the thing in the middle of such a relation is not a carrier but a party. {C3.039} In turn, this means that a person in a triad deals with an intermediary whose verdict she cannot set aside by agreeing with the other person, and, in the arrangements this paper concerns, one whose rule is hidden, gives no reasons, changes without notice, and takes her dealings with the other person as its evidence. {C3.040} The same point bears on Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) observation that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13). {C3.041} To date, however, this line of research has been concerned with what the arrangement is and how it controls people rather than with what a person inside it must be able to do and how far the arrangement demands it. {C3.042} Below I set out a framework that explains what that ability consists of and why each part of it is needed.

## Algorithmacy as the Ability to Work in a Triad

{C4.001} Based on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) account of coordination, which integrates the field's work on how people get interdependent tasks done, I develop a framework to explain what a person in a triad must be able to do: to "interpret" results, to "specify intent," and to "keep track" of the rule. {C4.002} As shown in Figure 1, this framework rests on the distinction between a moderator and a third party and on the idea that a third party takes from two people something they need in order to coordinate. {C4.003} Building directly on debates about what algorithmic systems do to accountability at work (e.g., Karunakaran et al., 2022; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), I argue that what the intermediary takes explains what the person has to supply. {C4.004} In brief, the account rests on two closely related arguments. {C4.005} First, like a text, an intermediary stands between two people, which makes it something each of them has to read. {C4.006} Second, an intermediary that evaluates and binds is also a party: it issues a verdict on their joint work that neither of them wrote.

### Moderators Versus Third Parties

{C4.007} The framework proceeds from the premise that coordination rests on conditions that the people involved produce between them (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). {C4.008} As names for these conditions, I draw on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) "integrating conditions," a notion from the coordination literature, which work on people and AI has begun to extend (e.g., Lu & Yan, 2026). {C4.009} Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) identify three such conditions: accountability, which concerns who is responsible for which part of a task; predictability, which concerns what will happen and when; and common understanding, which concerns a shared view of the work and of how each part contributes. {C4.010} Accordingly, I understand these conditions as what two people stand to lose when something comes between them. {C4.011} In line with Olson (1994), I hold that a written text records what was said and leaves open how it was meant. {C4.012} As moderators, texts come between people through what Cooren (2004) describes as their own contribution to organizing (i.e., reports, contracts, and work orders that "can be said to be performing something"; p. 373). {C4.013} It is through such texts that a manager and an employee meet over an appraisal form that goes on "presenting demands for the participants" (Lehtinen & Pälli, 2021, p. 47). {C4.014} Thus, a text between two people not only carries what one of them wrote but shapes what both can say, and the judgment still rests with a person who can be asked about it.

{C4.015} Central to the framework is the distinction between a moderator and a third party (Simmel, 1902b; see also Obstfeld et al., 2014). {C4.016} The two differ in who holds the verdict. {C4.017} For instance, an appraisal form presents demands, but the manager fills it in, signs it, and answers for the rating, with the assumption being that the employee can ask the manager why. {C4.018} A third party, in turn, holds the verdict itself, as an arbitrator does once the parties "have put this ultimate decision out of their own hands" (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172). {C4.019} Unlike an arbitrator, however, an algorithmic intermediary was not chosen by the two people, and its operator profits from what they do (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). {C4.020} For example, a seller on a marketplace is evaluated by a rule that draws on what buyers report (Curchod et al., 2020), in which case she did not choose the judge and cannot question it. {C4.021} Indeed, her remedy is to leave, and rebuilding a reputation elsewhere would be too costly to count as a remedy (Curchod et al., 2020). {C4.022} In line with Okhuysen and Bechky (2009), or also Bolton et al. (2013), I assume that two people in this position lose in part the conditions on which their coordination rested, since a verdict that fuses what each of them did with what the rule did makes it hard to say who was responsible for a result, what will follow from the next act, and what the other person meant by what she reported. {C4.023} This is because the intermediary, and not either person, now settles what each one's act leads to for the other.

### What a Participant Has to Settle

{C4.024} The framework rests on the idea that the participant, who is the person whose position is in view, does not lack information (Daft & Lengel, 1986). {C4.025} Here I draw on the notion of "equivocality" in the sense in which Daft and Lengel (1986) separate it from uncertainty: uncertainty is the absence of information, and equivocality is information that supports conflicting readings. {C4.026} In the framework, I use the notion to describe the participant's position—she has the result, what the counterpart said and did, and her own record, and she has several explanations that fit and no one to ask. {C4.027} This position is shown in Figure 1 as a cycle that runs from the intermediary's verdict to the participant, from her to the counterpart, and back to the intermediary. {C4.028} Consistent with the view that such an ability is acquired by doing the work (Sandberg, 2000), a person's algorithmacy grows through repeated turns of the cycle. {C4.029} Within this framework, the participant has three things to settle: (a) what caused a result; (b) how to make her meaning reach both the counterpart and the intermediary; and (c) whether the rule is still the one she has learned.

{C4.030} Regarding the first, the cause of a result can lie in the rule, in the counterpart, or in both (Rahman, 2021). {C4.031} For instance, a seller whose standing drops holds one buyer's rating, processed by a rule she cannot see (Curchod et al., 2020). {C4.032} Such a judgment can also be asked of an employee, as, for example, when her work is evaluated with generative AI, a kind of system whose inputs and outputs can be studied while "the process for getting there is opaque" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6; see also Tong et al., 2021). {C4.033} As Sundar and Nass (2000) have shown in an experiment, however, the attribution is not automatic: people who were given the same content rated it differently according to the source they were told it came from.

{C4.034} Second, regarding how her meaning reaches both, I posit that where the intermediary takes the two people's dealings as its evidence, whatever the participant says to the counterpart is also read and scored. {C4.035} This is because the place where she addresses the counterpart is the place where she is judged (Bucher et al., 2021). {C4.036} In Figure 1, this part of the cycle (see also Viduchinsky, 2026) is the arc from the participant to the counterpart, which the intermediary also reads.

{C4.037} Third, the participant keeps what she has worked out only as long as the rule stays the same. {C4.038} This is evident, for instance, in generative systems, whose functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4). {C4.039} However, a change in the rule is not announced, in which case the participant detects it only from its effects. {C4.040} In the case of hotels, for instance, valuations produced online by consumers differed in practice and in outcome from those of an older scheme—and their production came to reconfigure the everyday practices of the hotels being evaluated (see Orlikowski & Scott, 2014; Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). {C4.041} Telling a change in the rule from a change in the people (e.g., by comparing results across counterparts and across time) then becomes the work of keeping track (see DeVito, 2021).

### Practising the Three Parts, and Failing at Them

{C4.042} In this section, I describe how the three parts are practised in arrangements that organizational scholars have studied or anticipated, and how a person fails at them by mistaking the triad for a dyad. {C4.043} I treat each part here as something a person does repeatedly and learns by doing. {C4.044} Following an understanding of competence as lying in how a person understands her work and not in a list of attributes (Sandberg, 2000), I further stress that the parts are learned over time (see also Jablin & Sias, 2001). {C4.045} Below, I draw on published studies of marketplaces, two-sided review systems, hotel ranking, and evaluation with generative AI.

{C4.046} One of the most common occasions for interpreting is a result the participant did not expect, which she has to trace to the rule, to the counterpart, or to both (Bellesia et al., 2023; Chan, 2022). {C4.047} How people decide who or what is behind a message from a machine has long been studied in communication research (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996; Sundar & Nass, 2000). {C4.048} For example, Hohenstein and Jung (2020) studied chats between two people in which a system suggested replies (e.g., a short ready-made answer), and found that when a chat went badly people moved blame from their partner to the system. {C4.049} Conversely, for people who believe that machines are objective, research has shown the opposite lean: people told that a chat agent was a machine reported more willingness to give it a credit-card number than people told it was a human (Sundar & Kim, 2019).

{C4.050} Interpreting is sensemaking under a particular constraint (Weick et al., 2005). {C4.051} For instance, a seller can read what a buyer wrote and cannot read how the platform weighed it (Curchod et al., 2020), so that a fall in her standing follows from the rating, from the weighting, or from a change in the weighting. {C4.052} Two readings of a result lead to two courses of action, one toward the counterpart (in this case, the buyer) and one toward the rule (in this case, the platform's weighting) (Cameron & Rahman, 2022). {C4.053} If the counterpart judged the work poorly, the participant has reason to change the work or explain it better. {C4.054} Interpreting thereby restores a working answer to the question of who or what was responsible for a result, which is what accountability asks (in the sense of Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

{C4.055} Probably the most common occasion for specifying intent is writing something that the counterpart will read and the intermediary will process (Viduchinsky, 2026), as is the case for the ratings and reviews that follow an encounter on a platform (Cameron, 2022). {C4.056} Like any writer, the participant has to address a reader she cannot see and has to imagine (Goffman, 1981; Litt, 2012; Ong, 1975)—here two readers who want different things. {C4.057} Clark and Brennan (1991) describe how two people ordinarily check their understanding through small signals back and forth, and Suchman (2007) shows that a machine holds a thin record of what a person meant. {C4.058} Yet how far the second reader is the intermediary can vary. {C4.059} For example, while on the marketplace Curchod et al. (2020) studied the rule binds sellers and leaves buyers nearly free, the rental platform Fradkin et al. (2021) studied has host and guest each review the other and, in the experiment they report, held each review back until both were submitted. {C4.060} As this example illustrates, while a platform stands between two people in both arrangements, how far it binds both of them varies, and where it binds one the demand for the ability falls on that one.

{C4.061} In the same arrangements, keeping track is another common practice (Meng, 2026), often carried out by watching what counterparts do as well as one's own results (Cotter, 2019; DeVito et al., 2018). {C4.062} A way of working that used to succeed stops succeeding, for instance, when counterparts who used to be satisfied begin to object. {C4.063} Based on the expectation that "more and more functions are encapsulated as the GenAI learns and updates its model" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6), such change may leave an employee's understanding of an evaluation system out of date (see also Glaser et al., 2021). {C4.064} On one labor platform, for example, workers who faced criteria that shifted unpredictably either experimented, at risk to their standing, or cut back their activity to protect it (Rahman, 2021), and where the criteria can be learned, experience helps: across more than a million orders on a delivery platform, workers improved with experience and came to depart from the system's recommendations (Jiang & Sinchaisri, 2025).

{C4.065} Published studies also describe two ways of failing at these parts. {C4.066} The first is to treat the triad as a pair made up of the participant and the system, in which she explains every result by the rule and treats what the other people do as noise (cf. Heiland, 2025, on workers' false theories of an algorithm). {C4.067} In Lebovitz et al.'s (2022) study of diagnostic tools, for example, the authors describe what opacity does even to professionals who face a tool with no counterpart:

> In all three departments, this opacity resulted in professionals experiencing increased uncertainty because AI tool results often diverged from their initial judgment without providing underlying reasoning. (p. 126)

{C4.068} A reading of this kind is what a participant falls back on, for example, when she curtails her outreach to clients in order to keep the algorithm satisfied (Bucher et al., 2021), as the counterpart then receives less of what the joint work needs.

{C4.069} Another way of failing is to treat the triad as a moderated dyad, in which what passes through the system is treated as passing only between the two people (Bolton et al., 2013). {C4.070} Such a reading becomes visible where each side of a transaction rates the other and expects a rating in return, so that what each reports is shaped by what the other may report back (see also Fradkin et al., 2021). {C4.071} Readings of this kind imply that a rating is a message to the other person and not also evidence. {C4.072} As Hinds and von Krogh (2024) put it of managers and employees working with generative AI, the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). {C4.073} Both failures can be presumed to cost the participant her footing inasmuch as one corner of the triad, the rule or the counterpart, stands in for the whole.

## Discussion

{C5.001} In this paper, I have sought to advance the triadic view of platforms as an approach that offers a way out of the dyad dilemma. {C5.002} For this purpose, I first reviewed existing accounts of the ability to work through an algorithmic system. {C5.003} On that basis, I set out an account of algorithmacy.

{C5.004} The account rests on two proposals. {C5.005} In the first, I made the case for adopting what stands between two people as the criterion that distinguishes the abilities people need in order to work together. {C5.006} Second, I proposed that algorithmacy has three parts, each of which gives back in part a condition that the intermediary takes: interpreting gives back accountability, specifying intent gives back common understanding, and keeping track gives back predictability. {C5.007} With these proposals, I contribute in two main ways to the literatures on platform organization and on algorithmic management, which I discuss in turn.

### Placing the Abilities on One Criterion, From Dyads to Triads

{C5.008} As is usual for a subject studied in several fields at once, the ability to work through algorithmic systems (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020; Spitzberg, 2006; Sutherland et al., 2020) has been given many names (e.g., as reviewed by Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). {C5.009} Although research on these abilities is growing, and although scholars agree that the systems are more than tools, they differ in where they place the system in relation to the people who work with it (e.g., Anthony et al., 2023; Gibbs et al., 2021; Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock et al., 2020). {C5.010} By going back through this work, I sought to bring out the placement that each account assumes. {C5.011} In particular, I identified three placements: the system as the other party, the system as a channel, and the system as the thing a worker adapts to. {C5.012} While none of these is wrong within its range, insofar as each describes an arrangement that exists, the review has shown what each leaves out when two people work through a system that gives a verdict on their joint work. {C5.013} Setting them side by side may help readers compare earlier accounts.

{C5.014} Building on these accounts (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), I developed my own proposal, which distinguishes abilities by what stands between two people. {C5.015} And while other criteria could have been chosen, I have argued that this one has advantages. {C5.016} First, it is simpler than accounts that define the ability by a list (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou et al., 2025). {C5.017} Second, it agrees with the way organization theory already distinguishes forms (Powell, 1990; Puranam et al., 2014). {C5.018} Third, it reaches the abilities that came before algorithmacy, as well.

{C5.019} Key to the proposal is the distinction between a moderator and a third party. {C5.020} This distinction rests on the premise that the verdict on two people's joint work is held either by one of them or by what stands between them. {C5.021} Depending on who holds it, the arrangement is a moderated dyad or a triad. {C5.022} The abilities can therefore be understood as belonging to the form in which they are used. {C5.023} In contrast to accounts that list what a person should know (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou et al., 2025), my account relies on one question asked of the arrangement. {C5.024} As a result, arrangements can be compared more readily because each is assessed by what the thing in the middle does to the two people. {C5.025} By contrast, a list gives less guidance on which of its items matter once a second person is involved. {C5.026} And unlike the construct proposed by Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019), which also takes the worker's dealings with an evaluating algorithm as its subject, my account includes the counterpart and so covers the triad.

{C5.027} At the same time, the account is compatible with these and other existing approaches. {C5.028} This is because several of their concepts, and particularly reactivity (Espeland & Sauder, 2007; Rahman, 2021) and the folk theories people form about algorithms (Bucher, 2017; DeVito, 2021), can be read as descriptions of what a participant in a triad does. {C5.029} I thus propose one criterion with these and other concepts placed in relation to it, that is, by asking what each says about a person who has to work with another person through a third party that gives a verdict on both.

{C5.030} The account further provides a way out of the dyad dilemma identified at the outset. {C5.031} By treating the system as a party, in line with the triadic view (e.g., Curchod et al., 2020), I keep what accounts of platform work have established about its power (e.g., Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2021). {C5.032} At the same time, by defining the ability as work done with a counterpart, I keep the other person in view, as accounts of mediated communication do.

### The Co-opted Person's Side of Platform Organization

{C5.033} The framework also contributes an account of how the ability works over time (as the folk-theory literature has asked; DeVito, 2021). {C5.034} While Stelmaszak et al. (2026), drawing on Möhlmann et al. (2021), note that people who work under such a system learn to change how they behave (p. 350), this paper says what it is that they learn. {C5.035} More specifically, I explain the ability as a cycle in which an interpretation shapes the participant's next act and the next result shows whether the rule is still the same. {C5.036} Thereby I also show that the three parts depend on one another and that none is finished once learned. {C5.037} The framework then raises follow-up questions, for instance, how people who have little of the ability get by. {C5.038} Earlier studies point to help from others, within limits: workers' forums pass on what experience can teach and fail where only the platform has the answer or where sharing means losing an advantage (see Lee et al., 2015; Yao et al., 2021). {C5.039} The framework specifies what such help would have to supply, and what a broker who stands between a system and its users supplies when interpreting for them (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026; Waardenburg et al., 2022). {C5.040} Also, the framework may help explain why systems that allow adjustment, such as the evaluation tools managers were more willing to use when they could change how a rating was computed (Lin et al., 2026), ask less of the people under them.

{C5.041} With this account of the participant's side, I also contribute to debates on algorithmic management (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2021; Cameron, 2024; Faraj & Pachidi, 2021; Kellogg et al., 2020; Newlands, 2021; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Vallas & Schor, 2020). {C5.042} While prior work has traced how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers—and how accountability under them is "distributed, deflected, and denied" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14)—it has not explained what a person must be able to do when that accountability is deflected onto her. {C5.043} The framework developed here (shown in Figure 1) addresses this by describing the three parts of the ability and the condition each gives back. {C5.044} By studying these parts, therefore, it becomes possible to ask both how people whom a platform treats as identical come to different outcomes (see also Hargittai, 2002) and how their growing ability may keep a hidden rule in place, since a person placed in an automated system, in Elish's (2019) words, "may become simply a component—accidentally or intentionally—that bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilities when the overall system malfunctions" (p. 40).

## Conclusion and Research Agenda

{C6.001} In this paper, I have addressed the dyad dilemma in existing accounts of working through algorithmic systems by developing an account of algorithmacy based on what stands between two people. {C6.002} The account gets out of the dilemma and advances the triadic view by explaining what an intermediary that evaluates and binds takes from two people, and what a participant has to do to get it back in part. {C6.003} It treats the system as a party and keeps the other person in view.

{C6.004} The account opens a research agenda by raising four questions. {C6.005} First, it is worth asking: Are the three parts distinct, and is telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart a fourth? {C6.006} Although I have placed that task within keeping track, users have been observed revising their theories after a platform changed (DeVito, 2021), and telling such a change from a change in one's audience may be a task in itself. {C6.007} The second question is whether algorithmacy explains who does well once dependence on the system and earlier setbacks are taken into account. {C6.008} This is relevant since Rahman (2021) found that these two things sorted how workers responded to an evaluation they could not read. {C6.009} Third, the account raises the question: Is the triad in which the intermediary binds one side the same form as the triad in which it binds both? {C6.010} Exploring this question would matter most on marketplaces and in rankings, where one side is bound and the other is nearly free (Curchod et al., 2020). {C6.011} Finally, future research could examine whether the same ability is called for under a human third whose rule is hidden and whether algorithmacy differs in practice from general digital skill and from skill at communicating. {C6.012} For instance, related to current work on capabilities, future research could examine whether skill at working under an algorithmic intermediary is related to those older skills at all, given that in one study of chess the capabilities of people playing with a machine were "unrelated, or even negatively related, to traditional capabilities" (Krakowski et al., 2023, p. 1425).

{C6.013} In addition to these questions, the framework allows arrangements to be compared on one criterion and in that way connects organization theory to the study of communication, from which most accounts of the earlier abilities come (see also Thompson, 2020). {C6.014} By treating the algorithmic system as a party to a relation of three, and not as a tool, a medium, or the only thing a worker faces, the account widens the range of arrangements in which organizational scholars can study how people work together. {C6.015} As such, it invites researchers to look for algorithmacy wherever a system evaluates and binds people who depend on each other, including but not limited to marketplaces, two-sided review systems, rankings, and evaluations by generative AI (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). {C6.016} A task for future research will then be to show how far the ability explains what happens to people in these arrangements, and how far organizations that install such systems have checked that their people have it.
