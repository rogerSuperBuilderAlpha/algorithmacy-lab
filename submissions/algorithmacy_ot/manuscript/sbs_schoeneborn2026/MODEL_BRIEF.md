## The research question, wherever the model states it

1. Abstract, sentence 1: "how to conceptualize organization in a way sufficiently specific to distinguish
   it from other social phenomena yet sufficiently flexible to account for new organizational forms."
2. Introduction ¶3 (C1.010): the dilemma "consists in the challenge of defining the notion of organization
   in a sufficiently narrow and sufficiently specific way … while also ensuring the notion is broad and
   open enough …".
3. Introduction ¶4 (C1.015), as an aim: "specifying the processes through which social phenomena gain
   organizationality and, correspondingly, the dimensions along which varying degrees of organizationality
   can be distinguished."
4. Discussion ¶1: "an approach that offers a solution to the openness-specificity dilemma".
5. Conclusion ¶1: "we have addressed the openness-specificity dilemma in existing conceptualizations of
   organization".

It is never put as a question. It is put as a dilemma between two camps, named once ("openness-specificity
dilemma") and carried under that name to the last section.

## The thesis, and its shape

Organizationality is a matter of degree, and the degree is the degree of collective actorhood, which
rises and falls as people attribute agency to a collective social address or to individuals.

The shape is **two stacked choices**. First choose one criterion out of several on offer (collective
actorhood). Then make that criterion gradual (charged and discharged by attribution practices). Each
choice is called a "proposal" in the Introduction, a "conceptual choice" in section 3 and a "conceptual
move" in the Discussion, and each is defended by comparison with named rivals, not by evidence.

## Contribution moves, in the order made

1. Introduction ¶7: one framework that distinguishes degrees and explains change over time; a research
   agenda.
2. Section 2: the existing work sorted into three "logics" (configurational, stage-based, axial).
3. Section 3: the choice of one criterion, with three reasons.
4. Section 4: the framework (Figure 1), three aspects, five practices.
5. Discussion: two contributions, a subsection each — a single continuum; a dynamic account.
6. Conclusion: four research questions.

## Register

**Thirty of its verbs.** has attracted · has been driven · have advocated · proceed from · base · advocate ·
have proposed · present · consists in · has been proposed · provide · propound · conceptualizes · remains ·
has been criticized · aim · advance · specifying · make · suggest · depart · propose · define · argue ·
exhibit · sets out · explain · develop · posit · hold. All literal. The paper's verbs are the verbs of
proposing and conceptualizing; almost none describes anything happening in the world.

**Its metaphors.** Few, and each one is announced. "Charging" and "discharging" are introduced as a
borrowed figure with a source and a gloss ("like a battery") and then used as technical terms about forty
times. "Third way", "contours", "zooming in", "relapsing", "cross-fertilization" and "sliding bar" each
occur once or twice. Nothing is figurative in passing.

**What it does with its key terms.** Repeats them without variation. "Organizationality" appears in nearly
every paragraph; "collective actorhood", "collective social address", "attribution practices" and "charge /
discharge" are never replaced by synonyms.

**Abstraction level.** High and nominal. "The specification of criteria by which to define and distinguish
different degrees of organizationality." People appear as "scholars", "authors", "observers"; concrete
things appear only inside case tags (Mitsubishi, Dieselgate, a hashtag, a football club).

**Naming.** Plain labels for other people's positions (narrow, broad; configurational, stage-based, axial)
and one coined label for the problem ("openness-specificity dilemma").

**What it never does.** No aphorism, no epigram, no rhetorical question outside the research agenda, no
one-sentence paragraph, no "actually" or "of course", no second person, no joke. It never ends a paragraph
on a short sentence for effect.

**Hedging.** Constant and modal: "can", "may", "often", "tend to", "can be expected to", "can be presumed
to", "is likely to". The authors call their own choices "contingent" four times.

**Voice.** First person plural for both moves and claims, about 13 per 1,000 words: "we aim", "we make",
"we suggest", "we define", "we argue", "we posit", "we hold".

## Conflicts between the author's standing rules and the model

The author's rules for this paper, given on 8 October: plain words; say what you mean; no filler; no
signalling; no hedging; nothing cute.

1. **Signalling.** The model's Introduction is built from signalling sentences: the aim (C1.015), the
   count (C1.016), "First" and "Second" (C1.017, C1.019), "In sum" (C1.023), "More specifically" (C1.025).
   All three siblings do the same, so this is the genre's rule and not the model's habit. The skill sends
   a conflict of that kind to the author. **The author's later instruction, to map our work onto this
   paper almost sentence by sentence, is taken as the ruling: where the model signals, we signal.** The
   author can reverse it.
2. **Hedging.** The model hedges constantly; the venue does not agree with it (Kalkman asserts). The
   author's rule wins. Our claims are stated without "can", "may" or "is likely to", and each ledger entry
   whose counterpart hedges records the departure.
3. **Plain words.** The model's vocabulary is abstract and nominal ("propound a gradual understanding
   that conceptualizes"). Frames, connectives and single verbs are taken from the model. Its nominal
   style is not. Where the model writes "conceptualize X as Y" we write "treat X as Y" or "define X as
   Y", both of which the model also uses.
4. **Length of sentence.** The model's sentences average about thirty words. The author asked for plain
   statement, not short sentences, and the mapping instruction governs weight. Ours are written to
   within about 40% of the counterpart's weight.
