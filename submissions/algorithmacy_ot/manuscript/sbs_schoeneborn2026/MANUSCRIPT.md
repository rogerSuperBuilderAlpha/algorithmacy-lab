# Toward an Account of Algorithmacy: A Triadic View of Working Through Algorithms

## Abstract

This paper addresses a dilemma in accounts of how people work together through algorithmic systems: how to treat the system as something that decides without losing sight of the other person. Existing accounts are split between those that treat the system as the one a person deals with and those that treat it as a channel between two people. While the first leaves the other person out, the second leaves the system with nothing to decide. To resolve this dilemma, I advance the triadic view of platforms and add to it the concept of algorithmacy—the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. In particular, I distinguish abilities by the form of the relation in which they are used: oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads. The three parts of algorithmacy, in turn, follow from three features of the intermediary: interpreting from its opacity, specifying intent from its bindingness, and keeping track from its adaptivity. By taking the form of the relation as the criterion, I offer a framework that keeps what is known about the power of algorithmic systems while accounting for the person a participant still has to work with.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy, algorithmic management, coordination, literacy, platforms

## Introduction

The question of how algorithmic and generative AI systems change the way people work together has attracted growing interest among organizational scholars in recent years (e.g., Anthony et al., 2023; Hinds & von Krogh, 2024; Kellogg et al., 2020; Stelmaszak et al., 2026). Much of this work rests on one of two pictures of where such a system stands in relation to the people who work with it. On the one hand, some scholars treat the system as the one a person deals with: a communicative subject in its own right (Guzman & Lewis, 2020; see also Guzman, 2018), a counterpart in a system of work (Anthony et al., 2023), or an object the person has to understand (Long & Magerko, 2020). These scholars proceed from the finding that people respond to computers as they respond to other people (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), and on that premise the arrangement is a direct dyad, with the person as one member and the system as the other.

On the other hand, a longer line of work treats the system as something that passes between two people, a medium through which they communicate (Spitzberg, 2006; Walther, 1992, 1996) or a helper that writes on one person's behalf (Hancock et al., 2020). Proponents of this view base their accounts on the premise that the two people still judge each other and answer each other, and that the technology changes only the conditions under which they do so. On this premise the arrangement is a moderated dyad, in which the system stands between two people in the way a written text stands between a writer and a reader. For example, Hancock et al. (2020) define AI-mediated communication as communication in which a system "operates on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90).

These two pictures leave organization theory with a dilemma that bears directly on how it understands work under algorithmic management. The dilemma is that an account which keeps the other person in view treats the system as a channel, a helper, or an obstacle that decides nothing, while an account which treats the system as hidden and powerful reduces the other person to a source of ratings (Rahman, 2021; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025).

As one way out of this dyad dilemma, organization theorists have recently proposed a triadic view, in which a platform and the two parties it brings together form a relation of three (Cameron, 2024; Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Scholars who take this view hold that people on platforms are "co-opted" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 5), and that the platform rates and ranks the parties and controls what each can see of the other (Curchod et al., 2020; Kornberger et al., 2017). Although this work has established what the arrangement is and how it controls the people in it, it has not said what a person inside the arrangement must be able to do. The most recent relational account places the resulting capability in the organization and describes it as "not merely a human trait or skill" (Stelmaszak et al., 2026, p. 346), which leaves the person's own part without a name. In this paper, I therefore aim to advance the triadic view by specifying what a person must be able to do to work with another person through such a system and, correspondingly, the features of the system that make each part of that ability necessary. To this end, I make two proposals.

First, I propose to distinguish the abilities that working with others requires by one criterion only: the form of the relation between the people involved. On this criterion, oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads, and I thereby depart from earlier work that defines the ability either by a list of what a person should understand about the system (e.g., Long & Magerko, 2020) or by how well a worker understands and adapts to it (e.g., Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025).

Second, I propose that algorithmacy itself has three parts, each of which answers one feature of the system. Building on Simmel's (1902, 1950) account of the third party and on Daft and Lengel's (1986) account of equivocality, I define algorithmacy as the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. I argue that because the intermediary's rule is hidden the person must interpret its results, because its result binds both people she must specify her intent to the rule and to the other person at once, and because the rule changes she must keep track of it. Consequently, the three parts form a cycle that a person repeats, and people who have had more turns at it hold more of the ability than others in the same formal position.

In sum, this paper sets out an account of algorithmacy that is based on the form of the relation in which people work. By taking the form of the relation as the criterion, I not only provide one basis for distinguishing algorithmacy from oracy and literacy but also explain why existing accounts of digital skill, which treat the arrangement as a dyad, leave part of it out. More specifically, I develop a framework that explains how each of the three parts follows from one feature of the intermediary and how the parts depend on one another. In this way, I provide organization theory with the co-opted person's side of the form that Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe, and organizations with three courses of action: to disclose the rule, to place a person between the system and those it judges, or to see that their people acquire the ability.

## The Triadic View of Platforms

The view that a platform sets up a relation of three, and not of two, has gained ground in organization theory in recent years (e.g., Cameron, 2024; Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) applied the word "co-opted" to platforms in order to describe a form of organizing that is neither hierarchy, market, nor network. On this view, the people on a platform are not commanded, as in a hierarchy, do not contract, as in a market, and do not collaborate, as in a network, but are drawn in and organized by a platform that does not employ them (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 5). Scholars have used the view to study online marketplaces (Curchod et al., 2020), platform work (Cameron, 2024), and online dispute resolution, where the technology has been called a "fourth party" (Katsh & Rifkin, 2001).

What the view still has to specify is the ability a person needs in order to work with another person inside such a relation. Existing accounts of that ability, however, each turn the relation of three into a relation of two. In particular, different authors give the ability different names. These names include AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020), computer-mediated communication competence (Spitzberg, 2006), algorithmic competency (Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025), reactivity to opaque evaluation (Rahman, 2021), and gig literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020). Different authors have also placed the system differently in relation to the people involved.

Scholars who place the system as the other party treat the arrangement as a direct dyad, with the system as its second member. For example, Guzman and Lewis (2020) propose to study AI systems as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). This placement fits a person who is using a chatbot. For a person who works with other people through the system, however, it leaves those people out, and critics inside the field have said that it treats the person and the machine as an isolated pair when the machine also carries the rules of whoever deployed it (Gibbs et al., 2021). A similar placement is followed in work on AI literacy, whose best-known statement lists seventeen things a person should understand about AI, none of which describes another person as a party to the interaction (Long & Magerko, 2020; see also Iyamu et al., 2026; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). This placement is useful for studying how people respond to a machine socially, which is the finding it grew out of (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996).

Scholars who place the system as a channel treat the arrangement as a moderated dyad, with the system in the place of a text. For example, Spitzberg (2006) describes communicating well through a computer as a matter between two people who judge each other directly, with the technology changing conditions such as speed. Walther (1992, 1996) shows people learning from each other's replies to carry personal meaning in plain text; Hancock et al. (2020) add a system that "operates on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90); and research on machines as teammates has the machine advise or assist while the people decide (Sebo et al., 2020; Seeber et al., 2020; Traeger et al., 2020). Accordingly, the system in these accounts carries, helps, or advises, and what is studied is how its help changes what the two people think of each other (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020; Hohenstein et al., 2023). Like the first placement, this one is useful within its range, which is two people who answer each other through a technology that decides nothing.

Finally, scholars who study work on platforms place the system as the worker's only counterpart, the thing the worker has to understand and adapt to. Of particular relevance to the approach I develop in this paper, Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al. (2025) have built and tested a twelve-item questionnaire for what they term "algorithmic competency" (see also Hong et al., 2026). They define it as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2), and the customer appears in their items only as a source of ratings. Even the item about fixing an unfair result asks only whether the worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)" (p. 8). Compared to the first two placements, this one has the clear advantage of taking the system's power into account, since it treats the system as something that assigns and evaluates work. However, it does not explain how a worker deals with the other person, and the two studies that include that person in their fieldwork find workers reaching the person by leaving the system (Rahman, 2021; Sutherland et al., 2020). While these studies record that both parties are inside a relation of three, their constructs cover only one of them.

## The Form of the Relation as a Defining Criterion

The preceding section has shown that the triadic view still lacks an account of the ability it implies, and that existing accounts name that ability differently and place the system differently. To compare these accounts and to add to them, it is first necessary to settle what distinguishes one such ability from another. Only then can it be said what is new about working through an algorithm and what is not.

At present, the three placements summarized above each offer a different basis for the ability: what a person understands about the system, how two people communicate through it, and how well a worker adapts to it. A choice has to be made among these bases. The choice decides what researchers who adopt it will see: a basis in the person and the system shows no second person, and a basis in two people shows no system that decides. In making this point, I follow Georg Simmel's (1902, 1950) argument that the number of parties to a relation determines its form. As Simmel argued, a relation of three is a different kind of thing from a relation of two, because a third party can mediate between the other two, profit from their disagreement, or set them against each other, and in each case their relation passes through the third, who has aims of its own.

Over the following sections, I make the case for adopting the form of the relation as the criterion that distinguishes the abilities people need in order to work together. While aligning to a certain extent with Anthony et al. (2023), who sort accounts of technology by whether they treat it as a tool, a medium, or a counterpart, my criterion differs in sorting by the relation among the people, and it therefore reaches the case in which a system stands between two people and decides for both. The criterion also keeps what each of the three placements describes. I treat what they describe as "secondary" in the sense that a system does address the person who uses it, does carry what passes between two people, and does hold power over a worker, and none of these by itself defines the relation the person is in. This allows the three bodies of work to be read together.

I give the reasons for this choice in turn. First, basing the account on one criterion makes it simpler than accounts that define the ability by a list. Both the list of seventeen things a person should understand about AI (Long & Magerko, 2020) and the twelve-item questionnaire on algorithmic competency (Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025) require not only a reason why these items belong and others do not but also an account of how the items relate to each other. By contrast, the form of the relation requires no list. Instead, the relation itself (or, to be more precise, what the thing in the middle does to the two people) indicates what a person must be able to do, since each feature of the intermediary leaves one gap and each part of the ability fills one.

Second, although the form of the relation is one criterion among others that could have been chosen, it has advantages beyond its simplicity. One advantage is that organization theory already distinguishes forms of coordination by the position each gives the participant: an employee who follows legitimate instructions in a hierarchy (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978), a party who can accept or refuse a price in a market (Hayek, 1945), and a partner in a relationship either side can end in a network (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). Similarly, Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) define coordination by the conditions under which people get interdependent tasks done together, which are accountability, predictability, and common understanding. Building on the form of the relation therefore connects an account of individual ability to the field's accounts of coordination. As Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put it: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (p. 5). Other scholars on whom the triadic view draws also define an arrangement by the positions it gives people. Selznick (1949) describes co-optation as an organization giving a critic a seat on one of its boards, in contrast to a platform, which enrolls people on its own terms and gives them none. Puranam et al. (2014) similarly define a form of organizing as a set of solutions to four problems, which are dividing tasks, allocating them, providing rewards, and providing information, while Kornberger et al. (2017) show platforms coordinating people they do not employ through rating and ranking.

In addition to being shared within organization theory, the criterion has the advantage of reaching the abilities that came before algorithmacy. For example, oracy, the ability to take part in speaking and listening (Wilkinson, 1965), belongs to the direct dyad, in which I speak to you and you answer, and to chains of such dyads, in which I tell John and John tells Sally. Literacy belongs to the moderated dyad, in which a text stands between two people, fixes the writer's words, and leaves out the writer's tone and the reader's chance to ask (Olson, 1994). And this is in line with the history of American firms, which between about 1850 and 1920 came to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files and so came to assume that their people could read and write (Yates, 1989).

Having proposed the form of the relation as the criterion, I turn to specifying the form that is new. Scholars have put forward several overlapping descriptions of the relation of three: a lopsided relation on an online marketplace, held in place by differences in what each party can see (Curchod et al., 2020), an "algorithmic labor triangle" (Cameron, 2024), and a "fourth party" in online dispute resolution (Katsh & Rifkin, 2001). What these descriptions share is that the party in the middle acts on the other two. According to Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024), for example, when a user rates a provider, "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). Similarly, Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe the effects of a manager's use of generative AI to evaluate employees as running "between managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4). Leonardi and Leavell (2026) make a very similar point about an AI simulation used in urban planning, which in one organization became "the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533) between planners and stakeholders.

Accordingly, the thing in the middle of a triad is not a moderator but a party. In turn, this means that a person in a triad deals with an intermediary that is opaque, in that she cannot see its rule, binding, in that its result counts for both people and neither can set it aside, and adaptive, in that its rule changes. The same point bears on Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) observation that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13): the third party decides for the other two and presents itself as not a party. To date, however, this line of research has been concerned with what the arrangement is and how it controls people rather than with what a person inside it must be able to do. Below I set out a framework that explains what that ability consists of and why each part of it is needed.

## Algorithmacy as the Ability to Work in a Triad

Based on Daft and Lengel's (1986) distinction between lacking information and having information that can be read in several conflicting ways, I develop a framework that explains what a person in a triad must be able to do: to interpret results, to specify her intent, and to keep track of the rule. As shown in Figure 1, the framework rests on the distinction between a moderator and a third party and on the idea that each feature of a third party leaves the person without something she needs. Building directly on accounts of what coordination requires (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009), I argue that what the intermediary withholds explains what the person has to supply. In brief, the account rests on two closely related arguments. First, like a text, an intermediary stands between two people, but one that evaluates and binds them is a party to their relation and has features that a text does not have. Second, each of those features calls for one part of the ability: opacity for interpreting, bindingness for specifying intent, and adaptivity for keeping track.

### Moderators Versus Third Parties

The framework proceeds from the premise that coordination rests on three conditions, that is, accountability, predictability, and common understanding (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). For the parties to the relation I use three terms: the participant, who is the person whose position is in view; the counterpart, who is the other person she works with; and the intermediary, which is the system between them. A moderator can be defined as something that stands between two people and shapes what passes between them without being one of them. Accordingly, I understand a written text as the standard case of a moderator. In line with Olson (1994), I hold that a text fixes a writer's words and leaves out the writer's tone and the reader's chance to ask what was meant. A text judges no one, decides nothing, and does not change once it is written. In a chain of direct dyads, by contrast, a message passes through a person, who can change it, forget part of it, or add to it. Thus the reader of a text has the writer's own words and no one to ask.

Central to the framework is the distinction between a moderator and a third party (Simmel, 1950). The two differ in what they do to the people on either side of them. For instance, a text carries what one person says to another and takes no action of its own. A third party, in turn, acts: an algorithm that evaluates two people who are working together rewards one of them, penalizes the other, and changes the terms the next time. Unlike the arbitrator Simmel (1950) describes, however, an algorithmic third party was not given its power by the two people, and all they can do is leave, at the cost of a reputation they cannot rebuild elsewhere (Curchod et al., 2020). For example, a manager who uses generative AI to evaluate an employee, and the employee who is evaluated, can study what goes in and what comes out, while "the process for getting there is opaque" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6). Indeed, neither can see what the evaluation rests on, since the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4). In line with Okhuysen and Bechky (2009), I assume that two people in this position lose the conditions on which their coordination rested: accountability, because the intermediary decides and denies deciding; predictability, because the rule changes without notice; and common understanding, because neither can see how the other's part was weighed. This is because the intermediary, and not either person, now settles what each one's act leads to for the other.

### Three Features of the Intermediary and Three Parts of the Ability

The framework rests on the idea that the participant does not lack information (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Here I draw on the notion of "equivocality" in the sense in which Daft and Lengel (1986) separate it from uncertainty: uncertainty is too little data, and equivocality is data that can be read in several conflicting ways. I use the notion to describe the participant's position: she has the result, what the counterpart said and did, and her own record, and she has several explanations that all fit and no channel in which to settle among them. Figure 1 shows the three parts of the ability as a cycle around the participant, the counterpart, and the intermediary. Consistent with the view that the ability is acquired by taking part, a person's algorithmacy grows through repeated turns of the cycle. Within this framework, the participant has three things to settle: (a) what caused a result; (b) how to make her meaning reach both the counterpart and the intermediary; and (c) whether the rule is still the one she has learned.

Regarding the first, the cause of a result lies in the rule, in the people, or in both, and interpreting is working out which. For instance, an employee who receives a poor evaluation has to work out how much of it was her manager's judgment and how much was the system's (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). The same holds on a marketplace, where a seller whose standing drops holds one buyer's rating, processed by a rule she cannot see (Curchod et al., 2020). As Sundar and Nass (2000) have shown, however, the system does not supply this judgment: people who were given the same tutoring and evaluation on a screen rated it differently according to whether they were told it came from the computer or from a person.

Second, regarding how her meaning reaches both, I hold that under a binding intermediary whatever the participant says to the counterpart is also evidence that will be scored. This is because the place where she addresses the counterpart is the place where both of them are judged. In Figure 1, this part is the step from an interpretation to the participant's next act, which has to be made for two readers at once.

Third, the participant keeps what she has worked out only as long as the rule stays the same. This is evident, for instance, in generative systems, whose functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4). However, a change in the rule is not announced, and the participant detects it only from its effects. In the case of hotels, for instance, the inspection schemes through which hotels and guests once met gave way to anonymous reviews and ratings "aggregated into rankings via the platforms' proprietary algorithms" (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023, p. 3), so that what a hotelier had learned about earning a rating described a system that had been displaced. Keeping track then consists in separating two questions, whether the rule changed and whether the people did, and people who cannot separate them either experiment and risk their standing or cut back to protect it (Rahman, 2021).

### Practising the Three Parts, and Failing at Them

In this section, I describe how the three parts are practised in arrangements that organizational scholars have documented, and how a person fails at them by treating the triad as a dyad. I treat each part here as something a person does and learns by doing, and not as a procedure that can be written down and taught (Jablin & Sias, 2001; Sandberg, 2000). Because the operator does not disclose the rule, instruction cannot replace this learning, which is why scales that test what a course taught about AI do not measure it (Laupichler et al., 2023). Below, I draw on four published accounts: a manager and an employee under an evaluation by generative AI (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024), a buyer and a seller on a marketplace (Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), a hotel and its guests under a ranking (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023), and planners and stakeholders around a simulation model (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026).

*Practising the three parts.* One of the most common occasions for interpreting is a result the participant did not expect, which she has to trace to the rule, to the counterpart, or to both. How people decide who or what is behind a message from a machine has long been studied in communication research (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996; Sundar & Nass, 2000). For example, Hohenstein and Jung (2020) studied chats in which a system suggested replies and found that when a chat went badly, people blamed the system and trusted their human partner more. Conversely, a person who believes that machines are objective gives the system's part too much weight: people told that a chat agent was a machine were more willing to give it a credit-card number than people told it was a human (Sundar & Kim, 2019).

Interpreting works by setting the result beside what the counterpart visibly did. For instance, a hotelier can read what her guests wrote and cannot read how the platform weighed it, so that a fall in her ranking follows from the reviews, from the weighting, or from a change in the weighting (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023). Two courses of action follow from the two readings of a result. If the counterpart judged the work poorly, the participant should change the work or explain it better, and if the counterpart judged it acceptable and the rule pushed the result down, changing how she addresses the counterpart is wasted. Interpreting thereby restores accountability, because it settles who or what was responsible for a result.

Specifying intent is called for whenever the participant writes something that the counterpart will read and the intermediary will process, as when a manager enters an assessment of an employee into a system that will fold it into an evaluation (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). Like any writer, she has to put her meaning into words, and unlike other writers she has to put it in a form that survives the processing and still tells the counterpart what to do differently. Two people ordinarily check their understanding through small signals back and forth (Clark & Brennan, 1991), and when one side is a machine the checking breaks down, because the machine holds a thin record of what the person meant and the person does the repair (Suchman, 2007). Yet how much of her meaning reaches the counterpart varies with how she presents it. For example, in one of the two planning organizations that Leonardi and Leavell (2026) studied, planners showed stakeholders detailed, animated output from an AI simulation, while in the other they showed charts and called the model a tool (pp. 525–534). As this example illustrates, the planners' choices of how much detail to show, when to involve stakeholders, and what to call the model decided whether stakeholders took its output as a forecast or as an aid.

In the same arrangements, keeping track is practised by watching what the counterpart does as well as one's own results. A way of working that used to pass draws objections, and people who used to agree with the results are surprised by them. Based on the finding that users of social media notice when a feed algorithm has changed and revise their beliefs about it (DeVito, 2021; see also Duran, 1983), the practice is learned by taking part and is sometimes taught. In the case of generative AI, for example, "more and more functions are encapsulated as the GenAI learns and updates its model" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6), so that what an employee worked out about the system last quarter describes a system that no longer exists.

*Failing at the three parts.* Published studies also document two ways of failing at these parts. The first is to treat the triad as a direct dyad with the system, in which the participant explains every result by the rule, builds a theory of what the system rewards, and treats what the other people do as noise. Leonardi and Leavell (2026) give this failure a name, "artificial certainty," which they define as "the illusion that complex future outcomes are definitively knowable" (p. 516). Such certainty was evident in the planning organization that showed detailed output, where 83% of stakeholders' coded statements about what the model predicted were absolute, against 9% in the other organization (p. 535), and where the authors counted seventeen lawsuits (p. 537).

The second way of failing is to treat the triad as a moderated dyad, in which the system only carries what the participant says. This failure becomes visible when a participant writes for one reader only: she runs her explanation through a language model that tunes it for automated scoring and writes nothing a person can use, or she writes a clear note to the counterpart that the system cannot process. Either way, she acts as if one of the three parties were not there. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) expect generative AI to "minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7) at "the cost of weaker social ties and a reduced sense of team identity" (p. 9). A participant who writes only for the intermediary does this to her own working relationships, and a participant who reads the intermediary's result as the counterpart's opinion does the reverse, since in each case one part of the triad stands in for the whole.

## Discussion

In this paper, I have sought to advance the triadic view of platforms as an approach that offers a way out of the dyad dilemma. For this purpose, I first reviewed existing accounts of the ability to work through an algorithmic system and sorted them by where they place the system. On that basis, I set out an account of algorithmacy.

The account rests on two proposals. In the first, I made the case for adopting the form of the relation as the criterion that distinguishes the abilities people need in order to work together, so that oracy belongs to direct and chained dyads, literacy to moderated dyads, and algorithmacy to triads. Second, I proposed that algorithmacy has three parts, each called for by one feature of the intermediary: interpreting by its opacity, specifying intent by its bindingness, and keeping track by its adaptivity. With these proposals, I contribute in two main ways to the literatures on platform organization and on algorithmic management, which I discuss in turn.

### Placing the Abilities on One Criterion, From Dyads to Triads

The ability to work through algorithmic systems has been given many names (Long & Magerko, 2020; Rahman, 2021; Spitzberg, 2006; Sutherland et al., 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025). Although research on these abilities is growing, and although scholars agree that the systems are more than tools, they differ in where they place the system in relation to the people who work with it (e.g., Anthony et al., 2023; Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Hancock et al., 2020). By going back through this work, I sought to bring out the placement that each account assumes. In particular, I identified three placements: the system as the other party, the system as a channel, and the system as the worker's only counterpart. None of these is wrong within its range, and the review has shown what each leaves out when two people work through a system that decides for both. Setting them side by side allows earlier accounts to be compared.

Building on these accounts, I developed my own proposal, which distinguishes abilities by the form of the relation. And while other criteria could have been chosen, I have argued that this one has three advantages. First, it is simpler than accounts that define the ability by a list (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025). Second, it agrees with the way organization theory already distinguishes forms of coordination, which is by the position each gives the participant (Powell, 1990; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Third, it reaches the abilities that came before algorithmacy, as well.

Key to the proposal is the distinction between a moderator and a third party. This distinction rests on the premise that what stands between two people either carries what passes between them or also acts on them. Depending on which it does, the arrangement is a moderated dyad or a triad. The abilities are therefore distinguished by the form in which they are used, and one does not grow into the next: a good speaker cannot thereby read, and a good reader cannot thereby work through an algorithm that judges her. In contrast to accounts that list what a person should know (Long & Magerko, 2020; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025), my account relies on one criterion on which arrangements are placed. As a result, arrangements as different as an evaluation by generative AI, a marketplace rating, a hotel ranking, and a planning simulation can be compared, because each is assessed by what the thing in the middle does to the two people. By contrast, a list gives no guidance on which of its items matter once a second person is involved. And unlike the lenses of Anthony et al. (2023), which also sort accounts by where they place the technology, my criterion sorts by the relation among the people and so reaches the triad.

At the same time, the account is compatible with what recent articles in this journal have established. This is because several of their concepts can be read as descriptions of what a participant in a triad does: the attribution of agency to a collective or to individuals (Schoeneborn et al., 2026) is what a participant does when she interprets a result, and the "situated engagement with the non-present" that Porsfelt et al. (2026, p. 1) find in imagination is close to her engagement with a rule that is present and hidden. I thus propose one criterion, the form of the relation, with these and other concepts placed in relation to it, that is, by asking what each says about a person who has to work with another person through a third party that decides for both.

The account also provides a way out of the dyad dilemma identified at the outset. By treating the system as a party, in line with the triadic view (Curchod et al., 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024), I keep what accounts of platform work have established about its power (Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2021). At the same time, by defining the ability as work done with a counterpart, I keep the other person in view, as accounts of mediated communication do (Spitzberg, 2006; Walther, 1992, 1996).

### The Co-opted Person's Side of Platform Organization

The framework also contributes an account of how the ability works over time. While Stelmaszak et al. (2026) note that people who work under such a system learn to change how they behave (p. 350), this paper says what it is that they learn. More specifically, I explain the ability as a cycle in which an interpretation shapes the participant's next act and the next result shows whether the rule is still the same. Thereby I also show that the three parts depend on one another: without specifying intent, interpreting is a private diagnosis; without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting; and without keeping track, the other two run on an understanding that has expired. The framework then raises the question of how people who have little of the ability get by. Earlier studies point to two answers: they leave the system (Rahman, 2021; Sutherland et al., 2020), or an expert interprets for them, as the planners did who presented a model's output as one aid among others (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). The framework specifies what such an expert does for others and what a participant without one has to do for herself. It also explains why organizations fail with these systems: tools are installed across an organization and then ignored or worked around (Christin, 2017), because people who used to deal with a colleague directly, or through a channel that only carried messages, go on acting as before.

With this account of the participant's side, I also contribute to debates on algorithmic management (Cameron, 2024; Kellogg et al., 2020; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Vallas & Schor, 2020). While prior work has traced how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers, and how accountability under them is "distributed, deflected, and denied" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14), it has not said what a person must be able to do when that accountability is deflected onto her. The framework addresses this by describing the three parts of the ability and the feature of the intermediary that calls for each. By studying these parts, therefore, it becomes possible to explain both how people whom a platform treats as identical come to different outcomes, since they have had unequal numbers of turns at the cycle and unequal help from peers (Hargittai, 2002; Kellogg et al., 2020), and how their growing ability keeps an unfair arrangement running, since people who absorb quietly what would otherwise show lower the pressure to change it (Cameron, 2024; Elish, 2019).

## Conclusion and Research Agenda

In this paper, I have addressed the dyad dilemma in existing accounts of working through algorithmic systems by developing an account of algorithmacy based on the form of the relation. The account resolves the dilemma and advances the triadic view by explaining what a participant has to do because the intermediary is opaque, binding, and adaptive. It treats the system as a party and keeps the other person in view.

The account opens a research agenda by raising four questions. First, it is worth asking: Are the three parts distinct when they are measured, and is deciding whether the system or the other person caused a change a fourth part of its own? Although I have placed that decision within interpreting, one study indicates that it is a task in itself (Rahman, 2021). The second question is whether algorithmacy explains who does well once two other things are taken into account, namely how dependent a person is on the system and whether her standing has dropped sharply before. This is relevant because one study found that these two things drive how workers respond to an evaluation they cannot read (Rahman, 2021). Third, the account raises the question: Is algorithmacy different in practice from general digital skill and from ordinary skill at communicating? The argument here is that it is, because neither of those involves reading and addressing two parties through one hidden rule. Finally, future research could examine which of three courses organizations take when they put such a system between their people: disclosing the rule, placing a person between the system and those it judges, or seeing that their people acquire the ability. For instance, when a robot working with three people admitted its errors and its uncertainty, the people's talk with one another became more equal (Traeger et al., 2020), whereas a person who disputes a platform's result at present has to appeal to the system that produced it (Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy, 2017; Wing, 2016).

In addition to these questions, the framework allows arrangements to be compared on one criterion, and in that way connects organization theory to the study of communication, from which most accounts of the earlier abilities come (Spitzberg, 2006; Walther, 1992, 1996; Wilkinson, 1965). By treating the algorithmic system as a party to a relation of three, and not as a tool, a medium, or a counterpart, the account widens the range of arrangements in which organizational scholars can study how people work together. As such, it invites researchers to look for algorithmacy wherever a system evaluates and binds two people who depend on each other, including but not limited to evaluations by generative AI, marketplace ratings, hotel rankings, planning simulations, dispatch systems, piecework queues, and peer review with hidden scoring. A task for future research will then be to show how far the ability explains what happens to people in these arrangements, and how far organizations that install such systems have checked that their people have it.
