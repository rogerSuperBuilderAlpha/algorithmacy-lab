# Algorithmacy: Coordinating with another person through an algorithm that judges you both

## Abstract

More and more work is arranged so that two people depend on each other, and an algorithm sits between them. The algorithm scores what each of them does, its score counts for both of them, and neither of them can see how it is calculated. Organization theory has described this arrangement from the side of the platform that runs it. It has not described what a person inside the arrangement has to be able to do in order to work well with the other person. This article describes that. I call the ability algorithmacy, by analogy with literacy. It has three parts. The person has to work out, from a result she cannot fully explain, what the algorithm's rule probably is and what the other person probably meant. She has to act and write in a way that both the algorithm and the other person will take correctly. And she has to notice when the rule changes, because nobody tells her. I show that each of these three tasks follows from one feature of the algorithm: it is hidden, its result is binding, and it changes. I also show that existing accounts of digital skill each cover two of the three parties and leave out the third. The article ends with three propositions and with a warning. People who get good at this help themselves, and they also make an unfair arrangement run more smoothly.

*Keywords:* algorithmacy; algorithmic management; coordination; co-optation; platforms; generative AI; literacy

## Introduction

[AUTHOR, corrected] Organizations fail to adopt and understand the tsunami of new platforms and services enabled by generative AI. While global leaders promote these technologies, less visible displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are also reconfiguring how organizations perform (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023). Focusing on those less visible shifts allows us to recognize how generative AI sits inside the work and reshapes it, functioning more as—but not quite—a "counterpart" in a system of work that includes its design, implementation, and use (Anthony et al., 2023). Hoping to install a plug-and-play intelligence ignores that organizing now depends on relations among human and algorithmic actors (Stelmaszak et al., 2026), relations in which generative AI may expand the black box and reduce visibility and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024) while its detailed outputs give those who are shown them an illusion that uncertain outcomes are knowable (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a co-optive, opaque, adaptive intermediary.

[AUTHOR, corrected] If it were only a matter of how, this proposal would be less interesting. The how I propose also requires that we refactor the why.

[AUTHOR, corrected] Organizations have been doing the equivalent of giving books to people who lack literacy. We heap algorithmic management systems on people who lack algorithmacy, or the sensibility to navigate opaque triadic coordinative co-optation. The intermediaries in question are not neutral software. The platforms behind them hold collective actorhood by degree, and they work to downplay it (Schoeneborn et al., 2026); on them, as Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 5) put it, actors "are co-opted"—enrolled, evaluated, and bound. Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are strapping ourselves to the mast of a "literacy"-based understanding. We do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or reducing the triad to pairs, which leaves us blind to its emergent, binding power.

[AUTHOR, corrected] Oracy—the term is Wilkinson's (1965)—is the sensibility to navigate direct dyadic forms through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate chained or linked dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. Porsfelt et al. (2026, p. 1) propose treating imagination as "a situated engagement with the non-present"; algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with what is present and unseen. As a sensibility like literacy and oracy, it operates through the competencies and capacities to: 1) interpret, from an otherwise innocuous output, both the intermediary's rule and a counterpart's intentions; 2) specify intent so meaning survives the opaque, adaptive mediator; and 3) keep track as governance radically, repeatedly, and suddenly shifts. We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

The article has five parts. I first describe the situation I am writing about, using one example that I come back to throughout. I then go through the existing accounts and show what each one leaves out. Third, I define the arrangement and define algorithmacy. Fourth, and at most length, I explain how algorithmacy works: what each of its three parts is, why it is needed, and what happens when it is missing. Last, I say what this changes for organization theory, and I come back to the question of why organizations fail with these systems.

## The situation

Here is the example. A student in a software course submits her code for review. Several classmates read it. They leave comments, and each of them votes on whether it should pass. She can see the comments and the votes. Then a program takes the votes and other inputs, combines them by a formula, and returns one result: pass or fail. Nobody in the course can see the formula. The instructor has no vote and cannot change the result. The formula is adjusted during the term, and the adjustments are not announced. The classmates who review her are scored too, on how well their reviews match the final results.

Three parties are involved, and I will use the same three names for them from here on. The *participant* is the person whose position we are looking at, here the student. The *counterpart* is the other person she is working with, here a classmate who reviews her. The *intermediary* is the program that stands between them and produces the result.

The intermediary has three features, and the whole article depends on them. First, it is *opaque*. This means the participant cannot see the rule it uses. She sees what went in and what came out, and nothing in between. Second, it is *binding*. This means its result counts for both people and neither of them can set it aside. The student passes or fails, and the reviewer's own score moves, on the strength of the same result. Third, it is *adaptive*. This means the rule changes over time. What produced a pass in week four may not produce one in week ten.

The student has a practical problem, and it is not the problem of writing good code. She has to work with her reviewers, since they are the ones who read her work and whose reactions feed the result. But what reaches her from them comes through a rule she cannot see. If she fails, she does not know whether her reviewers thought the work was poor, or thought it was fine and were outweighed by something else in the formula. When she writes her next explanation, she is writing for people who need to understand it and also for a program that will score it. And whatever she learns about how the program behaves may stop being true without warning.

The same situation exists at work, where more depends on it. A ride-hailing driver and a passenger are matched by a dispatch system, each rates the other, and the system uses those ratings in ways neither can see. Manky (2025) studied forty drivers in Lima and found them studying the platform's figures on a passenger in order to judge whether that passenger was safe to pick up. A freelancer and a client work together on a platform that turns the client's private feedback into a score for the freelancer. Rahman (2021) studied one such platform and found that clients did not know how their feedback changed the freelancer's score, and that the platform had told them the feedback would never be shown to the freelancer directly (p. 960). In each case two people depend on each other, and the consequences of what each does for the other are set by a rule that neither can read.

This article is about what the participant needs in order to do well in that situation. I call it algorithmacy. Before defining it, I show that the existing accounts do not already cover it.

## What existing accounts leave out

### Organization theory describes the arrangement, not the person inside it

Organization theory has a good description of the arrangement as a whole. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) argue that platforms are not hierarchies, markets, or networks, and they put the difference in one sentence: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (p. 5). By this they mean that the platform does not employ the people who use it and does not simply sell to them. It draws them in and organizes what they do. Orlikowski and Scott (2023) add that digital systems can displace established rules and standards in ways that are hard to see while they are happening. These accounts are written from the point of view of the system. They explain what the platform does to people. They do not say what a person has to be able to do in return.

The most recent accounts come close to the situation I described and then stop. Stelmaszak et al. (2026) argue that artificial intelligence in an organization is not located in the software. It comes from "multiple, complex, and multifaceted relations among human and algorithmic actors" (p. 337). Their own example includes all three of my parties. The Uber system, they write, "includes the drivers (human actors), the riders (human actors), and the platform (algorithmic actors)" (p. 346). So they do count the rider. What they do not do is examine the relation between the driver and the rider. Every relation they analyze is between a person and an algorithm. They also say the resulting capability belongs to the organization and is "not merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346). They mention that drivers "learn how to change their behaviours" (p. 350), and they do not say what it is that the drivers learn.

Hinds and von Krogh (2024) reach the same point when they write about generative AI. They describe a manager who uses it to evaluate employees. The effects, they say, run "between managers, GenAI, and employees," and the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). That is a manager, an employee, and an opaque system between them. The authors are interested in what this does to the organization. They do not ask what the employee, or the manager, now has to be able to do.

### Why the third party matters

It could seem that adding an algorithm between two people is a small change, so I want to say why it is not. Simmel (1902) made the argument for groups of people more than a century ago. He pointed out that a group of three is a different kind of thing from a group of two. In a pair, each person deals directly with the other. Once there is a third, the third can take up positions that do not exist in a pair. The third can mediate between the other two, or profit from their disagreement, or set them against each other. In all of these cases the two no longer simply deal with each other. Their relation passes through the third, and the third has aims of its own (Simmel, 1950).

This gives a simple test to apply to any account of digital skill. What position does the account give the algorithm, and what happens to the other person? I apply that test to eight accounts from organization theory, communication research, and human–computer interaction. They fall into three groups. Table 1 lists them.

### Group one: the machine is the other party

The first group treats the machine as the one the person is dealing with. Human–machine communication is the clearest case. Guzman and Lewis (2020) argue that AI systems should be studied as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71). In other words, the machine is not a channel between people. It is the one you are talking to. This is the right account for a person using a chatbot or a voice assistant. For the student at the gate it leaves out her reviewers. A person could be very good at dealing with the machine and still have no idea what her reviewers meant. AI literacy belongs in the same group. Long and Magerko (2020) list seventeen things a person should understand about AI, such as what it is, what it can do, and how it works. None of the seventeen describes another person as a party to the interaction. Where other people appear in their list, they are the designers who built the system, or humans used as a point of comparison. The other person the participant is working with is not there.

### Group two: the machine is a channel between two people

The second group keeps both people and treats the machine as something messages pass through. Spitzberg (2006) describes what it takes to communicate well through a computer. In his model the two people judge each other directly, and the technology only changes conditions such as speed. Walther (1992, 1996) showed that people learn to get personal meaning across even in plain text, by adjusting their wording and timing. This works, in his account, because the person who receives the message is also the person who responds to it. The sender finds out what got across from the reply. That condition does not hold at the gate. The reviewer does reply, since her comment and her vote are visible. But the result that matters is produced by the program, and the program does not reply to anyone. It does not tell the student which part of her explanation worked. So the student cannot learn from the response in the way Walther's communicators do. Hancock et al. (2020) come nearer, because they put an AI system between two people. Their subject is AI that helps one person write to another, such as suggested replies. They define it as an agent that "operates on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90). The system works for one of the two people and changes that person's message. It does not judge both people, and it does not produce a result that binds them. That is the difference from the intermediary I am describing, which works for neither of them.

### Group three: the worker facing the system

The third group studies workers on platforms, so it is the closest to my subject. Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al. (2025) built and tested a twelve-item questionnaire that measures what they call algorithmic competency, which they define as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2). The customer appears in their items only as a source of ratings. One item reads, "I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback." The worker's attention in that item is on the platform's rules. The item that concerns fixing an unfair result asks whether the worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)" (p. 8). So the worker appeals to the platform, and never deals with the customer who gave the rating. A worker could score at the top on all twelve items and still have no ability to work out what a customer meant or to make herself understood by one. Rahman (2021) and Sutherland et al. (2020) both include the other person in their fieldwork, and both show what happens when workers try to reach that person. Rahman interviewed 80 freelancers and 18 clients (p. 956). His account is about how freelancers react to a score they cannot understand. In the one response where freelancers act toward the client, they move the relationship off the platform, which breaks the platform's rules. Sutherland et al. found the same thing. One of their freelancers, faced with a low rating that could not be repaired, took established clients elsewhere (p. 469). In both studies, the way to reach the other person is to leave the system. Neither study describes what it takes to work with the other person while staying inside it.

### What the eight have in common

All eight accounts leave out the same combination. Some of them include the other person, and in those the technology is a channel, a helper, or an obstacle. It never makes a decision that binds both people. Others treat the algorithm as hidden and powerful, and in those the other person becomes a number in a rating. None of them has an active other person and a binding, hidden algorithm at the same time.

I am not claiming that nobody has noticed the three-party shape. Curchod et al. (2020) describe a lopsided three-party relation on an online marketplace, kept in place by differences in what each party can see. Cameron (2024) writes of an "algorithmic labor triangle." Katsh and Rifkin (2001) called the technology in online dispute resolution a "fourth party." All of these describe the structure and how it controls people. My claim is narrower. No existing account describes the ability a person needs in order to do interdependent work with another person through an algorithm that judges and binds them both. Manky's (2025) drivers are using that ability when they read a passenger's figures. It has no name and no description. The rest of the article gives it both.

## The arrangement and the ability

### The arrangement: coordinative co-optation

I need a name for the arrangement, and I take it from the literature on platforms. The standard forms of coordination each give the participant a recognizable position. In a hierarchy she is an employee who follows legitimate instructions (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). In a market she is a buyer or seller who can accept or refuse a price (Hayek, 1945). In a network she is a partner in a relationship that either side can end (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). On a platform she is none of these. The platform's algorithms match her with people she did not choose, evaluate what she and they do, and can close her account (Stark & Pais, 2020).

I call this arrangement *coordinative co-optation*. The term is mine. I build it on Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) statement that on platforms people "are co-opted" (p. 5). The word needs explaining, because its classic meaning is different. Selznick (1949) used co-optation for what an organization does when it takes a critic or outsider onto one of its own boards. The outsider gets a seat and some formal standing, and in exchange stops being an opponent. A platform also draws outsiders in, but it gives them no seat and no standing. It enrolls them on its own terms, scores them, and can remove them. I add "coordinative" because the point of the arrangement, from the participant's side, is that it is how she and another person get joint work done.

The platform's position in this arrangement is unusual in two ways. The first comes from Simmel (1950). He describes the arbitrator as a third party who does not help the other two agree, but decides between them. A normal arbitrator has that power because the two parties gave it to him, and they can take it back. The platform decides between the two parties without either of them having given it that power. The only thing they can do about it is leave.

The second comes from Schoeneborn et al. (2026). They argue that being an organized actor is a matter of degree. A collective counts as an actor to the extent that people treat it as one and hold it responsible. They observe that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13). Platforms describe themselves as a marketplace, as infrastructure, or as a tool. So the participant is dealing with a third party that decides things for her and also presents itself as not being a party at all. This matters in practice. It is one reason she may not think to ask what the system is doing between her and the other person.

### The ability: algorithmacy

Algorithmacy is the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. It has three parts. The first is interpreting: working out, from a result, what the intermediary's rule probably is and what the counterpart probably meant. The second is specifying intent: acting and writing so that both the intermediary and the counterpart take one's meaning correctly. The third is keeping track: noticing when the rule changes.

I have been calling algorithmacy a sensibility, and I should say what I mean by that word. I mean a habit of seeing a situation a certain way, which comes before any particular skill. A person who can read does not decide each time to treat marks on a page as words. She simply sees them as words, and her reading skills work on top of that. In the same way, a person with algorithmacy sees a result from the system as something produced jointly by a rule and by other people. She sees her own next action as something that both the rule and the other people will receive. The three parts are the skills that work on top of this way of seeing. I use the word sensibility for the whole because a person can have some of the skills without it. Someone can be very good at guessing a platform's rule and never think about the person on the other side of the score.

Algorithmacy belongs to the individual, and that separates it from two nearby ideas. It is not a skill in the narrow sense of a fixed procedure that can be written down and taught, because the rule it deals with is secret and keeps changing. It is not an organizational capability in Teece et al.'s (1997) sense, meaning something a firm owns and uses. The firm owns the algorithm. The participant owns nothing in the arrangement. The closest existing idea is Sandberg's (2000). He studied engineers at Volvo and found that what made some of them more competent was not a longer list of skills. It was how they understood what their job was. Algorithmacy is competence of that kind, applied to work done through an algorithm. Saying the ability is individual does not mean people develop it alone. People pick it up from others in the same position, by comparing results and passing on what they have worked out. It is learned socially and used individually (Felin et al., 2015).

### Where it applies

Algorithmacy is needed only when all of the following are true. The intermediary is opaque, binding, and adaptive. The two people's work depends on each other. And the consequences of what one person does for the other pass through the intermediary. The last condition needs a comment. In my example the student can see her reviewers' comments and votes, so their exchange is partly visible. What she cannot see is how much weight each comment and vote carried in the result. It is the consequence that runs through the intermediary, not necessarily the communication.

Two contrasts show where the edges are. A dating or job-matching site that introduces two people and then lets them deal with each other directly has joint work and no binding result. A scoring system that rates a family and determines a caseworker's decision has a binding result, and the family and the system are not doing joint work. Neither case calls for algorithmacy. Freelance platforms sit near the edge, because people there can negotiate directly and can leave together (Sutherland et al., 2020). Dispatch systems, piecework queues, and peer-review systems with hidden scoring are the central cases.

The ability is also separate from the question of what rights the participant has. She might be legally an employee, a contractor, or a student. That is a question about her standing, and it matters a great deal for fairness. But it does not change what she has to be able to do. If she gains a right to appeal and the rule stays hidden, she still has to work out what the rule is. If the platform publishes the rule and fixes it, then the arrangement is no longer the one I am describing, and the need for the ability falls away.

## How algorithmacy works

### The participant's problem is too many explanations, not too little information

The participant's difficulty is easy to misdescribe. It looks like a lack of information, and that is not quite right. Daft and Lengel (1986) distinguish two problems. One is uncertainty, which means not having enough data. The other is equivocality, which means having data that can be read in several conflicting ways. The student at the gate is in the second position. She has the result, the comments, the votes, and her own record of earlier attempts. What she lacks is a way to decide among several explanations that all fit. Daft and Lengel argue that this second problem calls for a rich channel with feedback, one where people can go back and forth until they agree on a reading. The student has no such channel with the gate. The gate does not answer questions, and it is also the thing that judges her.

Each of the three features of the intermediary creates one specific gap, and each part of algorithmacy fills one gap. Because the intermediary is opaque, she does not know why a result came out as it did. Interpreting fills that gap. Because the intermediary is binding, anything she says to the other person is also evidence that will be scored. Specifying intent deals with that. Because the intermediary is adaptive, what she has worked out can go out of date. Keeping track deals with that. Table 2 sets this out. The three subsections below take the parts one at a time.

**Table 2.** How the three parts of algorithmacy follow from the three features of the intermediary.

| Feature of the intermediary | What the participant is missing as a result | What she has to do | What it looks like when she does not |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Opaque | The reason for a result, including who or what caused it | Interpret | She explains the result by the rule alone, or by the people alone |
| Binding | A way to speak to the other person that is not also being scored | Specify intent | She writes for the algorithm and loses the person, or the reverse |
| Adaptive | A rule that stays the same once she has learned it | Keep track | She keeps acting on an understanding that is out of date |

### Interpreting

Interpreting is working out, from a result, what probably caused it. It is needed because the intermediary is opaque. The cause has two possible sources, the rule and the people, and the participant has to consider both. Suppose the student fails. One explanation is that her reviewers judged the work to be poor. Another is that they judged it acceptable and some other input, or a higher threshold, pushed the result down. These lead to different actions. In the first case she should change the work, or explain it better to her reviewers. In the second case her reviewers are not the problem, and changing how she addresses them would be wasted effort.

Research on how people respond to computers shows that this kind of judgment comes from the person and is not given by the system. Sundar and Nass (2000) gave people the same tutoring and the same evaluation on a screen. Some were told it came from the computer and others were told it came from a person. People rated the identical content differently depending on who they believed was behind it. The authors call this source orientation, meaning the person's working belief about who is speaking. The student at the gate has to form that belief without being told anything. She has to decide how much of the result was her reviewers and how much was the formula.

Research on platforms has seen this problem and treated it as a problem about the score. In Rahman's (2021) study, a freelancer whose score drops cannot tell whether a client was unhappy or the platform changed its calculation. Rahman presents this as one of the reasons the score is impossible to understand. I am pointing at a different use of the same situation. The freelancer also needs to know what the client actually wanted, because she has to go on working with that client or with others like her. Manky's (2025) drivers show this second use. They read the platform's figures in order to learn something about the passenger.

Interpreting goes wrong in two ways. A participant can explain everything by the rule. She builds a theory of what the system rewards and treats the other people's reactions as noise. Or she can explain everything by the people. She takes the result as her reviewers' opinion of her and forgets that a formula stood between their opinions and the outcome. Both are reasonable guesses. Each one ignores half of the evidence she has.

### Specifying intent

Specifying intent is acting and writing so that one's meaning gets through to the other person and is also handled correctly by the intermediary. It is needed because the intermediary is binding. In an ordinary exchange, when the student writes an explanation of her code, the explanation is for her reviewer and it succeeds if the reviewer understands it. At the gate the same explanation is also an input to the result. The place where she writes to her reviewer is the place where she is judged. The reviewer is in the same position, since the reviewer's own score depends on how the review compares with the result.

So the student is writing for two readers at once. Her description of a code change has to be something the automated checks can process, and it also has to tell a human reviewer why the change matters. She does not know how much weight the system gives to the reviewer's understanding. She still has to produce one piece of writing that serves both.

The closest earlier work is Walther's (1992, 1996), described above. His communicators learn to get meaning across in a limited channel, and they learn it from replies. At the gate, the student gets a result and no reply. If she fails, the result tells her that this version did not pass. It does not tell her whether the reviewer misunderstood her or the program scored her down, or which sentence was the problem. Clark and Brennan (1991) describe how two people normally check that they have understood each other, through small signals back and forth. Here the reviewer's signals are visible, and the part that decides the outcome, which is how much they counted, is not.

Spitzberg's (2006) two standards for communicating well can still be used, with an adjustment. He asks whether a message is effective and whether it is appropriate. At the gate, a message is effective if it passes and the reviewer also understands what the student meant. It is appropriate if it fits what the reviewer expects, and the student only knows what the reviewer expects through her own interpreting. This is one of the places where the parts of algorithmacy depend on each other.

Specifying intent goes wrong when the message is built for only one of the two readers. A participant can run her explanation through a language model that tunes it for automated scoring, pass, and never write anything a person could use. Or she can write a clear, friendly note to her reviewer that the scoring program cannot process. Generative AI tools make the first of these very easy. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) expect generative AI to "minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7). A participant who writes only for the gate is doing that to her own working relationships.

### Keeping track

Keeping track is noticing that the rule has changed. It is needed because the intermediary is adaptive. Everything the student has worked out about the gate and about her reviewers comes from earlier results. If the formula is adjusted, some of that is no longer true, and nobody announces the adjustment. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) make the same observation about generative AI systems, whose functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4).

The difficulty is that she has to detect the change from indirect signs. Since she cannot see the rule, she can only see its effects. A style of submission that used to pass starts drawing objections. Reviewers who used to agree with the results start to be surprised by them. These signs show up in what the other people do, as well as in her own results. A participant who watches only her own numbers will often miss a change that would be obvious if she also watched how her reviewers were reacting.

Two earlier ideas are close to this. Duran (1983) described communicative adaptability, the ability to adjust to different partners and settings. His communicator can see the partner and the setting she is adjusting to. The participant here is adjusting to a rule she cannot see, so noticing the change is part of the work. DeVito (2021) studied how social media users notice that a feed algorithm has changed and update their beliefs about it. She shows that people learn this by taking part, and that it can be taught. Her users are managing their own feeds. There is no second person whose intentions they have to work out, and no result that binds two people who are working together. With those added, her finding supports what I am describing.

Keeping track goes wrong when the participant goes on acting on an old understanding. Rahman (2021) shows the cost. Freelancers who cannot tell what has changed either experiment and risk their scores, or cut back their activity to protect them. A participant who keeps track well asks two separate questions when results shift. Has the rule changed? Have the people changed? Rahman's freelancers could not separate these.

### How the three parts depend on each other

The three parts form a cycle. The participant gets a result she did not expect and interprets it. That interpretation shapes how she acts and writes the next time. The next result tells her something about whether the rule is the same as before, and that feeds into how she interprets the result after that. Figure 1 shows the cycle. The clearest way to see that the parts depend on each other is to remove one at a time. Without specifying intent, interpreting is only a private diagnosis. The student understands why she failed and her next submission is no different. Without interpreting, specifying intent is only formatting. She has no view of what either reader wants, so she has nothing to aim at. Without keeping track, the other two run on an understanding that may have expired. She will be confidently wrong, and the better she was at the other two, the longer she will stay that way.

This is also what I mean by calling the three-party arrangement irreducible. I mean something specific and limited. The participant cannot handle the arrangement as two separate two-party problems, first learning the machine and then dealing with the person, or the reverse. The result she reads was produced by the rule and the people together. The message she sends is received by the rule and the people together. Each part of her task involves all three parties at once.

The cycle also explains how people acquire algorithmacy. Jablin and Sias (2001) describe how newcomers in organizations learn to communicate competently. They are mostly not taught. They work it out from how others respond to them. The participant here learns in a similar way, by going around the cycle repeatedly. The difference is that she is reading results produced by a rule, not direct responses from the person she is trying to reach. Formal instruction cannot replace this, because the operator does not disclose the rule. It follows that people who have had more turns around the cycle, or who have had experienced peers to compare notes with, will have more of the ability than others who are in the same formal position.

## Discussion

### What this adds to research on algorithmic management

Research on algorithmic management has mostly studied control. Kellogg et al. (2020) summarize it as the study of how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers, and how workers resist. That research explains what the system does. Algorithmacy describes what a worker has to be able to do in order to get joint work done under that system. The two are compatible, and they point to different remedies. Making a system more transparent changes the information available. Developing algorithmacy changes what a person can do with whatever information there is. Algorithmacy also fills a gap that the relational accounts of AI leave open. Stelmaszak et al. (2026) say that AI capability belongs to the organization and arises from relations among people and algorithms. An account at that level cannot explain why two drivers on the same platform in the same week do differently. An ability that varies between individuals can. It also gives a name to what their drivers are learning when they "learn how to change their behaviours" (p. 350).

Leonardi and Leavell (2026) show what happens when there is someone whose job is to do the interpreting. They compared two urban planning organizations that used the same AI simulation tool. The planners stood between the tool and the officials, developers, and advocates who took part in planning. Where planners presented the tool's output as a prediction, the model became "the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533) and stakeholders "treated the outputs as binding forecasts" (p. 537). Where planners presented it as one aid among others, that did not happen. The authors describe the planners' work as "selecting, sequencing, qualifying, and presenting information so that others can act under uncertainty" (p. 518). In their case an expert does this work for other people. In the arrangement I am describing there is no such expert. The participant has to do the interpreting for herself.

### Why organizations fail with these systems

I said in the introduction that explaining how people coordinate through these systems would also change the explanation of why organizations fail with them. Here is that argument. The common explanations are that the tool was wrong for the job, that it was introduced badly, or that people were not trained to use it. All of these treat the problem as one of learning a tool.

My argument is that a system that judges and binds people changes the kind of coordination they are in. Before, a person dealt with a colleague directly, or through a channel that only carried messages. Now she deals with the colleague through something that scores them both. If nobody tells her the situation has changed in kind, she will go on acting as before. She will read a system's result as if it were her colleague's opinion. She will write to her colleague as if nothing were scoring what she wrote. Training in how to operate the tool does not fix this, because the tool's buttons are not what she has misunderstood. That is the point of the comparison with handing books to people who cannot read. The organization has supplied the object and assumed the ability to use it.

### A problem the idea creates

Algorithmacy has an uncomfortable consequence, and I want to state it directly. When individuals get better at working through an unfair arrangement, the arrangement works better, and there is less pressure to change it. A person's ability and the fairness of her situation are separate things. High algorithmacy does not mean she is being treated well.

There is a related risk in how the idea could be used. Elish (2019) describes how, when an automated system fails, blame tends to land on the nearest human. An idea like algorithmacy could be used the same way, to say that workers who do badly simply lack an ability. That would move the blame for a hidden rule onto the people subjected to it. I do not intend that use, and the argument does not support it. The reason the ability is needed is that the operator hides and changes the rule. Cameron (2024) shows workers on a platform coming to accept their conditions through the limited choices the platform leaves them. Widespread algorithmacy could contribute to the same result, by letting people absorb problems quietly that would otherwise be visible. Three propositions follow.

*Proposition 1.* A participant with more algorithmacy gets better results from working through the intermediary. This does not make the arrangement fairer. Her gains come from handling the system better in private. They do not come from the rule being disclosed, from appeals being granted, or from the system becoming easier for others to understand.

*Proposition 2.* Algorithmacy is unevenly spread among people the platform treats as identical. This produces differences in outcome among people who are formally in the same position. It is the same kind of gap Hargittai (2002) found in internet use, where people with the same access differed widely in what they could do with it.

*Proposition 3.* The more transparent the intermediary is, the less algorithmacy a participant needs. Disclosure by the operator and ability on the part of the participant are therefore two ways of dealing with the same difficulty. An organization or a regulator can choose between asking people to cope with a hidden rule and requiring the rule to be shown.

### Limits and open questions

Several things in this account are not yet established. I have argued that there are three parts and that they are distinct. Whether they turn out to be distinct when measured is an open question. It is also possible that there is a fourth part. Rahman's (2021) material suggests that deciding whether the platform or the client caused a change might be a task of its own. I have placed it inside interpreting, and that may be wrong.

The strongest alternative explanation also comes from Rahman. He found that how freelancers responded depended on how financially dependent they were on the platform and on whether their score had dropped sharply before. If those two things explain who does well, then algorithmacy adds little. That can only be settled by measuring dependence, past shocks, and algorithmacy in the same people.

Finally, I have not shown that algorithmacy is different in practice from general digital skill or from ordinary skill at communicating. The argument of this article is that it should be, because neither of those involves reading and addressing two parties through one hidden rule. Showing it requires measurement that this article does not provide.

## Conclusion

This article has described a situation that is now common and has had no adequate account. Two people depend on each other, and an algorithm between them judges both by a rule that neither can see and that changes without notice. Existing accounts of digital skill deal with a person and a machine, or with two people and a channel. None deals with a person who has to work with another person through a machine that binds them both.

I have named the ability this requires and said what it consists of. The participant has to interpret results, which means working out what the rule and the other person each contributed. She has to specify intent, which means acting and writing for the rule and the other person at once. She has to keep track of a rule that changes. Each of these follows from one feature of the intermediary, and the three depend on each other.

Naming the ability makes it possible to study. It can be measured, it can be compared across people who are treated as equivalent, and it can be weighed against the alternative of making the rule visible. It also makes one thing harder to overlook. When an organization puts a system of this kind between people, it is asking them for an ability that it has not checked they have and has not helped them to get.
