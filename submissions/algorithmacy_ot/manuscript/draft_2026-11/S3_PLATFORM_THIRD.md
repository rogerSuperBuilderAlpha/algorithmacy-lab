## The Platform as a Third That Decides

**[C: Each of the three placements in the previous section counts two parties: a person and a partner, two
people and a channel, a worker and an opponent. Organizing on platforms has three.]** This dilemma reflects
a basic sociological principle: group size dictates social form (Simmel, 1902a). Adding a third participant
fundamentally alters an interaction's structure rather than simply increasing the headcount (Simmel,
1902b).

### The Platform Form as a Triadic Architecture

**[C: Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) draw the platform as a triangle: "a triangular structure (Vallas &
Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners" (p. 14).
The triangle describes a form, not a firm. For Stark and Vanden Broeck an organizational form "is not
co-terminous with that of a company" (p. 7), and the form reaches people who never sign up as gig workers:
"Many professionals encounter algorithmic management as users" (p. 9). Generative AI brought into a firm
imports the same triadic architecture. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe a manager who uses it to
evaluate employees, where "new relations and functions may cascade between managers, GenAI, and
employees" (p. 4); the system sits where the operator sat, between two people whose work depends on each
other. The apex decides through systems that learn (pp. 4, 6) and produce "a probabilistic rendering" of
what they have seen (Cornelissen et al., 2024, p. 6), and it can hide human work as well: the vetting that lets Emily, in Gray and Suri's example (as cited in Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 10), meet her driver is done by someone "entirely invisible to both Uber customers
and drivers" (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 11). The people at the lower corners need not decide whether
the apex is code, staff or both. Workers, Bujold et al. (2026) argue, "ultimately recognize the
organization (not the algorithm) as the exchange partner" (p. 15).]**

### Mediator, Moderator, and a Third That Decides

To demonstrate how a third party transforms social relationships, Simmel contrasted a mediator with an
arbitrator:

> *So long as the third party works as a real mediator, the ending of the conflict rests finally in the
> hands of the parties themselves. By choice of the arbitrator they have put this ultimate decision out of
> their own hands.* (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)

While a mediator helps two parties reach an agreement, the participants retain final control over their
relationship. With an arbitrator, by contrast, the participants hand that authority over and the third
party settles the outcome (Simmel, 1902b). In written communication, physical texts serve as moderators
that preserve statements across space and time while leaving open how those words should be interpreted
(Olson, 1994). For example, in performance appraisals, a standardized review form structures what a
manager and an employee can discuss during an evaluation (Lehtinen & Pälli, 2021). Although text
moderates the conversation, a human supervisor still makes the final judgment and can be directly
questioned by the employee. The difference between a moderator and a third party hinges on who holds the
verdict, following Simmel's (1902b) contrast between a mediator and an arbitrator.

An algorithmic platform moves even further from neutral mediation because neither participant selects the
arbiter, and the platform operator, which sits at the apex of a triangle with producers and users (Stark &
Vanden Broeck, 2024), ~~profits directly from~~ **[C: benefits from]** the exchange. **[C: Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024)
have already read this position through Simmel, as "the third who rejoices" (preprint p. 6; VoR page owed);
their concern is how the platform owner can be held to account, and the concern here is what the two
people below it must be able to do.]** Yet classifying communication strictly by transmission channel
overlooks whether the technology evaluates and binds the participants. When an automated system steps
between two people and sets the terms of their exchange, it stops being a neutral transmission medium and
becomes an automated arbitrator.

### Two Features of the Triad

**[C: Two features of this arrangement are its normal condition, not exceptions to it. The first is
outcome interdependence within a single mediated exchange. Each person's outcome depends on the other's
conduct as the platform reads it: the provider's rank depends on the rater's rating, and what the rater
gets depends on the provider's conduct. The interdependence holds whether or not the two ever meet again.
The second is asymmetric binding. The platform binds a participant whenever overriding or bypassing its
output carries a practical cost, whether a penalty, a burden of justification or lost standing, and it
need not bind both participants equally. On eBay the rating costs the rater little and binds the provider
through rank (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Inside the firm, a recommendation from the system binds
de facto wherever setting it aside has to be explained. Neither feature requires a lasting relationship or
a symmetric one.]** 

### Oracy, Literacy, and Algorithmacy

**[C: Sorting communicative abilities by what stands between people turns on one question: can what stands
between render a verdict?]** ~~Distinguishing communicative sensibilities~~ **[C: Distinguishing
communicative abilities]** therefore requires examining how the intervening medium acts upon the people it
connects. Traditional media sociology classified communication into face-to-face interaction, mediated
interaction, and mediated quasi-interaction based on technical channels and audience scope (Thompson,
1995). Later scholarship updated this taxonomy to account for networked digital environments (Thompson,
2020).

Oracy is the ability to ~~coordinate~~ **[C: carry out an exchange]** directly face to face via speaking and hearing, or via physical gesture
and response. **[C: A chain of spoken messages still passes from person to person, so the chain stays
oral.]** Literacy is the ability to read and write texts that ~~coordinate across~~ **[C: carry]** two-person exchanges that
are separated in time and space. This text-based coordination transformed modern organizations between the
late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, as growing enterprises replaced personal oversight with
memos, standard forms, and filing systems (Yates, 1989). Yet even in a text-moderated relationship, a
human actor still interprets the page and delivers the evaluation. **[C: A text is static and can be
inspected; it cannot change its own rule or render a verdict. Algorithmacy belongs to the triad, where an
opaque and adaptive third renders the verdict and the two people cannot easily go around it.]** An
algorithmic platform departs from this history by placing an automated third party between participants
that evaluates their interaction and enforces binding outcomes.

**[C: Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) ask for this comparison. Like Merton and Lazarsfeld, they write,
"further research should study the corresponding field of communication," but "in our case,
organizational form and communication field are conjoined" (p. 15); "Like Yates, we study how organization
and communication are intertwined" (p. 16). Literacy was the person's part of the communication field
that went with bureaucracy. Algorithmacy is the person's part of the field that goes with the platform.
Table 1 sets the three side by side.]**

**Table 1. Three communicative abilities, by what stands between the people who use them**
[C: cells from reviewer 4's draft, adopted 23 Nov]

| | Oracy | Literacy | Algorithmacy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form of relation | Direct dyad | Mediated dyad | Irreducible triad |
| What stands between | A shared physical or vocal channel | A static, inspectable text | An opaque, adaptive intermediary |
| Who holds the verdict | The two participants | The two participants, or a named human authority | The intermediary, or a person bound by its scoring |
| Can the two bypass it? | Not applicable: nothing stands between | Yes, by speaking directly or revising the text | No, or only at a cost |
| Primary demand on the person | Speaking, listening, adjusting in real time | Reading, writing, interpreting fixed records | Diagnosing cause, writing for two audiences, tracking a shifting rule |
| Characteristic failure | Mishearing; loss of shared presence | Misreading; missing context | Collapsing into compliance with the system, or treating it as a neutral channel |
