## Algorithmacy: What the People at the Corners Must Be Able to Do

**[C: Once the third holds the verdict, three properties follow in practice: it is opaque, it binds, and it
adapts. Each property makes a demand on the people at the lower corners, and algorithmacy is the name for
meeting all three. I define it as the ability to carry out an interdependent exchange with another person
through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. Opacity calls for interpreting, binding for
specifying intent, and adaptation for keeping track.]** Working under an algorithmic intermediary does not
mean facing a simple absence of information. Instead, workers confront equivocality, dealing with
confusing data that supports multiple, conflicting explanations (Daft & Lengel, 1986). A participant
receives an evaluation, observes what the counterpart did, and keeps her own work record, yet several
explanations fit, and no supervisor clarifies the outcome. **[C: The askers' own cases show the three
demands, and I take them in turn, each on the case that shows it most plainly, before following one case
through all three.]**

### Interpreting: Finding What Caused an Outcome

The first challenge requires interpreting ambiguous outcomes to identify their underlying cause. Because
the extent to which each party affects an opaque evaluation score is unspecified, an unexpected outcome
may stem from the algorithmic formula, the counterpart's behavior, or both (Rahman, 2021). **[C: Hinds and
von Krogh (2024) give the case inside the firm. When generative AI is "used by managers to augment their
ability to motivate and evaluate employees, such as determining job assignments or pay increases, new
relations and functions may cascade between managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4). An employee who is
passed over for an assignment cannot tell from the outcome whether the manager judged her work, the
system scored it, or the manager adopted the system's score. The manager may not know either, since the
system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making
decisions" (p. 4). Neither person faces a missing fact. Each faces an outcome with three possible authors.]**

**[C: The same demand appears on the platforms Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe, where ratings
"circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped feedback loops" (p. 14).]** In online
marketplaces, an independent seller whose search visibility drops only sees a buyer's review score,
leaving the platform's hidden weighting formula ~~completely~~ concealed (Curchod et al., 2020). Deducing
the cause of a score determines how the worker responds. If the customer was dissatisfied, the worker can
improve service delivery; if the platform updated its formula, the worker must change how she interacts
with the software. Disentangling these causes is complicated because people evaluate an identical
interaction differently when they attribute it to a computer rather than to a person (Sundar & Nass,
2000). **[C: Interpreting, as I use the term, settles what caused an outcome. It does not settle who
answers for it; that question belongs to the next section, where the two come apart.]**

### Specifying Intent: Making It Survive the Platform

The second challenge requires specifying intent so that a human counterpart can understand what an action
meant despite algorithmic filtering. **[C: Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) rater shows why. "When a user
rates a provider on eBay, for example, she is not acting as a subordinate to whom eBay has delegated
authority. The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). Her rating is
addressed to the provider and to the buyers who come after her, but the platform reads it into a ranking
she does not set, with consequences for the provider she cannot see. What she means and what the platform
does with it can part company. The binding here is asymmetric: the rating costs her little, and it binds
the provider through rank.]** Specifying intent departs from conventional software prompting because the
message must satisfy two different audiences at once: a human partner seeking genuine collaboration and an
automated system scoring metric compliance.

**[C: The provider's side of the same exchange has the same structure. Justesen and Plesner (2024)
describe workers who must "'swallow their negative emotions both during interactions with clients, but
also in replying to negative reviews'" (p. 15, quoting Bucher et al., 2021, p. 57). A reply to a review is
written to the client and read by the system that ranks the business.]** In direct conversation, two
people establish common understanding through immediate verbal and nonverbal feedback (Clark & Brennan,
1991). Machines, by contrast, capture only thin digital traces of situated human actions (Suchman, 2007).
Communicating through an intermediary therefore requires writing that survives algorithmic filtering.
Where evaluative pressure is asymmetric, the party facing greater scrutiny bears the burden of specifying
intent.

### Keeping Track: A Rule That Changes Without Notice

The third challenge requires tracking an evaluation rule that changes continuously without formal notice.
**[C: Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) hiring case puts it plainly. "If a manager is using GenAI for hiring …
the AI would likely continually improve on its ability to select promising candidates. Over time as
managers and applicants interact with the system, the evaluation algorithm may become increasingly refined
and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). The rule changes because it is used. "The way GenAI learns and
generates insights are functions not likely to be well defined or transparent" (p. 6), so no notice of the
change arrives; the people using the system learn of it, if at all, from what it starts to do differently.
With such systems, Hinds and von Krogh write, "rather than being fixed, these functions and relations are
likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4). The rule can move within a single exchange, not only
between versions.]** Platform operators rarely publish updates, forcing workers to infer changes entirely
from shifts in performance patterns. Tracking differs from interpreting a single score; it requires sensing
broader patterns across multiple counterparts over long stretches of time. Social media users, for
instance, notice algorithmic change through shifts in content distribution patterns and in the engagement
their posts receive (DeVito, 2021).

### The Three Parts as a Cycle

**[C: The three parts are separable in principle and joined in practice. Follow the provider in Stark and
Vanden Broeck's (2024) case through one ongoing exchange; the sequence is my reasoning on their case, not
their report. The provider's ranking drops after a sale. She asks whether the buyer's rating, the
platform's weighting or both moved it: interpreting. She revises her listing so that it speaks to the next
buyer and still scores well with the platform: specifying intent. She watches the rankings that follow, to
learn whether the weighting itself has changed: keeping track. What she learns at the third step changes
what she will read at the first step of the next exchange.]** Competence in this environment develops as a
participant's understanding of the work changes across successive encounters with it (Sandberg, 2000).
Yet over time, direct experience lets workers anticipate automated patterns. Delivery drivers with long
tenure on platforms learn to recognize shifting system prompts and selectively depart from algorithmic
guidance to improve delivery routes (Jiang & Sinchaisri, 2025).

**[C: Turns at the cycle build the ability, and people with more turns may hold more of it than others in
the same formal position. The hardest case is the pair that meets once. TripAdvisor's reviewers are
"unseen and unnamed adversaries who run away immediately and have no stake in the future" (Orlikowski &
Scott, 2014, AAM p. 42; VoR page owed), and food-delivery customers "were unlikely to meet the same driver
again" (Chong et al., 2024, p. 10). Interdependence holds within the one exchange (§3), but nothing learned
about this counterpart carries to the next. What carries over is learned about the platform.]** `[Figure 1
here: the triangle, with the cycle of the three parts drawn between the two lower corners.]`

### Two Ways to Fail

Workers fail to navigate these demands when they mistake the three-way relationship for a two-way
exchange. The first failure mode occurs when a worker collapses the triad into a pair of herself and the
machine, treating the software as the only thing that matters and viewing the counterpart as mere noise.
**[C: Hinds and von Krogh (2024) name the risk for teams. Mayo's medical teams showed "emergent
interdependence," which "was organic and associated with better patient outcomes. With the introduction of
GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members blindly follow guidance from the
AI?" (p. 7). Guidance binds de facto wherever setting it aside carries a cost (§3), and a member who
follows it blindly has let the colleague drop out of the exchange. The askers' own picture of the
technology invites this collapse. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) see a line crossed "when technologies are
no longer merely the means of communication among parties but themselves become communication partners"
(p. 9), and Phillips, Kalvapalle and Kennedy (2024) describe a mode in which the system "remains, in this
case, very much subordinate to the human partner" (p. 9). Each picture has two parties. The collapse
treats the second person as one of them.]** `[NEEDS: "one of them" — check this joint reads as you intend:
the point is that the person treats the system as their only partner.]`

The second failure mode occurs when participants treat the platform as a neutral channel between two
people, ignoring the evaluating software. **[C: This is the other half of Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024)
contrast: the technology as "merely the means of communication among parties" (p. 9).]** In two-sided
review systems, buyers and sellers often trade positive reviews, and buyers hold back negative ones to
avoid seller retaliation (Bolton et al., 2013). Participants then treat ratings as personal compliments
rather than evaluative data. It also blinds participants to how the platform aggregates their reviews.
Treating the software as a transparent channel leaves that basis unexamined. In both failure modes,
participants lose their footing by focusing on one half of the triad and ignoring the other. **[C: Both
failures leave the cause of an outcome misread, and a misread cause is where misplaced responsibility
begins, which the next section takes up.]**
