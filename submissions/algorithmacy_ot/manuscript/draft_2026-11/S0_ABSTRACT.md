**[C: When a platform decides between two people and declines to be the actor that decides, who answers
for the outcome? *Organization Theory* authors ask this question of the collective and the operator, and
they ask, separately, what happens to the person who works with the system; the second person in the
exchange drops out of both.]** Most studies still rely on two-way models: a single person facing a machine,
technology as a neutral channel between people, or the other person ignored. **[C: This paper asks the two
questions as one, from the side of the two people.]** Organizations increasingly manage workers through
algorithms without teaching them how those systems operate, much like handing printed books to people who cannot
read. **[C: I sort communicative abilities by what stands between the people who use them: oracy for direct
dyads, literacy for dyads mediated by a static text, and algorithmacy for triads in which an opaque,
binding and adaptive third renders the verdict. Algorithmacy, the ability to carry out an interdependent
exchange with another person through such an intermediary, has three parts: interpreting what caused an
outcome, specifying intent so that it survives the platform, and keeping track of a rule that changes
without notice. Reinterpreting the askers' own cases, from eBay ratings to generative AI in hiring and pay,
I show how the parts fail, how failure misplaces responsibility between the two people, and how the
ability that lets them finish an exchange may also absorb the friction that would otherwise challenge the
platform's declined actorhood. Generative AI imports the same triadic architecture into the firm and
quickens its tempo. The paper ends with five questions for research.]**

Keywords: algorithmacy, platforms, actorhood, accountability, algorithmic management, generative AI
