## Where Declined Responsibility Comes to Rest

**[C: The two failures in the previous section misplace responsibility; the ability itself may do
something less visible. The same adaptive ability that lets people finish an exchange can absorb routine
friction before it ever challenges the platform's declined actorhood. This section traces both paths and
returns to Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl's (2026) question about individual and collective
responsibility.]**

### Readings Come Before Any Voiced Attribution

**[C: For Schoeneborn et al., "collective actorhood is not something given but something ascribed in
communication" (p. 6), and the practices that ascribe it are "recurrent instances of communication"
(p. 9). Before anyone voices that the platform, the algorithm or the other person answers for an outcome,
someone has read the outcome. Interpreting, as the previous section defines it, produces that reading.
The reading is not itself an attribution practice: it may never be said aloud, and it need not recur.
It is where attribution among the platform, its algorithm and the counterpart is still undecided, and
what a person can do with an outcome depends on how it is settled there.]**

### When the Reading Fails, Responsibility Is Misplaced

**[C: Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe what the platform gains from the arrangement. Ratings and
rankings "circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped feedback loops. Their
twisted character makes it possible for the platform operator to deflect accountability" (p. 14). What is
deflected does not vanish.]** When an automated system enters the exchange, it breaks the normal chains
of accountability by shifting the blame onto the people doing the work (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark &
Vanden Broeck, 2024). **[C: Where a person cannot tell the platform's part from the counterpart's
conduct, the blame for what the system did lands on the counterpart. Lee et al.'s (2015) drivers report
that passengers "misattributed system faults and negative experiences that drivers could not control to
drivers themselves, which in turn resulted in lower ratings" (p. 1608), and a food-delivery customer in
Chong et al. (2024) tipped his driver less although the order was, as he put it later, "obviously more of
the app's fault than the driver's fault" (p. 7).]** The frontline worker then becomes the moral crumple
zone (Elish, 2019) in exactly the same way that the front of a car is designed to crumple and absorb the
impact of a crash to protect the rest of the car. The worker absorbs the blame and the penalties when the
automated system fails.

**[C: The direction is not fixed, and finding the cause does not settle who answers. Fu et al. (2026,
abstract) describe consumers who "transfer responsibility for service failures to a platform, even after
recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem." Zheng and Zhu (2026, abstract) find
consumers turning against the platform after a worker-caused failure when dispatch is framed as
algorithm-directed, an effect "mediated by responsibility attribution to the platform." Responsibility
moves with what each person can see. The same deflection runs inside the firm. Patriotta, Augustine and
Voronov (2025) recount how "thousands of employees of the UK Post Office were falsely accused of stealing
from local branch accounts over the course of a decade" before "it became clear that the Post Office's IT
system, called Horizon, was at fault" (p. 11); the organization's first answer was that "Horizon is an
extremely robust system" (p. 11). There the employer, not a platform, stood at the apex, and the people
at the corner could not show the cause.]**

### When the Reading Succeeds, the Friction Is Absorbed

**[C: The ability has a second effect, which the paper proposes and does not demonstrate.]** As the
worker develops their algorithmacy in order to cope with the pressures of the work, that very adaptation
of the worker ~~actually serves~~ **[C: may serve]** to keep the platform's opaque authority going. **[C: Cameron (2024) shows a
related effect for consent rather than competence: a system "in which individuals have circumscribed
choices while working is crucial to keeping the platform system running smoothly, because workers can
invest in their work and develop a sense of mastery" (p. 494), while platform owners "hide behind an
opaque management system" (p. 493). A person who reads an outcome correctly, specifies intent so that it
survives and keeps track of the changing rule carries out work that Justesen and Plesner (2024) would
count among the "unintended tasks associated with digitalization" (p. 22), and one who cannot may suffer
what Hinds and von Krogh (2024) call "a loss of orientation" (p. 11). I conjecture that competent
absorption keeps routine breakdowns from surfacing as the public cases in which Schoeneborn et al. expect
their question to be "especially salient" (p. 14). Horizon is a caution against reading this as an
explanation: there the misplacement ran for a decade before it surfaced. The conjecture is a question for
research, not a finding.]**

### What This Answers

**[C: Schoeneborn et al. (2026) ask, "What does the recurrent charging or discharging of a collective
social address with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective responsibility?" (p. 14). At
the level this paper works, between the individual and the collective sits the exchange. When a platform
downplays its actorhood, the burden of assigning responsibility is carried there, by two people, one
outcome at a time, and how well they carry it depends on algorithmacy. Whether their readings, once
voiced, charge the platform with actorhood or discharge it stays open, as Schoeneborn et al. leave open
"whether these always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary depending on the particular
context" (p. 14).]**
