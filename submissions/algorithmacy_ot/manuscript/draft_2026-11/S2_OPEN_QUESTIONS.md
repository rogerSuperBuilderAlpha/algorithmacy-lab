## Two Sets of Open Questions About Platforms

**[C: The questions *Organization Theory* authors leave open about platforms come from two places. The
first set is asked from the apex. Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) hold that "collective actorhood
is not something given but something ascribed in communication" (p. 6) and see platforms that "rather
strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt
regulation" (p. 13). Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) find that the platform's feedback loops make it
"possible for the platform operator to deflect accountability" (p. 14), and Patriotta, Augustine and
Voronov (2025) follow Uber "refusing to accept their drivers as employees" (p. 14). Outside the journal,
Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024) review how a platform owner "can claim to merely be a conduit for a
service" (preprint p. 6) and how it might be held to account, and Buhmann et al. (2021, abstract) show
that the metaphors used to portray an organization shape how much collective actorhood individuals
attribute to it. These studies take the collective, the operator or the portrayal as their object; none
follows an outcome to the two people it reaches.]**

There is, however, a divergence of opinion as to what kind of entity the technology in the middle
actually is. Grodal, Ha, Hood and Rajunov (2024) pose the question: “[W]ill we come to accept that
generative AI is a machine? Or will we continue to perceive it as closer to humans […]?” (p. 8). Hsu and
Bechky (2024) argue for “a need to consider technology as an independent force or actor in the relational
network of agents that collectively influence the social construction of categories” (p. 12). Phillips,
Kalvapalle and Kennedy (2024) ask, “How does the introduction of artificial persons into an organization
change how we think about what organizations are, how they are constructed and how they function?”
(p. 11).

**[C: The second set is asked of a person and the technology.]** Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask, “[W]hat
are individuals’ psychological responses to the new relational fabric in which they are embedded?”
(p. 11). **[C: Their questions about teams keep the colleague in view only as someone the system might
replace or guide. In both sets the second person, whose conduct the system scores and who must go on with
the exchange, has no place.]**

### Three Placements of the System

Although organization theory recognizes that platforms establish a three-way relationship, it has not yet
explained what practical ~~sensibility~~ **[C: ability]** an individual needs to coordinate with another
person inside that arrangement. Existing accounts of this capability remain fragmented across
communication studies, computer science, and sociology. Yet these conflicting terms point to a deeper
conceptual limitation. ~~Every existing account~~ **[C: Each of these accounts]** counts fewer than three
parties. **[C: These frameworks]** ~~They~~ do this by either ignoring the third party altogether
(treating the algorithmic intermediary as a neutral pass-through), or by assuming that every interaction
is a two-way exchange. That latter assumption fails to account for the binding pressure that is created
when a third party is in fact setting the rules of the two-person relationship.

The first group of scholars treats the automated system as an interactive partner, reducing the work
arrangement to a pair made up of one person and one machine. Under this view, communicative AI
technologies function as communicative subjects rather than mere interactive objects (Guzman & Lewis,
2020). **[C: The askers draw the same picture. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) see a line crossed "when
technologies are no longer merely the means of communication among parties but themselves become
communication partners" (p. 9), and in the prompt-and-answer mode Phillips et al. (2024) describe, the
system "remains, in this case, very much subordinate to the human partner" (p. 9).]** Research on
artificial intelligence literacy adopts this same two-party focus. The most prominent frameworks in that
field define literacy as a checklist of concepts an individual should understand about machine learning,
without mentioning coordination with a second person through the software (Long & Magerko, 2020). A
recent review finds that measures of algorithmic literacy still lie mostly in the cognitive dimension,
stopping at an individual’s awareness or knowledge of algorithms (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025). While
this perspective explains how a person forms impressions of a machine, it cannot explain what happens when
that machine steps between two people who depend on each other to get work done.

The second group of scholars treats the system as a channel, viewing the arrangement as two people
connected by a digital wire. Alongside those findings, Spitzberg (2006) modeled computer-mediated
communication competence as the motivation, knowledge, and skills with which a person interacts
appropriately and effectively through digital tools. Recent scholarship extends this channel perspective
to artificial intelligence, examining how software modifies, augments, or generates messages on a
person’s behalf (Hancock et al., 2020). Researchers treat the technology as an active messenger, but it
remains a channel. That framing works well when people exchange emails or text messages, but it fails in
organizational platforms where the software does not simply deliver words. In algorithmic management, the
system evaluates the exchange, ~~scores both participants~~ **[C: scores the participants]**, and enforces
rules that neither person can dispute.

Finally, scholars who study work on platforms treat the system as something workers must understand and
adapt to. ~~Of particular relevance to the approach I develop in this paper,~~ Jarrahi and Sutherland
(2019) have proposed “algorithmic competency” for workers’ understanding of the algorithms that assign
and assess their work and their learning to work with and around them. On that basis, Zhou et al. (2025)
have built and tested a questionnaire that measures workers’ “understanding of platform algorithms that
assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms” (p. 2).
Compared with the first two placements, this one clearly accounts for the system’s power, treating it as
something that assigns and evaluates work. However, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) do not carry the client
into the construct, although clients appear throughout their interviews. While their workers watch clients
and keep records in case of a dispute, their figure has two nodes, the worker and the algorithm, and the
construct covers the same two.

**[C: Communication research has begun to count a third. Ohme et al. (2025) model platform communication
by "shifting from a dyadic to a triadic communication model of content flow" (adv. p. 1; VoR page owed),
but their third "provides a service for each or both" of the others and may "be bypassed" (adv. pp. 5, 6).
The third at issue here decides between the two and cannot be bypassed without cost. None of the three
placements, and not the serving third, puts the system above two people who depend on each other.]**
