Three things about how they cite, beyond the totals:

1. **There are two kinds of article.** Two cite lightly (36 and 40 references) and two cite heavily (97 and
   137). The light pair build on their own earlier fieldwork or on one named perspective. The heavy pair
   position a construct against a literature, which is what this paper does.
2. **They rarely make the cited author the subject of the sentence.** Between 13% and 33% of their
   citations name the author in the running text. The rest sit in parentheses at the end of the claim,
   often several together. The 7 October draft did the opposite: three citations in four named the
   author ("Rahman (2021) studied…").
3. **Citations sit where the paper reports other people's work, and are absent where it states its own
   claims.** In Orlikowski and Scott, 55 of 69 citations fall in the first 21 paragraphs and the
   theorizing section has three. A third to a half of all paragraphs in the four carry no citation.

**Cost in length.** Seventy-five references take about 1,730 words. With a body of 7,950, the abstract and
the two tables, the manuscript is near 10,300 words against the journal's 11,000. There is room for
about 700 more words in total. More references mean less body.

## Where the draft still differs from the models

1. **Almost nothing from *Organization Studies*.** Schoeneborn et al. cite it 16 times, and it is the
   sister journal. The draft cites it zero times. The lab's libraries hold few OS articles on this
   topic; finding them is a search, not a selection.
2. **Only six references to *Organization Theory* itself.** The models carry between two and six, so this
   is in range, but the author's stated aim is to build on the journal's own articles. Räcker, Geiger &
   Seidl (2024), already analysed in the Lima arm, is the obvious seventh.
3. **The transition argument has three references, all at summary depth.** See the next section.
4. **Multi-citation parentheses are at the low end** (13 against 14–40). The places that could carry more
   are the first paragraph of "What existing accounts leave out" and the Discussion's first paragraph.

## The gap that matters most

The author's statement of the argument is that the move from literacy to algorithmacy is like the earlier
move from oracy to literacy. The draft now says this in its own section, "From oracy to literacy to
algorithmacy". That section rests on:

- Wilkinson (1965), held as metadata only. Nobody in the lab has read the article.
- Yates (1989), carded from the publisher's description and reviews.
- Olson (1994), the same.

The standard sources on the move from oral to written culture — Ong (1982), Goody and Watt (1963),
Havelock (1963) — are not carded and not cited. Zuboff (1988), on what computerized work asked of
workers, is carded as metadata only and is not cited. A reviewer who knows this literature will look for
these names in the first two pages. Reading them is the next piece of research, ahead of the six lines in
`../RESEARCH_PLAN.md`.
