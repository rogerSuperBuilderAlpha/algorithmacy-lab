## E. Tension between the two sections

- **The three operations differ.** §1 ¶4 has the person interpret "a variable counterpart's intentions"; the Lima paper and the other assistant's variant have her interpret the intermediary's rule *and* the counterpart's intention from one composite signal. Which is it?
- **Genus.** §1 calls algorithmacy a *sensibility* that "operates through the competencies and capacities"; the Lima paper argued "Competency, Not Skill or Capability". §2 ends on "a distinct sensibility". The construct section has to settle this before anything else.
- **Overlap.** §1 ¶3–4 and §2 ¶1 and ¶4 make the same three citations do the same work (Schoeneborn, Stark, Porsfelt), in nearly the same words. One of the two passes will have to go.
- **What the sources offer that the draft does not yet use.** Hinds and von Krogh describe cascades "between managers, GenAI, and employees" in which the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4) — a manager, an employee and an AI between them. Leonardi and Leavell study "the relationships between the planners, lay people, and the model" (p. 523); at one site the model became "the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533) and stakeholders "treated the outputs as binding forecasts" (p. 537). In their account the *expert* is the intermediary, and the capacity they describe — "selecting, sequencing, qualifying, and presenting information so that others can act under uncertainty" (p. 518) — is the expert's; they say nothing about what the lay party must be able to do. Both papers are nearer the triad than the sentences they are cited for.
