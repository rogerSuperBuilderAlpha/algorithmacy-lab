## The common skeleton

1. **Introduction:** phenomenon → the field's two views or a dilemma → the gap, once, in plain words →
   "we propose" → contributions (often numbered) → sometimes a roadmap.
   - Schoeneborn et al. name a dilemma (p. 2), say "there remains a need for further theorization"
     (p. 2), and "we make two proposals" (p. 2).
   - Patriotta et al.: "less attention has been given to how organizations construct accounts" (p. 2).
   - Hinds & von Krogh: the literature "has to date not considered how" (p. 2).
   - Stark & Vanden Broeck end on a roadmap, "In the pages that follow" (p. 3); Schoeneborn et al. have
     none.
2. **The view to be displaced**, 10–20% of the text (Schoeneborn et al.'s "Third-Way" section; Stark &
   Vanden Broeck's "New Problems and New Answers"; Hinds & von Krogh's "Relational Perspective").
3. **The construct defined**, then **elaborated into named parts** that carry the argument: charging and
   discharging; five dimensions; three types of invisible work; stages. 45–60% of the text.
4. **One visual** carries the parts: Schoeneborn et al. Figure 1 (a sliding bar, p. 7); Stark & Vanden
   Broeck Table 1 (five dimensions × three forms, p. 14); Justesen & Plesner Table 1 (pp. 18–19); Patriotta et
   al. Figure 1 (pp. 8–9).
5. **Cases, not propositions.** Four of the five core theory articles state no propositions. Mechanisms
   ride on short real cases (eBay, John Deere, Horizon, Uber) or one thought experiment (Hinds & von
   Krogh: "an illustration as well as a thought experiment", p. 6). Only Guercini & Lechner state
   propositions.
6. **Discussion:** contributions restated in the intro's order; boundary or failure conditions (Patriotta
   et al.); a research agenda, often as explicit questions.
7. **Conclusion:** short (0–620 words) or none. Schoeneborn et al. end on four research questions
   (p. 14); Stark & Vanden Broeck have no conclusion.

## Five moves the strongest three share (Schoeneborn et al., Patriotta et al., Stark & Vanden Broeck)

1. The construct is defined in one sentence **in the introduction**, before the review.
2. The gap is stated **once**; the contributions are numbered in the introduction and come back **in
   the same order** in the Discussion.
3. The construct is broken into **named parts** that become the section's subheadings.
4. **One figure or table** holds the parts together.
5. The paper ends on **explicit research questions**, which is how the askers we answer ended theirs.

## What this means for our paper

- The introduction (v6, ~1,870 words) is 1.7–2.7 times the slot; trim to ~1,100 (Roger's ruling), and
  move the Common-ground detail into the review section.
- Algorithmacy is not yet defined in one sentence in the introduction; the Course-of-action block gives
  three definitions (v6 flag 6). Pick one and put it there.
- Give the three parts their own subsections, carried on the askers' own cases
  (`CASES_REINTERPRETED.md`), not propositions.
- One visual: a comparison table of oracy, literacy and algorithmacy (Stark & Vanden Broeck's Table 1 is
  the model), or a cycle figure of the three parts. The outline proposes the table as the one visual and
  the cycle as an optional second.
- Restate the three contributions in the Discussion in the intro's order and end on numbered research
  questions.
- Avoid the visible template of a single model (S13 §1.5): follow the skeleton the set shares, not one
  paper's frames.
