# The askers' own cases, reinterpreted through algorithmacy

[Written by Claude (the lead), 23 Nov 2026, by hand, from the text layers of the *Organization Theory*
papers the introduction answers. Every quotation was checked on the page given (check run at the end of
`OUTLINE.md`). Readings marked "Reading" are Claude's interpretation for Roger to accept, change or drop.]

Roger's ruling: use the examples the askers themselves use, and reinterpret them with the algorithmacy
framework, rather than introduce a new worked case. Apex ruling: the platform; AI is a platform;
platforms operate on algorithms, notably stochastic ones.

**The frame used in every reading.** Three corners: a participant, a counterpart, and the platform at
the apex, which decides through algorithms. Three parts of algorithmacy (brief): *interpreting* an outcome
to find its cause (answers opacity), *specifying intent* so it survives the platform and reaches the
counterpart (answers bindingness), *keeping track* of a rule that changes without notice (answers
adaptivity). Two failure modes: *collapse* into a person–machine dyad (the counterpart drops out); the
*neutral channel* (the platform's verdict drops out).

## C1. The eBay rater and the provider (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14)

- **Source.** "When a user rates a provider on eBay, for example, she is not acting as a subordinate to
  whom eBay has delegated authority. The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its
  agent" (p. 14). The ratings "circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped
  feedback loops. Their twisted character makes it possible for the platform operator to deflect
  accountability" (p. 14).
- **Corners.** User (rater), provider (seller), eBay (apex).
- **What the platform decides.** It turns one person's rating of another into a ranking that it applies.
- **Reading.** The rater addresses the provider and is read by the platform: a double address
  (*specifying intent*). The provider receives a ranking and must work out whether it reflects the user,
  the platform's weighting, or both (*interpreting*). The feedback loops change as ratings accumulate
  (*keeping track*). Stark and Vanden Broeck tell the case from the apex (the platform "leverages rather
  than delegates", p. 14); algorithmacy tells it from the two lower corners.
- **Carries.** Section "The Platform as a Third That Decides" (the triangle, the deflection); first worked
  case for all three parts. Supporting detail, if needed: Curchod et al. (2020) on eBay sellers (AAM
  pp. 10, 21–22; verified S14).

## C2. Professionals as users; the algorithm as partner (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 9)

- **Source.** "A line is crossed, however, when technologies are no longer merely the means of
  communication among parties but themselves become communication partners (Esposito, 2022)" (p. 9).
  "Many professionals encounter algorithmic management as users" (p. 9).
- **Reading.** The askers name two pictures of the technology: a means of communication *among* parties
  (the channel) and a *partner* (the dyad). Algorithmacy's two failure modes are these two pictures each
  taken as the whole. The professional-users line carries the third contribution (gig to professional
  work).
- **Carries.** Review section (the two placements); failure modes; Discussion (professional work).
- **Read owed.** Pullen-Blasnik et al. (2024), cited here by Stark and Vanden Broeck for medicine, is
  titled "'Is your accuser me, or is it the software?' Ambiguity and contested expertise in probabilistic
  DNA profiling" (reference list, p. 22). Unread; possibly the best professional case of *interpreting*.

## C3. Managers evaluating employees with generative AI (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4)

- **Source.** "if GenAI is used by managers to augment their ability to motivate and evaluate employees,
  such as determining job assignments or pay increases, new relations and functions may cascade between
  managers, GenAI, and employees, as well as within the AI as it learns" (p. 4). It "may ultimately
  obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4).
- **Corners.** Employee, manager, generative AI (the apex; Roger: AI is a platform).
- **What the platform decides.** Job assignments and pay increases, or the recommendation the manager
  adopts.
- **Reading.** The employee must work out whether a pay decision came from the manager or the system
  (*interpreting*); the manager must make intent survive a system that both see only through outputs
  (*specifying intent*); the basis shifts "as the AI learns" (*keeping track*). Both people lose
  visibility; neither holds the verdict alone.
- **Carries.** The professional-work case for all three parts; the apex as a learning system. Limit
  (S13 §1.8): the source shows evaluation of one party and decisions that reach both, not binding of both.

## C4. Hiring through a learning system (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 6)

- **Source.** "Over time as managers and applicants interact with the system, the evaluation algorithm may
  become increasingly refined and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). "The way GenAI learns and generates
  insights are functions not likely to be well defined or transparent" (p. 6).
- **Corners.** Applicant, manager, hiring system.
- **Reading.** The clearest case of *keeping track*: the rule refines itself while the people use it, and
  becomes inaccessible. It is also a one-off pair (N1): applicant and manager may never work together.
- **Carries.** Keeping track; the "stochastic/learning" premise for the apex (with Cornelissen et al.,
  2024, p. 6, "a probabilistic rendering", as the only source in the corpus using such a term).

## C5. Emergent interdependence and blind following (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 7)

- **Source.** Mayo's medical teams: "emergent interdependence was organic and associated with better
  patient outcomes. With the introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because
  team members blindly follow guidance from the AI?" (p. 7).
- **Corners.** Team member, colleague, generative AI.
- **Reading.** Blind following is the *collapse* failure mode: the member treats the system as the party
  that matters and the colleague drops from view. Algorithmacy names what a member who does not collapse
  the triad keeps doing. Scope cost already ruled (S13): "guidance" widens "binding".
- **Carries.** Failure modes; the third contribution (answers OQ-hinds2024-04 in part).

## C6. The theme park (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4)

- **Source.** "the GenAI might place food orders based on a surge of customers, dynamically route workers
  to busier rides to avoid lines, and trigger discounts for toys that are not selling well" (p. 4).
- **Reading.** Workers and customers meet through decisions neither made: the worker routed to a ride
  meets a customer the system sent. A short illustration of *relational cascades* reaching both lower
  corners. Optional.

## C7. The Post Office Horizon system (Patriotta, Augustine & Voronov, 2025, pp. 10–11)

- **Source.** "thousands of employees of the UK Post Office were falsely accused of stealing from local
  branch accounts over the course of a decade. Eventually, it became clear that the Post Office's IT
  system, called Horizon, was at fault for producing these false losses" (p. 11). The Post Office's first
  answer: "Horizon is an extremely robust system … There is no evidence that points to any fault with the
  technology" (p. 11, quoting Thomson, 2009).
- **Reading.** Where declined responsibility comes to rest when no one can tell the system's output from a
  person's conduct: on the sub-postmaster, the moral crumple zone (Elish, 2019). The organization charged
  the system with reliability and discharged itself; the people at the lower corner could not show the
  cause. The sharpest case for the section "Where Declined Responsibility Comes to Rest".
- **Limit.** Not a platform between two people: the Post Office is employer and accuser, and the
  counterpart corner is thin (branch customers, auditors). Use for where responsibility lands, not for
  the triad's form.

## C8. Uber refusing its drivers as employees (Patriotta, Augustine & Voronov, 2025, p. 14)

- **Source.** Uber "initially came under scrutiny for entering markets without proper licensing (civic
  OOW challenge) and refusing to accept their drivers as employees" (p. 14), and made "very minor
  discursive or material concessions" (p. 14).
- **Reading.** An organization-level instance of the platform declining the status that would make it
  answer, the move Schoeneborn et al. name for platforms (p. 13). Pairs with C1 at the apex.
- **Carries.** Review section, beside Schoeneborn et al. p. 13.

## C9. Emily, the driver, and the ghost worker (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, pp. 10–11)

- **Source.** Quoting Gray and Suri: "Neither Emily nor the driver knows that their meeting hinges on
  another woman, two oceans away" (p. 10); such workers "are entirely invisible to both Uber customers and
  drivers" (p. 11).
- **Reading.** The apex is not only code: the platform's decision can rest on hidden human work. For the
  two people at the lower corners this changes nothing they can see, which is the point: the
  intermediary's opacity covers people as well as algorithms. Useful against reading "the platform" as
  "the algorithm" (N3 settled: the platform).

## C10. Frontline staff helping citizens through digital self-service (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 13)

- **Source.** "many citizens need help with helping themselves, which is why frontline employees must
  spend time showing them how to navigate digital systems (Pors, 2015)" (p. 13).
- **Reading.** One person exercising algorithmacy on another's behalf, inside the triad: the employee
  interprets and specifies for the citizen. An instance of Justesen and Plesner's "unintended tasks"
  (p. 22) that the paper can name. Supports the Concern and the Discussion (who holds the ability).

## C11. Answering negative reviews (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 15)

- **Source.** Employees "'swallow their negative emotions both during interactions with clients, but also
  in replying to negative reviews'" (p. 15, quoting Bucher et al., 2021, p. 57).
- **Reading.** *Specifying intent* under a double address: the reply goes to the client and is read by
  the review system. Flag (REPORT): check the "pacifying the algorithm" pairing against Bucher et al.
  before using Bucher for the collapse mode.

## C12. Scapegoating (Schoeneborn, Dobusch & Seidl, 2026, p. 11)

- **Source.** Practices of "'scapegoating' … with a focus on how the blame for corporate wrongdoings is
  often strategically attributed to specific individual actors rather than the collective (Roulet &
  Pichler, 2020)" (p. 11).
- **Reading.** The askers' own discharge case runs from the collective to a named individual. The paper's
  case runs from the platform to whichever of two people is blamed, outcome by outcome. Carries the link
  to OQ-schoeneborn2026-06.

## C13. The prompt-and-answer mode (Phillips, Kalvapalle & Kennedy, 2024, p. 9)

- **Source.** "an individual provides prompts and the AI system provides answers … the generative AI
  system remains, in this case, very much subordinate to the human partner" (p. 9).
- **Reading.** The dyad in its plainest form, offered by the askers as one mode among four (Table 1,
  p. 10). Algorithmacy begins where a second person stands on the other side of the system's answer.
  Review section (the partner placement).
