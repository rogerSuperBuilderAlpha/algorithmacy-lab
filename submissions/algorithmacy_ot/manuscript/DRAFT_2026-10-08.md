# Algorithmacy: Coordinating with another person through an algorithm that judges you both

## Abstract

More and more work is arranged so that two people depend on each other, and an algorithm sits between them. The algorithm scores what each of them does, its score counts for both of them, and neither of them can see how it is calculated. Organization theory has described this arrangement from the side of the platform that runs it. This article describes what a person inside it has to be able to do. I call the ability algorithmacy, and I place it beside two older ones. Oracy is the ability to deal with people directly, in pairs or in chains of pairs. Literacy is the ability to deal with a person through a text that stands between the two of you. Algorithmacy is the ability to deal with a person when an algorithm stands between you and acts on you both, which makes three parties and not two. It has three parts. The person has to work out, from a result she cannot fully explain, what the algorithm's rule probably is and what the other person probably meant. She has to act and write in a way that both the algorithm and the other person will take correctly. And she has to notice when the rule changes, because nobody tells her. I show that each of these three tasks follows from one feature of the algorithm: it is hidden, its result is binding, and it changes. Existing accounts of digital skill treat the arrangement as a dyad of one kind or the other. The article ends with three propositions and a warning: people who get good at this also make an unfair arrangement run more smoothly.

*Keywords:* algorithmacy; algorithmic management; coordination; co-optation; platforms; generative AI; literacy

## Introduction

While global leaders promote these technologies, less visible displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are also reconfiguring how organizations perform (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023). Hoping to install a plug-and-play intelligence ignores that organizing now depends on relations among human and algorithmic actors (Stelmaszak et al., 2026), relations in which generative AI may expand the black box and reduce visibility and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024) while its detailed outputs give those who are shown them an illusion that uncertain outcomes are knowable (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a co-optive, opaque, adaptive intermediary.

Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are strapping ourselves to the mast of a "literacy"-based understanding. We do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or reducing the triad to pairs, which leaves us blind to its emergent, binding power.

[AUTHOR, corrected] Oracy—the term is Wilkinson's (1965)—is the sensibility to navigate direct dyads, and chains of them, through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. Porsfelt et al. (2026, p. 1) propose treating imagination as "a situated engagement with the non-present"; algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with what is present and unseen. As a sensibility like literacy and oracy, it operates through the competencies and capacities to: 1) interpret, from an otherwise innocuous output, both the intermediary's rule and a counterpart's intentions; 2) specify intent so meaning survives the opaque, adaptive mediator; and 3) keep track as governance radically, repeatedly, and suddenly shifts. We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

The article has six parts. I first set out the comparison that carries the argument: oracy belongs to direct and chained dyads, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads. I then describe the situation I am writing about, using one example that I return to throughout. Third, I show what existing accounts leave out. Fourth, I define the arrangement and the ability. Fifth, and at most length, I explain how algorithmacy works. Last, I say what this changes for organization theory and why organizations fail with these systems. Throughout, I build on what recent articles in this journal have established about platforms, algorithms, and generative AI, and I add what they leave open: what the person inside these arrangements has to be able to do.

## From oracy to literacy to algorithmacy

The argument of this article is about three ways in which people can be connected when they deal with each other, and about the ability that each one calls for.

The first is the direct dyad. A dyad is a pair. In a direct dyad two people deal with each other with nothing in between: I speak to you, and you hear me and answer. Dyads can also be chained. I tell John, and John tells Sally. A chain reaches further than a single pair, but it is still made of direct dyads, because at each link two people are speaking and listening to each other. The ability to take part in direct and chained dyads is oracy (Wilkinson, 1965).

The second is the moderated dyad. Here something stands between the two people and shapes what passes between them, without being one of them. A written text does this. When I write to you, there are still only two of us, but the text is now between us. It fixes my words, it carries them across distance and time, and it leaves out my tone and your chance to ask me what I meant (Olson, 1994). The text moderates our exchange. It does not take part in it. It does not judge either of us, it does not decide anything, and it does not change after it is written. The ability to take part in moderated dyads is literacy. Organizations came to depend on it. Between about 1850 and 1920, American firms came to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files (Yates, 1989), and an organization that runs on documents assumes that its people can read and write them.

The third is the triad. A triad is a group of three in which each one affects the other two. This is the arrangement that algorithms now create. When an algorithm evaluates two people who are working together, and its result counts for both of them, the thing in the middle is no longer a moderator. It is a third party. It acts on what passes between the two people, it keeps its rule to itself, and it changes that rule over time. The ability to take part in a triad of this kind is what I call algorithmacy.

**Table 1.** Three ways of being connected, and the ability each calls for.

| How the people are connected | What stands between them | What it does | The ability |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Direct dyad, or a chain of them | Nothing, or another person | — | Oracy |
| Moderated dyad | A text | Carries and fixes what is said; does not act | Literacy |
| Triad | An algorithm | Evaluates both people, binds both, and changes its rule | Algorithmacy |

The three abilities are different in kind, and one does not grow into the next. Being a good speaker does not make a person able to read. In the same way, being a good reader does not make a person able to work through an algorithm that judges her, because a text and an algorithm of this kind do different things. The mistake I describe in the rest of the article is to treat a triad as if it were one of the two earlier forms. Some accounts treat the algorithm as the person one is dealing with, which makes the arrangement a direct dyad. Others treat the algorithm as a channel between two people, which makes it a moderated dyad. Both leave out what is new.

## The situation

Here is the example. A student in a software course submits her code for review. Several classmates read it. They leave comments, and each of them votes on whether it should pass. She can see the comments and the votes. Then a program takes the votes and other inputs, combines them by a formula, and returns one result: pass or fail. Nobody in the course can see the formula. The instructor has no vote and cannot change the result. The formula is adjusted during the term, and the adjustments are not announced. The classmates who review her are scored too, on how well their reviews match the final results.

Three parties are involved, and I will use the same three names for them from here on. The *participant* is the person whose position we are looking at, here the student. The *counterpart* is the other person she is working with, here a classmate who reviews her. The *intermediary* is the program that stands between them and produces the result.

The intermediary has three features, and the whole article depends on them. First, it is *opaque*. This means the participant cannot see the rule it uses. She sees what went in and what came out, and nothing in between. Second, it is *binding*. This means its result counts for both people and neither of them can set it aside. The student passes or fails, and the reviewer's own score moves, on the strength of the same result. Third, it is *adaptive*. This means the rule changes over time. What produced a pass in week four may not produce one in week ten.

The student's practical problem is not writing good code. She has to work with her reviewers, since their reactions feed the result, and what reaches her from them comes through a rule she cannot see. If she fails, she does not know whether her reviewers thought the work was poor or were outweighed by something else in the formula. When she writes her next explanation, she is writing for people and also for a program that will score it. And whatever she learns about the program may stop being true without warning.

The same situation exists in organizations, and the articles this paper builds on describe it. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe a manager who uses generative AI to evaluate employees, where neither the manager nor the employee can see what the evaluation is based on. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe a user rating a provider on a platform. The user is not the platform's employee and has been given no authority by it, and yet the platform turns her rating into a ranking that affects the provider (p. 14). Leonardi and Leavell (2026) describe city planners and the officials, developers, and advocates they work with, where a simulation model stood between the two groups and, in one of the organizations they studied, came to settle their disagreements. In each case two people depend on each other, and the consequences of what each does for the other are set by a rule that neither can read.

This article is about what the participant needs in order to do well in that situation. I call it algorithmacy. Before defining it, I show that the existing accounts do not already cover it.

## What existing accounts leave out

### Organization theory describes the arrangement, not the person inside it

Organization theory has a good description of the arrangement as a whole. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) argue that platforms are not hierarchies, markets, or networks, and they put the difference in one sentence: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (p. 5). By this they mean that the platform does not employ the people who use it and does not simply sell to them. It draws them in and organizes what they do. Other work shows how this is done. Platforms coordinate people they do not employ through systems of rating and ranking (Kornberger et al., 2017), they control what each side is able to see (Curchod et al., 2020; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), they use algorithms to direct, evaluate, and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020), and they set the terms of the work while leaving the people who do it formally free (Vallas & Schor, 2020). Digital systems can also displace established rules and standards in ways that are hard to see while they are happening (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023), and the technology has to be understood as part of the organizing and not as something outside it (Faraj & Pachidi, 2021). These accounts are written from the point of view of the system. They explain what the platform does to people. They do not say what a person has to be able to do in return.

The most recent accounts come close to the situation I described and then stop. One group of authors argues that artificial intelligence in an organization is not located in the software. It comes from "multiple, complex, and multifaceted relations among human and algorithmic actors" (Stelmaszak et al., 2026, p. 337), and related work places agency in combinations of people and technology (Glaser et al., 2024; Murray et al., 2021). These authors count every kind of person involved. What they do not examine is the relation between one person and another, because every relation they analyze runs between a person and an algorithm. They say the resulting capability belongs to the organization and is "not merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346), and they note in passing that people under such a system learn to change how they behave (p. 350), without saying what they learn. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) reach the same point from generative AI. In their example of a manager who uses it to evaluate employees, the effects run "between managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4). That is a triad. The authors ask what it does to the organization, and not what the manager or the employee now has to be able to do.

### Why the third party matters

It could seem that adding an algorithm between two people is a small change. Simmel (1902) argued, for groups of people, that it is not. A group of three is a different kind of thing from a group of two. In a pair each person deals directly with the other. A third can mediate between the other two, profit from their disagreement, or set them against each other, and in each case the relation between the two now passes through the third, who has aims of its own (Simmel, 1950).

This gives a simple test to apply to any account of digital skill. What position does the account give the algorithm, and what happens to the other person? I apply that test to eight accounts from organization theory, communication research, and human–computer interaction. They fall into three groups. Table 2 lists them.

### Group one: the machine is the other party

The first group treats the machine as the one the person is dealing with. In the terms set out above, it treats the arrangement as a direct dyad, with the machine as the second member. Human–machine communication is the clearest case. It grew out of the finding that people respond to computers socially (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), and its central claim is that AI systems should be studied as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (Guzman & Lewis, 2020, p. 71; see also Guzman, 2018). This is the right account for a person using a chatbot. For the student at the gate it leaves out her reviewers, and critics inside the field have said that it treats the person and the machine as an isolated pair (Gibbs et al., 2021). AI literacy belongs in the same group. Its best-known statement lists seventeen things a person should understand about AI, and none of them describes another person as a party to the interaction (Long & Magerko, 2020). Reviews of the wider literature on algorithmic literacy find the same (Iyamu et al., 2026; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025).

### Group two: the machine is a channel between two people

The second group keeps both people and treats the machine as something messages pass through. It treats the arrangement as a moderated dyad, with the machine in the place of the text. In Spitzberg's (2006) account of communicating well through a computer, the two people judge each other directly and the technology only changes conditions such as speed. Walther (1992, 1996) showed that people learn to get personal meaning across even in plain text, and that they learn it from the other person's replies. I return to that finding below, because at the gate the result that matters is not a reply. Work on AI-mediated communication comes nearer, since it puts an AI system between two people. But that system "operates on behalf of a communicator" (Hancock et al., 2020, p. 90). It helps one person write to the other, and the research asks how the help changes what the two think of each other (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020; Hohenstein et al., 2023). Research on machines as teammates is similar, in that the machine advises or assists and the people decide (Sebo et al., 2020; Seeber et al., 2020; Traeger et al., 2020). In none of this work does the machine judge both people and bind them.

### Group three: the worker facing the system

The third group studies people who work on platforms, so it is the closest to my subject. It also ends up with a dyad, this time between the worker and the platform. Two recent studies set out the skills such work takes (Hong et al., 2026; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025). Zhou and colleagues built and tested a twelve-item questionnaire for what they call algorithmic competency, defined as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2). The customer appears in their items only as a source of ratings, and the item about fixing an unfair result asks whether the worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)" (p. 8). The worker appeals to the platform and never deals with the customer who gave the rating. Two further studies include the other person in their fieldwork, and both find that workers reach that person by leaving the system. They move the relationship off the platform (Rahman, 2021), or take established clients elsewhere after a rating that could not be repaired (Sutherland et al., 2020, p. 469). Neither describes what it takes to work with the other person while staying inside.

**Table 2.** Eight existing accounts, and where each puts the algorithm and the other person.

| Account | What the algorithm is | Where the other person is |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Human–machine communication (Guzman & Lewis, 2020) | The party you communicate with | Not in the account |
| AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020) | Something to understand | Not in the account, except as designer |
| Computer-mediated communication competence (Spitzberg, 2006) | A channel | Present, and judges you directly |
| Social information processing (Walther, 1992, 1996) | A narrow channel that people learn to work around | Present, and replies to you |

| Reactivity to opaque evaluation (Rahman, 2021) | Something to figure out | In the fieldwork, not in the theory |
| Gig literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020) | Something to get around | Present, and reached by leaving the platform |

### What the eight have in common

All eight accounts turn the triad into a dyad of one kind or the other, and so all eight leave out the same combination. Some of them include the other person, and in those the technology is a channel, a helper, or an obstacle. It never makes a decision that binds both people. Others treat the algorithm as hidden and powerful, and in those the other person becomes a number in a rating. None of them has an active other person and a binding, hidden algorithm at the same time.

I am not claiming that nobody has noticed the three-party shape. It has been described as a lopsided relation on an online marketplace, kept in place by differences in what each party can see (Curchod et al., 2020), as an "algorithmic labor triangle" (Cameron, 2024), and, in online dispute resolution, as a "fourth party" (Katsh & Rifkin, 2001). All of these describe the structure and how it controls people. A separate body of work shows people forming their own working theories of ranking and feed algorithms and adjusting how they present themselves (Bucher, 2017; Cotter, 2019; DeVito et al., 2018; Litt, 2012). There the algorithm ranks one person's output for an audience. It does not bind two people who are doing joint work. My claim is narrower than either. No existing account describes the ability a person needs in order to do interdependent work with another person through an algorithm that judges and binds them both. The rest of the article names that ability and describes it.

## The arrangement and the ability

### The arrangement: coordinative co-optation

I need a name for the arrangement, and I take it from the literature on platforms. By coordination I mean the way people get interdependent tasks done together (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Whether a way of organizing counts as new is a question the field has asked before (Puranam et al., 2014). The standard forms of coordination each give the participant a recognizable position. In a hierarchy she is an employee who follows legitimate instructions (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). In a market she is a buyer or seller who can accept or refuse a price (Hayek, 1945). In a network she is a partner in a relationship that either side can end (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). On a platform she is none of these. The platform's algorithms match her with people she did not choose, evaluate what she and they do, and can close her account (Stark & Pais, 2020).

I call this arrangement *coordinative co-optation*. The term is mine. I build it on Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) statement that on platforms people "are co-opted" (p. 5). The word needs explaining, because its classic meaning is different. Selznick (1949) used co-optation for what an organization does when it takes a critic or outsider onto one of its own boards. The outsider gets a seat and some formal standing, and in exchange stops being an opponent. A platform also draws outsiders in, but it gives them no seat and no standing. It enrolls them on its own terms, scores them, and can remove them. I add "coordinative" because the point of the arrangement, from the participant's side, is that it is how she and another person get joint work done.

The platform's position in this arrangement is unusual in two ways. The first comes from Simmel (1950). He describes the arbitrator as a third party who does not help the other two agree, but decides between them. A normal arbitrator has that power because the two parties gave it to him, and they can take it back. The platform decides between the two parties without either of them having given it that power. The only thing they can do about it is leave.

The second comes from Schoeneborn et al. (2026). They argue that being an organized actor is a matter of degree. A collective counts as an actor to the extent that people treat it as one and hold it responsible. They observe that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13). Platforms describe themselves as a marketplace, as infrastructure, or as a tool. So the participant is dealing with a third party that decides things for her and also presents itself as not being a party at all. This matters in practice. It is one reason she may not think to ask what the system is doing between her and the other person.

### The ability: algorithmacy

Algorithmacy is the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. It has three parts. The first is interpreting: working out, from a result, what the intermediary's rule probably is and what the counterpart probably meant. The second is specifying intent: acting and writing so that both the intermediary and the counterpart take one's meaning correctly. The third is keeping track: noticing when the rule changes. The word should not be confused with algocracy, which names rule by algorithm as a way of organizing (Aneesh, 2009; Danaher, 2016). Algorithmacy names what a person inside such an arrangement is able to do.

I have been calling algorithmacy a sensibility, and I mean by that a habit of seeing a situation a certain way, which comes before any particular skill. A person who can read does not decide each time to treat marks on a page as words. She sees them as words, and her reading skills work on top of that. A person with algorithmacy sees a result from the system as something produced jointly by a rule and by other people, and sees her own next action as something both will receive. The three parts are the skills that work on top of this way of seeing. A person can have some of the skills without it. Someone can be very good at guessing a system's rule and never think about the person on the other side of the score.

Algorithmacy belongs to the individual, and that separates it from two nearby ideas. It is not a skill in the narrow sense of a fixed procedure that can be written down and taught, because the rule it deals with is secret and keeps changing. It is not an organizational capability, meaning something a firm owns and uses (Teece et al., 1997). The firm owns the algorithm. The participant owns nothing in the arrangement. The closest existing idea is Sandberg's (2000). He studied engineers at Volvo and found that what made some of them more competent was not a longer list of skills. It was how they understood what their job was. Algorithmacy is competence of that kind, applied to work done through an algorithm. Saying the ability is individual does not mean people develop it alone. People pick it up from others in the same position, by comparing results and passing on what they have worked out. It is learned socially and used individually (Felin et al., 2015).

### Where it applies

Algorithmacy is needed only when all of the following are true. The intermediary is opaque, binding, and adaptive. The two people's work depends on each other. And the consequences of what one person does for the other pass through the intermediary. The last condition needs a comment. In my example the student can see her reviewers' comments and votes, so their exchange is partly visible. What she cannot see is how much weight each comment and vote carried in the result. It is the consequence that runs through the intermediary, not necessarily the communication.

Two contrasts show where the edges are. A dating or job-matching site that introduces two people and then lets them deal with each other directly has joint work and no binding result. A scoring system that rates a family and determines a caseworker's decision has a binding result, and the family and the system are not doing joint work. Neither case calls for algorithmacy. Freelance platforms sit near the edge, because people there can negotiate directly and can leave together (Sutherland et al., 2020). Dispatch systems, piecework queues, and peer-review systems with hidden scoring are the central cases.

The ability is also separate from the participant's rights. Whether she is an employee, a contractor, or a student matters for fairness, and it does not change what she has to be able to do while the rule stays hidden. If the operator publishes the rule and fixes it, the arrangement is no longer the one I am describing, and the need for the ability falls away.

## How algorithmacy works

### The participant's problem is too many explanations, not too little information

The participant's difficulty is easy to misdescribe. It looks like a lack of information, and that is not quite right. Daft and Lengel (1986) distinguish two problems. One is uncertainty, which means not having enough data. The other is equivocality, which means having data that can be read in several conflicting ways. The student at the gate is in the second position. She has the result, the comments, the votes, and her own record of earlier attempts. What she lacks is a way to decide among several explanations that all fit. Daft and Lengel argue that this second problem calls for a rich channel with feedback, one where people can go back and forth until they agree on a reading. The student has no such channel with the gate. The gate does not answer questions, and it is also the thing that judges her.

Each of the three features of the intermediary creates one specific gap, and each part of algorithmacy fills one gap. Because the intermediary is opaque, she does not know why a result came out as it did. Interpreting fills that gap. Because the intermediary is binding, anything she says to the other person is also evidence that will be scored. Specifying intent deals with that. Because the intermediary is adaptive, what she has worked out can go out of date. Keeping track deals with that. Table 3 sets this out. The three subsections below take the parts one at a time.

**Table 3.** How the three parts of algorithmacy follow from the three features of the intermediary.

| Feature of the intermediary | What the participant is missing as a result | What she has to do | What it looks like when she does not |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Opaque | The reason for a result, including who or what caused it | Interpret | She explains the result by the rule alone, or by the people alone |
| Binding | A way to speak to the other person that is not also being scored | Specify intent | She writes for the algorithm and loses the person, or the reverse |
| Adaptive | A rule that stays the same once she has learned it | Keep track | She keeps acting on an understanding that is out of date |

### Interpreting

Interpreting is working out, from a result, what probably caused it. It is needed because the intermediary is opaque. The cause has two possible sources, the rule and the people, and the participant has to consider both. Suppose the student fails. One explanation is that her reviewers judged the work to be poor. Another is that they judged it acceptable and some other input, or a higher threshold, pushed the result down. These lead to different actions. In the first case she should change the work, or explain it better to her reviewers. In the second case her reviewers are not the problem, and changing how she addresses them would be wasted effort.

Research on how people respond to computers shows that this kind of judgment comes from the person and is not given by the system. Sundar and Nass (2000) gave people the same tutoring and the same evaluation on a screen. Some were told it came from the computer and others were told it came from a person. People rated the identical content differently depending on who they believed was behind it. The authors call this source orientation, meaning the person's working belief about who is speaking. The student at the gate has to form that belief without being told anything. She has to decide how much of the result was her reviewers and how much was the formula.

The organizational accounts describe the same problem. In Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) example, the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). An employee who receives a poor evaluation is then in the student's position. She has to work out how much of it was her manager's judgment and how much was the system's. She needs the answer for a practical reason, which is that she has to go on working with that manager.

Interpreting goes wrong in two ways. A participant can explain everything by the rule. She builds a theory of what the system rewards and treats the other people's reactions as noise. This is easier to fall into if she believes that machines are objective, a belief that has been shown to change how people act toward them (Sundar & Kim, 2019). Or she can explain everything by the people. She takes the result as her reviewers' opinion of her and forgets that a formula stood between their opinions and the outcome. Both are reasonable guesses. Each one ignores half of the evidence she has.

### Specifying intent

Specifying intent is acting and writing so that one's meaning gets through to the other person and is also handled correctly by the intermediary. It is needed because the intermediary is binding. In an ordinary exchange, when the student writes an explanation of her code, the explanation is for her reviewer and it succeeds if the reviewer understands it. At the gate the same explanation is also an input to the result. The place where she writes to her reviewer is the place where she is judged. The reviewer is in the same position, since the reviewer's own score depends on how the review compares with the result.

So the student is writing for two readers at once. Her description of a code change has to be something the automated checks can process, and it also has to tell a human reviewer why the change matters. She does not know how much weight the system gives to the reviewer's understanding. She still has to produce one piece of writing that serves both.

The closest earlier work is Walther's (1992, 1996), described above. His communicators learn to get meaning across in a limited channel, and they learn it from replies. At the gate, the student gets a result and no reply. If she fails, the result tells her that this version did not pass. It does not tell her whether the reviewer misunderstood her or the program scored her down, or which sentence was the problem. Two people normally check that they have understood each other through small signals back and forth (Clark & Brennan, 1991). When one side is a machine, that checking breaks down, because the machine holds only a thin record of what the person meant and the person is left to do the repair (Suchman, 2007). Here the reviewer's signals are visible, and the part that decides the outcome, which is how much they counted, is not.

Spitzberg's (2006) two standards for communicating well can still be used, with an adjustment. He asks whether a message is effective and whether it is appropriate. At the gate, a message is effective if it passes and the reviewer also understands what the student meant. It is appropriate if it fits what the reviewer expects, and the student only knows what the reviewer expects through her own interpreting. This is one of the places where the parts of algorithmacy depend on each other.

Specifying intent goes wrong when the message is built for only one of the two readers. A participant can run her explanation through a language model that tunes it for automated scoring, pass, and never write anything a person could use. Or she can write a clear, friendly note to her reviewer that the scoring program cannot process. Generative AI tools make the first of these very easy. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) expect generative AI to "minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7). A participant who writes only for the gate is doing that to her own working relationships.

### Keeping track

Keeping track is noticing that the rule has changed. It is needed because the intermediary is adaptive. Everything the student has worked out about the gate and about her reviewers comes from earlier results. If the formula is adjusted, some of that is no longer true, and nobody announces the adjustment. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) make the same observation about generative AI systems, whose functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4).

The difficulty is that she has to detect the change from indirect signs. Since she cannot see the rule, she can only see its effects. A style of submission that used to pass starts drawing objections. Reviewers who used to agree with the results start to be surprised by them. These signs show up in what the other people do, as well as in her own results. A participant who watches only her own numbers will often miss a change that would be obvious if she also watched how her reviewers were reacting.

Two earlier ideas are close to this. Duran (1983) described communicative adaptability, the ability to adjust to different partners and settings. His communicator can see the partner and the setting she is adjusting to. The participant here is adjusting to a rule she cannot see, so noticing the change is part of the work. DeVito (2021) studied how social media users notice that a feed algorithm has changed and update their beliefs about it. She shows that people learn this by taking part, and that it can be taught. Her users are managing their own feeds. There is no second person whose intentions they have to work out, and no result that binds two people who are working together. With those added, her finding supports what I am describing.

Keeping track goes wrong when the participant goes on acting on an old understanding. Research on opaque evaluation shows the cost. People who cannot tell what has changed either experiment and put their standing at risk, or cut back what they do in order to protect it (Rahman, 2021). A participant who keeps track well asks two separate questions when results shift. Has the rule changed? Have the people changed? Without keeping track, she cannot tell these apart.

### How the three parts depend on each other

The three parts form a cycle. The participant gets a result she did not expect and interprets it. That interpretation shapes how she acts and writes the next time. The next result tells her something about whether the rule is the same as before, and that feeds into how she interprets the result after that. Figure 1 shows the cycle. The clearest way to see that the parts depend on each other is to remove one at a time. Without specifying intent, interpreting is only a private diagnosis. The student understands why she failed and her next submission is no different. Without interpreting, specifying intent is only formatting. She has no view of what either reader wants, so she has nothing to aim at. Without keeping track, the other two run on an understanding that may have expired. She will be confidently wrong, and the better she was at the other two, the longer she will stay that way.

This is also what I mean by calling the three-party arrangement irreducible. I mean something specific and limited. The participant cannot handle the arrangement as two separate two-party problems, first learning the machine and then dealing with the person, or the reverse. The result she reads was produced by the rule and the people together. The message she sends is received by the rule and the people together. Each part of her task involves all three parties at once.

The cycle also explains how people acquire algorithmacy. Newcomers in organizations learn to communicate competently in much the same way. They are mostly not taught. They work it out from how others respond to them (Jablin & Sias, 2001). The participant here learns in a similar way, by going around the cycle repeatedly. The difference is that she is reading results produced by a rule, not direct responses from the person she is trying to reach. Formal instruction cannot replace this, because the operator does not disclose the rule. It follows that people who have had more turns around the cycle, or who have had experienced peers to compare notes with, will have more of the ability than others who are in the same formal position.

## Discussion

The argument so far can be put briefly. Speech connects people in direct dyads and chains of them, a text connects them in a moderated dyad, and an algorithm that evaluates and binds connects them in a triad. Each calls for its own ability, and the ability for the third has three parts that depend on each other.

### What this adds to research on algorithmic management

Research on algorithmic management has mostly studied control: how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers, and how workers resist (Kellogg et al., 2020). That research explains what the system does. Algorithmacy describes what a worker has to be able to do in order to get joint work done under that system. The two are compatible, and they point to different remedies. Making a system more transparent changes the information available. Developing algorithmacy changes what a person can do with whatever information there is. Algorithmacy also fills a gap that the relational accounts of AI leave open. Stelmaszak et al. (2026) say that AI capability belongs to the organization and arises from relations among people and algorithms. An account at that level cannot explain why two people in the same position under the same system do differently. An ability that varies between individuals can. It also gives a name to what people are learning when, as those authors note, they learn to change how they behave.

Leonardi and Leavell (2026) show what happens when there is someone whose job is to do the interpreting. They compared two urban planning organizations that used the same AI simulation tool, where the planners stood between the tool and the stakeholders. Where planners presented the tool's output as a prediction, the model became "the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533) and stakeholders "treated the outputs as binding forecasts" (p. 537). The authors describe the planners' work as "selecting, sequencing, qualifying, and presenting information so that others can act under uncertainty" (p. 518). In their case an expert does this work for other people. In the arrangement I am describing there is no such expert, and the participant has to do the interpreting for herself.

### Why organizations fail with these systems

I said in the introduction that explaining how people coordinate through these systems would also change the explanation of why organizations fail with them. Here is that argument. The common explanations are that the tool was wrong for the job, that it was introduced badly, or that people were not trained to use it. All of these treat the problem as one of learning a tool. Field studies fit that picture only partly. They find algorithmic tools installed across an organization and then ignored or worked around by the professionals meant to use them (Christin, 2017).

My argument is that a system that judges and binds people changes the kind of coordination they are in. Before, a person dealt with a colleague directly, or through a channel that only carried messages. Now she deals with the colleague through something that scores them both. If nobody tells her the situation has changed in kind, she will go on acting as before. She will read a system's result as if it were her colleague's opinion, which is to treat a triad as a direct dyad. She will write to her colleague as if the system only carried her words, which is to treat it as a moderated dyad. Training in how to operate the tool does not fix this, because the tool's buttons are not what she has misunderstood. That is the point of the comparison with handing books to people who cannot read. The organization has supplied the object and assumed the ability to use it.

### A problem the idea creates

Algorithmacy has an uncomfortable consequence, and I want to state it directly. When individuals get better at working through an unfair arrangement, the arrangement works better, and there is less pressure to change it. A person's ability and the fairness of her situation are separate things. High algorithmacy does not mean she is being treated well.

There is a related risk in how the idea could be used. When an automated system fails, blame tends to land on the nearest human (Elish, 2019). An idea like algorithmacy could be used the same way, to say that workers who do badly simply lack an ability. That would move the blame for a hidden rule onto the people subjected to it. I do not intend that use, and the argument does not support it. The reason the ability is needed is that the operator hides and changes the rule. Workers on platforms have been shown to come to accept their conditions through the limited choices the platform leaves them (Cameron, 2024). Widespread algorithmacy could contribute to the same result, by letting people absorb problems quietly that would otherwise be visible. Three propositions follow.

*Proposition 1.* A participant with more algorithmacy gets better results from working through the intermediary. This does not make the arrangement fairer. Her gains come from handling the system better in private. They do not come from the rule being disclosed, from appeals being granted, or from the system becoming easier for others to understand.

*Proposition 2.* Algorithmacy is unevenly spread among people the platform treats as identical. This produces differences in outcome among people who are formally in the same position. It is the same kind of gap that was found in internet use, where people with the same access differed widely in what they could do with it (Hargittai, 2002).

*Proposition 3.* The more transparent the intermediary is, the less algorithmacy a participant needs. Disclosure by the operator and ability on the part of the participant are therefore two ways of dealing with the same difficulty. An organization or a regulator can choose between asking people to cope with a hidden rule and requiring the rule to be shown. At present the usual arrangement is neither. A person who disputes a result has to appeal to the same system that produced it (Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy, 2017; Wing, 2016).

### Questions for research

The account raises three questions that argument cannot settle. Are the three parts distinct when measured, and is deciding whether the system or the other person caused a change a fourth part of its own? Does algorithmacy explain who does well once two other things are taken into account, namely how financially dependent a person is on the system and whether her standing has dropped sharply before, which one study found to drive how workers respond (Rahman, 2021)? And is it different in practice from general digital skill and from ordinary skill at communicating, as the argument here says it should be?

## Conclusion

This article has described a situation that is now common and has had no adequate account. Two people depend on each other, and an algorithm between them judges both by a rule that neither can see and that changes without notice. Existing accounts of digital skill deal with a person and a machine, or with two people and a channel. None deals with a person who has to work with another person through a machine that binds them both. In the terms of this article, they describe dyads, direct or moderated, and the situation is a triad.

I have named the ability this requires and said what it consists of. The participant has to interpret results, which means working out what the rule and the other person each contributed. She has to specify intent, which means acting and writing for the rule and the other person at once. She has to keep track of a rule that changes. Each of these follows from one feature of the intermediary, and the three depend on each other.

Naming the ability makes it possible to study. It can be measured, it can be compared across people who are treated as equivalent, and it can be weighed against the alternative of making the rule visible. It also makes one thing harder to overlook. When an organization puts a system of this kind between people, it is asking them for an ability that it has not checked they have and has not helped them to get.
