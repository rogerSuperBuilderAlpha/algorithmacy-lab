# Algorithmacy: Coordinating with another person through an algorithm that judges you both

## Abstract

People deal with each other in three ways. In a direct dyad, or a chain of them, they speak and listen, and the ability this takes is oracy. In a moderated dyad a text stands between two people and carries their words without acting on them, and the ability this takes is literacy. In a triad an algorithm stands between two people, evaluates both, binds both, and changes its rule, and the ability this takes is algorithmacy. Organizations now put such algorithms between their people and assume the people can cope, as they once assumed everyone could read. Existing accounts of digital skill turn the triad back into a dyad: they treat the algorithm as the one a person deals with, or as a channel. Algorithmacy has three parts, one for each feature of the algorithm. Because the rule is hidden, a person must interpret results. Because the result binds, she must specify her intent to the algorithm and the other person at once. Because the rule changes, she must keep track of it. The three depend on one another. People who acquire the ability do better, and they also keep an unfair arrangement running.

*Keywords:* algorithmacy; algorithmic management; coordination; co-optation; platforms; generative AI; literacy

## Introduction

[AUTHOR, corrected] Organizations fail to adopt and understand the tsunami of new platforms and services enabled by generative AI. While global leaders promote these technologies, less visible displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are also reconfiguring how organizations perform (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023). Focusing on those less visible shifts allows us to recognize how generative AI sits inside the work and reshapes it, functioning more as—but not quite—a "counterpart" in a system of work that includes its design, implementation, and use (Anthony et al., 2023). Hoping to install a plug-and-play intelligence ignores that organizing now depends on relations among human and algorithmic actors (Stelmaszak et al., 2026), relations in which generative AI may expand the black box and reduce visibility and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024) while its detailed outputs give those who are shown them an illusion that uncertain outcomes are knowable (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a co-optive, opaque, adaptive intermediary.

[AUTHOR, corrected] Organizations have been doing the equivalent of giving books to people who lack literacy. We heap algorithmic management systems on people who lack algorithmacy, or the sensibility to navigate opaque triadic coordinative co-optation. The intermediaries in question are not neutral software. The platforms behind them hold collective actorhood by degree, and they work to downplay it (Schoeneborn et al., 2026); on them, as Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 5) put it, actors "are co-opted"—enrolled, evaluated, and bound. Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are strapping ourselves to the mast of a "literacy"-based understanding. We do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or reducing the triad to pairs, which leaves us blind to its emergent, binding power.

[AUTHOR, corrected] Oracy—the term is Wilkinson's (1965)—is the sensibility to navigate direct dyads, and chains of them, through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. Porsfelt et al. (2026, p. 1) propose treating imagination as "a situated engagement with the non-present"; algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with what is present and unseen. As a sensibility like literacy and oracy, it operates through the competencies and capacities to: 1) interpret, from an otherwise innocuous output, both the intermediary's rule and a counterpart's intentions; 2) specify intent so meaning survives the opaque, adaptive mediator; and 3) keep track as governance radically, repeatedly, and suddenly shifts. We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

## Oracy, literacy, algorithmacy

People deal with each other in three ways, and each takes its own ability.

In a direct dyad two people deal with each other with nothing between them. I speak to you, and you answer. Dyads chain: I tell John, and John tells Sally. A chain reaches further than a pair, and each link is still two people speaking and listening. Taking part in direct and chained dyads takes oracy (Wilkinson, 1965).

In a moderated dyad something stands between the two people and shapes what passes without being one of them. A text does this. It fixes my words and carries them across distance and time, and it leaves out my tone and your chance to ask what I meant (Olson, 1994). It judges no one, decides nothing, and does not change once written. Taking part in moderated dyads takes literacy, and organizations run on it. Between about 1850 and 1920 American firms came to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files (Yates, 1989), and a firm that runs on documents assumes its people can read and write them.

In a triad the thing in the middle acts. An algorithm that evaluates two people who are working together, and whose result counts for both, is a third party. It keeps its rule to itself and changes it over time. Taking part in such a triad takes algorithmacy.

**Table 1.** Three ways of being connected, and the ability each calls for.

| How the people are connected | What stands between them | What it does | The ability |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Direct dyad, or a chain of them | Nothing, or another person | — | Oracy |
| Moderated dyad | A text | Carries and fixes what is said; does not act | Literacy |
| Triad | An algorithm | Evaluates both people, binds both, and changes its rule | Algorithmacy |

One ability does not grow into the next. A good speaker cannot thereby read, and a good reader cannot thereby work through an algorithm that judges her, because a text and such an algorithm do different things. Current thinking makes one of two mistakes. It treats the algorithm as the one a person deals with, which turns the triad into a direct dyad, or it treats the algorithm as a channel, which turns it into a moderated dyad.

## The situation

A student in a software course submits her code for review. Classmates read it, comment, and vote on whether it should pass, and she can see the comments and votes. A program then combines the votes with other inputs by a formula and returns pass or fail. Nobody in the course can see the formula. The instructor has no vote and cannot change the result. The formula is adjusted during the term without notice. The reviewers are scored too, on how well their reviews match the results.

I call the student the participant, a classmate who reviews her the counterpart, and the program the intermediary. The intermediary is opaque: the participant cannot see its rule. It is binding: its result counts for both people and neither can set it aside. It is adaptive: its rule changes.

The student has to work with her reviewers, because their reactions feed the result, and what reaches her from them comes through a rule she cannot see. If she fails, she does not know whether they thought the work poor or were outweighed by something else in the formula. Her next explanation is written for them and for a program that will score it. What she learns about the program can stop being true without warning.

The same situation occurs in organizations. A manager uses generative AI to evaluate employees, and neither can see what the evaluation rests on (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). A user rates a provider on a platform that has given her no authority, and the platform turns her rating into a ranking that affects the provider (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). City planners and the officials, developers, and advocates they work with face a simulation model that, in one organization, came to settle their disagreements (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026). In each case two people depend on each other, and a rule neither can read sets the consequences of what each does for the other.

## What existing accounts leave out

Organization theory describes the arrangement from the platform's side. Platforms are not hierarchies, markets, or networks: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 5). Platforms coordinate people they do not employ through rating and ranking (Kornberger et al., 2017), control what each side can see (Curchod et al., 2020; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), direct, evaluate, and discipline by algorithm (Kellogg et al., 2020), and set the terms of work while leaving people formally free (Vallas & Schor, 2020). Digital systems displace established rules and standards out of sight (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023), and the technology is part of the organizing, not outside it (Faraj & Pachidi, 2021). All of this says what the platform does to people. None of it says what a person must be able to do in return.

The most recent accounts reach the triad and stop. Stelmaszak et al. (2026) locate artificial intelligence in "multiple, complex, and multifaceted relations among human and algorithmic actors" (p. 337), and others place agency in combinations of people and technology (Glaser et al., 2024; Murray et al., 2021). Every relation Stelmaszak et al. analyze runs between a person and an algorithm, never between one person and another, and the capability that results belongs to the organization and is "not merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346). People under such a system learn to change how they behave, they note (p. 350), and they do not say what those people learn. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe effects running "between managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4), which is a triad, and ask what it does to the organization, not what the manager or the employee must now be able to do.

A third party changes what two people can do with each other. In a pair each deals directly with the other. A third can mediate, profit from the pair's disagreement, or set them against each other, and in each case their relation passes through the third, who has aims of its own (Simmel, 1902, 1950). The question to put to any account of digital skill is therefore where it places the algorithm and what becomes of the other person. Eight accounts answer in three ways (Table 2).

The first group makes the machine the one the person deals with, which is a direct dyad with the machine as its second member. Human–machine communication grew out of the finding that people respond to computers socially (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), and it holds that AI systems are "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (Guzman & Lewis, 2020, p. 71; see also Guzman, 2018). That fits a person using a chatbot. For the student it leaves out her reviewers, and critics inside the field say it treats the person and the machine as an isolated pair (Gibbs et al., 2021). AI literacy does the same. Its best-known statement lists seventeen things a person should understand about AI, and none describes another person as a party to the interaction (Long & Magerko, 2020). Reviews of the literature on algorithmic literacy find the same (Iyamu et al., 2026; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025).

The second group keeps both people and makes the machine something messages pass through, which is a moderated dyad with the machine in the place of the text. In Spitzberg's (2006) account of communicating well through a computer, the two people judge each other directly and the technology changes conditions such as speed. Walther (1992, 1996) showed that people learn to get personal meaning across in plain text, and that they learn it from the other person's replies. AI-mediated communication puts an AI system between two people, and that system "operates on behalf of a communicator" (Hancock et al., 2020, p. 90): it helps one person write to the other, and the research asks how the help changes what the two think of each other (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020; Hohenstein et al., 2023). In research on machines as teammates the machine advises or assists and the people decide (Sebo et al., 2020; Seeber et al., 2020; Traeger et al., 2020). In none of this work does the machine judge both people and bind them.

The third group studies people who work on platforms and ends up with a dyad between the worker and the platform. Two recent studies set out the skills such work takes (Hong et al., 2026; Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025). Zhou and colleagues built a twelve-item questionnaire for algorithmic competency, defined as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2). The customer appears in the items only as a source of ratings, and the item about fixing an unfair result asks whether the worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)" (p. 8). The worker appeals to the platform and never deals with the customer who gave the rating. Two further studies include the other person in their fieldwork, and in both the workers reach that person by leaving the system: they move the relationship off the platform (Rahman, 2021), or take established clients elsewhere after a rating that could not be repaired (Sutherland et al., 2020, p. 469).

**Table 2.** Eight existing accounts, and where each puts the algorithm and the other person.

| Account | What the algorithm is | Where the other person is |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Human–machine communication (Guzman & Lewis, 2020) | The party you communicate with | Not in the account |
| AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020) | Something to understand | Not in the account, except as designer |
| Computer-mediated communication competence (Spitzberg, 2006) | A channel | Present, and judges you directly |
| Social information processing (Walther, 1992, 1996) | A narrow channel that people learn to work around | Present, and replies to you |
| AI-mediated communication (Hancock et al., 2020) | A helper acting for one person | Present, and receives an altered message |
| Algorithmic competency (Zhou, Lei, Liu, et al., 2025) | Something to navigate and comply with | Present only as a source of ratings |
| Reactivity to opaque evaluation (Rahman, 2021) | Something to figure out | In the fieldwork, not in the theory |
| Gig literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020) | Something to get around | Present, and reached by leaving the platform |
| Algorithmacy (this article) | A hidden, changing rule that judges both people | Present, and reached through the algorithm |

All eight turn the triad into a dyad. Where an account includes the other person, the technology is a channel, a helper, or an obstacle, and never decides anything that binds both people. Where an account treats the algorithm as hidden and powerful, the other person appears only as a rating.

Others have drawn the three-party shape: a lopsided relation on an online marketplace held in place by differences in what each party can see (Curchod et al., 2020), an "algorithmic labor triangle" (Cameron, 2024), a "fourth party" in online dispute resolution (Katsh & Rifkin, 2001). They describe the structure and how it controls people. Work on people's own theories of ranking and feed algorithms (Bucher, 2017; Cotter, 2019; DeVito et al., 2018; Litt, 2012) concerns an algorithm that ranks one person's output for an audience, not one that binds two people doing joint work. No account describes the ability a person needs to do interdependent work with another person through an algorithm that judges and binds them both.

## The arrangement and the ability

### Coordinative co-optation

Coordination is how people get interdependent tasks done together (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009), and each standard form of it gives the participant a position. In a hierarchy she is an employee who follows legitimate instructions (Simon, 1997; Weber, 1978). In a market she can accept or refuse a price (Hayek, 1945). In a network she is a partner in a relationship either side can end (Granovetter, 1985; Powell, 1990). On a platform she is none of these. Algorithms match her with people she did not choose, evaluate what she and they do, and can close her account (Stark & Pais, 2020).

I call the arrangement *coordinative co-optation*, after Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) statement that on platforms people "are co-opted" (p. 5). In Selznick's (1949) co-optation an organization takes a critic onto one of its boards: the outsider gets a seat and formal standing and stops being an opponent. A platform also draws outsiders in, and it gives them no seat. It enrolls them on its own terms, scores them, and can remove them. The arrangement is coordinative because it is how the participant and another person get joint work done.

The platform is an arbitrator of an unusual kind. Simmel's (1950) arbitrator decides between two parties who gave him that power and can take it back. The platform decides between two parties who gave it nothing, and all they can do is leave. It also denies being a party. Collectives count as actors to the degree that others hold them responsible, and platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (Schoeneborn et al., 2026, p. 13), presenting themselves as a marketplace, as infrastructure, or as a tool. The participant deals with a third party that decides for her and presents itself as not a party.

### Algorithmacy

Algorithmacy is the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. It has three parts. Interpreting is working out from a result what the intermediary's rule is and what the counterpart meant. Specifying intent is acting and writing so that both take one's meaning correctly. Keeping track is noticing when the rule changes. Algocracy names rule by algorithm as a way of organizing (Aneesh, 2009; Danaher, 2016); algorithmacy names what a person inside it can do.

Algorithmacy is a sensibility: a habit of seeing a situation a certain way, which comes before any skill. A reader does not decide each time to treat marks on a page as words. She sees words, and her reading skills work on top of that. A person with algorithmacy sees a result as produced jointly by a rule and by other people, and sees her next action as something both will receive. The three parts are skills that work on top of this, and a person can have some of them without it: she can be expert at guessing a system's rule and never think of the person on the other side of the score.

It belongs to the individual. It is not a skill in the narrow sense of a procedure that can be written down and taught, because the rule is secret and changes. It is not an organizational capability, something a firm owns and uses (Teece et al., 1997): the firm owns the algorithm and the participant owns nothing. It is competence in Sandberg's (2000) sense, where what separates the more competent is how they understand what their job is. People learn it from others in the same position and use it alone (Felin et al., 2015).

Algorithmacy is needed when the intermediary is opaque, binding, and adaptive, the two people's work is interdependent, and the consequences of what one does for the other pass through the intermediary. The exchange itself can be visible. The student sees her reviewers' comments and votes; she does not see what weight each carried. A site that introduces two people and lets them deal directly has joint work and no binding result. A system that scores a family and determines a caseworker's decision has a binding result and no joint work. Neither takes algorithmacy. Freelance platforms, where people can negotiate and leave together, sit at the edge (Sutherland et al., 2020). Dispatch systems, piecework queues, and peer review with hidden scoring are the centre. The participant's legal standing matters for fairness and does not change what she must be able to do while the rule stays hidden. Publish the rule and fix it, and the need for the ability goes.

## How algorithmacy works

The participant does not lack information. Daft and Lengel (1986) separate uncertainty, which is too little data, from equivocality, which is data that can be read in several conflicting ways, and they hold that equivocality needs a rich channel where people go back and forth until they agree on a reading. The student has the result, the comments, the votes, and her record. She has several explanations that all fit and no channel to settle among them, because the gate answers no questions and is also her judge.

Each feature of the intermediary leaves one gap, and each part of algorithmacy fills one (Table 3). Opacity hides why a result came out as it did, and interpreting supplies the reason. Bindingness makes everything she says to the counterpart evidence that will be scored, and specifying intent handles that. Adaptivity dates what she has worked out, and keeping track renews it.

**Table 3.** How the three parts of algorithmacy follow from the three features of the intermediary.

| Feature of the intermediary | What the participant is missing as a result | What she has to do | What it looks like when she does not |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Opaque | The reason for a result, including who or what caused it | Interpret | She explains the result by the rule alone, or by the people alone |
| Binding | A way to speak to the other person that is not also being scored | Specify intent | She writes for the algorithm and loses the person, or the reverse |
| Adaptive | A rule that stays the same once she has learned it | Keep track | She keeps acting on an understanding that is out of date |

### Interpreting

Interpreting is working out what caused a result. The cause lies in the rule, in the people, or in both. If the student fails because her reviewers judged the work poor, she should change the work or explain it better to them. If they judged it acceptable and another input or a higher threshold pushed the result down, her reviewers are not the problem and changing how she addresses them is wasted.

The system does not supply this judgment. The person does. Sundar and Nass (2000) gave people the same tutoring and evaluation on a screen, told some it came from the computer and others that it came from a person, and found that people rated identical content differently by source. The student forms her belief about the source with nobody telling her anything.

Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) employee is in the same position. The system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4), so an employee with a poor evaluation must work out how much was her manager's judgment and how much the system's, and she must go on working with that manager.

Interpreting fails in two directions. A participant explains everything by the rule, builds a theory of what the system rewards, and treats other people's reactions as noise, which is easier if she believes machines are objective (Sundar & Kim, 2019). Or she explains everything by the people, takes the result as her reviewers' opinion of her, and forgets the formula between their opinions and the outcome. Each ignores half her evidence.

### Specifying intent

Specifying intent is acting and writing so that one's meaning reaches the counterpart and is handled correctly by the intermediary. In an ordinary exchange the student's explanation of her code is for her reviewer and succeeds if the reviewer understands it. At the gate the same explanation is also an input to the result, and the place where she writes to her reviewer is the place where she is judged. The reviewer is judged there too. The student writes for two readers at once: the automated checks must be able to process her description of a change, and a human reviewer must learn from it why the change matters.

Walther's (1992, 1996) communicators learn from the replies they receive. At the gate there is a result and no reply. A failure says this version did not pass. It does not say whether the reviewer misunderstood or the program scored her down, or which sentence was at fault. Two people ordinarily check their understanding through small signals back and forth (Clark & Brennan, 1991), and when one side is a machine the checking breaks down, because the machine holds a thin record of what the person meant and the person does the repair (Suchman, 2007). Here the reviewer's signals are visible and their weight is not.

Spitzberg's (2006) two standards still apply. A message is effective if it passes and the reviewer understands what the student meant. It is appropriate if it fits what the reviewer expects, which the student knows only through interpreting.

Specifying intent fails when the message is built for one reader. A participant runs her explanation through a language model that tunes it for automated scoring, passes, and writes nothing a person can use. Or she writes a clear note to her reviewer that the program cannot process. Generative AI makes the first failure easy. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) expect it to "minimize the relations between team members" (p. 7), and a participant who writes only for the gate does that to her own working relationships.

### Keeping track

Keeping track is noticing that the rule has changed. What the student has worked out comes from earlier results, and an adjustment to the formula makes some of it false without announcement. Generative systems behave the same way: their functions, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024, p. 4).

She detects the change from its effects. A style of submission that used to pass draws objections, and reviewers who used to agree with the results are surprised by them. The signs show in what the other people do as well as in her own results, so a participant who watches only her own numbers misses changes that her reviewers' reactions would reveal.

Duran's (1983) communicative adaptability is adjustment to partners and settings the communicator can see. Here the rule cannot be seen, so noticing is part of the work. DeVito (2021) shows social media users noticing that a feed algorithm has changed and updating their beliefs, learning this by taking part and sometimes by being taught. Her users manage their own feeds, with no second person to read and no result binding two people.

Keeping track fails when the participant acts on an old understanding. People who cannot tell what changed either experiment and risk their standing or cut back to protect it (Rahman, 2021). A participant who keeps track separates two questions: whether the rule changed, and whether the people did.

### The three parts depend on each other

The parts form a cycle (Figure 1). The participant interprets an unexpected result, the interpretation shapes how she acts and writes next, and the next result tells her whether the rule is the same, which feeds her next interpretation. Remove one part and the others fail. Without specifying intent, interpreting is a private diagnosis: she knows why she failed and her next submission is no different. Without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting, with nothing to aim at. Without keeping track, the other two run on an expired understanding, and the better she is at them the longer she stays wrong.

The triad is irreducible in this sense: it cannot be handled as two dyads, first learning the machine and then dealing with the person, or the reverse. The rule and the people together produce the result she reads, and together they receive the message she sends.

People acquire algorithmacy by going round the cycle. Newcomers in organizations learn to communicate competently from how others respond to them, mostly without being taught (Jablin & Sias, 2001). The participant learns the same way from results produced by a rule. Instruction cannot replace this, because the operator does not disclose the rule. People who have had more turns, or experienced peers to compare notes with, have more of the ability than others in the same formal position.

## Discussion

### What this adds

Research on algorithmic management studies control: how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline workers, and how workers resist (Kellogg et al., 2020). Algorithmacy describes what a worker must be able to do to get joint work done under that control. Transparency changes the information available. Algorithmacy changes what a person can do with it.

Relational accounts place AI capability in the organization (Stelmaszak et al., 2026). An organizational capability cannot explain why two people in the same position under the same system do differently. An individual ability can, and it names what people learn when they learn to change how they behave.

Leonardi and Leavell (2026) show the interpreting done by an expert. In two urban planning organizations using the same AI simulation tool, planners stood between the tool and the stakeholders. Where planners presented its output as a prediction, the model became "the primary arbiter of planning decisions" (p. 533) and stakeholders "treated the outputs as binding forecasts" (p. 537). The planners' work was "selecting, sequencing, qualifying, and presenting information so that others can act under uncertainty" (p. 518). In coordinative co-optation there is no such expert, and the participant interprets for herself.

### Why organizations fail with these systems

The usual explanations are that the tool was wrong for the job, introduced badly, or not taught. Field studies find something else: tools installed across an organization and then ignored or worked around by the professionals meant to use them (Christin, 2017).

A system that judges and binds people changes the kind of coordination they are in. A person who used to deal with a colleague directly, or through a channel that only carried messages, now deals with the colleague through something that scores them both. Unless told, she acts as before. She reads the system's result as her colleague's opinion, treating a triad as a direct dyad. She writes to her colleague as if the system only carried her words, treating it as a moderated dyad. Training in the tool's buttons does not fix this, because the buttons are not what she has misunderstood. The organization has supplied the system and assumed the ability.

### The cost

When individuals get better at working through an unfair arrangement, the arrangement works better and the pressure to change it falls. Ability and fairness are separate: high algorithmacy does not mean a person is treated well. When an automated system fails, blame lands on the nearest human (Elish, 2019), and algorithmacy must not be used that way. The ability is needed because the operator hides and changes the rule, and the fault for a hidden rule lies with whoever hides it. Workers on platforms come to accept their conditions through the limited choices the platform leaves them (Cameron, 2024), and widespread algorithmacy does the same work by letting people absorb quietly what would otherwise show.

*Proposition 1.* More algorithmacy gets a participant better results and leaves the arrangement no fairer.

*Proposition 2.* Algorithmacy is unevenly spread among people the platform treats as identical, so people in the same formal position get different outcomes, as people with the same internet access differ in what they can do with it (Hargittai, 2002).

*Proposition 3.* The more transparent the intermediary, the less algorithmacy a participant needs. Disclosure and ability are alternatives, and at present people get neither: a person who disputes a result appeals to the system that produced it (Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy, 2017; Wing, 2016).

## Conclusion

Speech connects people in dyads. A text moderates a dyad. An algorithm that evaluates and binds makes a triad, and a triad takes an ability that neither oracy nor literacy supplies: interpreting results, specifying intent to the rule and the other person at once, and keeping track of a rule that changes. An organization that puts such a system between its people is asking them for that ability. It has not checked that they have it and has not helped them get it.
