# [Title]

## Abstract

When a platform decides between two people and declines to be the actor that decides, who answers for the outcome? *Organization Theory* authors ask this question of the collective and the operator, and they ask, separately, what happens to the person who works with the system; the second person in the exchange drops out of both. Most studies still rely on two-way models: a single person facing a machine, technology as a neutral channel between people, or the other person ignored. This paper asks the two questions as one, from the side of the two people. Organizations increasingly manage workers through algorithms without teaching them how those systems operate, much like handing printed books to people who cannot read. I sort communicative abilities by what stands between the people who use them: oracy for direct dyads, literacy for dyads mediated by a static text, and algorithmacy for triads in which an opaque, binding and adaptive third renders the verdict. Algorithmacy, the ability to carry out an interdependent exchange with another person through such an intermediary, has three parts: interpreting what caused an outcome, specifying intent so that it survives the platform, and keeping track of a rule that changes without notice. Reinterpreting the askers' own cases, from eBay ratings to generative AI in hiring and pay, I show how the parts fail, how failure misplaces responsibility between the two people, and how the ability that lets them finish an exchange may also absorb the friction that would otherwise challenge the platform's declined actorhood. Generative AI imports the same triadic architecture into the firm and quickens its tempo. The paper ends with five questions for research.
 Keywords: algorithmacy, platforms, actorhood, accountability, algorithmic management, generative AI

## Introduction

When a digital platform is facilitating an exchange between two parties and yet claims to be nothing more
than a neutral software tool, who is actually in control and who is the one to be held to account if
things go wrong? Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) look at how groups come to be treated as single
decision-making units (what they term “collective actorhood”) and ask what happens when that status is
repeatedly ascribed to or taken away from an organization: “What does the recurrent charging or
discharging of a collective social address with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective
responsibility?” and “whether these always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary
depending on the particular context” (p. 14). Patriotta, Augustine and Voronov (2025) highlight “the need
to examine how legitimacy tests” (the everyday tests by which a practice or organization is judged to be
legitimate) “are co-produced by both human and technological actors in the contemporary digital
landscape” (p. 19). Two years prior, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put the issue of accountability quite
simply: “But who or what is accountable when an algorithm exercises decisions that occur faster and with
a vastly larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend and react to […]?” (p. 14,
adapting Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137).

Underpinning that question of organizational responsibility is a second issue: the people in the middle.
Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask whether generative AI in fact undermines emergent interdependence—the
natural, mutual adjustment that colleagues make to each other as they get on with the work: “With the
introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members blindly follow
guidance from the AI?” (p. 7). They call for “theorizing that captures what shapes team participants’
dynamic relations with AI to coordinate their work” (p. 8). Justesen and Plesner (2024) suggest that “we
could account more fully for the emotional costs, mental loads and unintended tasks associated with
digitalization” (p. 22). Organizational researchers have long moved beyond the idea of software as a mere
tool. They haven’t yet really explained how two people work together through an opaque, self-adjusting
system that pulls them in and yet will not take responsibility for the outcomes. This paper brings those
two questions together: if the platform that is making the decision between two people is itself refusing
to be the accountable party, what must each person be able to do in order to work with the other, and
where does the responsibility that the platform is refusing to take actually reside? I call the
ability this question asks about algorithmacy: the ability to carry out an interdependent exchange with
another person through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive.

The researchers are in agreement about the nature of the workplace as it is changing. As the boundaries of
the workplace become increasingly porous, platforms are able to manage people outside the firm without
actually employing them. They do this via ratings and rankings which in turn dictate how the independent
workers and the customers behave (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Imagine a triangle with the platform
operator at the apex and the producers and users at the two bottom corners. The platform operator is in
control of both without formally employing either of them (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Getting work done
is increasingly going to be a matter of the ongoing interaction between people and learning algorithms
(Stelmaszak et al., 2026), which may well be opaque to the people who are doing the work (Hinds & von
Krogh, 2024).

These two sets of questions approach the issue from opposite directions and rarely come together. The questions of responsibility and organizational status are all posed from the top down (the
company, the platform operator at the apex of the triangle, how the scholars categorize the technology).
The questions about the people are all posed from the bottom up, looking at just one person in interaction
with a machine. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe algorithmic management as a “new sociality”—when
the partner with whom you are communicating is an algorithm— and refer to it as “a critical component of
further research on algorithmic management” (p. 16). When the interaction is treated as a one-to-one
between a person and an algorithm, the other human is completely invisible. The partner is the algorithm
itself rather than the colleague, customer or driver whose behavior the algorithm is scoring. Inside
*Organization Theory*, these two conversations have remained largely
disconnected; Schoeneborn et al. (2026) cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) nor Hinds and von
Krogh (2024).

You can’t work out where the responsibility goes by looking at just one corner of the triangle.
When a platform ceases to be an accountable actor, the practical burden of assigning
responsibility shifts onto the people who must continue the exchange. Schoeneborn et al. pose the puzzle in terms of major organizational wrongdoing, where it
"would be especially salient" (p. 14); the same charging and discharging run through routine exchanges in
which a person must decide, instance by instance, who answers. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) say that
“further research should study the corresponding field of communication” that is generated by the platform
model (p. 15); the people who are left to work out between themselves what the platform has just done are
the missing half of that field.

Building on organizational theory and models of Human-Machine Communication and AI literacy, I would argue
that the current frameworks are attempting to force a reading and writing model of competence onto a
problem that literacy simply cannot solve. I’m looking at the problem from the participant’s point of view
at the bottom of the triangle. Where Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) have “the platform operator at
the apex and producers and users at the other corners” (p. 14), I would say that the two people at the
bottom corners are dependent on each other in order for the exchange to work. To subject people to
algorithmic management without in any way enabling them to develop algorithmacy is akin to handing books
to people who can’t read. I categorize human communication abilities according to what lies between the
people using them.

First of all, this paper takes Schoeneborn et al.’s (2026) question of individual and organizational
responsibility (p. 14) and extends it to an everyday context which they don’t actually look at. When a
platform downplays its own role as an actor, the people who are caught in the middle have to assign
responsibility themselves, one instance at a time. The same adaptive ability that lets people finish
an exchange can absorb routine friction before it ever challenges the platform's declined actorhood.
Second, it provides the human participant’s side of the “corresponding field of communication” which Stark
and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 15) ask us to consider, positioning algorithmacy as a parallel form to oracy
and literacy under a single overarching rule. Third, it provides a name for the thing that Hinds and von
Krogh (2024, p. 7) refer to as team members who “blindly follow guidance from the AI”. At the same time,
the paper doesn’t resolve the very question that Schoeneborn et al. (2026) also leave hanging: do our
everyday judgments ultimately lead us to assign responsibility back to the platform or do they lead us to
absolve it (p. 14)?

## Two Sets of Open Questions About Platforms

The questions *Organization Theory* authors leave open about platforms come from two places. The
first set is asked from the apex. Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) hold that "collective actorhood
is not something given but something ascribed in communication" (p. 6) and see platforms that "rather
strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt
regulation" (p. 13). Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) find that the platform's feedback loops make it
"possible for the platform operator to deflect accountability" (p. 14), and Patriotta, Augustine and
Voronov (2025) follow Uber "refusing to accept their drivers as employees" (p. 14). Outside the journal,
Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024) review how a platform owner "can claim to merely be a conduit for a
service" (preprint p. 6) and how it might be held to account, and Buhmann et al. (2021, abstract) show
that the metaphors used to portray an organization shape how much collective actorhood individuals
attribute to it. These studies take the collective, the operator or the portrayal as their object; none
follows an outcome to the two people it reaches.

There is, however, a divergence of opinion as to what kind of entity the technology in the middle
actually is. Grodal, Ha, Hood and Rajunov (2024) pose the question: “[W]ill we come to accept that
generative AI is a machine? Or will we continue to perceive it as closer to humans […]?” (p. 8). Hsu and
Bechky (2024) argue for “a need to consider technology as an independent force or actor in the relational
network of agents that collectively influence the social construction of categories” (p. 12). Phillips,
Kalvapalle and Kennedy (2024) ask, “How does the introduction of artificial persons into an organization
change how we think about what organizations are, how they are constructed and how they function?”
(p. 11).

The second set is asked of a person and the technology. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask, “[W]hat
are individuals’ psychological responses to the new relational fabric in which they are embedded?”
(p. 11). Their questions about teams keep the colleague in view only as someone the system might
replace or guide. In both sets the second person, whose conduct the system scores and who must go on with
the exchange, has no place.

### Three Placements of the System

Although organization theory recognizes that platforms establish a three-way relationship, it has not yet
explained what practical ability an individual needs to work with another person inside that arrangement. Existing accounts of this capability remain fragmented across
communication studies, computer science, and sociology. Yet these conflicting terms point to a deeper
conceptual limitation. Each of these accounts counts fewer than three
parties. These frameworks do this by either ignoring the third party altogether
(treating the algorithmic intermediary as a neutral pass-through), or by assuming that every interaction
is a two-way exchange. That latter assumption fails to account for the binding pressure that is created
when a third party is in fact setting the rules of the two-person relationship.

The first group of scholars treats the automated system as an interactive partner, reducing the work
arrangement to a pair made up of one person and one machine. Under this view, communicative AI
technologies function as communicative subjects rather than mere interactive objects (Guzman & Lewis,
2020). The askers draw the same picture. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) see a line crossed "when
technologies are no longer merely the means of communication among parties but themselves become
communication partners" (p. 9), and in the prompt-and-answer mode Phillips et al. (2024) describe, the
system "remains, in this case, very much subordinate to the human partner" (p. 9). Research on
artificial intelligence literacy adopts this same two-party focus. A prominent framework in that field defines literacy as a checklist of concepts an individual should understand about machine learning,
without mentioning work with a second person through the software (Long & Magerko, 2020). A recent review finds that "measures of algorithmic literacy lie mostly in the cognitive dimension," stopping at "awareness or knowledge of algorithms" (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025, p. 695). While
this perspective explains how a person forms impressions of a machine, it cannot explain what happens when
that machine steps between two people who depend on each other to get work done.

The second group of scholars treats the system as a channel, viewing the arrangement as two people
connected by a digital wire. Alongside those findings, Spitzberg (2006) modeled computer-mediated
communication competence as the motivation, knowledge, and skills with which a person interacts
appropriately and effectively through digital tools. Recent scholarship extends this channel perspective
to artificial intelligence, examining how software modifies, augments, or generates messages on a
person’s behalf (Hancock et al., 2020). Researchers treat the technology as an active messenger, but it
remains a channel. That framing works well when people exchange emails or text messages, but it fails in
organizational platforms where the software does not simply deliver words. In algorithmic management, the
system evaluates the exchange, scores the participants, and enforces rules that neither person can readily dispute.

Finally, scholars who study work on platforms treat the system as something workers must understand and
adapt to. Jarrahi and Sutherland
(2019) have proposed “algorithmic competency” for workers’ understanding of the algorithms that assign
and assess their work and their learning to work with and around them. On that basis, Zhou et al. (2025)
have built and tested a questionnaire that measures workers’ “understanding of platform algorithms that
assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms” (p. 2).
Compared with the first two placements, this one clearly accounts for the system’s power, treating it as
something that assigns and evaluates work. However, Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) do not carry the client
into the construct, although clients appear throughout their interviews. While their workers watch clients
and keep records in case of a dispute, their figure has two nodes, the worker and the algorithm, and the
construct covers the same two.

Communication research has begun to count a third. Ohme et al. (2026) model platform communication
by "shifting from a dyadic to a triadic communication model of content flow" (adv. p. 1; VoR page owed),
but their third "provides a service for each or both" of the others and may "be bypassed" (adv. pp. 5, 6).
The third at issue here decides between the two and cannot be bypassed without cost. None of the three
placements, and not the serving third, puts the system above two people who depend on each other.

## The Platform as a Third That Decides

Each of the three placements in the previous section counts two parties: a person and a partner, two
people and a channel, a worker and an opponent. Organizing on platforms has three. This dilemma reflects
a basic sociological principle: group size dictates social form (Simmel, 1902a). Adding a third participant
fundamentally alters an interaction's structure rather than simply increasing the headcount (Simmel,
1902b).

### The Platform Form as a Triadic Architecture

Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) draw the platform as a triangle: "a triangular structure (Vallas &
Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners" (p. 14).
The triangle describes a form, not a firm. For Stark and Vanden Broeck an organizational form "is not
co-terminous with that of a company" (p. 7), and the form reaches people who never sign up as gig workers:
"Many professionals encounter algorithmic management as users" (p. 9). Generative AI brought into a firm
imports the same triadic architecture. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe a manager who uses it to
evaluate employees, where "new relations and functions may cascade between managers, GenAI, and
employees" (p. 4); the system sits where the operator sat, between two people whose work depends on each
other. The apex decides through systems that learn (pp. 4, 6) and produce "a probabilistic rendering" of
what they have seen (Cornelissen et al., 2024, p. 6), and it can hide human work as well: the vetting that lets Emily, in Gray and Suri's example (as cited in Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 10), meet her driver is done by someone "entirely invisible to both Uber customers
and drivers" (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 11). The people at the lower corners need not decide whether
the apex is code, staff or both. Workers, Bujold et al. (2026) argue, "ultimately recognize the
organization (not the algorithm) as the exchange partner" (p. 15).

### Mediator, Moderator, and a Third That Decides

To demonstrate how a third party transforms social relationships, Simmel contrasted a mediator with an
arbitrator:

> *So long as the third party works as a real mediator, the ending of the conflict rests finally in the
> hands of the parties themselves. By choice of the arbitrator they have put this ultimate decision out of
> their own hands.* (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)

While a mediator helps two parties reach an agreement, the participants retain final control over their
relationship. With an arbitrator, by contrast, the participants hand that authority over and the third
party settles the outcome (Simmel, 1902b). In written communication, physical texts serve as moderators
that preserve statements across space and time while leaving open how those words should be interpreted
(Olson, 1994). For example, in performance appraisals, a standardized review form structures what a
manager and an employee can discuss during an evaluation (Lehtinen & Pälli, 2021). Although text
moderates the conversation, a human supervisor still makes the final judgment and can be directly
questioned by the employee. The difference between a moderator and a third party hinges on who holds the
verdict, following Simmel's (1902b) contrast between a mediator and an arbitrator.

An algorithmic platform moves even further from neutral mediation because neither participant selects the
arbiter, and the platform operator, which sits at the apex of a triangle with producers and users (Stark &
Vanden Broeck, 2024), benefits from the exchange. Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024)
have already read this position through Simmel, as "the third who rejoices" (preprint p. 6; VoR page owed);
their concern is how the platform owner can be held to account, and the concern here is what the two
people below it must be able to do. Yet classifying communication strictly by transmission channel
overlooks whether the technology evaluates and binds the participants. When an automated system steps
between two people and sets the terms of their exchange, it stops being a neutral transmission medium and
becomes an automated arbitrator.

### Two Features of the Triad

Two features of this arrangement are its normal condition, not exceptions to it. The first is
outcome interdependence within a single mediated exchange. Each person's outcome depends on the other's
conduct as the platform reads it: the provider's rank depends on the rater's rating, and what the rater
gets depends on the provider's conduct. The interdependence holds whether or not the two ever meet again.
The second is asymmetric binding. The platform binds a participant whenever overriding or bypassing its
output carries a practical cost, whether a penalty, a burden of justification or lost standing, and it
need not bind both participants equally. On eBay the rating costs the rater little and binds the provider
through rank (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Inside the firm, a recommendation from the system binds
de facto wherever setting it aside has to be explained. Neither feature requires a lasting relationship or
a symmetric one. 

### Oracy, Literacy, and Algorithmacy

Sorting communicative abilities by what stands between people turns on one question: can what stands
between render a verdict? Distinguishing
communicative abilities therefore requires examining how the intervening medium acts upon the people it
connects. Traditional media sociology classified communication into face-to-face interaction, mediated
interaction, and mediated quasi-interaction based on technical channels and audience scope (Thompson,
1995). Later scholarship updated this taxonomy to account for networked digital environments (Thompson,
2020).

Oracy is the ability to carry out an exchange directly face to face via speaking and hearing, or via physical gesture
and response. A chain of spoken messages still passes from person to person, so the chain stays
oral. Literacy is the ability to read and write texts that carry two-person exchanges that
are separated in time and space. This text-based coordination transformed modern organizations between the
late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, as growing enterprises replaced personal oversight with
memos, standard forms, and filing systems (Yates, 1989). Yet even in a text-moderated relationship, a
human actor still interprets the page and delivers the evaluation. A text is static and can be
inspected; it cannot change its own rule or render a verdict. Algorithmacy belongs to the triad, where an
opaque and adaptive third renders the verdict and the two people cannot easily go around it. An
algorithmic platform departs from this history by placing an automated third party between participants
that evaluates their interaction and enforces binding outcomes.

Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) ask for this comparison. Like Merton and Lazarsfeld, they write,
"further research should study the corresponding field of communication," but "in our case,
organizational form and communication field are conjoined" (p. 15); "Like Yates, we study how organization
and communication are intertwined" (p. 16). Literacy was the person's part of the communication field
that went with bureaucracy. Algorithmacy is the person's part of the field that goes with the platform.
Table 1 sets the three side by side.

**Table 1. Three communicative abilities, by what stands between the people who use them**

| | Oracy | Literacy | Algorithmacy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form of relation | Direct dyad | Mediated dyad | Irreducible triad |
| What stands between | A shared physical or vocal channel | A static, inspectable text | An opaque, adaptive intermediary |
| Who holds the verdict | The two participants | The two participants, or a named human authority | The intermediary, or a person bound by its scoring |
| Can the two bypass it? | Not applicable: nothing stands between | Yes, by speaking directly or revising the text | No, or only at a cost |
| Primary demand on the person | Speaking, listening, adjusting in real time | Reading, writing, interpreting fixed records | Diagnosing cause, writing for two audiences, tracking a shifting rule |
| Characteristic failure | Mishearing; loss of shared presence | Misreading; missing context | Collapsing into compliance with the system, or treating it as a neutral channel |

## Algorithmacy: What the People at the Corners Must Be Able to Do

Once the third holds the verdict, three properties follow in practice: it is opaque, it binds, and it
adapts. Each property makes a demand on the people at the lower corners, and algorithmacy is the name for
meeting all three. I define it as the ability to carry out an interdependent exchange with another person
through an intermediary that is opaque, binding, and adaptive. Opacity calls for interpreting, binding for
specifying intent, and adaptation for keeping track. Working under an algorithmic intermediary does not
mean facing a simple absence of information. Instead, workers confront equivocality, dealing with
confusing data that supports multiple, conflicting explanations (Daft & Lengel, 1986). A participant
receives an evaluation, observes what the counterpart did, and keeps her own work record, yet several
explanations fit, and no supervisor clarifies the outcome. The askers' own cases show the three
demands, and I take them in turn, each on the case that shows it most plainly, before following one case
through all three.

### Interpreting: Finding What Caused an Outcome

The first challenge requires interpreting ambiguous outcomes to identify their underlying cause. Because
the extent to which each party affects an opaque evaluation score is unspecified, an unexpected outcome
may stem from the algorithmic formula, the counterpart's behavior, or both (Rahman, 2021). Hinds and
von Krogh (2024) give the case inside the firm. When generative AI is "used by managers to augment their
ability to motivate and evaluate employees, such as determining job assignments or pay increases, new
relations and functions may cascade between managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4). An employee who is
passed over for an assignment cannot tell from the outcome whether the manager judged her work, the
system scored it, or the manager adopted the system's score. The manager may not know either, since the
system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making
decisions" (p. 4). Neither person faces a missing fact. Each faces an outcome with three possible authors.

The same demand appears on the platforms Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe, where ratings
"circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped feedback loops" (p. 14). In online
marketplaces, an independent seller whose search visibility drops only sees a buyer's review score,
leaving the platform's hidden weighting formula concealed (Curchod et al., 2020). Deducing
the cause of a score determines how the worker responds. If the customer was dissatisfied, the worker can
improve service delivery; if the platform updated its formula, the worker must change how she interacts
with the software. Disentangling these causes is complicated because people evaluate an identical
interaction differently when they attribute it to a computer rather than to a person (Sundar & Nass,
2000). Interpreting, as I use the term, settles what caused an outcome. It does not settle who
answers for it; that question belongs to the next section, where the two come apart.

### Specifying Intent: Making It Survive the Platform

The second challenge requires specifying intent so that a human counterpart can understand what an action
meant despite algorithmic filtering. Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) rater shows why. "When a user
rates a provider on eBay, for example, she is not acting as a subordinate to whom eBay has delegated
authority. The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). Her rating is
addressed to the provider and to the buyers who come after her, but the platform reads it into a ranking
she does not set, with consequences for the provider she cannot see. What she means and what the platform
does with it can part company. The binding here is asymmetric: the rating costs her little, and it binds
the provider through rank. Specifying intent departs from conventional software prompting because the
message must satisfy two different audiences at once: a human partner seeking genuine collaboration and an
automated system scoring metric compliance.

The provider's side of the same exchange has the same structure. Justesen and Plesner (2024)
describe workers who must "'swallow their negative emotions both during interactions with clients, but
also in replying to negative reviews'" (p. 15, quoting Bucher et al., 2021, p. 57). A reply to a review is
written to the client and read by the system that ranks the business. In direct conversation, two
people establish common understanding through immediate verbal and nonverbal feedback (Clark & Brennan,
1991). Machines, by contrast, capture only thin digital traces of situated human actions (Suchman, 2007).
Communicating through an intermediary therefore requires writing that survives algorithmic filtering.
Where evaluative pressure is asymmetric, the party facing greater scrutiny bears the burden of specifying
intent.

### Keeping Track: A Rule That Changes Without Notice

The third challenge requires tracking an evaluation rule that changes continuously without formal notice.
Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) hiring case puts it plainly. "If a manager is using GenAI for hiring …
the AI would likely continually improve on its ability to select promising candidates. Over time as
managers and applicants interact with the system, the evaluation algorithm may become increasingly refined
and inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). The rule changes because it is used. "The way GenAI learns and
generates insights are functions not likely to be well defined or transparent" (p. 6), so no notice of the
change arrives; the people using the system learn of it, if at all, from what it starts to do differently.
With such systems, Hinds and von Krogh write, "rather than being fixed, these functions and relations are
likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4). The rule can move within a single exchange, not only
between versions. Platform operators rarely publish updates, forcing workers to infer changes entirely
from shifts in performance patterns. Tracking differs from interpreting a single score; it requires sensing
broader patterns across multiple counterparts over long stretches of time. Social media users, for
instance, notice algorithmic change through shifts in content distribution patterns and in the engagement
their posts receive (DeVito, 2021).

### The Three Parts as a Cycle

The three parts are separable in principle and joined in practice. Follow the provider in Stark and
Vanden Broeck's (2024) case through one ongoing exchange; the sequence is my reasoning on their case, not
their report. The provider's ranking drops after a sale. She asks whether the buyer's rating, the
platform's weighting or both moved it: interpreting. She revises her listing so that it speaks to the next
buyer and still scores well with the platform: specifying intent. She watches the rankings that follow, to
learn whether the weighting itself has changed: keeping track. What she learns at the third step changes
what she will read at the first step of the next exchange. Competence in this environment develops as a participant's understanding of the work changes (Sandberg, 2000).
Yet over time, direct experience lets workers anticipate automated patterns. Delivery workers with long tenure rely less on the platform's order recommendations (Jiang & Sinchaisri, 2025).

Turns at the cycle build the ability, and people with more turns may hold more of it than others in
the same formal position. The hardest case is the pair that meets once. TripAdvisor's reviewers are
"unseen and unnamed adversaries who run away immediately and have no stake in the future" (Orlikowski &
Scott, 2014, AAM p. 42; VoR page owed), and food-delivery customers "were unlikely to meet the same driver
again" (Chong et al., 2024, p. 10). Interdependence holds within the one exchange (§3), but nothing learned
about this counterpart carries to the next. What carries over is learned about the platform. `[Figure 1
here: the triangle, with the cycle of the three parts drawn between the two lower corners.]`

### Two Ways to Fail

Workers fail to navigate these demands when they mistake the three-way relationship for a two-way
exchange. The first failure mode occurs when a worker collapses the triad into a pair of herself and the
machine, treating the software as the only thing that matters and viewing the counterpart as mere noise.
Hinds and von Krogh (2024) name the risk for teams. Some of the medical teams Mayo (2022, as cited in Hinds & von Krogh, 2024) studied showed "emergent interdependence," which "was organic and associated with better patient outcomes. With the introduction of
GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members blindly follow guidance from the
AI?" (p. 7). Guidance binds de facto wherever setting it aside carries a cost (§3), and a member who
follows it blindly has let the colleague drop out of the exchange. The askers' own picture of the
technology invites this collapse. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) see a line crossed "when technologies are
no longer merely the means of communication among parties but themselves become communication partners"
(p. 9), and Phillips, Kalvapalle and Kennedy (2024) describe a mode in which the system "remains, in this
case, very much subordinate to the human partner" (p. 9). Each picture has two parties, a person and the system, and the collapse leaves the other person out of it.

The second failure mode occurs when participants treat the platform as a neutral channel between two
people, ignoring the evaluating software. This is the other half of Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024)
contrast: the technology as "merely the means of communication among parties" (p. 9). In two-sided
review systems, buyers and sellers often trade positive reviews, and buyers hold back negative ones to
avoid seller retaliation (Bolton et al., 2013). Participants then treat ratings as personal compliments
rather than evaluative data. It also blinds participants to how the platform aggregates their reviews.
Treating the software as a transparent channel leaves that basis unexamined. In both failure modes,
participants lose their footing by focusing on one half of the triad and ignoring the other. Both
failures leave the cause of an outcome misread, and a misread cause is where misplaced responsibility
begins, which the next section takes up.

## Where Declined Responsibility Comes to Rest

The two failures in the previous section misplace responsibility; the ability itself may do
something less visible. The same adaptive ability that lets people finish an exchange can absorb routine
friction before it ever challenges the platform's declined actorhood. This section traces both paths and
returns to Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl's (2026) question about individual and collective
responsibility.

### Readings Come Before Any Voiced Attribution

For Schoeneborn et al., "collective actorhood is not something given but something ascribed in
communication" (p. 6), and the practices that ascribe it are "recurrent instances of communication"
(p. 9). Before anyone voices that the platform, the algorithm or the other person answers for an outcome,
someone has read the outcome. Interpreting, as the previous section defines it, produces that reading.
The reading is not itself an attribution practice: it may never be said aloud, and it need not recur.
It is where attribution among the platform, its algorithm and the counterpart is still undecided, and
what a person can do with an outcome depends on how it is settled there.

### When the Reading Fails, Responsibility Is Misplaced

Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe what the platform gains from the arrangement. Ratings and
rankings "circulate among them and the platform operator in peculiarly shaped feedback loops. Their
twisted character makes it possible for the platform operator to deflect accountability" (p. 14). What is
deflected does not vanish. When an automated system enters the exchange, it breaks the normal chains
of accountability by shifting the blame onto the people doing the work (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark &
Vanden Broeck, 2024). Where a person cannot tell the platform's part from the counterpart's
conduct, the blame for what the system did lands on the counterpart. Lee et al.'s (2015) drivers report
that passengers "misattributed system faults and negative experiences that drivers could not control to
drivers themselves, which in turn resulted in lower ratings" (p. 1608), and a food-delivery customer in
Chong et al. (2024) tipped his driver less although the order was, as he put it later, "obviously more of
the app's fault than the driver's fault" (p. 7). The frontline worker then becomes the moral crumple
zone (Elish, 2019) in exactly the same way that the front of a car is designed to crumple and absorb the
impact of a crash to protect the rest of the car. The worker absorbs the blame and the penalties when the
automated system fails.

The direction is not fixed, and finding the cause does not settle who answers. Fu et al. (2026,
abstract) describe consumers who "transfer responsibility for service failures to a platform, even after
recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem." Zheng and Zhu (2026, abstract) find
consumers turning against the platform after a worker-caused failure when dispatch is framed as
algorithm-directed, an effect "mediated by responsibility attribution to the platform." Responsibility
moves with what each person can see. The same deflection runs inside the firm. Patriotta, Augustine and
Voronov (2025) recount how "thousands of employees of the UK Post Office were falsely accused of stealing
from local branch accounts over the course of a decade" before "it became clear that the Post Office's IT
system, called Horizon, was at fault" (p. 11); the organization's first answer was that "Horizon is an
extremely robust system" (p. 11). There the employer, not a platform, stood at the apex, and the people
at the corner could not show the cause.

### When the Reading Succeeds, the Friction Is Absorbed

The ability has a second effect, which the paper proposes and does not demonstrate. As the
worker develops their algorithmacy in order to cope with the pressures of the work, that very adaptation
of the worker may serve to keep the platform's opaque authority going. Cameron (2024) shows a
related effect for consent rather than competence: a system "in which individuals have circumscribed
choices while working is crucial to keeping the platform system running smoothly, because workers can
invest in their work and develop a sense of mastery" (p. 494), while platform owners "hide behind an
opaque management system" (p. 493). A person who reads an outcome correctly, specifies intent so that it
survives and keeps track of the changing rule carries out work that Justesen and Plesner (2024) would
count among the "unintended tasks associated with digitalization" (p. 22), and one who cannot may suffer
what Hinds and von Krogh (2024) call "a loss of orientation" (p. 11). I conjecture that competent
absorption keeps routine breakdowns from surfacing as the public cases in which Schoeneborn et al. expect
their question to be "especially salient" (p. 14). Horizon is a caution against reading this as an
explanation: there the misplacement ran for a decade before it surfaced. The conjecture is a question for
research, not a finding.

### What This Answers

Schoeneborn et al. (2026) ask, "What does the recurrent charging or discharging of a collective
social address with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective responsibility?" (p. 14). At
the level this paper works, between the individual and the collective sits the exchange. When a platform
downplays its actorhood, the burden of assigning responsibility is carried there, by two people, one
outcome at a time, and how well they carry it depends on algorithmacy. Whether their readings, once
voiced, charge the platform with actorhood or discharge it stays open, as Schoeneborn et al. leave open
"whether these always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary depending on the particular
context" (p. 14).

## Discussion

### Three Contributions to Questions Left Open

The paper set out to answer, in part, three questions that *Organization Theory* authors left open,
and it takes them in the order the introduction gave them. The first is Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl's
(2026) question about what charging or discharging a collective with actorhood "impl[ies] for individual
as opposed to collective responsibility" (p. 14). Their examples run from the collective to a named
individual, the scapegoat or the heroized chief executive (p. 11). On a platform the line runs from the
platform to whichever of two people an outcome is pinned on, and it is drawn one exchange at a time. The
paper places the burden of assigning responsibility in that exchange and names the ability on which
carrying it depends. It does not supply an attribution practice in their sense, and it leaves open, as
they do, whether the readings it describes charge the platform or discharge it.

The second is Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) call to study "the corresponding field of
communication" for the platform form (p. 15). The paper supplies the person's part of that field by
sorting communicative abilities on one criterion, what stands between the people who use them (Table 1).
The boundary between these organizational forms depends on who holds the final say over joint work. When
an inscribed document moderates an interaction, human participants retain ultimate authority to interpret
the text and resolve disputes between themselves. When an algorithmic platform arbitrates the
relationship, the software extracts supervisory power and issues binding outcomes that neither participant can readily overturn. The comparison does not claim that the triad, or a third that decides, is new; Simmel
drew the arbitrator, and Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024) have read the platform through him. What
the comparison adds is the ability that goes with the form.

The third is Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) question whether "team members blindly follow guidance
from the AI" (p. 7). Blind following is the first failure in §4: the member collapses the triad into a pair
of herself and the machine, and the colleague drops out. Algorithmacy names what such a member lacks.
This structural distinction clarifies what prior accounts of platform labor leave out. While pioneering
models of algorithmic competency examined how workers navigate automated task allocation, they framed the relationship as a struggle between an individual worker and software (Jarrahi & Sutherland,
2019). The triadic framework brings the counterpart back into view, showing that workers do not simply
manage a machine; they work with clients, peers, and supervisors through an intermediary that
evaluates them.

Adopting a triadic standard does not discard established findings on how individuals adapt to automated
systems. Instead, it places those findings into a coherent relational framework. Sociological research
shows that public rankings induce reactivity, forcing organizations and individuals to alter their
behavior to satisfy metric evaluations (Espeland & Sauder, 2007). In platform environments, this
reactivity changes form: workers who cannot identify opaque evaluation criteria either keep experimenting
with their routines or limit their activity on the platform (Rahman, 2021). Through a triadic lens, these
behaviors are no longer isolated coping tactics. They become ways of meeting the three demands, and
their failures become the two ways of losing the counterpart. Moving from dyads to triads reconciles two
traditions in organizational research that have largely operated in isolation. Research on algorithms at
work has documented how algorithmic control operates, tracking how employers use automated systems to
direct, evaluate, and discipline workers (Kellogg et al., 2020). Conversely, communication research has
studied how individuals present themselves, form impressions, and maintain relationships across digital
media (see Hancock et al., 2020).

### Conditions

Because §3 defines the triad by outcome interdependence within one exchange and by asymmetric
binding, two conditions that might have limited the argument are part of it: the pair that meets once,
and the platform that binds one side harder than the other. Two others remain. Systems that permit human adjustment may reduce the competence required of frontline staff: when the advised rating is low, managers allowed to adjust how the algorithm computes it use the algorithm more (Lin et al., 2026, abstract). Where a
person can change the verdict, the third is a moderator again, and literacy may suffice. Platforms often
enforce asymmetric governance, binding one participant with strict performance metrics while giving the
other considerable latitude (Curchod et al., 2020). Research should determine whether the burden of
algorithmacy inevitably falls on the party facing the greatest performance scrutiny, or whether
unconstrained participants also adapt their communication to protect collaborative relationships.

### Deskilling and the Demand for Algorithmacy

The argument suggests a proposition about skill that the paper states and does not test. Classic labor process theory established that managerial control systematically strips craft knowledge from manual tasks to discipline labor (Braverman, 1974). Gandini (2019) carries labor process theory over to gig platforms, and platform organizing extends this dynamic by partitioning work into simple tasks that are algorithmically assigned (Faraj & Pachidi, 2021). Yet treating algorithmic management solely as task deskilling misses a crucial organizational paradox. As an automated system simplifies physical or administrative tasks, it may increase the relational demands of the triadic relationship. The less craft skill a task requires, the more a worker’s standing depends on her ability to interpret opaque verdicts, specify intent through algorithmic filters, and track silent rule changes. Task deskilling and the demand for algorithmacy are two sides of the same organizational arrangement. If the proposition holds, the same arrangement that strips skill from the task asks more of the person at each corner, which is the third research question below.

### Generative AI: The Same Form, a Faster Tempo

While early platform research focused on gig work and digital retail, generative artificial intelligence
is now entering professional work (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024), which carries triadic governance beyond those
settings. When generative models screen job candidates, evaluate performance reviews, or mediate peer
collaboration, they act as automated arbiters between people. These are the cases §4 took from Hinds
and von Krogh: the manager who evaluates employees with generative AI (p. 4) and the hiring system that
grows "inaccessible to the manager" (p. 6). In Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) terms, the platform form
arrives inside the firm, where "Many professionals encounter algorithmic management as users" (p. 9).
Generative AI doesn't change this platform form; it simply scales it. It's like a printing
press that takes the automated control that already exists in the gig economy and pushes it into the
white-collar office. As learning systems update their rules inside ongoing interactions (Hinds &
von Krogh, 2024, p. 4), the basic three-way structure holds, but keeping track of how the third party
behaves becomes a continuous demand. What learning changes is the tempo: "rather than being fixed,
these functions and relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024,
p. 4), so the third part of algorithmacy, keeping track, is asked of people within one exchange rather
than between versions. 

### Questions for Research

Five questions follow, each from a part of the argument.

First, do people's readings, once voiced, charge the platform with actorhood or discharge it, and
does the direction depend on what the interface lets them see? Schoeneborn et al. (2026) ask whether
attribution practices "always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary depending on the
particular context" (p. 14), and service research already finds the direction varying (Fu et al., 2026,
abstract).

Second, who holds algorithmacy, and who exercises it for others? When individuals lack sufficient
experience to navigate this cycle alone, they rely on social and organizational buffers. Frontline
employees who show citizens "how to navigate digital systems" (Justesen & Plesner, 2024, p. 13) exercise
the ability on someone else's behalf, inside the triad.

Third, does the ability grow with turns at the cycle, and is it new? Organizational scholars should
investigate whether individuals with higher algorithmacy achieve better outcomes over time, or whether
structural platform dependency limits the effectiveness of individual competence. Scholars should test
whether algorithmacy is a novel capability or whether it builds on established interpersonal communication
skills and general digital literacy.

Fourth, how do the three parts change when the third is generative AI inside the firm? Future
research should examine how enterprises deploy these systems, whether managers verify that employees have
the algorithmacy needed to navigate them, and how workers sustain their exchanges when an automated third
party evaluates their professional relationships.

Fifth, does competent absorption keep routine breakdowns from becoming public? §5 proposed that it
may; whether it does is an empirical question, and the Horizon case shows that a misplacement can run for
years before it surfaces (Patriotta et al., 2025, p. 11).

## Conclusion

*Organization Theory* authors have asked who acts and who answers when a platform decides between
people, and they have asked what happens to the people in the middle; they have not yet asked the two
questions as one. This paper has. When the party that decides between two people does not present itself
as an actor, the burden of assigning responsibility shifts onto the people who must continue the exchange,
and what they can do with it depends on an ability this paper calls algorithmacy: interpreting what caused
an outcome, specifying intent so that it survives the platform, and keeping track of a rule that changes
without notice. Literacy was the person's part of the communication field that went with bureaucracy;
algorithmacy is the person's part of the field that goes with the platform. Treating the intermediary as
an active party preserves the reality of algorithmic power without losing sight of the human counterpart on
the other side of the exchange. We can't read our way out of or into that condition, just as we can't
listen to a book.
