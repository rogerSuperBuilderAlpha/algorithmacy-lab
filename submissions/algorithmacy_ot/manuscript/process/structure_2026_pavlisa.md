# Structure map: Pavlisa, Harvey & Doh (2026)

**How I measured.** I read the article in full from the PDF (25 printed pages; body pp. 2–20, references pp. 21–25). I set paragraph boundaries from the typeset first-line indents (line coordinates from `pdftotext -bbox-layout`; a 12-pt indent opens a paragraph, and the first paragraph under a heading sits flush left), so column and page breaks do not split paragraphs. I took heading levels from the fonts (`pdftohtml -xml`). Word counts come from a script over the de-hyphenated paragraph text and are good to within a few words. "Cites" counts author–year mentions (a parenthesis listing four works counts four), with stray calendar years and the double year in "2011[2005]" removed by hand; treat these as rough.

## 1. Bibliographic line and front matter

- **As printed:** Karina Pavlisa, William S. Harvey, and Jonathan Doh. Stakeholder Governance and Inequality: A Theory of Reciprocal Exchange. *Organization Theory*, Volume 7: 1–25. © The Author(s) 2026. DOI 10.1177/26317877261456299. Open access, CC BY 4.0.
- **Article type label:** "Theory Article" (italic, above the title).
- **Abstract:** 192 words, one paragraph, unstructured, seven sentences. Order: phenomenon, what the literature does and omits, the gap as a missing mechanism, "We argue" diagnosis, "Using Pierre Bourdieu's concepts ... we theorise" move, a second "We argue" stating the claim, and a last sentence on practical use.
- **Keywords:** 10, alphabetical, lower case except the proper name.
- **Running heads** carry "Pavlisa et al." (odd pages) and "Organization Theory" (even pages).

## 3. Devices inventory

**Tables (1).**
- "Table 1. Bourdieu's Concepts Applied to Firm–Stakeholder Exchange Relationships and Stakeholder Governance." Page 8, a full page set landscape. It has two stacked panels. The upper panel, "Concept definition", gives one-line definitions of eight terms (economic, social, cultural and symbolic capital; habitus; field; economic field; homologous fields). The lower panel, "Concept application to stakeholder governance", restates five of them (capitals, habitus, field, economic field, homologous fields) for firm–stakeholder exchange. It belongs to subsection 3.1 and the text calls it out once (second paragraph of 3.1).

**Figures (2).**
- "Figure 1. Capital distribution, stakeholder disadvantage and inequality in the context of stakeholder governance." Page 10, full width at the top of the page. A box-and-arrow cycle: Stakeholder governance → Capital re-distribution → Stakeholder advantage or disadvantage → Societal economic inequality → a dashed feedback arrow back to Stakeholder governance. A fifth box, "Field conditionings" (capital convertibility, capital weightings), feeds into the link between redistribution and advantage. It sits in 3.1, which introduces it in its sixth paragraph and walks through it in its seventh. The text calls it out five times, and each later subsection points back to the box it treats.
- "Figure 2. Illustration of capital redistribution and stakeholder disadvantage in the context of Rio Tinto." Page 16, full width at the top of the page, printed above the Discussion heading. It is Figure 1 redrawn with the Rio Tinto examples written into each box. The text calls it out once, in the third paragraph of 3.5.

**Propositions, hypotheses, research questions.** No numbered propositions and no hypotheses. One research question, unnumbered, stated in a run-in sentence at the end of Introduction paragraph 3 (p. 2). One named "principle" (reciprocal exchange) stated in prose in 3.4; it is not set off or numbered.

**Cases and examples that run more than two sentences.** The paper has one running case and nothing else of length.

- **Rio Tinto** — a real, named company. The authors assemble the case from published secondary sources: a 2011 case study co-authored by the second author (Olegario, Harvey & Mueller), an NGO resource page, press reports and an archaeology article. It is not original data and the paper does not say how the material was selected. The case appears only inside the theory section and runs to about 930 words, roughly 10% of the body and 22% of the theory section. The theory section's lead-in announces it only as "real company examples". Episodes:
  - 3.2, paragraph 2 (p. 11), about 200 words: what capitals the firm must hold to lead in mining and keep its licence to operate.
  - 3.2, paragraph 3 (p. 11), about 65 words: how the firm converts one capital into another in a cycle.
  - 3.3, paragraphs 2–3 (pp. 12–13), about 315 words: the Madagascar biodiversity partnership with Kew and the Smithsonian, read first as what the firm gained and then as what each partner gained (positive reciprocity).
  - 3.3, paragraph 4 (p. 13), about 65 words: water contamination and the resulting loss of reputation (negative reciprocity).
  - 3.3, paragraph 5 (p. 13), about 160 words: the destruction of Juukan Gorge in 2020 and the retaliation that followed.
  - 3.4, paragraph 4 (p. 14), about 120 words: Juukan Gorge again, now the 2025 co-management agreement, used to show weighting and convertibility.
  - 3.5, paragraph 3 (p. 15): one sentence handing all of the above to Figure 2.
  The case recurs in three consecutive subsections and in a figure, and one episode (Juukan Gorge) is used twice for two different points. It does not appear in the Introduction, the Literature Review, the Discussion or the Conclusion.
- **Short examples (one or two sentences each, none recurring).** BlackRock Foundation's clean-energy pledge (2.1, 47 words, real, from the foundation's website); ecologically inefficient production by global firms (2.1, 48 words, generic, from the literature); employees investing human capital (2.3, 52 words, generic); a firm's technological and commercial capital (3.1, 39 words, generic); a government's four-capital framework from Schwab and Vanham (3.2, 89 words, published). The paper uses "For example" or "For instance" 14 times in the body.
- **Hypothetical cases or vignettes:** none.

**Block quotations.** None. Seven quotations of a sentence or so run in the text with page numbers (two in 2.1, two in 2.2, two in 3.1, one in the Discussion).

**Footnotes and endnotes.** None. The only superscripts are the author affiliation marks on page 1.

- **Roadmap paragraph:** none. The Introduction ends on the contribution statement and the reader meets the section order only through the headings. Roadmaps appear later and locally: the last paragraph of 2.1 previews "the next section", and the second lead-in paragraph of section 3 and the last paragraph of 3.1 preview the theory section.
- **Contribution statement:** yes, paragraph 6, four numbered contributions in one paragraph. The Discussion restates the same four at length.
- **Statement of the approach to theorizing:** partial. Paragraphs 4 and 5 name the lens and the literatures combined, but the Introduction says nothing about the kind of theorizing or about the use of a case. The nearest statement is the 56-word second lead-in paragraph of section 3 (p. 7), which says the theorizing uses a Bourdieusian lens and real company examples.
- **Where the central term is first defined:** not in the Introduction. "Stakeholder governance" is glossed in passing in paragraph 2 and "endowments" in a parenthesis there. "Reciprocal exchange" first appears in paragraph 6 inside the contribution list, undefined; the Literature Review defines it as a form of exchange in 2.2 (p. 5), and the paper's own principle of reciprocal exchange is defined only in the first paragraph of 3.4 (p. 14), about 6,100 words into a 9,600-word body.

## 5. How the core argument is built

**Lead-in (2 paragraphs, 180 words, p. 7).** The first paragraph bridges from the review by naming three ways, lettered (a) to (c), in which the analysis can be improved. The second states the approach in one sentence and lists what the section will explain.

**3.1 sets up the apparatus (7 paragraphs, 1,059 words, pp. 7–10).** This subsection defines the borrowed concepts and assembles them into the framework; Table 1 and Figure 1 both sit here.
1. Gives the warrant for bringing Bourdieu to stakeholder governance.
2. Presents the economy as a system of fields structured by four capitals, points to Table 1, and treats the firm as a field in its own right.
3. Defines accumulation and capitals-based power, with a brief generic example of a firm's kinds of cultural capital.
4. Treats the volume and structure of capital as strategic assets and introduces convertibility.
5. Shows that the value of each capital varies by field, and names this "weighting".
6. Introduces homologous fields and cross-field exchange, lists the ideas the theory rests on, and introduces Figure 1.
7. Walks through Figure 1 box by box and says the subsections that follow track its dimensions.

**3.2 to 3.5 follow the figure: one subsection per element of Figure 1.**
- 3.2 (5 paragraphs, 843 words): accumulation, the motive that drives the cycle.
- 3.3 (6 paragraphs, 1,058 words): the "Capital re-distribution" box.
- 3.4 (5 paragraphs, 688 words): the "Field conditionings" box and the step to advantage or disadvantage. The paper's named principle is defined here.
- 3.5 (4 paragraphs, 470 words): the "Societal economic inequality" box and the feedback arrow.

**The named principle is defined in 3.4, in five paragraphs.** (1) States the principle of fair reciprocal exchange in one sentence and casts firm and stakeholders as investors in and recipients of capital. (2) Maps what a firm gives onto the four capitals and says the receiving field sets their value. (3) States the fairness condition as equal value perceived by both sides, while the mix of capitals exchanged may differ. (4) Shows weighting and convertibility at work in the Juukan Gorge settlement. (5) Places weighting and convertibility inside the wider mechanism and states the outcome: inadequate or poorly convertible capital slows a stakeholder's accumulation and produces disadvantage.

**Internal order.** Subsections 3.2 to 3.4 share a loose order and do not follow a fixed template. Each opens on an abstract claim tied to stakeholder theory and Bourdieu, moves to one or more Rio Tinto episodes, restates the episode in the vocabulary of capitals, and closes on a summary or hand-off (3.3 ends on a paragraph that opens "To summarise"). Subsection 3.5 breaks the order: it brings no new episode, generalizes from the mechanism to society, sends the reader to Figure 2 for the collected examples, and ends on the feedback loop. The subsections shorten as they go (843, 1,058, 688, 470 words), and citation density falls with them.

## 6. How the paper ends

**Last two level-1 sections:** "Discussion" (14 paragraphs, 2,087 words) and "Conclusion" (2 paragraphs, 258 words).

**Discussion.** It has one subsection, "Managerial implications", placed last. The 12 paragraphs before it run in this order:
- 1–2: re-situate the paper in the debate over stakeholder capitalism and restate the argument.
- 3–8: the four contributions, opened by a sentence announcing four and marked First, Second, Third, Finally. The first and third take two paragraphs each; the second and fourth take one.
- 9: compares the lens with institutional theory.
- 10–11: limitations, opened with the words "As a limitation". The paragraphs name the assumptions the theory inherits (moral and rational actors) and two ways those assumptions can fail.
- 12: reads those limits as boundary conditions and points to wider Bourdieusian analysis of fields.

**Limitations:** yes, about 290 words in two paragraphs inside the Discussion, with no heading of their own.
**Future research:** no section and no list of questions. The twelfth Discussion paragraph gestures at further development, and the last sentence of the Conclusion hopes for future conceptual, empirical and practical research.
**Practical implications:** yes, the headed subsection "Managerial implications" (2 paragraphs, 285 words), and practical use is also claimed in the abstract's last sentence and the Introduction's last two sentences.

**Conclusion.** Two paragraphs with no citations. The first restates the problem and the gap. The second and final paragraph restates what the paper built, repeats the claim that the theory is a practical analytic tool, and ends on a one-sentence invitation to future research on the capitals lens and on operationalising the principle.

## 7. References, voice, citation density

- **References:** 97 entries, APA style with DOIs, pp. 21–25. They include seven non-academic sources (reports, a foundation web page, press articles).
- **Voice:** first person plural. "We" appears 34 times and "our" 18 times in the body, about 5.4 per 1,000 words. First person clusters where the authors state their own moves: the Introduction (10), the theory section's lead-in and 3.1 (9 of that section's 11), and the Discussion (26). Literature Review paragraphs are almost wholly impersonal (2), as are subsections 3.2 to 3.5. Literature is often reported with agentless constructions ("Prior literature has ...", "has been defined as"). Spelling is British, with some "-ize" forms left in.
- **Citations per paragraph (rough):**
  - Introduction: about 35 in 6 paragraphs, close to 6 per paragraph.
  - Literature Review: about 105 in 17 paragraphs, close to 6 per paragraph.
  - Theory-building section: about 62 in 29 paragraphs, close to 2 per paragraph. Subsection 3.1 runs at about 2.4, and density falls to about 1.5 by 3.5.
  - Discussion: about 78 in 14 paragraphs, between 5 and 6 per paragraph.
  - Conclusion: none.
