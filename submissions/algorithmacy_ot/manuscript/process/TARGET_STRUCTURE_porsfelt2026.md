# Target structure: Porsfelt, Vestergaard & Hjorth (2026)

*Drafted by Claude on 2026-10-08 from a full read of the PDF. Nothing here is the author's text. Verbatim material from the paper is limited to headings, captions, keywords, table headings and row names, paragraph openers, and eight short phrases in quotation marks.*

**Source.** Porsfelt, R., Vestergaard, A., & Hjorth, D. (2026). "Image, Imagination, Imaginaries: A Genealogy of Imago-Concepts in Organisational Research." *Organization Theory*, Volume 7: 1–35. DOI 10.1177/26317877261484615. Article-type label: "Theory Article". The PDF prints the volume and no issue number; "7(3)" comes from the citation supplied to me. I read pp. 1–26 (body, table, figure caption, notes) line by line from `pdftotext`; I counted the reference list (pp. 26–35) and did not read it entry by entry.

**How I counted.** I set paragraph breaks from the typeset first-line indent (line coordinates from `pdftohtml -xml`: a body line starting 18 px right of the column margin opens a paragraph, as does the first line under a heading) and checked doubtful cases against `pdftotext -layout`. Two block quotations sit inside their paragraphs, because the line after each is flush left; their words are in the counts. One forced line break without an indent (p. 20, inside paragraph 60) is not a paragraph break. I set heading levels from the typeface. Word and sentence counts come from a script and are good to a few words per paragraph; the sentence splitter does not split at semicolons. "Cites" counts author–year works (years inside citations), so a parenthesis listing four works counts four. A "citation group" is one parenthesis or one narrative Author (year). Narrative means the year parenthesis follows a name in the running sentence; a thinker named with no year at all ("For Taylor, …") is not counted. Printed page numbers equal PDF page numbers.

**Three findings that change the brief.**

1. **"Moving Forward" is typeset as a level-2 heading**, in the same italic sans face as "Image", "Imagination" and "Imaginaries", so on the page it is the fourth subsection of the Inventory. Table 1 agrees: its "Section" column has four groups, the fourth labelled "Moving forward". The prose treats it as the paper's conclusion (its first sentence calls it the final section; the introduction and the Imaginaries subsection call it the concluding discussion). I report it below as typeset level 2, functioning as the closing section. There is no heading named Discussion or Conclusion anywhere.
2. **Table 1 has no "diagnosis" column.** The diagnosis lives only in the prose. The table records uses and extensions; an extension is a row, set in italics, whose Concept cell carries a label.
3. **The three Inventory subsections do not share one paragraph order.** They share four jobs (survey, diagnosis, intervention, close) but place and size them differently, and the third one's intervention is a distinction, not a new concept. Details in section 4.

## 1. Front matter

**Title form.** Three one-word nouns built on one root, separated by commas, ordered from the smallest unit to the largest: singular thing, faculty, plural collective. A colon follows, then a subtitle of the form *A [method] of [umbrella term] in [field]*. The umbrella term ("Imago-Concepts") is a coinage that first appears in the subtitle and is never formally defined in the body; the body uses it seven times as a settled label. Printed in title case, in three lines.

**Abstract.** 270 words, one paragraph, nine sentences. No citations. First person from sentence 2 on.

| # | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 44 | States the phenomenon (one concept's rise) and, after a semicolon, the problem (its meaning and its relation to neighbours have blurred). |
| 2 | 37 | Says what the paper does first: places the concept in a genealogy, and gives the genealogy's start point and end point. |
| 3 | 53 | Says what the paper does second: extends the genealogy into an inventory, and states the diagnosis as a three-part list, one fault per concept, in the order A, B, C. |
| 4 | 7 | Hinge: announces "three interventions". |
| 5 | 17 | First: the intervention on concept A, with the new term in parentheses. |
| 6 | 19 | Second: the intervention on concept B. |
| 7 | 25 | Third: the intervention on concept C. |
| 8 | 36 | Names the new concept that the final part develops, and the need it answers. |
| 9 | 32 | Adds the second new account from the final part, phrased as a complement, and ends on a contrast (constituted in an encounter, not owned by a subject). |

The abstract has no sentence on implications or future research. It ends on the paper's last new idea.

**Keywords.** Five, not alphabetical: `creativity, fiction, Ricoeur, post-humanism, intra-subjunctive imagination`. None of the three title terms is a keyword. The list holds one neighbouring concept, one sub-type, one thinker, one tradition, and one of the paper's own coinages.

## 4. Section templates

### Introduction (8 paragraphs, 1,503 words)

1. The fashionable term and the problem with it; the aim.
2. The parent notion, its history, the term's spread; the problem restated as a doubt.
3. The premise of the paper and the difficulty in its way.
4. The field's prior work in four clusters.
5. The fault in the field's use; a precedent elsewhere; the method stated in the last sentence.
6. Two moves.
7. Three interventions.
8. The closing section previewed; one sentence on the paper's contribution.

Section 6 below gives the detail.

### Genealogy lead-in (1 paragraph, 54 words)

Two sentences: what the section does; that it has three parts matching three historical foci. A note on the state of the wider literature hangs off the last word.

### Genealogy subsections

**The Mental Image (5 paragraphs, 908 words)**

1. The oldest question, split into two standing problems; the heuristic thinker introduced.
2. Figure walk-through; last sentence states the direction of travel for the whole genealogy.
3. The founding thinker on problem one; the model named and glossed; a plain example.
4. A second thinker on problem two.
5. The frame's persistence: four later thinkers at a sentence each.

Opens on a nominal clause naming the question. Closes on a subordinate clause and a citation. No hand-off.

**The Productive and Creative Imagination (4 paragraphs, 599 words)**

1. The thinker who broke with the old model; his account.
2. What the break changed; the consequence for today's vocabulary.
3. The break followed through to problem two (short).
4. A parallel strand and its chief author, with one running example; placed one step further along, at today's common understanding.

Opens by naming the break from the previous subsection's model, so the backward hook does the hand-off. Closes on the present-day meaning of the concept.

**The Collective Imagination (4 paragraphs, 748 words)**

1. Recap of stages one and two; announcement of stage three; where the new term arose.
2. First source of the term's spread: one theorist, one precursor, one offshoot.
3. Second source: one theorist, block-quoted and glossed; pointer forward.
4. "To conclude": the common thread conceded, the tension named, the need for clarity stated.

Opens on a first-person recap and turn. Closes the whole Genealogy and motivates the Inventory.

**Do the three follow one order?** Loosely. Each runs *the stage and its question → its principal thinkers in date order, one or two paragraphs each → what the stage left to the present vocabulary*. The first two also share a hidden pair: each treats the same two standing problems in the same order (paragraphs 12–13 and 15–17). Beyond that they differ by position. The first carries two extra paragraphs of framing (heuristic and figure) because it comes first. The third carries a recap opening and a concluding paragraph because it comes last. The second is the plain case and the shortest. Only the third ends by handing on; the other two rely on the next subsection's first sentence.

**Reusable template for a Genealogy subsection (4–5 paragraphs, 600–900 words):**

1. Where the stage begins: the question it asked or the break it made from the stage before.
2. The thinker who defined it, expounded in his own terms, with the technical term in italics and glossed.
3. What the change did to the standing problem or problems the first subsection set up.
4. A second thinker or strand, with one concrete example.
5. What the stage left behind in present usage. In the last subsection only: a concluding paragraph that names the unresolved tension.

### Inventory lead-in (1 paragraph, 87 words)

Three sentences: what the Inventory is, with the single callout to Table 1; what it shows (inherited habits guide and limit use); what recovering the history opens up.

### Inventory subsections

**Image (16 paragraphs, 2,707 words)**

- Paragraphs 1–9, survey (1,408 words): entry into the field; one landmark work; three auxiliary uses; a two-sentence verdict on what these share; one author's stocktaking; then four later streams in date order, each introduced as a reaction to the one before.
- Paragraph 10, diagnosis (275 words): the fault, a concession, what remains undeveloped.
- Paragraphs 11–15, proposal (894 words): return to Figure 1; set it in motion with invented examples; name the first new term; justify it against the Genealogy; introduce a second new term through an everyday case; tie it to media and technology.
- Paragraph 16, close (130 words): three-sentence summary and an explicit hand-off sentence to the next concept.

**Imagination (15 paragraphs, 3,103 words)**

- Paragraph 1, opening (213 words): contrast with the previous concept; the diagnosis stated in advance; the extension announced.
- Paragraphs 2–11, survey (2,009 words): the fault's origin in the Genealogy; the concept's marginal standing; the subfield that took it up, with its early author, its recent work and a dissenting current; then four smaller streams.
- Paragraph 12, diagnosis (158 words): the fault restated, a rhetorical question, the need for a wider view.
- Paragraphs 13–15, proposal (723 words): the imported definition; its three-step model; its application to organisations with one published case.
- No closing paragraph and no hand-off. The subsection ends on why the wider concept matters.

**Imaginaries (17 paragraphs, 2,896 words)**

- Paragraph 1, opening (153 words): the concept, its uptake, a twofold aim.
- Paragraphs 2–3, survey (355 words): what kind of concept it is and what it has been used for.
- Paragraphs 4–7, definitional analysis (697 words): a question; the field's fullest definition in a block quotation; two of its elements examined in turn; the concept's distinctive value fixed.
- Paragraph 8, summary and roadmap (170 words): three numbered points; two lineages announced.
- Paragraphs 9–11, the two lineages (525 words): each expounded, each with its followers in the field; one published study as the worked example.
- Paragraphs 12–15, implications (753 words): for each lineage in turn, what it gives the field, then its limit.
- Paragraph 16, comparison (118 words). Paragraph 17, purpose and hand-off to the closing section (125 words).

**Do the three follow one order?** No, not closely. They share four jobs in one sequence (survey of the field's use; diagnosis; the authors' intervention; close) and three habits: each opens by setting its concept against the other two; each calls back to the Genealogy by name at the point of diagnosis; and each marks its turn with a paragraph that opens on "Our" or "We". The differences are these.

**Reusable template for an Inventory subsection (15–17 paragraphs, 2,700–3,100 words), taken from Image as the plainest case:**

1. Open by placing this concept against the other two; say when and through which turn it entered the field.
2. One landmark work, expounded, with a one-line example.
3. to 8. The streams that followed, in date order, one paragraph each, each opened as a response to the last. Between two of them, a two-sentence verdict on what the streams so far share, tied to a Genealogy stage.
9. The newest stream, the one closest to the authors' own position.
10. Diagnosis: what the survey shows is wrong (not neglect; a specific narrowing or splitting). Concede what is already known. Say what remains undeveloped.
11. Begin the proposal from something already on the page (the figure, a Genealogy term).
12. Work it through examples; name the new term in italics.
13. Say what the new term fixes, by reference to the Genealogy.
14. to 15. A second term or a second aspect; its wider conditions.
16. Close: one sentence on what was surveyed, one on what was missed, one on what the reframing does, one handing over to the next concept.

**How one concept is handed to the next.**

- Genealogy 1 → 2: by the next subsection's opening sentence only.
- Genealogy 2 → 3: by a first-person recap and an announced turn at the start of 3.
- Genealogy → Inventory: the last Genealogy paragraph ends on a need; the Inventory lead-in answers it.
- Image → Imagination: the only full hand-off sentence in the paper, the last sentence of Image, which names the next concept and says that a similar narrowing shaped it.
- Imagination → Imaginaries: none at the end of Imagination; Imaginaries opens with a first-person turn.
- Imaginaries → Moving Forward: two closing sentences, one promising the supplement and one stating the claim the closing section develops.

**Cross-references.** The Inventory points back to the Genealogy by name at least eight times (paragraphs 25, 29, 32, 36, 38, 41, 51, 65) and forward to the closing section four times (38, 46, 53, 71). The Genealogy points forward to the Inventory once (21), and Imaginaries points sideways to the Image and Imagination subsections (66, 69). These pointers are what make the two halves read as one argument.

## 9. Voice and sentence habits

**First person.** 119 tokens: "we" 49, "our" 66, "us" 4, or 8.4 per 1,000 words. "I" never appears. By part, per 1,000 words: Introduction 16.0; The Mental Image 15.4; The Productive and Creative Imagination 3.3; The Collective Imagination 9.4; Image 6.3; Imagination 4.2; Imaginaries 7.6; Moving Forward 9.2. It is used for five kinds of statement:

- **Signposting:** turning, returning, extending, summarising, pointing forward or back. The largest group.
- **The paper's own moves:** propose, argue, show, disentangle, make an intervention. "we propose" twice, "we argue" three times.
- **The paper's parts as subjects:** "our genealogy" nine times, "our inventory" eight, "our paper" six. These phrases carry many of the diagnosis sentences, so the claim is attributed to the section, not to "we".
- **The field as "our field"**, six times.
- **Generic "we"** for people in general (how we imagine the world, how we apprehend a painting), about ten times. This accounts for the high rate in The Mental Image.

Belief is marked as belief twice. First person is absent from the exposition of other authors.

**Tense.** Simple past for what historical thinkers did. Present for the content of any account, old or new. Present perfect for what the field has done, very often in the passive: "has/have been" plus participle occurs 30 times and "is/are/was/were" plus participle about 50. Present for the authors' own claims; present perfect for the retrospective in the last paragraph. Future for forward pointers.

**Hedging.** Frequent and spread evenly: "can be" 29, "may" 20, "often" 17, "could" 16, "tend/tends/tendency" 14, "perhaps" 7, "might" 6, "potentially" 6, "essentially" 5, "arguably" 2. The coinages are hedged at the point of naming. Verdicts on other authors are softened with "risks", "tends to", "arguably".

**Paragraph openers (79).**

| Type | Count | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| Transition or contrast that hooks back | 33 | "This/These/Such" + noun; Another; Unlike; Compared with; Whereas; Although; Despite; By contrast; "As noted" formulae; "In this line of thinking"; "From this perspective" |
| Claim about the subject matter | 20 | A definite noun phrase and a present or perfect verb; includes the two enumerators "A first/second direction concerns" |
| Self-reference (we, our, this section, the paper) | 17 | "Our genealogy/inventory/analysis…"; "In this section, we…"; "We can summarise…" |
| Named author or study in the first eight words | 8 | Name with year in possessive or subject position |
| Question | 1 | "But what is it that…" |

No paragraph opens on an emphasis marker. Sentence-initial connectives are sparse: Although 11, For example 6, Whereas 5, From this perspective 5, Yet 4, By contrast 4, Indeed 4, As such 4, However 2 (it sits mid-sentence seven more times), Moreover 1. Three questions in the whole body.

**How terms are introduced and held.**

- A new term appears in italics at first use, glossed in the same sentence by an appositive, "that is", "in the sense of" or "as in". After that it is set roman, except the three title terms, which go into italics again whenever they are mentioned as words.
- The structural labels are held fixed: genealogy (23 uses of the stem), inventory (14), imago-concepts (7), intervention (4).
- The descriptive labels are not held fixed. The two lineages of concept C are called lineages, strands, versions, orientations and accounts. The paper's key idea of what is not present is called non-present, absent and what is not immediately given. One row name in the table ("Distal sphere imagination") never appears in that form in the prose, and "intra-subjunctive" appears only twice in the body.
- Foreign-language originals of technical terms are given once, in italics, after the English.

**Italics.** 218 runs. Five uses: the title terms as words; coinages and technical terms at first use; foreign words; titles of works; contrastive stress on a single word (new, without, between, proper), about 25 times.

**Lists inside paragraphs.** First/Second/Third with capitals: paragraphs 7 and 62 and the abstract. first/second in lower case inside a sentence: paragraphs 6, 8 and 10. One/second/third/fourth for clusters: paragraph 4. Roman numerals (i), (ii), (iii) inline: paragraphs 6, 71 and 79 (twice). A two-sided "one hand / other" pair: paragraph 56. No bulleted or displayed lists.

**A caution for the builder.** The template is the structure, not the sentence habits. The agentless passive in the survey paragraphs, the 33-word mean sentence and the citation-final sentences are this paper's own and conflict with the house style for the algorithmacy manuscript.
