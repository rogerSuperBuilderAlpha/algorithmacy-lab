**Date:** 2026-10-07 · **Written by:** Claude (agent read; nothing here is the author's text)
**Source:** Stark, D., & Vanden Broeck, P. (2024). Principles of algorithmic management. *Organization Theory, 5*(2), 1–24. DOI 10.1177/26317877241257213.
**Read from:** publisher version of record, `pyphi-experiments/submissions/lima_pdw/literature/pdfs/starkvandenbroeck2024.pdf`, body pp. 1–16 and notes pp. 17–18 read in full; references pp. 18–23 searched, not read.
**Page numbers** are the printed running-head numbers (p. 1 = title page). I checked every quotation below against the extracted text by script; the check is reported at the end.

## 1. Does "coordinative co-optation" occur?

No. The compound appears nowhere in the article — not in the abstract, body, table, notes, keywords or reference list — and neither does the adjective "coordinative" on its own. The existing card is right on this point. The nearest phrase sits on p. 5, where the authors set the platform against "the three forms of coordination (hierarchies, markets, and networks)" and write that "on platforms they are co-opted"; the noun phrase they repeat is "the co-optation of actors, assets, and activities" (pp. 1, 6). Their own table pulls the two words apart rather than joining them: Table 1 (p. 14) gives "Coordinate specialists" as the object of *collaborative* management and "Co-opt many types of user" as the object of *algorithmic* management. The one passage that licenses reading co-optation as a kind of coordination is the sentence on p. 5 that calls the new "means of coordination more novel". The compound is therefore a fair coinage for the algorithmacy paper to make in its own name, and an inaccurate thing to put in quotation marks under theirs.

## 3. What the article establishes

**What co-optation is, and who is co-opted by what.** The authors never give co-optation a dictionary-style definition. They specify it by its objects, its agent and its operation.

- Objects: "actors, assets, and activities" (pp. 1, 2, 6) that "are not located within the firm" (pp. 1, 2); in the table, "many types of user" (p. 14); at the limit, "the broader world", "an addressee that can be co-opted and mobilized" (p. 15).
- Agent: "algorithmic management" (pp. 1, 6), "Algorithms" (p. 8), "algorithmic practices" (p. 9). They define the last term on p. 4: "algorithmic management refers to algorithmic practices—not algorithms alone but configurations of humans and machines".
- Operation: "Algorithms co-opt and organize users. First, by categorizing them." (p. 8); then "Categorizing users, here by this principle, there by another; interacting with the user and facilitating her interactions with other users; guiding, nudging, limiting, or expanding possibilities; all these and more are managed algorithmically." (p. 9). The mechanism is recursive: "it is in using the algorithm that one is folded into algorithmic management" (p. 9).
- The sharpest gloss comes under Accountability (p. 14): "platform operators enroll users and providers in a system of ratings that often circulate in feedback loops as rankings. When a user rates a provider on eBay, for example, she is not acting as a subordinate to whom eBay has delegated authority. The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent"; "The platform operator leverages rather than delegates."

**Both contrasts are in the article, at different levels.** Against forms of coordination: hierarchy, market, network versus platform (abstract p. 1; p. 5), followed on p. 6 by the reason none of the three will do — "they will not be managed by supervisory ordering within the firm; cannot be negotiated by contracts across firms; and are of a scale, scope, and type too complex and vastly too large to be amenable to relations of trust and practices of reciprocity." Against styles of management: scientific and collaborative versus algorithmic, which is the organizing comparison of the whole main section and of Table 1 (p. 14). Cooperation is not a term of the argument: "cooperative" appears once, inside a quotation in note 7 (p. 17), and "cooperate" only in two reference-list titles.

**"Match", "evaluate", "bind".**

- "match": zero occurrences in the body, notes or table. The card's finding stands.
- "bind"/"binding": zero occurrences. ("bound", "bounded", "boundless" occur, always about organizational boundaries.) "unilateral": zero.
- "evaluate": the only verb use has *users* as subject — "They search for, evaluate, store, and share cultural products." (p. 8). The platform-side equivalent is the vocabulary of "ratings and rankings (of and by providers and users, for example)" (p. 4), the "system of ratings that often circulate in feedback loops as rankings" (p. 14), and note 7: "On platforms, by contrast, reputation takes the form of a score rather than being built through interactive engagements that shape a trusting relationship." (p. 17). In their account the users and providers do the rating and the operator leverages it; the article does not say that the algorithm evaluates an exchange between two parties.
- Nearest thing to pairing parties: "interacting with the user and facilitating her interactions with other users" (p. 9).
- Nearest thing to constraint: "guiding, nudging, limiting, or expanding possibilities" (p. 9), and the operator's ability "to deflect accountability" (p. 14). Neither amounts to binding parties to outcomes.

**The triangle.** Yes, twice, and both times borrowed. On p. 4 they report that Curchod et al. (2020) find "“power asymmetries” in triangular relations among platform operators, producers, and end users". Across pp. 13–14 (the sentence opens "As Stark" at the foot of p. 13 and resumes under Table 1): "As Stark and Pais (2020) argue, the power relations of algorithmic management in the platform economy should not be represented as a hierarchy of supervision. Instead of a bureaucratic structure in which those who supervise are also supervised up a chain of command, they observe a triangular structure (Vallas & Schor, 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners." The words "triad" and "triadic" do not occur. The triangle is credited to Stark & Pais (2020) and Vallas & Schor (2020), not claimed as this article's own.

**The "principles".** The article numbers no principles and never lists them as propositions. It presents them as cells in a comparison along "the following five dimensions: organizational form, the object of management, ideology, modality, and accountability" (p. 6). Table 1 (p. 14), "Algorithmic Management in Historical Perspective", verbatim:

| | Scientific management | Collaborative management | Algorithmic management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Organizational form | The factory | The project | The platform |
| Object of management | Supervise labor | Coordinate specialists | Co-opt many types of user |
| Ideology | Efficiency | Flexibility | Immediacy/Totality |
| Modality | Standardized | Diversified | Synthetic |
| Accountability | Vertical | Horizontal | Twisted |

The authors call the cells "signposts" (p. 14). The only numbered list in the article, "(1) ... (2) ... (3)" on p. 12, enumerates positions in the literature that they reject.

**First definition of the central construct.** Algorithmic management is first defined on p. 2 (item 4 in the table above) and given its ontological specification on p. 4 ("configurations of humans and machines"). Co-optation is introduced in the abstract (p. 1) and restated as the argument on p. 9 (item 8).

## 4. Verdicts on the draft's three uses

**(a) intro: algorithmic intermediaries "co-opt (Stark, 2024 - OT), ie evaluate and bind".**
*Half supported.* The verb "co-opt" is theirs, with algorithms as its subject (p. 8, item 7). Two corrections. The citation must be Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024); the article has two authors. And the gloss "evaluate and bind" is not their gloss. Where they gloss the verb they write "co-opt and organize" (pp. 8, 9) and "leverages rather than delegates" (p. 14). "Evaluate" has an indirect warrant in the ratings-and-rankings passages (pp. 4, 14, 17); "bind" has none. As punctuated, "ie evaluate and bind" reads as their meaning of the word.

**(b) intro, AI-drafted variant: "what Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) identify as "coordinative co-optation.""**
*Contradicted.* They identify no such thing; the phrase is not in the article, and the quotation marks present it as their words. Table 1 (p. 14) assigns "Coordinate" and "Co-opt" to different management styles.

**(c) section 2: "Rather than fitting into classical archetypes of hierarchy, market, or network, digital platforms govern through "coordinative co-optation" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024)—deploying algorithms to match unchosen parties, evaluate their exchanges, and unilaterally bind them to outcomes."**
*First clause supported; quoted term and the three-verb gloss not.* The contrast with hierarchy, market and network is exactly theirs (pp. 5–6). The quoted compound is not in the source. "Match" and "bind" have no counterpart in the article, and "unchosen", "exchanges" and "unilaterally" none either. "Evaluate" corresponds loosely to "a system of ratings that often circulate in feedback loops as rankings" (p. 14), with the difference noted above: their users rate and the operator leverages. One smaller point: they attribute co-optation to "algorithmic management", with the platform as its organizational form, and speak of "governance principles" (pp. 1, 3) rather than of platforms governing. The existing card attributes the matching vocabulary to Stark & Pais (2020); I did not read that article in this pass and cannot confirm it here.

## 5. Suggested attribution (a suggestion for the author — Claude-drafted, not the author's wording)

> Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 5) set the platform beside the three familiar forms of coordination—"whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted"—and I call this fourth form *coordinative co-optation*.

This keeps their sentence in their words, keeps the compound, and makes the compound the algorithmacy paper's own coinage. If the match/evaluate/bind gloss stays, it needs to follow the coinage as the paper's own specification (with Stark & Pais 2020 for matching only once that source is checked), not sit inside the Stark & Vanden Broeck parenthesis.
