# Structure map: Werner, Cornelissen & Turkenburg (2026)

**Source.** Read in full from `werner-et-al-2026-moral-selves-organizational-character-and-the-pursuit-of-purpose.pdf` (27 printed pages; body pp. 2–22, references pp. 22–26, author biographies pp. 26–27).

**How I counted.** I set paragraph boundaries from the typeset first-line indents (line coordinates from `pdftohtml -xml`; a line starting 18 px right of the column margin opens a paragraph, as does the first line under a heading) and checked doubtful cases against `pdftotext -layout`. I set heading levels from the typeface: level 1 is 18-pt bold sans, level 2 is 18-pt italic sans. Word counts come from a script and are good to within a few words per paragraph. "Cites" counts author–year mentions by counting years inside citations, so a parenthesis listing four works counts four; it is a rough measure. Printed page numbers equal PDF page numbers.

## 1. Bibliographic line and front matter

- **As printed (masthead, p. 1):** Mirjam D. Werner, Joep Cornelissen, and Krijn Turkenburg. "Moral Selves: Organizational character and the pursuit of purpose." *Organization Theory*, Volume 7: 1–27. © The Author(s) 2026. DOI: 10.1177/26317877261423230. Open access, CC BY 4.0.
- **Article type label:** "Theory Article" (printed above the title).
- **Abstract:** 156 words, one paragraph, five sentences. It opens on a question about the phenomenon, then states the answer, the theoretical source, the two deliverables (typology, process model) and the implications.
- **Keywords:** 6, in alphabetical order (character, CSR, ethics, hybrid organizations, organization theory, purpose).

## 3. Devices inventory

**Tables (1).**
- "Table 1. Three Ideal-Types of Organizational Character." Page 9, full page, set landscape. It falls physically inside "Role performers" and is called out once, in the lead-in paragraph of "Organizational Character Types" (p. 8). It is a 7 × 3 grid: columns are the three types (Role performer, Institutional idealist, Moral steward); rows are Definition, Moral scope, Moral reasoning, Metaphor for describing this character, Aspirations, Commitments, Possible pathologies. Each of the scope and reasoning cells opens on a one-word italic label (Narrow / Concrete / Broad; Automatic / Algorithmic / Deliberate) followed by a gloss. The table rows are the outline of each type subsection (see section 5).

**Figures (1).**
- "Figure 1. Processes between organizational characters." Page 17, top of page, inside "Organizational Character Pathways"; first called out on p. 16. It is a process diagram: the three character types sit at three corners (Role Performer bottom left, Institutional Idealist top, Moral Steward bottom right); a dashed central box labelled "Accounting for oneself" carries two continua (moral reasoning, automatic to deliberate; moral scope, narrow to broad); three loops of arrows connect the box to the corners, and each arrow carries an italic process label. The body walks the figure label by label, using the form "(Figure 1: *label*)" eleven times for ten labels.

**Numbered propositions, hypotheses, research questions.** None numbered. The paper states no propositions and no hypotheses. Three research questions appear once, unnumbered and in italics, run together in a single sentence in the third paragraph of the Introduction (p. 2). The authors name their style of theorizing as typological rather than propositional (pp. 8, 20, 21).

**Cases and examples.** All are real, named firms, described from published or public sources. The paper has no hypothetical vignette, no composite case and no data of the authors' own collected for this paper. Those running more than two sentences:

The pattern: **one extended real-firm example per type**, each introduced by a formula ("A good example of…", "A well-known example of…"), each a paragraph of 165–170 words placed directly after the paragraph on that type's moral reasoning. The same firms then come back as short tags in the process section, so that each path in Figure 1 ends on a firm the reader already knows. The opening vignette also pays off inside the typology: its two firms are assigned to two different types.

**Metaphors as a device.** Each type gets a named human figure in quotation marks (politician, theologian, debater), two of the three borrowed from Tetlock (2002). The table and the text disagree on the first: Table 1 prints "Pragmatic politician" while the text (p. 11) uses "intuitive politician."

**Block quotations (1).** Bernard Williams (1985, p. 9), 67 words, p. 13, inside the first paragraph of "Moral stewards." It marks the difference between the third type and the other two. All other quotations are run in.

**Footnotes and endnotes.** None. The paper has no Notes section.

## 4. The Introduction, paragraph by paragraph

Six paragraphs, 982 words, pp. 2–3.

| ¶ | Opens | Words | Cites | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "From the start of 2025, the second Trump" | 171 | 2 | Opens on a current event in which firms answer the same political pressure differently, and ends on the puzzle as a question: what makes an organization hold firm. |
| 2 | "Previous work suggests that the concept of" | 237 | 15 | Names the concept that could answer the puzzle (organizational character), credits Basu and Palazzo and Selznick for it, then states the gap: both describe it only in broad terms and nobody has theorized it explicitly. |
| 3 | "Accordingly, in this paper, we aim to address" | 207 | 6 | States the aim and three italicized research questions, names the theoretical source (moral philosophy of the self, Charles Taylor), names the view it is set against (organizations as actors), and gives the thesis in one sentence. |
| 4 | "By intersecting moral selves with actor roles" | 150 | 2 | Previews the two things the paper builds, a typology of ideal types and an account of how organizations shift between them, and ties character to purpose. |
| 5 | "With our theory development, we make two" | 119 | 2 | States two contributions, each in one or two sentences: a fuller conceptualization with a typology; a process model with triggers and enablers. |
| 6 | "In what follows, we first briefly review" | 98 | 0 | Roadmap: walks the remaining sections in order in four sentences. |

- **Roadmap paragraph:** yes, the last paragraph (¶6).
- **Contribution statement:** yes, ¶5, explicit and numbered in prose ("two primary contributions").
- **Statement of the approach to theorizing:** partly. ¶3 names the source theory and the opposing view; ¶4 says the typology is ideal-typical. The explicit statement of theorizing style (typological, citing Cornelissen, 2017, and Delbridge & Fiss, 2013, and contrasted with a taxonomy) waits for the lead-in to "Organizational Character Types" on p. 8, and the Discussion repeats it twice (pp. 20, 21).
- **Where the central term is first defined:** not in the Introduction. ¶2 introduces "organizational character" through other scholars' use of it and ¶3 gives a working gloss inside the thesis sentence. The formal definition comes in the first sentence of the third section, "Organizational Character and the Moral Background" (p. 5), where the authors define character as an organization's internalized sense of self, grounded in a capacity for moral reflection that exists outside any actor role. The last paragraph of the preceding section (p. 5) anticipates it in a subordinate clause.
- The Introduction carries no section-level literature review; that work moves to the second section. Citations concentrate in ¶2 (15 of the Introduction's 27).

## 5. How the core construct is built

**Stage one: clear the ground (section 2, "Organizations as Moral Subjects," 6 paragraphs, 1,228 words).** The section defines the construct by its opposite before the construct appears. Paragraph 1 states the prevailing view (organizations as "actors" in institutionally defined roles). Paragraph 2 shows the same premise inside two ethics traditions (contractualist and virtue-based). Paragraphs 3 and 4 give two limitations: a "thin," procedural account of morality, and functional explanation. Paragraph 5 turns to the alternative, organizations as "subjects" with a sense of self. Paragraph 6 is a bridge that announces the next section and names the source theory and the aim (to add a "thick" morality to the thin one).

**Stage two: define (section 3, "Organizational Character and the Moral Background," 12 paragraphs, 2,152 words, no subheadings).**

| ¶ | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 153 | Gives the definition, then defends the human–organization analogy as a correspondence of reasoning capacity, not an ascription of human properties. |
| 2 | 214 | Supplies the mechanism that lets an organization have a self: members speak on its behalf, and their shared premises harden into a self-view. |
| 3 | 131 | Says the resulting character is an institutionalized fixture that members keep up by acting on its premises. |
| 4 | 283 | Fences the key word "reasoning": descriptive, not prescriptive; not tied to logic, a class of expressions or a procedure. Argues that stance-taking constitutes the self. |
| 5 | 191 | Imports the organizing vocabulary from Charles Taylor: the background framework behind moral judgments. |
| 6 | 159 | Defines the first constituent dimension, commitment. |
| 7 | 175 | Extends the frame through time: character shows in a history of framed stances. |
| 8 | 238 | Says what character is not (an object, or an abstract "who we are") and what it is (an orientation), and that it can change. Sets up the second dimension. |
| 9 | 170 | Defines the second constituent dimension, aspiration. |
| 10 | 173 | Compresses the construct into one sentence (character joins aspiration and commitment), then separates it from identity. |
| 11 | 184 | Separates it from values. |
| 12 | 81 | Bridge to the typology; names the small prior literature on organizational character that the next section absorbs. |

The order is: definition, mechanism, scope of a key term, borrowed vocabulary, dimension one, dimension two, one-sentence synthesis, differentiation, bridge. The definition comes first and the two dimensions (commitment, aspiration) arrive only after the borrowed vocabulary makes them definable.

**Separation from neighbours.** Three neighbours, three sizes:
- *Actorhood* gets the whole of section 2 (1,228 words) and is the main foil throughout.
- *Identity* gets about 95 words at the end of ¶10, plus a clause in ¶8. The criterion is that character is moral, personal and aspirational, while identity is a categorical definition at a point in time. The authors close the point by quoting an identity theorist (Whetten, 2006) ruling aspiration out of identity.
- *Values* gets one paragraph (¶11, 184 words). The move is to concede what values are, deny that values and morality are the same (again on a cited authority), and then demote values to raw material that character appropriates.

In each case the authors state the neighbour fairly in one sentence, name one criterion of difference, and cite a scholar from the neighbour's own literature to hold the line. They place the differentiation **after** the positive definition, at the end of the section, not before it. There is no comparison table of neighbouring constructs.

**Stage three: how it varies (section 4, "Organizational Character Types").** A 101-word lead-in states the style of theorizing and points to Table 1. Then **one level-2 subsection per type**, and each subsection follows the same internal order, which is the row order of Table 1 with the example inserted in the middle:

1. definition of the type (the second and third also contrast themselves with the type before);
2. moral scope;
3. moral reasoning, each with a one-word label from Stanovich (automatic, algorithmic, deliberate);
4. the extended real-firm example;
5. the metaphor;
6. aspirations and commitments (instrumental; instrumental and normative; normative and existential);
7. value created, then possible pathologies. Every subsection ends on the type's dark side.

Role performers and Institutional idealists run 7 paragraphs each (1,302 and 1,226 words); Moral stewards runs 9 (1,616), adding a paragraph on the type's relation to its environment and one on collaboration. The repetition is close enough that a reader can read the three subsections across, row by row.

**Stage four: how it works and changes (section 5, "Organizational Character Pathways," 9 paragraphs, 2,540 words, no subheadings).** The section is organized as a walk through Figure 1, not as one subsection per element:

| ¶ | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 275 | Re-reads the three types as stages in a developmental progression and says why moving between them is hard. |
| 2 | 139 | Section roadmap; introduces Figure 1 and its centre; concedes the theorizing is stylized. |
| 3 | 357 | Starts at Role Performer: outside demands, accountability pressure, a public pledge, creeping commitment (built on Haack et al.). |
| 4 | 303 | The authors' own claim ("We argue"): demands that single the organization out trigger it to account for itself; otherwise it reverts (Booking.com). |
| 5 | 202 | The fork: retrospective or prospective deliberation decides which of two paths follows. |
| 6 | 356 | Path to Institutional Idealist: three labelled steps, ending on Shell. |
| 7 | 310 | Path to Moral Steward, first half: two labelled steps. |
| 8 | 299 | Path to Moral Steward, second half: what leaders must do to keep the process open (Buurtzorg). |
| 9 | 299 | Returns to the figure as a whole; says the model can be entered from any type; gives two reverse movements (Fairphone, Unilever). |

Each path is told in the same grammar: a trigger, a named process in italics keyed to the figure, an enabling or failing condition, the resulting type, and a firm from the typology as the end tag.

## 6. How the paper ends

- **Last two level-1 sections:** "Discussion" (1,033 words, 7 paragraphs) and "Conclusion" (112 words, 1 paragraph).
- **Discussion subsections:** one only, "Theoretical and research implications" (860 words, 6 paragraphs), after a 173-word lead-in that restates what the paper did and the gap it answered.
- **Inside the subsection:** ¶1 restates the typological style of theorizing; ¶2 proposes research on each ideal type (differences in moral scope; whether an existential commitment or "calling" exists); ¶3 raises a question about consistency between aspirations and commitments; ¶4 proposes research on the process model and its boundary conditions; ¶5 is "a final critical reflection" on the choice to theorize the process of moral reasoning and not its content; ¶6 turns that choice into two questions for normative research.
- **Limitations:** no heading and no paragraph labelled as such. The nearest thing is ¶5, a scope choice defended and then opened up in ¶6. The word "stylized" does the limitation work elsewhere (pp. 16, 21).
- **Future research:** yes, but woven through all six paragraphs of the subsection and not set apart. Each implication is stated as a direction or question for further work.
- **Practical implications:** no heading and no paragraph. Practice gets one clause, in the last sentence of the Conclusion.
- **Final paragraph:** three sentences. The first restates the core claim (organizations as moral agents that vary in character and so in purpose). The second restates the contribution in one sentence. The third expresses the hope that the theory will prompt research and help organizations wishing to change. It introduces nothing new and cites nothing.

## 7. References, voice, citation density

- **References:** 124 entries (pp. 22–26). They include four corporate or news web sources (Patagonia, Shell, Starbucks, Niewold) and one newspaper article (Speed, 2024), which support the firm examples.
- **Voice:** first person plural throughout ("we," "our"). 88 instances in the body, 7.2 per 1,000 words, unevenly spread: about 22 per 1,000 in the Introduction and the Discussion, 9 in the construct-definition section, 2.4 in Pathways and 1.4 in the Types section. First person marks the authors' moves (we argue, we define, we develop, we propose) and the framing; the descriptions of the types run impersonally with the organization or the type as subject. "I" occurs only inside the Williams quotation.
- **Citations per paragraph (rough):** Introduction 4.5 (heavily skewed: 15 in ¶2, between 0 and 6 elsewhere). Positioning section ("Organizations as Moral Subjects") 9.8. Construct definition 7.8. Types 4.7 (Role performers 6.9, Institutional idealists 3.4, Moral stewards 4.2). Pathways 4.1 per paragraph, but its paragraphs are long, so density is the lowest of any theory section at about 15 citations per 1,000 words against about 44 in the construct-definition section. Discussion 2.6. Conclusion 0. Citation density falls steadily as the paper moves from positioning to its own theory.
