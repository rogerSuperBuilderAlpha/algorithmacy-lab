## 5. How the core construct is built

The paper builds in four moves, each a level-1 section, and each section's opening paragraph points back to what the last one established.

**Section 2 (8 ¶, no subsections) sets up two rival conceptions.** One paragraph poses two questions and announces the contrast; three paragraphs give the representationalist view (its lineage, its practical consequence, the builder/user split); three give the pragmatist view (models as mediating signs, models as resources not sources, modeling as inseparable from inquiry); one bridging paragraph applies the contrast to the paper's field and announces the next section. The section leans on one interlocutor (Lorino) throughout.

**Section 3 diagnoses the literature.** A lead-in paragraph, then 3.1 extracts four features of the target models (one framing paragraph, then features in 3, 2, 1 and 2 paragraphs), then 3.2 derives two named problems from those features (one framing paragraph; the first problem in 3 paragraphs; the second in 5, split into two "uses"; one "In short" summary paragraph).

**Section 4 supplies the philosophical ground.** A lead-in argues the two problems share one root. 4.1 (4 ¶) restates what a problem is; 4.2 (2 ¶) restates how problem definition relates to change; 4.3 (12 ¶) sets out four features of Deweyan problem solving.

**Section 5 defines the construct itself**, in 9 paragraphs and 1,661 words:
- lead-in (1 ¶, 87 words): names the construct, gives the acronym, lists the three dimensions, and says which two problems it answers;
- 5.1 Modeling Activity (3 ¶): what a tool is for Dewey; how that joins the earlier pragmatist account and a recent philosophy-of-modeling source; what follows for scholar–practitioner co-inquiry;
- 5.2 Modeling Actions (3 ¶): one paragraph motivating a specific set of actions and warning that they are not sequential stages; one 362-word paragraph on the three construction actions; one 340-word paragraph on the three use actions;
- 5.3 Assessing Modeling Retrospectively (2 ¶): the contrast with the target models, then the questions the approach asks in place of theirs.

**Organization of the "how it works" material.** Two sections carry it, and they are organized differently.

- *Section 5* has one subsection per dimension (activity, actions, consequences), in the order announced in the Introduction and again in the lead-in. The subsections do not share an internal template, and they are uneven (499, 844, 231 words). Inside 5.2 the six actions are not given subsections or even separate paragraphs: each triple is one long paragraph run as "First… Second… Third…", with each action tied back to one of the Deweyan features from 4.3.
- *Section 4.3* does have a repeated internal order. Each of the four features gets (a) a short paragraph stating Dewey's claim with a quotation, (b) a paragraph applying it to corporate sustainability change ("In the context of…", "Applied to…", "For corporate sustainability change…", "For corporate sustainability, this means…"), and (c) a paragraph of published empirical studies that show the feature in practice. Features 1 and 3 follow the order exactly; feature 2 folds (b) and (c) together and adds a paragraph on a further question; feature 4 has (a) and (b) only, with measurement literature in place of empirical studies.

**Symmetry across sections.** The counts mirror one another: four features of the criticized models (3.1) against four Deweyan features (4.3); two problems (3.2) that section 5 claims to answer together; three dimensions; three plus three actions; three contributions (Introduction) against three implications (Discussion).

## 6. How the paper ends

The last two level-1 sections are **Discussion** (7 ¶, 1,119 words, p. 16) and **Conclusion** (1 ¶, 159 words, p. 18). Neither has subsections.

**Discussion, paragraph by paragraph:**
1. (61 words) Restates the core claim in one sentence and announces three theoretical implications, each with avenues for research.
2. (235) "First": implication for the organizing-sustainably agenda, then a conceptual question and an empirical question.
3. (174) "Second": implication for impactful knowledge production, then further theorizing and empirical research needed.
4. (149) More future work under the second implication, closing on a caution that the account offers no shortcut to impact.
5. (174) "Third": the six actions as a vocabulary for pragmatist theorizing, then extension to other settings.
6. (86) Boundary extension: other sectors (public administration, third sector).
7. (240) "A final reflection concerns a limitation": three limits (how participants interpret consequences; obstacles other than modeling assumptions; disagreement within co-inquiry), the last turned into a future-research question.

- **Limitations:** yes, but not under a heading. One paragraph, the last of the Discussion.
- **Future research:** yes, but not under a heading. It is woven into every implication paragraph; each implication ends on one or two named avenues, often phrased as questions.
- **Practical implications:** no section and no paragraph addressed to practitioners. Practice enters only through the argument that scholars and practitioners should model together.
- **The Discussion's three implications repeat the Introduction's three contributions**, in the same order and against the same literatures.

**Final paragraph (the whole Conclusion).** Four sentences. "In this paper we have shown" restates the diagnosis (the separation and the two problems); "In response, we have developed" restates the alternative; "In doing so, we have reframed" restates what modeling becomes; the last sentence is a "We hope" line inviting further theory, empirical research and practical experimentation. It adds no new claim and carries no citations.
