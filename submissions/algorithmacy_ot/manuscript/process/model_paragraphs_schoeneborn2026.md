# Model paragraph map: Schoeneborn, Dobusch & Seidl (2026)

**Source.** Schoeneborn, D., Dobusch, L., & Seidl, D. (2026). Toward a theory of organizationality: A gradual view on collective actorhood. *Organization Theory*, 7, 1–19. Theory Article. Read in full from `pyphi-experiments/submissions/lima_pdw/literature/pdfs/schoeneborn2026.pdf` (body runs pp. 2–14; references pp. 14–19).

**How I counted.** I read the body from the first paragraph of the Introduction to the last paragraph of the Conclusion and found **44 paragraphs, about 7,270 words**. I set paragraph boundaries from the typeset indents (word coordinates extracted with `pdftotext -bbox-layout`), not from column or page breaks. Four paragraphs (13, 15, 17, 31) carry a displayed block quotation and then resume flush left without an indent; I count each as one paragraph, quotation included, and mark it "+bq". Word counts come from a script and are good to within a few words. Sentence counts include the sentences inside block quotations. `Cites` counts every author–year mention, so a parenthesis listing four works counts four and a work cited twice counts twice; page-only follow-ups such as "(p. 292)" do not count. The opening words restore the line-break hyphens that text extraction drops.

## 2. Section by section

### Introduction — 7 paragraphs, 874 words, mean 125

The Introduction is a funnel in four steps: two paragraphs of opposed literature (1–2), one short paragraph that names the dilemma (3), one that introduces the existing remedy and its shortfall and ends on the aim (4), then three short paragraphs of contribution (5–7). Each paragraph opens on a connective or back-pointer that names the previous paragraph's subject ("On the other hand," "These two … stances," "this openness-specificity dilemma," "To this end," "Second," "In sum"), so the hand-off is explicit every time. Citations crowd into paragraphs 1–4 (32 of the section's 37) and sit in parentheses at clause ends; the contribution paragraphs carry few or none. First sentences run long in the literature paragraphs (26–42 words) and very short where the authors turn to their own proposals (7 and 12 words).

### Organizationality as a "Third-Way" Approach — 4 paragraphs, 843 words, mean 211

One paragraph of concept background, then a three-part typology of prior work, one logic per paragraph. Paragraphs 9–11 repeat a fixed inner sequence: name the logic, give one named exemplar study ("For example, Dobusch and Schoeneborn (2015) proposed …"), spell out what that study's scheme looks like, and close by saying what the logic is good for. Only the last of the three, the one the authors will adopt, also ends on a shortcoming, and that shortcoming is the opening the next section walks through. Paragraph 9 is overloaded by comparison: it states the gap, lists the criteria, and reviews the first logic in one 287-word block.

### Collective Actorhood as a Defining Criterion of Organizationality — 6 paragraphs, 1,359 words, mean 226

This is the paper's longest-winded section and its argumentative core: a 71-word bridge (12), a 364-word paragraph that justifies making a choice at all and then announces it (13), three enumerated reasons (14–16, "First," "Second," "In addition"), and a 341-word definition paragraph (17). The authors lean hardest on other voices here. Three of the paper's four block quotations sit in this section, and paragraphs 15 and 17 advance mainly by lining up named scholars as witnesses in narrative citations ("King et al. (2010) explicitly refer …," "Schreyögg and Sydow (2010, p. 1254) similarly refer …"). The section ends on a gap-and-hand-off pair: prior work asked whether organizations are actors, not how or how much, and "Below we elaborate …".

### Organizationality as the Degree of Collective Actorhood — 15 paragraphs, 2,425 words, mean 162

- Lead-in: 1 paragraph, 200 words.
- *Individual Versus Collective Social Addresses*: 2 paragraphs, 549 words, mean 274.
- *Charging With Actorhood Versus Discharging of Actorhood*: 5 paragraphs, 574 words, mean 115.
- *Charging and Discharging Actorhood Via Specific Attribution Practices*: 7 paragraphs, 1,102 words, mean 157 (1 lead-in; 4 charging, 629 words; 2 discharging, 367 words).

Every subsection opens with an orienting sentence in the first person plural that says what the framework "rests on," "proceeds from," or will "theorize," and the section head paragraph (18) ends by reducing the whole framework to two numbered arguments before any detail arrives. The two definitional paragraphs (19–20) are the longest and most heavily cited in the section; the paragraphs that state the authors' own propositions (22, 24) are the shortest. From paragraph 23 onward the inner order is nearly fixed: claim sentence, "For instance/For example" plus a published or everyday case in the second or third sentence, then either a complication ("However," "Yet," "Vice versa") or a closing sentence that restates the case in the framework's vocabulary.

### Discussion — 9 paragraphs, 1,243 words, mean 138

- Lead-in: 2 paragraphs, 164 words.
- *Conceptualizing Organizationality as a Continuum …*: 5 paragraphs, 665 words, mean 133.
- *Dynamizing Organizationality*: 2 paragraphs, 414 words, mean 207.

Two short uncited paragraphs recap the paper and announce two contributions; each contribution then gets its own subsection. The first subsection mostly restates the framework and positions it against each rival in turn, in near-identical terms to the earlier sections, and its last paragraph (39) returns to the dilemma from the Introduction. The second subsection is where new material appears: implications, a follow-up question, and a second literature the paper speaks to, each introduced by "Our framework also …" or "we also contribute to …" and anchored by a "While prior work … we …" sentence.

### Conclusion and Research Agenda — 3 paragraphs, 528 words, mean 176

A three-sentence summary, one long paragraph of four enumerated research questions, and a broadening close. In paragraph 43 each question is followed by exactly one sentence of motivation or operationalisation, and two of the four are phrased as direct questions. The final paragraph ends on work for others to do, not on an epigram.

## 3. Whole-paper patterns

**Paragraph length.** Mean 165 words, median 164, range 31–364. Nine paragraphs fall under 100 words and seven exceed 250. The short ones are all doing structural work (bridge, recap, signpost, the authors' own proposals in the Introduction); the long ones are all literature-facing (review, witness-stacking, borrowed definitions) or the research-questions paragraph. Paragraphs average 5.5 sentences, and sentences run long: the mean first sentence is 30 words (range 7–65).

**How paragraphs open.** By my reading of each first sentence:

| Opening type | Paragraphs | Share |
|---|---|---|
| The authors' own claim, premise or proposal | 3, 6, 9, 14–16, 19–21, 23–25, 27–30, 32, 37–41, 44 (23) | 52% |
| Transition, recap, signpost or roadmap | 5, 7, 12, 13, 17, 18, 22, 26, 33, 34, 36, 42, 43 (13) | 30% |
| A report of what the literature says or does | 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 11, 31, 35 (8) | 18% |

No paragraph opens on a bare narrative citation ("Smith (2020) argues …"); the literature-led openers name a group ("some scholars," "Scholars who have adopted …," "Prior scholarship") and put the citations in parentheses. Two further regularities cut across the three types. Twenty-five of 44 paragraphs begin with a connective, enumerator or back-pointer in their first few words. And 21 of 44 have "we" or "our" in the first sentence — every paragraph from 33 to 44 except 35, and most of the framework section.

**Citations.** 222 author–year mentions in all, about 5 per paragraph, unevenly spread: seven paragraphs carry none (7, 12, 22, 33, 34, 38, 42) and all seven are bridges, recaps or signposts. Citations cluster in the first one or two sentences of a paragraph, where they establish that a topic is studied, and thin toward the end, where the authors speak for themselves. Direct quotation is frequent for a theory paper: four block quotations and about fifteen run-in quotations, almost always used to let a source state a definition or concede a point the authors need.

**The Introduction, paragraph by paragraph.** (1) The question is live; camp A and its premise. (2) Camp B and its premise, with one quoted extreme. (3) The two camps make a dilemma; the dilemma defined in one 65-word sentence. (4) An existing third way; its promise; a published criticism of it; "In this paper, we thus aim to …". (5) "To this end, we make two proposals" — proposal one and what it departs from. (6) Proposal two, with the working definition and the mechanism in three sentences. (7) "In sum" — what the paper sets out and what the field gets. The Introduction has no section-by-section roadmap, no illustrative case or vignette, and no separate contributions list; paragraph 7 does the roadmap's work by restating the deliverables. The dilemma gets a hyphenated name in paragraph 4 and that name returns in paragraphs 39 and 42.

**How the construct is defined.** The definition is spread over roughly seven paragraphs and arrives in layers, each built from named sources before the authors add their own step:

1. Paragraph 6 (Introduction): a one-sentence working definition.
2. Paragraph 8: the parent concept, organizationality, defined by what it is not (neither noun nor verb but adjective).
3. Paragraph 13: the choice of criterion announced and distinguished from its nearest precursor.
4. Paragraphs 14–16: three reasons for the choice. These justify the definition; they do not extend it.
5. Paragraph 17: the criterion's meaning specified by synthesis — "a unifying aspect across these literatures" — from three quoted sources, then restated in the authors' own sentence, then a gap.
6. Paragraph 18: the definition compressed into two numbered arguments.
7. Paragraphs 19–20: the building-block concept (social address) defined by quotation, then the individual/collective distinction that makes the construct gradual.

There is no single boxed or italicised definition and no table of dimensions. The authors define by justified choice among alternatives, and they call such choices contingent four times (13, 15, 35, 36).

**How the mechanism section is run.** Seven paragraphs (26–32) under one heading and two run-in subheadings, covering five practices:

| Practice | Paragraphs | What each paragraph does | Where the example sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section lead-in | 26 | Defines "attribution practice"; says examples come from the literature | none |
| Charging 1: anthropomorphizing metaphor | 27, 28 | 27 names it and reports a study; 28 explains why it works | 27, sentence 3 (Halgin et al.'s media study) and sentence 4 (reverse case); 28, sentence 2 ("corporate citizens") |
| Charging 2: legal personhood | 29 | Names it, quotes a source, then shows the degree varies | sentences 5–6 (Germany versus Austria) |
| Charging 3: irresponsibility accusations | 30 | Names it, says who and where, states the effect | last sentence (Dieselgate) |
| Discharging 1: scapegoating | 31 | Names it from the literature, block-quotes its aim | last sentence (Dieselgate again) |
| Discharging 2: leader heroization | 32 | Names it, illustrates, quotes support, states the effect | sentence 2 (two sayings about founders) |

So the ratio is one paragraph per practice, with a second paragraph only for the first practice, where the authors pause to explain the underlying mechanism (metaphor). Each practice paragraph opens by naming the practice in a long sentence (24–48 words) that ranks or links it ("One of the most common …," "Probably the most common and consequential …," "In the same context …," "Another example …") and carries the citations showing others have studied it. Examples are short — one or two sentences — and every one is either a published study or a publicly known case; none is developed at length and none is invented. The same case, Dieselgate, appears on both sides (charging in 30, discharging in 31), which shows the two directions operating on one collective. The closing sentence of a practice paragraph translates the example back into the framework's terms and is usually hedged ("can vary," "tend to elevate," "can be presumed to have a discharging effect"). The section has no summary table, no propositions and no closing paragraph; it stops after the fifth practice and the Discussion begins.

A parallel run sits just before it (paragraphs 22–25): a one-sentence list of three aspects, then one paragraph per aspect, each opening with an enumerator and each built claim → "for instance" → complication.

**How the Discussion and Conclusion are built.** The Discussion spends its first two paragraphs and most of its first subsection restating: what the paper did (33), the two moves (34), the three logics (35), the three advantages (36), the comparison with rivals (37). New claims begin at 38 (rivals reread as practices), 39 (the dilemma resolved), 40 (dynamics, with implications) and 41 (a second literature addressed). Contributions are framed additively and against named work: "While Dobusch and Schoeneborn's (2015) study … described …, our paper provides the foundations for theorizing …". The Discussion contains no limitations paragraph and no boundary conditions. Its hedging lives in single words ("may help," "at least the partial integration") and in the repeated admission that the conceptual choice is contingent. The Conclusion follows the same order at a smaller scale — recap, agenda, broadening — and the research agenda sits there, not in the Discussion.

**Habits that hold across the paper.**

- Enumerate, then deliver one paragraph per item: two proposals (5–6), three logics (9–11), three reasons (14–16), three aspects (22–25), five practices (27–32), two contributions (35–41), four questions (43). The count is announced before the items in every run but the practices.
- Restate freely. The two moves appear in paragraphs 5–6, 13 and 34, and in brief in 42; the three advantages in 14–16 and again in 36; the comparison with rivals in 5, 13, 37.
- Keep the same handful of terms throughout ("collective social address," "charge/discharge," "attribution practices") with almost no elegant variation.
- Mark the authors' own steps in the first person plural (98 instances of we/our/us, about 13 per 1,000 words) and mark other people's steps by naming them.
- Give paragraph endings a job: a hand-off ("Below we elaborate …"), a gap, or a restatement of the example in framework terms. Ten of 44 paragraphs end on a citation parenthesis (1, 8, 16, 19, 25, 27, 31, 32, 41, 43); none ends on an aphorism.
