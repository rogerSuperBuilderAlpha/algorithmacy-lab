# Structure map: Kalkman (2026), *Organization Theory*

**Source.** Read in full from `scratchpad/ot2026/kalkman-2026-the-purpose-of-crisis-management-normative-directions-for-a-care-based-approach.pdf` (23 pages; body pp. 2–16, references pp. 16–23).

**How I counted.** I set paragraph boundaries from the typeset first-line indents, using word coordinates from `pdftotext -bbox-layout`; a paragraph that runs across a column or page break counts once. I set heading levels from the typeface (`pdftohtml -xml`). Word counts are whitespace tokens after rejoining line-break hyphens, so they are good to within a few words. "Cites" counts every author–year mention (a parenthesis listing four works counts four; "Weick, 1988, 1990, 1993" counts three; page-only locators do not count). Sentence counts are approximate.

## 3. Devices inventory

**Tables: 1. Figures: 0.**

- **Table 1.** Caption verbatim: "Table 1. An Alternative, Care-Based Crisis Management Approach." Page 7, set across both columns at the top of the page. It sits in the lead-in of "An Alternative Purpose for Crisis Management"; the single callout comes in the seventh lead-in paragraph (p. 8, top). It is a 3 × 6 matrix: the three normative directions as columns, and six rows — mission, vision, value, aspirational outcome, actionable pathways (three bullets per column), relevant literature (one named OMS literature per column). The five substantive row labels are the five components of organizational purpose that the Introduction quotes from an earlier source, so the table is the paper's whole framework on one page, and the three subsections walk it column by column.

**Numbered propositions, hypotheses, research questions: none.** The nearest thing is the nine "actionable pathways", ordinal-numbered in prose ("A first / second / third actionable pathway"), three per direction. The empirical-implications subsection lists open questions in running text, unnumbered.

**Cases, examples, illustrations.** The paper has **no sustained case, no vignette, and no data of the author's own**. Illustration is done by one- or two-sentence examples introduced with "For instance" (15 times), "For example" (5) or "e.g." (12), nearly all of them pointers to published studies. Only three stretches run past two sentences:

- **Roadmap paragraph:** yes, the last paragraph (¶8, 96 words, four sentences).
- **Contribution statement:** yes, ¶7, a full paragraph. It lists three theoretical contributions, and the three paragraphs of "Theoretical Implications" later take them up in the same order.
- **Statement of the approach to theorizing:** yes, in two places. ¶4 opens by calling the paper a "theoretical provocation" and cites the journal's own typology of theorizing forms for the label; ¶5 grounds the approach in the author's positionality. The theory section's second lead-in paragraph restates the stance (the aim is to provoke rethinking, not to present a new truth), and the Discussion repeats the "theoretical provocation" label.
- **Where the central term is first defined:** the care-based approach is *named* in ¶4 with its three directions in a parenthesis, but the Introduction does not define it. ¶6 defines the two terms the title rests on — crisis and (organizational) purpose — and the five-part definition of purpose it quotes becomes the row structure of Table 1. The care-based approach itself is defined in the lead-in of section 4.
- **Sequence in one line:** field's origin and dominant aim → gap → why it matters → what I do → who I am → definitions → contributions → roadmap.

## 5. How the core construct is built

**Where.** Section 4, "An Alternative Purpose for Crisis Management" (pp. 6–13; 30 paragraphs, 4,431 words). Its eight-paragraph lead-in defines the construct; three parallel subsections explain how each element works; a fourth subsection pulls them together.

**The lead-in (8 paragraphs, 1,073 words) — what each does.**

| ¶ | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 18 | 87 | Announces the source theory (feminist ethics of care), concedes other normative bases are possible, gives two reasons for this one. |
| 19 | 137 | States the epistemological stance (situated knowledge) and the aim: provoke rethinking of the five purpose components, not find a new truth. |
| 20 | 98 | Traces the origin of care ethics to its two founders and what they rejected. |
| 21 | 142 | Defines the ethics of care with one quoted definition, then lays out the stages of caring practice. |
| 22 | 129 | Explains why an *ethics* of care is needed and what makes caring ethical. |
| 23 | 201 | Sets the scope: care is not women-only and not private; closes on the care ethicists' vision of a restructured society. |
| 24 | 149 | Introduces the construct: three normative directions and the framework, with the callout to Table 1; defines each direction in one sentence tied to named care-ethics sources. |
| 25 | 130 | Names the OMS literature that supplies building blocks for each direction (compassion organizing, responsible management, grand challenges) and hands off to the subsections. |

So the construct arrives late: six paragraphs establish the borrowed theory before paragraph 24 states the paper's own three-part construct. First person appears in ¶18, ¶19 and ¶24 only.

**The "how it works" part: one subsection per element, identical internal order.** Each of the three direction subsections has six paragraphs in the same sequence:

1. **Opening paragraph** (157–203 words): names the direction with an ordinal, states its mission (the Table 1 "Mission" cell, almost word for word), grounds it in care ethics and the matching OMS literature.
2. **Agent paragraph** (82–138 words): says who carries the change — any organization member (4.1), middle and senior managers (4.2), emerging collectives of change agents (4.3).
3. **First actionable pathway** (130–264 words).
4. **Second actionable pathway** (192–246 words).
5. **Third actionable pathway** (189–287 words).
6. **One-sentence closing paragraph** (30–38 words) in a fixed formula: the value, then the vision, then the aspirational outcome — the remaining three cells of that Table 1 column.

Each pathway paragraph opens on the ordinal phrase, states the pathway, supports it with a run of cited findings (3–10 cites), adds an instance or two, and ends on the effect. The three pathway labels per subsection match the three bullets in the table. The subsections lengthen slightly as they go (869, 910, 1,069 words) and none contains "I".

**Integration subsection** ("Toward a New Purpose for Crisis Management", 4 paragraphs, 510 words): (1) contrasts the new purpose with the existing one on mission, values and success measure; (2) says the directions interact and can form a virtuous cycle; (3) names the facilitating structures each direction needs and links them to feminist organizing; (4) anticipates resistance and says why change agents can still succeed. "I" returns here.

## 6. How the paper ends

- **Last two level-1 sections:** "Discussion" (9 paragraphs, 1,462 words, 16.9%) and "Conclusion" (1 paragraph, 103 words).
- **Discussion subsections:** a one-paragraph lead-in (84 words) that restates the proposal and signposts the three subsections; then "Theoretical Implications" (3 paragraphs, 518 words), "Implications for Empirical Research" (3 paragraphs, 501 words), "Consequences for Practice" (2 paragraphs, 359 words). The order theory → empirical research → practice is announced in the abstract, the roadmap and the Discussion lead-in.
- **Theoretical implications:** one paragraph per contribution, in the order the Introduction listed them — opening space for ethical theorizing; a relational ontology; a process view that also says how a purpose is achieved. Each closes on a sentence stating what the paper adds or how theorizing shifts.
- **Limitations:** no section, subsection or paragraph. The only concessions are scattered sentences: other normative approaches are possible (¶18, and again as an invitation in ¶49), crisis management will never be perfect (¶55), and the ethics of care has critics whose objections empirical work would have to settle (last sentences of the empirical subsection).
- **Future research:** yes, as the whole of "Implications for Empirical Research": how to assess interventions with the framework, how organizations of different character reorient, and a run of open questions.
- **Practical implications:** yes, "Consequences for Practice": a paragraph urging a pragmatic mindset, then a paragraph of concrete steps for managers and members.
- **The final paragraph (Conclusion, 103 words, 4 sentences):** opens on one cited claim about the present era, restates in the present perfect what the paper criticized and what it advocated, names the three directions once more, and ends on what they can be used for (new research directions and more caring crisis management). It adds no new material and no limitation.

## 7. References, first person, citation density

**Reference count: 149 entries** (pp. 16–23), APA style with DOIs.

**First person.** The body has 38 "I", 15 "my" and 3 "me": 56 first-person singular tokens, about 6.5 per 1,000 words. They cluster. The Introduction holds 33 of the 56 (20 in the positionality paragraph alone). The two literature sections (¶9–17) and the three direction subsections (¶26–43) have none. "I" comes back in the integration subsection, the Discussion and the Conclusion. The author uses "I" for four kinds of statement:

1. **The paper's moves** — the largest group: I problematize, build on, develop, provide, unveil, propose, introduce, discuss, elaborate, present, show, have identified. Past moves are recalled in the present perfect ("I have advocated", "I have criticized").
2. **The normative claim itself.** "I advocate", "I argue that we need to replace", "I propose that ethical theorizing … should". Here "I" carries the argument, which fits a paper that calls itself a provocation.
3. **Stance and aim.** "My aim … is not … but", "My hope is that", "I therefore take a deliberately critical stance", "I consider".
4. **Autobiography** in the positionality paragraph.

"We/us/our" appears 9 times as the generic inclusive (the field, or people in general), once inside a quotation; it never stands for the author.
