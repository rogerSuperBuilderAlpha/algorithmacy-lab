# Structure record: Friedrich (2026), *Organization Theory*

**Source.** I read the PDF in full (`friedrich-2026-beyond-human-democratic-organizing-posthuman-affirmations.pdf`, 26 pages; body pp. 2–20, references pp. 20–26).

**How I counted.** I set headings and heading levels from the typeset fonts and paragraph breaks from the typeset indents (line coordinates from `pdftohtml -xml`, checked against `pdftotext -layout`), not from column or page breaks. A paragraph is a block that starts on an indented line or on the first line under a heading. Roman-numbered list items are counted separately as "items" and their words are included in the section totals. Word counts come from a script and are good to within a few words. "Cites" counts every author–year mention (a parenthesis with four works counts four; "2013, 2019a, 2022" counts three), so it is a rough density measure. I removed false hits by hand (the vignette's dates, four-digit page numbers).

## 3. Devices inventory

**Tables: 2. Figures: 0.**

**Numbered propositions and hypotheses: none.**

**Research question: one,** unnumbered, set in italics as a run-in sentence. It appears twice word for word: at the end of the third paragraph of §1 (p. 3) and at the end of the fifth paragraph of §2 (p. 6).

**Question lists: 9 "propositional questions,"** three per register, at the end of each §5 subsection (pp. 12, 13, 14). Each list is introduced by a stem sentence that closes the preceding paragraph, numbered I–III in roman numerals, set with a hanging indent, 26–47 words per item (105, 109 and 119 words per list). Two of the nine items hold two questions each. They stand where another paper would put propositions; nothing follows them in the subsection.

**One further roman-numbered list** in §4 (p. 9): three "elements" of the approach to theorizing, 81–104 words each, each a short paragraph in its own right.

**Vignette: one, the author's own speculative fiction, hypothetical.**

- *What it is.* A scene set in 2050 in a São Paulo office tower where consultants hold a democratic assembly that seats absent and nonhuman parties (a mineral vein, an extinct bee, a heatwave, a child of 2090).
- *Where and how long.* It is the first thing in the body: all of p. 2, both columns, 614 words in six paragraphs (a four-word dateline, then 86, 130, 156, 168 and 70 words).
- *How it is introduced.* It is not introduced. No heading, label, epigraph line or framing sentence precedes it; the title page ends and the scene begins. The first headed section starts at the top of p. 3.
- *How it is set off.* Entirely in italics, in the body typeface and size, in the normal two-column measure. No box, rule, indent or "Vignette" label. It carries no citations.
- *How it is keyed to the argument.* The second, third and fourth scene paragraphs each end on a parenthetical tag that gives a roman numeral and the name of one register, so the scene is pre-partitioned into the three parts §5 will theorize. The opening and closing scene paragraphs are untagged frame.
- *How it recurs.* Seven later points. (1) The first paragraph of §1 points back to it once, in a dash-enclosed aside. (2) §4, items II and III, name it as part of the method. (3–5) Each §5 subsection has one paragraph that retells the matching tagged scene paragraph in roman type and in the paper's analytic vocabulary: 77, 109 and 108 words, with no citations. (6) One paragraph of §6.1 (p. 17) cites the chair rearrangement as an example of a practice the paper speculates about. (7) The final paragraph of the paper returns to it by name. The abstract also describes it.
- *A caution for anyone copying the device.* The retellings paraphrase and do not match the scene exactly: the mineral vein is from one country on p. 2 and another on p. 11, two separately listed figures on p. 2 merge into one on p. 11, and the first retelling lists the extinct bee, which the corresponding scene paragraph does not name.

**Other examples that run more than two sentences.** One, published: Pavlovich and Roche's (2024) Māori-grounded "ecologies of care" in the literature paragraph of §5.3 (p. 13), about 110 words over three sentences, with its three named principles in italics; it returns as a row entry in Table 2. Everything else is one or two sentences: a hypothetical "dirty industry" bind (§2, one sentence), published studies of breathing, cheesemaking, fermentation, Amazon fieldwork and organic cotton (§5.1–5.2, one or two sentences each), and a speculative-fiction trilogy analysed by other authors (§5.3, one sentence). The paper has no empirical case of its own.

**Block quotations: none.** All quotation is run-in and it is heavy: most literature paragraphs carry three to six quoted phrases with page numbers.

**Footnotes and endnotes: none.** The only note is the affiliation footnote on p. 1. No appendix, no supplementary material.

## 5. How the core construct is built

The paper builds in two steps and puts a method section between them.

**Step 1: the borrowed concepts — §3, "Posthuman Feminism and Subjectivity" (6 paragraphs, 1,397 words, 16% of the body).**

| Paragraph | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Lead-in 1 | 229 | Places the source field, says what it reacts to, and ends on one sentence stating what this paper does with it. |
| Lead-in 2 | 267 | Sets out the field's critique of the humanist subject through the philosopher's own argument, largely in her quoted words. |
| Lead-in 3 | 139 | Locates her among neighbouring traditions, then announces the three concepts as a run-in list (i–iii) and says what they jointly reposition. |
| 3.1 | 252 | Concept 1: its source, what it rejects, what it asserts, the feminist amendment; closes on the consequence for the democratic subject. |
| 3.2 | 250 | Concept 2: glosses each of its three components, contrasts it with the humanist subject, adds affect; closes on what the resulting subject is exposed to. |
| 3.3 | 260 | Concept 3: two enumerated grounds ("First," "Second"), the central formula, what enacting it requires; closes with the Table 1 call-out. |

Each concept gets exactly one subsection, each subsection is exactly one paragraph of about 250 words, and Table 1 then restates the three as one-sentence definitions. The subsection order (ontology, subject, ethics) fixes the order of everything after it.

**Method interlude — §4, "On Theorizing with Posthuman Feminism" (490 words, 6%).** One lead paragraph (82 words) says an analytical strategy is needed; three roman-numbered items state it (meta-theorizing of assumptions; imagination and speculation, naming the vignette and the question lists as its instruments; provisionality, announcing that each later section ends on questions); a closing paragraph (127 words) says the result is not a contest between two paradigms and hands off to §5. This section is where the paper tells the reader how to take the vignette, the questions and the unfinished feel.

**Step 2: how it works — §5, "Toward Posthuman Democratic Organizing" (14 paragraphs + 9 items, 2,325 words, 27%).**

- *Lead-in (1 paragraph, 187 words).* Restates the guiding question as a shift (from adding entities to organizing with them), then previews the three registers in three sentences marked "First," "Second," "Third," each a one-sentence gloss. This is the nearest the paper comes to a definition of its construct; there is no single definitional sentence and no formal definition box.
- *One subsection per register,* in the order of §3's three concepts, each mapped to one concept. The three subsections run to 697, 668 and 773 words.

## 6. How the paper ends

- **One closing level-1 section,** "Posthuman democracy and affirmative organizational theory: Contributions and conclusions" (pp. 16–20; 12 paragraphs, 2,373 words, 28% of the body, the largest section and the most heavily cited at about 10 cites per paragraph). There is no separate Discussion and no separate Conclusion; the section before it is §5, the theory-building section.
- **Lead-in (168 words):** restates the intervention in three sentences and ends on a one-sentence itinerary of the three subsections as a roman-numbered run-in list — the only roadmap sentence in the paper.
- **Subsections, one per contribution, in the order the introduction's sixth paragraph gave them:**
  - "Posthuman democratic openings" (5 paragraphs, 985 words): contribution to democratic-organizing scholarship. One paragraph restates the departure and re-names the coined problem; the next three each take one adjacent literature (body and affect; care; relational and processual alternative organizing), say how the paper extends it, and close on a future-research sentence; the fifth positions the paper against rights-of-nature approaches and closes on a research agenda.
  - "Posthuman feminism for organization theory" (4 paragraphs, 809 words): contribution to posthuman and new-materialist organization theory, including the paper's one stated weakness and a caution about technology.
  - "On affirmative and speculative theorizing" (2 paragraphs, 411 words): contribution to how theorizing is done.
- **Limitations:** no section and the word does not occur. One sentence in §6.2 (p. 19) concedes a "challenge" in the source philosopher's eclecticism and sets a more parsimonious rival beside it; the next sentence turns it into a strength.
- **Future research:** no section. Eight future-research or research-agenda statements are distributed through §6, usually as the last sentence or two of a contribution paragraph (pp. 16–19). §6.3 has none.
- **Practical implications:** no section. Practice-level content lives in §5 (the fourth slot of each subsection and the question lists) and in the bottom row of Table 2.
- **Final paragraph (152 words, p. 20):** it is about the mode of theorizing, not about democracy. It says the paper's approach has implications for the process of theorizing, returns to the vignette by name, defends imagination against a narrow standard of rigour with two citations, and ends on a dash-appended phrase that names the mode of theorizing the paper has practised. It makes no call to action and no closing image. The body runs straight into Acknowledgements.

## 7. References, first person, citation density

- **Reference count: 143** (pp. 20–26; counted two ways, by hanging-indent entries and by year-in-parentheses, both 143). APA-style, sentence-case titles, italic journal and volume, DOIs as URLs. Six entries are sole-authored by the source philosopher and a seventh is co-authored by her.
- **First person.** The author uses "I" **five times in 8,579 body words** (0.6 per 1,000), plus twice in the Acknowledgements:
  - twice to coin the named problem (an "I term" formula, pp. 3 and 6);
  - once to announce the choice of theoretical resource ("I turn to," p. 3);
  - once to re-label a tension under the coined name ("I frame," p. 16);
  - once as a back-reference to an earlier suggestion (p. 19).
  
  Every "I" marks an act of naming, choosing or pointing back. No "I" carries a claim, a contribution or a statement of method. Those are carried by the document as agent — "this paper," "this text," "this study," "this research," "this work," "the present paper," 19 times in all — with verbs such as introduces, theorizes, contributes, advances, argues, extends, speculates, opens. The contribution paragraph of the introduction and the whole of §4 contain no "I." "We" appears once for the field ("We lack theories," p. 3) and "us" once (p. 16); "our time(s)" appears four times as a general phrase. There is no "my."
