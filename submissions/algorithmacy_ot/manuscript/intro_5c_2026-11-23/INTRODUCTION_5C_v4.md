**[C: The newest theory in *Organization Theory* ends on a question it cannot yet answer: when organizing
runs through a party that is not treated as an actor, who acts, and who answers? Schoeneborn, Dobusch and
Seidl (2026) close their theory of organizationality by asking "What does the recurrent charging or
discharging of a collective social address with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective
responsibility?" and whether attribution practices "always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ways
or vary depending on the particular context" (p. 14). Patriotta, Augustine and Voronov (2025) end theirs
with "the need to examine how legitimacy tests are co-produced by both human and technological actors in
the contemporary digital landscape" (p. 19). Two years earlier, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) had put the
question to algorithmic management in its plainest form: "But who or what is accountable when an
algorithm exercises decisions that occur faster and with a vastly larger number of inputs than a human
can physiologically comprehend and react to (Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137)?" (p. 14).]**

**[C: Behind the question of who acts lies a second one, which the same journal asks of the people in the
middle. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask, "With the introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be
disrupted because team members blindly follow guidance from the AI?" (p. 7), and call for "theorizing that captures what shapes team
participants' dynamic relations with AI to coordinate their work" (p. 8). Justesen and Plesner (2024) want
research to "account more fully for the emotional costs, mental loads and unintended tasks associated
with digitalization" (p. 22).]** While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of
"tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a cooptive, opaque, adaptive
intermediary. **[C: This paper asks the two questions as one. When the party that decides between two
people does not present itself as an actor, what must each of them be able to do to work with the other,
and where does the responsibility that party declines come to rest?]** [N1]

### Common ground

**[C: The askers share a picture of the setting.]** As company boundaries blur, platforms manage people
outside the firm through co-optation, enrolling them in systems of ratings and rankings (Stark & Vanden
Broeck, 2024). Platforms place an operator at the apex of ~~that~~ **[C: a]** triangle, co-opting
producers and users without formally employing either side (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Organizing now
increasingly depends on relations between people and learning algorithms (Stelmaszak et al., 2026). These
setups ~~can~~ **[C: may]** reduce what workers can see and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). **[C: The
askers also share a sense of what is unsettled in it: whether the technology in the middle is a thing or
an actor. Grodal, Ha, Hood and Rajunov (2024) end on "will we come to accept that generative AI is a
machine? Or will we continue to perceive it as closer to humans" (p. 8); Hsu and Bechky (2024) see "a
need to consider technology as an independent force or actor" (p. 12); Phillips, Kalvapalle and Kennedy
(2024) ask "How does the introduction of artificial persons into an organization change how we think about
what organizations are" (p. 11).]**

### Complication

**[C: The two sets of questions are asked from different places, and they do not meet. Those on actorhood
and accountability are asked from where the organizing is done—of the collective that is charged or
discharged, of the operator, of the field that categorizes the technology. Those on the people are asked
of a person and the technology: Hinds and von Krogh ask about "individuals' psychological responses to
the new relational fabric in which they are embedded" (p. 11), and Stark and Vanden Broeck name the
person's side of algorithmic management as "This new sociality—when the partner with which we are
communicating is an algorithm" (p. 16). In that last question the other person has dropped out: the
partner is the algorithm, not the colleague, customer or driver whose conduct the algorithm scores. In
*Organization Theory* the two sets have not yet met. Schoeneborn et al. (2026), who ask the newest
question about responsibility, cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) nor Hinds and von Krogh
(2024).]**

### Concern

**[C: The gap matters because the questions on responsibility cannot be answered from one side. If a
platform can discharge itself of actorhood, Schoeneborn et al.'s question, what that implies "for
individual as opposed to collective responsibility" (p. 14), turns on where the declined responsibility
lands, and it lands between people.]** When automated systems enter this exchange, they disrupt
traditional lines of workplace accountability by deflecting and redistributing responsibility onto the
people doing the work ~~(Karunakaran et al., 2022)~~ **[C: (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark & Vanden Broeck,
2024)]**. Frontline workers may then become what Elish (2019) calls a moral crumple zone, absorbing the
blame when the automated system malfunctions. **[C: Schoeneborn et al. gloss their question with
"organizational wrongdoing and scandals" (p. 14); the everyday case, a person deciding outcome by outcome
whom to hold to account, is not among the cases they name. Hinds and von Krogh's question about "a loss of
orientation for individuals" (p. 11) and Justesen and Plesner's "unintended tasks" (p. 22) point at the
same case from the other side without asking where responsibility goes. Stark and Vanden Broeck ask for
the "corresponding field of communication" that goes with the platform form (p. 15); the people who must
work out, between them, what the platform did are the part of that field no one has yet described.]** [N2]

### Course of action

Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine
Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are trying to preserve a "literacy"-based understanding. We
do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or relying
on a dyadic ontology that is blind to the emergent, binding power of the triad. **[C: I take the question
from the participant's side. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put "the platform operator at the apex and
producers and users at the other corners" (p. 14); I add that the two at the other corners depend on each
other.]** [N3] Subjecting people to algorithmic management without helping them build algorithmacy is
like handing books to people who cannot read. **[C: I therefore sort communicative sensibilities by what
stands between the people who use them.]** Oracy is the sensibility to navigate direct dyadic forms
through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the
competency to read and write texts that moderate chained or linked dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility
to navigate irreducible triads.

### Contribution

**[C: The paper answers three open questions in part. First, it takes Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026)
question on individual and collective responsibility (p. 14) to a case they do not name: when a platform
downplays its actorhood, the people it stands between have to place responsibility themselves, outcome by
outcome. Second, it supplies the person's part of the "corresponding field of communication" that Stark
and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 15) ask scholars to study, by placing algorithmacy after oracy and literacy on
a single criterion. Third, it gives Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) question about members who "blindly
follow guidance from the AI" (p. 7) a name for what they lack.]** ~~Furthermore, as~~ **[C: As]** workers
develop algorithmacy to navigate these pressures, their adaptive competence inadvertently preserves the
platform's opaque authority. Generative artificial intelligence does not change this platform dynamic; it
scales it, acting like a printing press that pushes automated control from gig jobs into professional
office work. [N4] **[C: The paper leaves open what Schoeneborn et al. also leave open, whether people's
readings charge the platform or discharge it (p. 14).]** We cannot read our way out of or into this, just
as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

## Decisions only you can make

- **[N1] The pair.** In hotel reviews and food delivery the two people meet once (Orlikowski & Scott,
  2014, AAM p. 42; Chong et al., 2024, p. 10). Do they depend on each other within one exchange, or across
  many? "Work with the other" has to hold for such a pair.
- **[N2] Cause or responsibility.** Is interpreting, the first of the three parts, about what caused an
  outcome or about who answers for it? The Concern turns on responsibility.
- **[N3] The apex.** The platform as a collective social address, or the algorithm as an
  "other-than-human" individual address (Schoeneborn et al., 2026, p. 11)? Grodal et al.'s and Hsu and
  Bechky's questions (Common ground) ask the field the same thing about the technology.
- **[N4] The printing press.** Hinds and von Krogh write that with GenAI "rather than being fixed, these
  functions and relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4). Add your sentence on
  why learning scales rather than changes the dynamic, or let the tension stand.
