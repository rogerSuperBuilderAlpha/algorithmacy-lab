**[C: Digital platforms decide between the people who work through them, and they decide while
downplaying that they are actors at all. Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) describe platforms that
"rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt
regulation" (p. 13); two years earlier, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) had found accountability on
platforms "distributed, deflected, and denied" (p. 14).]** While the academic debate has clearly moved past
the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a cooptive, opaque, adaptive
intermediary. **[C: This paper asks that question from the side of the people the intermediary stands
between. When the party that decides between two people is not treated as an actor, what must each of
them be able to do to work with the other, and where does the responsibility the platform declines come
to rest?]**

### Common ground

**[C: Schoeneborn et al. (2026) treat actorhood as a matter of degree. In their account "collective
actorhood is not something given but something ascribed in communication" (p. 6), and practices raise or
lower it by attributing actions either to the collective, "thereby 'charging' it with actorhood," or to
"individual (human or other-than-human) social addresses," which discharges the collective (p. 11).]** As
company boundaries blur, platforms manage people outside the firm through co-optation, enrolling them in
systems of ratings and rankings (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Platforms place an operator at the apex of
~~that~~ **[C: a]** triangle, co-opting producers and users without formally employing either side (Stark
& Vanden Broeck, 2024). **[C: The same triangle now reaches professional work.]** Organizing now
increasingly depends on relations between people and learning algorithms (Stelmaszak et al., 2026). These
setups can reduce what workers can see and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). **[C: Hinds and von Krogh
trace how a generative AI system placed among managers and employees sets off "relational cascades" and
"may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making
decisions" (p. 4).]**

### Complication

**[C: Both conversations look at the arrangement from where the organizing is done—the collective that is
or is not charged, the operator at the apex—and neither follows a decision to the people on either side
of it. Schoeneborn et al. (2026) report that "a high degree of digitalization can impede
anthropomorphization and thus also attributions of collective actorhood" (p. 10), so the party that
decides may be the party least likely to be charged with deciding. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put the
question of accountability directly—"But who or what is accountable when an algorithm exercises decisions
that occur faster and with a vastly larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend
and react to" (p. 14)—note that some scholars answer it with a legal theory of accountability
distributed across persons and algorithms, and conclude that on platforms accountability is deflected. When they turn to the people involved, they cast the algorithm as the person's
partner: they are interested "in algorithmic practices in which the technologies are themselves our
communication partners" and call this "new sociality" a critical component of further research (p. 16).
That picture has two parties, the person and the algorithm. The other person, whose conduct the algorithm
scores and whose standing it sets, drops out. The two conversations have not yet met: Schoeneborn et al.
(2026) cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) nor Hinds and von Krogh (2024).]**

### Concern

**[C: Deflected responsibility does not disappear. It lands on the people the platform stands
between.]** When automated systems enter this exchange, they disrupt traditional lines of workplace
accountability by deflecting and redistributing responsibility onto the people doing the work (Karunakaran
et al., 2022). In online marketplaces, an independent seller whose search visibility drops only sees a
buyer's review score, leaving the platform's hidden weighting formula completely concealed (Curchod et al.,
2020). **[C: The seller must settle alone whether the drop came from the platform, from the buyer, or from
both, and no one on the other side of the formula answers for it. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask the same
question of teams: "With the introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because
team members blindly follow guidance from the AI?" (p. 7). Schoeneborn et al. (2026) state its general
form and leave it open: "What does the recurrent charging or discharging of a collective social address
with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective responsibility?" (p. 14). Their own gloss
points to "organizational wrongdoing and scandals" (p. 14). The everyday case—a person who must decide,
outcome by outcome, whom to hold to account—is not among the cases they name.]** Frontline workers may
then become what Elish (2019) calls a moral crumple zone, absorbing the blame when the automated system
malfunctions. **[C: The stakes are not only the person's. Where people cannot tell the intermediary's
verdict from each other's conduct, they follow the system and lose sight of the counterpart, or they hold
the counterpart to account for what the system decided, and in either case the platform's discharge of
actorhood holds.]** `[NEEDS: one published case of a counterpart blamed for a platform's verdict, or cut
the second half of that sentence. Hohenstein & Jung (2020) show blame moving onto the software, the
opposite direction.]`

### Course of action

Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine
Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are trying to preserve a "literacy"-based understanding. We
do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or relying
on a dyadic ontology that is blind to the emergent, binding power of the triad. **[C: I take the question
from the participant's side and, with Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024), treat the platform as the apex of a
triangle whose other two corners are people who depend on each other.]** `[NEEDS: your decision on what
the apex is: the platform as a collective social address, or the algorithm as an "other-than-human"
individual address (Schoeneborn et al., 2026, p. 11). The two give opposite effects on actorhood, and the
versions now slide between "platform", "algorithm", "system" and "intermediary".]` Subjecting people to
algorithmic management without helping them build algorithmacy is like handing books to people who
cannot read. **[C: I therefore sort communicative sensibilities by what stands between the people who
use them.]** Oracy is the sensibility to navigate direct dyadic forms through speaking and hearing (or
feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts
that moderate chained or linked dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads.
**[C: I develop the argument through published studies of online marketplaces, ride-hailing and hotel
reviews, and carry it into the professional settings Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe.]**

### Contribution

**[C: The paper makes three contributions, each to a question already open in *Organization Theory*.
First, it describes the receiving end of discharged actorhood, which Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) question
on responsibility leaves open: when a platform is not charged as the actor, the people it stands between
must locate responsibility themselves, outcome by outcome.]** ~~Furthermore, as~~ **[C: As]** workers
develop algorithmacy to navigate these pressures, their adaptive competence inadvertently preserves the
platform's opaque authority. **[C: Second, it supplies the person's part of the communication field that
Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) ask scholars to study alongside the platform form—"further research should
study the corresponding field of communication" (p. 15)—by placing algorithmacy after oracy and literacy
on a single criterion. Third, it carries the question from gig platforms into professional work.]**
Generative artificial intelligence does not change this platform dynamic; it scales it, acting like a
printing press that pushes automated control from gig jobs into professional office work. **[C: The paper
offers no attribution practice in Schoeneborn et al.'s sense, since they define attribution practices as
"recurrent instances of communication" (p. 9) and a person's reading of an outcome need not be voiced. It
also leaves open whether such readings charge the platform or discharge it, a direction the authors ask
about themselves (p. 14).]** We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should
not—listen to a book.
