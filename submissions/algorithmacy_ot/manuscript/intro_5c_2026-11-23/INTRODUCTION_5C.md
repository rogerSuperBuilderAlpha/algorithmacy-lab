**[C: Digital platforms decide between the people they bring together, and some decide while downplaying
that they are actors. Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) describe platforms that "rather strategically
downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability or to preempt regulation" (p. 13); two
years earlier, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) had called accountability in algorithmic management
"distributed, deflected, and denied" (p. 14). Rahman, Karunakaran and Cameron (2024) describe the same
move in their review—"the platform owner can claim to merely be a conduit for a service" (preprint p. 6;
VoR page owed)—and eBay's sellers met it in disputes with buyers, where the company's answer was "we're
only a platform" (Curchod et al., 2020, AAM p. 21; VoR page owed).]** While the academic debate has
clearly moved past the surface of "tooling," it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a
cooptive, opaque, adaptive intermediary. **[C: This paper asks that question from the side of the people
the intermediary stands between. When the party that decides between two people does not present itself
as an actor, what must each of them be able to do to work with the other, and where does the
responsibility the platform declines come to rest?]**

### Common ground

**[C: Schoeneborn et al. (2026) treat actorhood as a matter of degree. In their account "collective
actorhood is not something given but something ascribed in communication" (p. 6), and practices raise or
lower it by attributing actorhood either to the collective, "thereby 'charging' it with actorhood," or to
"individual (human or other-than-human) social addresses," which discharges the collective (p. 11).]** As
company boundaries blur, platforms manage people outside the firm through co-optation, enrolling them in
systems of ratings and rankings (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Platforms place an operator at the apex of
~~that~~ **[C: a]** triangle, co-opting producers and users without formally employing either side (Stark
& Vanden Broeck, 2024). **[C: The arrangement is not confined to gig work: "Many professionals encounter
algorithmic management as users" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 9).]** Organizing now increasingly
depends on relations between people and learning algorithms (Stelmaszak et al., 2026). These setups
~~can~~ **[C: may]** reduce what workers can see and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). **[C: Hinds and
von Krogh suggest that a generative AI system placed among managers and employees may set off "relational
cascades" and "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI
is making decisions" (p. 4).]**

### Complication

**[C: Both conversations look at the arrangement from where the organizing is done—the collective that is
or is not charged, the operator at the apex—and neither asks what the people on either side of a decision
must do with it. Schoeneborn et al. (2026) name those who attribute actorhood—"external observers, such
as the media … or other external stakeholders," and the collective's own members (p. 9)—and do not say
where the two people a platform decides between belong. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put the question
of accountability directly—"But who or what is accountable when an algorithm exercises decisions that
occur faster and with a vastly larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend and
react to […]?" (p. 14)—and note that some scholars would answer it with a legal theory of accountability
distributed across persons and algorithms. When they turn to the people involved, they cast the algorithm
as the person's partner: they are interested "in algorithmic practices in which the technologies are
themselves our communication partners" and call this "new sociality" a critical component of further
research (p. 16). That picture has two parties, the person and the algorithm. The other person, whose
conduct the algorithm scores and whose standing it sets, drops out. In *Organization Theory* the two
conversations have not yet met: Schoeneborn et al. (2026) cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) nor
Hinds and von Krogh (2024).]** **[HOLD: Outside the journal the parts of the question are studied apart.
Fu et al. (2025) and Zheng and Zhu (2026) measure whom a customer holds responsible after a service
failure. Lee et al. (2015) and Curchod et al. (2020) record what the blamed driver or seller bears.
Buhmann et al. (2021) test how calling an organization a "platform" bears on the collective actorhood
individuals attribute to it, and Rahman et al. (2024) review how providers and customers can hold a
platform owner to account. None of them asks what the two people must be able to do to place
responsibility and go on working together, or what their placing does to the platform's standing as an
actor.]**

### Concern

**[C: Deflected responsibility does not disappear. The platform leaves it to be placed in the exchange
between the two people it stands between.]** When automated systems enter this exchange, they disrupt
traditional lines of workplace accountability by deflecting and redistributing responsibility onto the
people doing the work ~~(Karunakaran et al., 2022)~~ **[C: (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark & Vanden Broeck,
2024)]**. In online marketplaces, an independent seller whose search visibility drops only sees a buyer's
review score, leaving the platform's hidden weighting formula ~~completely~~ concealed (Curchod et al.,
2020). **[C: The seller must settle alone whether the drop came from the platform, from the buyer, or from
both, and no one on the other side of the formula answers for it: "There's no human mediation. It's a
robot" (Curchod et al., 2020, AAM p. 22; VoR page owed). Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask a related question
of teams: "With the introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team
members blindly follow guidance from the AI?" (p. 7). Schoeneborn et al. (2026) state its general form
and leave it open: "What does the recurrent charging or discharging of a collective social address with
actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective responsibility?" (p. 14). Their own gloss points
to "organizational wrongdoing and scandals" (p. 14). The everyday case—a person who must decide, outcome
by outcome, whom to hold to account—is not among the cases they name.]** Frontline workers may then become
what Elish (2019) calls a moral crumple zone, absorbing the blame when the automated system malfunctions.
**[C: The stakes are not only the person's. Where people cannot tell the intermediary's verdict from each
other's conduct, they hold the counterpart to account for what the system decided: drivers report that
passengers "misattributed system faults and negative experiences that drivers could not control to
drivers themselves, which in turn resulted in lower ratings" (Lee et al., 2015, p. 1608).]** **[HOLD: The
direction is not fixed. Where the platform intermediates heavily, or is shown assigning the work,
customers are more likely to place responsibility on the platform (Zheng & Zhu, 2026), "even after
recognizing the individual provider as the source of the problem" (Fu et al., 2025).]**
`[NEEDS: your decision whether interpreting, the first of the three parts, is about what caused an outcome
or about who answers for it. The concern now slides between the two ("whether the drop came from the
platform" is cause; "whom to hold to account" is responsibility), and Fu et al.'s finding is that knowing
the first does not settle the second.]`

### Course of action

Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine
Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are trying to preserve a "literacy"-based understanding. We
do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or relying
on a dyadic ontology that is blind to the emergent, binding power of the triad. **[C: I take the question
from the participant's side. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put "the platform operator at the apex and
producers and users at the other corners" (p. 14); I add that the two at the other corners depend on each
other.]** `[NEEDS: your decision on what the apex is: the platform as a collective social address, or the
algorithm as an "other-than-human" individual address (Schoeneborn et al., 2026, p. 11). Sources that bear
on it: the apex is "the platform operator" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14); workers "ultimately
recognize the organization (not the algorithm) as the exchange partner" (Bujold et al., 2026, p. 15); a
seller calls eBay "a robot" (Curchod et al., 2020, AAM p. 22).]` Subjecting people to algorithmic
management without helping them build algorithmacy is like handing books to people who cannot read.
**[C: I therefore sort communicative sensibilities by what stands between the people who use them.]**
Oracy is the sensibility to navigate direct dyadic forms through speaking and hearing (or feeling and
tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate
chained or linked dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. **[C: I develop
the argument through published studies of online marketplaces (Curchod et al., 2020), ride-hailing
(Cameron, 2024; Lee et al., 2015) and hotel reviews (Karunakaran et al., 2022; Orlikowski & Scott, 2014),
and carry it into the professional settings Hinds and von Krogh (2024) describe.]**

### Contribution

**[C: The paper makes three contributions, each to a question already open in *Organization Theory*.
First, it takes Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) question on individual and collective responsibility (p. 14)
to a case they do not name: when a platform downplays its actorhood, the people it stands between have to
place responsibility themselves, outcome by outcome.]** ~~Furthermore, as~~ **[C: As]** workers develop
algorithmacy to navigate these pressures, their adaptive competence inadvertently preserves the
platform's opaque authority. **[C: Second, it supplies the person's part of the communication field that
Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) ask scholars to study as conjoined with the platform form—"further research
should study the corresponding field of communication" (p. 15)—by placing algorithmacy after oracy and
literacy on a single criterion. Third, it carries the question from gig platforms into professional work,
where "the study of the dynamics of the relations between professional expertise and algorithmic
practices is in its infancy" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 9).]** Generative artificial intelligence
does not change this platform dynamic; it scales it, acting like a printing press that pushes automated
control from gig jobs into professional office work. `[NEEDS: your sentence on why learning scales the
dynamic rather than changing it, or your decision to let the tension stand. Hinds and von Krogh, whom the
introduction cites four times, write that with GenAI "rather than being fixed, these functions and
relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4).]` **[C: The paper offers no
attribution practice in Schoeneborn et al.'s sense, since they define attribution practices as "recurrent
instances of communication" (p. 9) and a person's reading of an outcome need not be voiced. It also leaves
open whether such readings charge the platform or discharge it, a direction the authors ask about
themselves (p. 14).]** We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen
to a book.

## What the gap search found (S14a–e)

- **The narrow claim holds.** In Consensus, OpenAlex and Crossref, with citation snowballs from six works
  (23 Nov 2026), I found no work that treats the placing of responsibility by the people a platform
  stands between as an attribution of the platform's actorhood, or as something a person must be able to
  do to go on working with the other. Scholar Gateway refused access and Semantic Scholar returned nothing
  usable; rerun both before submission. The Fable ruling offers this as an optional search sentence for a
  methods note, not for the introduction.
- **The parts are studied.** Customers' blame after platform failures (Fu et al., 2025; Zheng & Zhu,
  2026, abstracts); blamed drivers and sellers (Lee et al., 2015; Curchod et al., 2020; Muralidhar et al.,
  2022; Chan, 2022); individuals' attribution of actorhood to platforms (Buhmann et al., 2021, abstract);
  platform accountability from the providers' and customers' side (Rahman et al., 2024). The introduction
  may therefore not say that the everyday case is unstudied, that no one follows a decision to both
  sides, or that responsibility lands on people as a rule.
- **Net effect:** a stronger question and a narrower claim. Blame moves in both directions depending on
  who attributes and what the platform's interface lets them see, which is what gives the person something
  to be able to do.
