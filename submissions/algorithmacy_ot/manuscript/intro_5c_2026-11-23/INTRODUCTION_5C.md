When a digital platform manages an exchange between two people while claiming it is just a neutral
software tool, who is actually in charge, and who answers when something goes wrong? Schoeneborn,
Dobusch, and Seidl (2026) study how groups come to be treated as unified decision-makers—what they call
"collective actorhood"—and ask what happens to individual versus organizational responsibility when a
company repeatedly claims or sheds that status, and whether those patterns change depending on the
situation **[C: —"What does the recurrent charging or discharging of a collective social address with
actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective responsibility?" and "whether these always charge
or discharge actorhood in the same ways or vary depending on the particular context"]** (p. 14).
Patriotta, Augustine, and Voronov (2025) ask how humans and technologies jointly establish whether an
organization's practices count as legitimate **[C: , pointing to "the need to examine how legitimacy tests
are co-produced by both human and technological actors in the contemporary digital landscape"]** (p. 19).
Two years earlier, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) asked who or what is accountable when an algorithm
makes decisions faster and across far more data points than any human can understand or react to
**[C: : "But who or what is accountable when an algorithm exercises decisions that occur faster and with a
vastly larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend and react to […]?"]** (p. 14,
adapting Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137).

Behind this question of organizational responsibility sits a second problem about the people caught in
the middle. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask whether generative AI breaks down teamwork—specifically the
natural, mutual adjustment coworkers rely on—when team members follow AI advice without questioning it
**[C: : "With the introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members
blindly follow guidance from the AI?"]** (p. 7), and they call for research on how people coordinate with
AI on the job **[C: —"theorizing that captures what shapes team participants' dynamic relations with AI to
coordinate their work"]** (p. 8). Justesen and Plesner (2024) argue that researchers need to measure the
stress, mental effort, and ~~extra unpaid~~ **[C: unintended]** tasks that digital tools push onto workers
**[C: : "we could account more fully for the emotional costs, mental loads and unintended tasks associated
with digitalization"]** (p. 22). Organizational researchers have moved past treating software as a passive
tool, yet they still have not explained how two people work together through an opaque, self-adjusting
system that pulls them in while refusing to take responsibility for the outcomes. This paper combines
those two questions into one: when the platform deciding between two people refuses to act as an
accountable party, what must each person be able to do to work with the other, and where does the
responsibility the platform refuses actually land?

**Common ground**

These researchers agree on how the workplace is changing. As company boundaries blur, platforms control
people outside the firm without hiring them, using ratings and rankings to steer how independent workers
and customers behave (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Picture a triangle: the platform operator sits at the
top point, managing producers and users at the two bottom corners without formally employing either side
(Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Getting work done increasingly depends on ongoing interactions between
people and learning algorithms—software that updates its own rules based on new data (Stelmaszak et al.,
2026)—which can limit what workers are able to see and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). At the same
time, researchers disagree on what kind of entity the technology in the middle actually is. Grodal, Ha,
Hood, and Rajunov (2024) ask whether people will eventually accept generative AI as a machine or keep
treating it like a human being **[C: : "will we come to accept that generative AI is a machine? Or will we
continue to perceive it as closer to humans"]** (p. 8). Hsu and Bechky (2024) argue that scholars need to
treat technology as an independent force or actor in its own right **[C: —"a need to consider technology
as an independent force or actor in the relational network of agents that collectively influence the
social construction of categories"]** (p. 12), while Phillips, Kalvapalle, and Kennedy (2024) ask how
bringing artificial agents into a workplace changes our basic definition of what an organization is
**[C: : "How does the introduction of artificial persons into an organization change how we think about
what organizations are, how they are constructed and how they function?"]** (p. 11).

**Complication**

These two groups of questions look at the problem from opposite angles and never connect. Questions about
responsibility and organizational status are asked from the top down—looking at the company claiming or
shedding blame, the platform operator at the top of the triangle, or how scholars classify the
technology. Questions about the people involved, by contrast, look only at a single person interacting
with a machine. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) study how individuals psychologically react to working inside
these new technical setups **[C: , asking "what are individuals' psychological responses to the new
relational fabric in which they are embedded?"]** (p. 11), while Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe
algorithmic management as a "new sociality" where "the partner with which we are communicating is an
algorithm" **[C: and name it "a critical component of further research on algorithmic management"]**
(p. 16). Treating the interaction as a pair between one person and one algorithm pushes the other human
participant out of view. The partner is treated as the software itself, rather than the coworker,
customer, or driver whose behavior the software is scoring. Inside *Organization Theory*, these two
conversations remain completely separate; Schoeneborn et al. (2026) cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck
(2024) nor Hinds and von Krogh (2024).

**Concern**

You cannot figure out where responsibility goes by looking at only one corner of the triangle. When a
platform sheds its status as an accountable actor, Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) question about individual
versus organizational responsibility (p. 14) comes down to where that refused responsibility lands—and it
lands in the space between the two people trying to work or transact together. When automated systems
enter an exchange, they break normal chains of accountability by shifting blame onto the people doing the
work (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Frontline workers then become what Elish
(2019) calls a moral crumple zone: just as the front end of a car is built to crumple and absorb the impact
of a crash to protect the frame, workers absorb the blame and penalties when an automated system fails.
Schoeneborn et al. illustrate their puzzle with major "organizational wrongdoing and scandals" (p. 14),
missing the routine, everyday situation where an ordinary person has to decide, one transaction at a
time, whom to blame. Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) concern that individuals lose their bearings at work
**[C: —"What are the conditions under which progressive encapsulation leads to a loss of orientation for
individuals"]** (p. 11) and Justesen and Plesner's (2024) "unintended tasks" (p. 22) describe this exact
burden from below without tracing where the responsibility actually goes. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024)
ask researchers to study the "corresponding field of communication" that comes with the platform model
**[C: —"further research should study the corresponding field of communication"]** (p. 15); the people
left to figure out between themselves what the platform just did are the missing half of that field.

**Course of action**

Building on organizational theory alongside models of Human-Machine Communication and AI literacy, I argue
that current frameworks try to force a reading-and-writing model of competence onto a problem that
literacy cannot solve. They do this by ignoring the third party in the exchange, treating the algorithmic
intermediary as a neutral pass-through channel, or assuming every interaction is a two-way exchange—a view
that misses the binding pressure created when a third party sets the rules of a two-person relationship.
I look at the problem from the participant's corner at the bottom of the triangle. Where Stark and Vanden
Broeck (2024) put "the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners"
(p. 14), I show that the two people at the bottom corners depend on each other to make the exchange work.
Subjecting people to algorithmic management without helping them build algorithmacy—the practical ability
to read, interpret, and coordinate through algorithmic intermediaries—is like handing books to people who
cannot read. I sort human communication abilities according to what stands between the people using them.
Oracy is the ability to coordinate directly face-to-face through speaking and hearing, or physical gesture
and response. Literacy is the ability to read and write texts that coordinate across linked two-person
exchanges over time and distance. Algorithmacy is the ability to coordinate inside three-way relationships
where two people cannot simply bypass the algorithmic third party mediating between them.

**Contribution**

First, this paper extends Schoeneborn et al.'s (2026) question on individual and organizational
responsibility (p. 14) to an everyday setting they do not examine: when a platform downplays its role as
an actor, the people caught between its decisions have to assign responsibility themselves, one outcome at
a time. As workers build algorithmacy to handle these pressures, however, their adaptive skill
unintentionally keeps the platform's opaque authority running smoothly. Second, it supplies the human
participant's half of the "corresponding field of communication" that Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024,
p. 15) ask scholars to study, placing algorithmacy alongside oracy and literacy under a single organizing
rule. Third, it gives Hinds and von Krogh's (2024) puzzle of team members who blindly follow AI guidance
**[C: ("blindly follow guidance from the AI")]** (p. 7) a clear name for what those workers are missing.
Generative artificial intelligence does not change this platform dynamic; it scales it, acting like a
printing press that pushes automated control from gig work into white-collar offices. At the same time,
the paper leaves open a question Schoeneborn et al. (2026) also leave unresolved: whether people's
everyday judgments ultimately pin responsibility back onto the platform or let it off the hook (p. 14). We
cannot read our way out of or into this condition, just as we cannot listen to a book.

## Flags in your text (reported, not fixed)

1. **"when a company repeatedly claims or sheds that status".** In Schoeneborn et al. actorhood is
   charged or discharged by *attribution practices*, "recurrent instances of communication" by external
   observers or members (p. 9), not by the company claiming it. The company downplaying its own status is
   their separate remark about platforms (p. 13). Their question (now quoted) says "the recurrent charging
   or discharging of a collective social address". A minimal fix would be "when that status is repeatedly
   attributed to it or withdrawn".
2. **Justesen & Plesner "argue that researchers need to measure".** Their words are "we could account more
   fully for" (p. 22): a suggestion, and "account for", not "measure". The quotation now shows it; the verb
   is yours to soften.
3. **Hsu & Bechky's call is about categories.** They ask for technology as an actor "in the relational
   network of agents that collectively influence the social construction of categories" (p. 12), not in
   general. The full quotation now shows the scope.
4. **"which can limit what workers are able to see and control".** Hinds & von Krogh hedge: the system
   "may ultimately obscure" visibility (p. 4).
5. **"these two conversations remain completely separate".** The evidence is that Schoeneborn et al. cite
   neither paper, and the run found no citation among the three. "Completely" claims more than that.
6. **Algorithmacy is now defined three ways** in the Course of action: "the practical ability to read,
   interpret, and coordinate through algorithmic intermediaries"; "the ability to coordinate inside
   three-way relationships where two people cannot simply bypass the algorithmic third party"; and in the
   brief, "the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that is
   opaque, binding, and adaptive". Your text now says *ability* throughout, which settles the old
   sensibility-or-ability flag.
7. **Literacy and "linked two-person exchanges"** still conflicts with your 8 Oct ruling that put chained
   dyads under oracy.
8. **Open questions vs claims.** Two sentences still rest on claims, not questions: "When automated systems
   enter an exchange…" (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016, p. 3772; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14) and the
   Elish sentence (your car analogy is Elish's own, pp. 40–41). Both are checked and can stay.
9. **The printing press sentence** still sits against Hinds & von Krogh's "rather than being fixed, these
   functions and relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4).
10. **The [C: …] insertions repeat your gloss.** Each quotation follows your plain-language version of the
    same question. If you want the quotation alone, cut your gloss; if you want the gloss alone, cut the
    quotation and keep the page.
