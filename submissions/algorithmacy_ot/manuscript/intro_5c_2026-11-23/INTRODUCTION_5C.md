When a digital platform is facilitating an exchange between two parties and yet claims to be nothing
more than a neutral software tool, who is actually in control and who is the one to be held to account if
things go wrong? Schoeneborn, Dobusch and Seidl (2026) look at how groups come to be treated as single
decision-making units (what they term “collective actorhood”) and ask what happens when that status is
repeatedly ascribed to or taken away from an organization: “What does the recurrent charging or
discharging of a collective social address with actorhood imply for individual as opposed to collective
responsibility?” and “whether these always charge or discharge actorhood in the same ~~way or whether it
varies~~ **[C: ways or vary]** depending on the particular context” (p. 14). Patriotta, Augustine and
Voronov (2025) highlight “the need to examine how legitimacy tests” (the everyday tests by which a
practice or organization is judged to be legitimate) “are co-produced by both human and technological
actors in the contemporary digital landscape” (p. 19). Two years prior, Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) put
the issue of accountability quite simply: “But who or what is accountable when an algorithm ~~makes
decisions that occur at a rate and with a volume of inputs far beyond what a human can possibly comprehend
and react to in a physiological sense~~ **[C: exercises decisions that occur faster and with a vastly
larger number of inputs than a human can physiologically comprehend and react to […]]**?” (p. 14,
adapting Vesa & Tienari, 2022, p. 1137).

Underpinning that question of organizational responsibility is a second issue: the people in the middle.
Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask whether generative AI in fact undermines emergent interdependence—the
natural, mutual adjustment that colleagues make to each other as they get on with the work: “With the
introduction of GenAI, might emergent interdependence be disrupted because team members ~~simply follow
the AI’s guidance~~ **[C: blindly follow guidance from the AI]**?” (p. 7). They call for “theorizing that
captures what shapes team participants’ dynamic relations with AI to coordinate their work” (p. 8).
Justesen and Plesner (2024) suggest that “we could account **[C: more fully]** for the emotional costs,
mental loads and unintended tasks associated with ~~digitalisation~~ **[C: digitalization]**” (p. 22).
Organizational researchers have long moved beyond the idea of software as a mere tool. They haven’t yet
really explained how two people work together through an opaque, self-adjusting system that pulls them in
and yet will not take responsibility for the outcomes. This paper brings those two questions together: if
the platform that is making the decision between two people is itself refusing to be the accountable
party, what must each person be able to do in order to work with the other**[C: ,]** and where does the
responsibility that the platform is refusing to take actually reside?

Common ground

The researchers are in agreement about the nature of the workplace as it is changing. As the boundaries
of the workplace become increasingly porous, platforms are able to manage people outside the firm without
actually employing them. They do this via ratings and rankings which in turn dictate how the independent
workers and the customers behave (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Imagine a triangle with the platform
operator at the apex and the producers and users at the two bottom corners. The platform operator is in
control of both without formally employing either of them (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Getting work
done is increasingly going to be a matter of the ongoing interaction between people and learning
algorithms (Stelmaszak et al., 2026)**[C: ,]** which may well be opaque to the people who are doing the
work (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). There is**[C: ,]** however**[C: ,]** a divergence of opinion as to what
kind of entity the technology in the middle actually is. Grodal, Ha, Hood and Rajunov (2024) pose the
question: “~~Will~~ **[C: [W]ill]** we come to accept that generative AI is a machine? Or will we continue
to perceive it as closer to ~~a human?~~ **[C: humans […]?]**” (p. 8). Hsu and Bechky (2024) argue for “a
need to consider technology as an independent force or actor in the relational network of agents that
collectively influence the social construction of categories” (p. 12). Phillips, Kalvapalle and Kennedy
(2024) ask**[C: ,]** “How does the introduction of artificial persons into an organization change ~~our
understanding of what an organisation is, how it is constructed and how it functions~~ **[C: how we think
about what organizations are, how they are constructed and how they function]**?” (p. 11).

Complication

These two sets of questions approach the issue from completely opposite directions and never come
together. The questions of responsibility and organizational status are all posed from the top down (the
company, the platform operator at the apex of the triangle, how the scholars ~~categorise~~
**[C: categorize]** the technology). The questions about the people are all posed from the bottom up,
looking at just one person in interaction with a machine. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask**[C: ,]**
“~~What are the individuals’~~ **[C: [W]hat are individuals’]** psychological responses to the new
relational fabric in which they are embedded?” (p. 11). Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe
algorithmic management as a “new ~~sociality “—when~~ **[C: sociality”—when]** the partner with whom you
are communicating is an ~~algorithm-~~ **[C: algorithm—]** and refer to it as “a critical component of
further research on algorithmic management” (p. 16). ~~By treating~~ **[C: When]** the interaction
**[C: is treated]** as a ~~one to one~~ **[C: one-to-one]** between a person and an algorithm, the other
human is completely invisible. The partner is the algorithm itself rather than the colleague, customer or
driver whose behavior the algorithm is scoring. Inside *Organization Theory***[C: ,]** these two
conversations remain completely separate; Schoeneborn et al. (2026) cite neither Stark and Vanden Broeck
(2024) nor Hinds and von Krogh (2024).

Concern

You can’t work out where the responsibility goes by looking at just one corner of the triangle. When a
platform ceases to be an accountable actor, Schoeneborn et al.’s (2026) question about individual versus
collective responsibility ~~(p.14)~~ **[C: (p. 14)]** is really just a question of where that
responsibility is dumped. It gets dumped in the space between the two people who are trying to work
together or transact. When an automated system enters the exchange**[C: ,]** it breaks the normal chains
of accountability by shifting the blame onto the people doing the work (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Stark &
Vanden Broeck, 2024). The frontline worker then becomes the moral crumple zone (Elish, 2019) in exactly
the same way that the front of a car is designed to crumple and absorb the impact of a crash to protect
the rest of the car. The worker absorbs the blame and the penalties when the automated system fails.
Schoeneborn et al. pose their puzzle in terms of **[C: major]** “~~major organisational~~
**[C: organizational]** wrongdoing and scandals” ~~(p.14)~~ **[C: (p. 14)]**, which is a bit of a
misrepresentation of the reality of the situation. What we’re talking about is the routine, day-to-day
situation where a person has to make a decision on a ~~transaction by transaction~~
**[C: transaction-by-transaction]** basis as to who to blame. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) ask “What are the
conditions under which progressive encapsulation leads to a loss of orientation for ~~individuals?”~~
**[C: individuals […]?”]** ~~(p.11)~~ **[C: (p. 11)]**, and Justesen and Plesner (2024) point to
“unintended tasks” ~~(p.22)~~ **[C: (p. 22)]**. Both of these describe the burden that comes from below
without actually going on to say where the responsibility is. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) say that
“further research should study the corresponding field of communication” that is generated by the
platform model ~~(p.15)~~ **[C: (p. 15)]**; the people who are left to work out between themselves what
the platform has just done are the missing half of that field.

Course of action

Building on organizational theory and models of Human-Machine Communication and AI literacy, I would argue
that the current frameworks are attempting to force a reading and writing model of competence onto a
problem that literacy simply cannot solve. They do this by either ignoring the third party altogether
(treating the algorithmic intermediary as a neutral pass-through), or by assuming that every interaction
is a two-way exchange. That latter assumption fails to account for the binding pressure that is created
when a third party is in fact setting the rules of the two-person relationship. I’m looking at the
problem from the participant’s point of view at the bottom of the triangle. Where Stark and Vanden Broeck
(2024) have the “~~ platform operator at the apex and the producers~~ **[C: the platform operator at the
apex and producers]** and users at the other corners” (p. 14), I would say that the two people at the
bottom corners are dependent on each other in order for the exchange to work. To subject people to
algorithmic management without in any way enabling them to develop algorithmacy is akin to handing books
to people who can’t read. I ~~categorise~~ **[C: categorize]** human communication abilities according to
what lies between the people using them. Oracy is the ability to ~~co-ordinate~~ **[C: coordinate]**
directly face to face via speaking and hearing, or via physical gesture and response. Literacy is the
ability to read and write texts that ~~co-ordinate~~ **[C: coordinate]** across ~~two person~~
**[C: two-person]** exchanges that are separated in time and space. Algorithmacy is the practical ability
to read, interpret and ~~co-ordinate~~ **[C: coordinate]** within ~~three way~~ **[C: three-way]**
relationships where two people cannot get round the algorithmic third party.

Contribution

First of all, this paper takes Schoeneborn et al.’s (2026) question of individual and organizational
responsibility (p. 14) and extends it to an everyday context which they don’t actually look at. When a
platform downplays its own role as an actor, the people who are caught in the middle have to assign
responsibility ~~to themselves~~ **[C: themselves]**, one instance at a time. As the worker develops their
algorithmacy in order to cope with the pressures of the work, that very adaptation of the worker actually
serves to keep the platform’s opaque authority going. Second, it provides the human participant’s side of
the “corresponding field of communication” which Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024, p. 15) ask us to
consider, positioning algorithmacy as a parallel form to oracy and literacy under a single overarching
rule. ~~Thirdly~~ **[C: Third,]** it provides a name for the thing that Hinds and von Krogh (2024, p. 7)
refer to as team members who “blindly follow ~~the guidance of the AI~~ **[C: guidance from the AI]**”.
Generative AI doesn’t change this platform dynamic at all; it simply scales it. It’s like a printing press
that takes the automated control that already exists in the gig economy and pushes it into the
~~white collar~~ **[C: white-collar]** office. At the same time**[C: ,]** the paper doesn’t resolve the
very question that Schoeneborn et al. (2026) also leave hanging: do our everyday ~~judgements~~
**[C: judgments]** ultimately lead us to assign responsibility back to the platform or do they lead us to
absolve it (p. 14)? We can’t read our way out of or into that condition, just as we can’t listen to a
book.

## Flags (not grammar, not fixed)

1. **"which is a bit of a misrepresentation of the reality of the situation."** Schoeneborn et al. do not
   claim that their question concerns only scandals; they say exploring it "would be especially salient in
   cases of organizational wrongdoing and scandals" (p. 14). Calling that a misrepresentation is stronger
   than the text supports, and David Seidl is an associate editor of *Organization Theory* (p. 19 of their
   article). Version 5 had: "missing the routine, everyday situation…".
2. **Register.** The contractions (can't, doesn't, it's, I'm, they haven't, don't, what we're) and the
   phrases "dumped", "a bit of", "at all" read as spoken. Not errors; your call for a theory article.
3. **"how the independent workers and the customers behave" via ratings that "dictate" it.** Stark &
   Vanden Broeck say platform operators "enroll users and providers in a system of ratings" (p. 14);
   "dictate" is stronger than their wording.
4. **"Getting work done is increasingly going to be a matter of…"** Stelmaszak et al. write that "AI is
   increasingly embedded in organizational life" (p. 335); "going to be" makes it a forecast.
5. **"which may well be opaque to the people who are doing the work (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024)".** Their
   wording is that the system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on
   which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). Close; "may well" is yours.
6. **"Inside Organization Theory these two conversations remain completely separate".** The evidence is
   that Schoeneborn et al. cite neither paper; "completely" claims more.
7. **Literacy "across two-person exchanges that are separated in time and space"** still sits against your
   8 Oct ruling on chained dyads.
8. **The printing press sentence** still sits against Hinds & von Krogh: "rather than being fixed, these
   functions and relations are likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4).
