# Algorithmacy: Working With Another Person Through a Third That Judges Both

## Abstract

People at work increasingly depend on one another through algorithmic systems that evaluate them. Accounts of what a person needs in order to manage this come in two kinds. One treats the system as the thing a person deals with and loses sight of the other person. The other treats the system as a channel between two people and leaves it nothing to decide. Organization theorists have described the arrangement itself as a relation of three, and field studies have recorded what people do inside it, but no account names the ability it asks of them or defines that ability over all three parties. I define it. Algorithmacy is the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both by a rule that is hidden, cannot be questioned, changes without notice, and takes their dealings with each other as its evidence. Building on Simmel's account of the third party, I distinguish three forms in which people work together: direct and chained dyads, which rest on oracy; dyads moderated by a text, which rest on literacy; and triads of this kind, which rest on algorithmacy. I then argue that algorithmacy has three parts—interpreting results, specifying intent for two readers at once, and keeping track of the rule—and tie each part to the feature of the intermediary that calls for it and to the condition of coordination it helps recover. The account says when the ability is needed and when it is not, and it sets out questions for research.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy, algorithmic management, coordination, literacy, platforms, Simmel, triad

## Introduction

Much work that once passed directly between two people now passes through an algorithmic system that evaluates it. A host and a guest on a rental platform review each other, and the platform decides what later users see of each. A seller and a buyer on a marketplace deal with each other under a scoring rule that neither wrote. A manager and an employee may soon meet across an evaluation that generative AI helped produce (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024). Organizational scholars have shown how such systems direct, evaluate, and discipline the people who work under them (Cameron, 2024; Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2021; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Vallas & Schor, 2020), and how emerging technologies change the relations among people at work (Anthony et al., 2023; Bailey et al., 2022; Faraj & Pachidi, 2021). This paper asks a narrower question: what must a person be able to do to work with another person through a system of this kind?

Existing answers start from one of two pictures. In the first, the system is what the person deals with. Guzman and Lewis (2020) propose to study AI systems as "communicative subjects, instead of mere interactive objects" (p. 71); Long and Magerko (2020) list what a person should understand about AI; studies of professionals record how they handle a tool whose reasoning they cannot see (Lebovitz et al., 2022); and theories of how people and AI coordinate take the pair of them as the unit (Lu & Yan, 2026; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). This picture grew from the finding that people respond to computers socially (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), and it contains one person and one machine. In the second picture, the system is a channel between two people. Walther (1992, 1996) shows people learning to carry personal meaning in plain text, Spitzberg (2006) describes competence at communicating through a computer, and Hancock et al. (2020) add a system that "operates on behalf of a communicator" (p. 90). This picture contains two people and a technology that carries or helps.

Each picture drops something the arrangements above contain. The first has no second person. The second has no system that decides. Several accounts come closer than either. Sutherland et al. (2020) count building relationships with clients among their "gig literacies"; Rahman (2021) shows workers unsure whether a platform or a client stands behind an evaluation; and Gibbs et al. (2021) object that the machine carries the rules of whoever deployed it. The closest is Jarrahi and Sutherland's (2019) "algorithmic competency," which they define as "workers' understanding of algorithms that assign and assess work conducted on gig platforms, and learning how to work with and around those algorithms" (p. 9) [AUTHOR: page 9 is the page in the authors' deposited copy; check it against the LNCS pagination, 578–589]. Their interviews are full of clients. Their figure has two nodes, the worker and the algorithm. The observations in this literature are triadic and the constructs are dyadic.

Organization theory has the triad. Sociologists of service work described a triangle of worker, manager, and customer more than three decades ago and have debated since how far the customer reaches into the organization of work (Fuller & Smith, 1991; Havard et al., 2009; Korczynski, 2013; Lopez, 2010). Students of platforms have redrawn it with an algorithm at one corner. Kornberger et al. (2017) show platforms coordinating people they do not employ through rating and ranking; Curchod et al. (2020) describe buyers and the platform as in effect allied against the seller; Cameron (2024) draws an "algorithmic labor triangle" (p. 466); Chan (2022) writes of a "trilateral relationship" (p. 1) among platforms, workers, and customers; and Cameron and Rahman (2022) show that workers' dealings with customers are a way of influencing the algorithm. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024), following Stark and Pais (2020), give the form its name: "whereas actors in hierarchies command, in markets they contract, and in networks collaborate, on platforms they are co-opted" (p. 1). This work establishes what the arrangement is and how it controls people, and it records much of what they do in response (Bellesia et al., 2023; Bucher et al., 2021; Cameron, 2022). It does not name one ability or define it over the three parties. Stelmaszak et al. (2026) place the relevant capability in the organization and call it "not merely a human trait or skill" (p. 346), which leaves the person's part undescribed.

I describe the person's part in two steps. First, I sort the forms in which people do interdependent work by how many parties the form has and by what the thing between two people does to them. Following Simmel (1902a, 1902b, 1950), I hold that a relation of three differs in kind from a relation of two. On this sorting, oracy belongs to direct dyads and chains of them, literacy to dyads moderated by a text, and algorithmacy to triads in which the third evaluates and binds both. Second, I argue that algorithmacy has three parts, each called for by a feature of the intermediary and each a partial recovery of one of the conditions on which coordination depends (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

The claim is limited in three ways. The paper reports no data; its cases come from published studies and from one thought experiment, and I say which is which. The parts of the ability are not all new, since two of the three have been named before; what I add is the definition over the triad and the tie between each part and a feature of the intermediary. I searched Consensus, OpenAlex, Crossref, and the 2022–2026 contents of *Administrative Science Quarterly*, *Organization Science*, *Organization Studies*, and *Organization Theory*, and found no work that names this ability or defines it in this way. [AUTHOR: this sentence reports the lab's search of 9 October 2026; confirm it, and decide how to disclose your own earlier online use of the term without breaking anonymity.] Finally, triads with a third that judges both parties are older than software, and I do not claim otherwise. I claim that algorithmic systems have made a demanding version of that triad an ordinary condition of work.

## Simmel on the Third Party

Simmel argued that the number of members conditions the form of a group (1902a, 1902b; see also 1950). In a relation of two, each party faces only the other, and whatever the relation becomes depends on what each does toward the other. A third member changes more than the count. The two no longer have their relation to themselves: the third can stand between them, side with one of them, or use both.

Simmel described three positions a third can take. The first is the non-partisan, who either mediates or arbitrates. The mediator works on the parties' relation and leaves the outcome to them; Simmel wrote that such a third "seeks to eliminate himself" (1902b, p. 167) and acts "as a means to the ends of the group" (p. 174). The arbitrator is different in one respect that matters for everything that follows:

> So long as the third party works as a real mediator, the ending of the conflict rests finally in the hands of the parties themselves. By choice of the arbitrator they have put this ultimate decision out of their own hands. (Simmel, 1902b, p. 172)

The second position is the *tertius gaudens*, the third who profits from the others' disagreement and turns their dealings to its own purposes. The third position is that of the party who divides the other two in order to rule them (Simmel, 1902b).

I take three points from this account. The first is the line between mediating and arbitrating, which is a line about who holds the decision. Under a mediator the two parties still settle matters between themselves. Under an arbitrator the third settles them. I draw the boundary between a moderated dyad and a triad at the same place. The second point is that the arbitrator's power is conferred. The parties choose the arbitrator, and Simmel observed that "the voluntary appeal to an arbitrator, to whom the parties subordinate themselves a priori, presupposes a greater subjective confidence in the objectivity of the judgment than any other form of decision" (1902b, pp. 172–173). An algorithmic system that scores two people decides as an arbitrator does, but they did not choose it, and their confidence in it was never asked for. The third point concerns interest. The mediator has no stake. A platform does: as Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) write of a user who rates a provider, "The platform benefits from her agency without her acting as its agent" (p. 14). A platform that decides between two parties and profits from what they do combines positions that Simmel kept apart.

Two questions about this borrowing need an answer. One is whether the third must be a person. Simmel wrote about people, and Pyyhtinen (2018) has since asked what becomes of the triad when the third is not human. The other question is who the party is: the algorithm or the organization that operates it. Organizational research on third parties concerns people, such as the broker who carries information between others, exploits their separation, or joins them (Obstfeld et al., 2014). In Simmel's sense the party with interests is the operator, which sets the rule and benefits from it. But the participant does not meet the operator. She meets the rule as it is applied to her: a score, a ranking, a visibility she gains or loses. I therefore use three terms. The *participant* is the person whose position is in view. The *counterpart* is the other person she works with. The *intermediary* is what stands between them, which is the rule as applied, with its operator behind it. Where the operator is a stranger to both, as on a marketplace, the triad is the one Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) describe. Where the operator is the employer of both, as when a firm runs evaluations through generative AI, one corner of the triad is closer to one party than to the other, and I return to that case below.

## Three Forms of Working Together

I sort the forms in which two people do interdependent work by one question asked of whatever stands between them: does it evaluate them and bind them? If nothing stands between them, or what does is a person who only passes things on, the form is a direct or chained dyad. If a text stands between them, the form is a moderated dyad. If what stands between them evaluates both and binds both, the form is a triad. By "moderated" I mean only that the text tempers what passes between two people; I do not mean the statistical sense of the word. The sorting is mine. Its nearest antecedent is Thompson's (1995, 2020) distinction among face-to-face interaction, mediated interaction, mediated quasi-interaction, and, later, mediated online interaction. Thompson sorts by the medium and by whether the audience is specific. I sort by what the thing in the middle does to the two people, because that is what decides what each must be able to do. Sorting forms is familiar in organization theory, which sets hierarchy, market, and network side by side (Powell, 1990) and treats a form of organizing as a set of solutions to problems that every organization has to solve (Puranam et al., 2014).

The sorting covers interdependent work between particular people, and it is not a theory of speech or of writing. Goffman (1981) showed that even face-to-face talk involves more than a speaker and a hearer, and Ong (1975) showed that a writer has to invent the reader she writes for. Neither point touches a claim about two people who depend on each other to get something done. It does limit the claim: when I say that oracy belongs to the dyad, I mean oracy in its coordinating use.

In a direct dyad I speak to you and you answer. In a chain I tell John and John tells Sally. What these forms ask of a person is the ability to speak and to listen, which Wilkinson (1965) named oracy. A chain adds a relay, and a relay can change a message, forget part of it, or add to it. But the relay is a person who can be asked what was said, and the relay holds no verdict over the people at either end. The relay is what Obstfeld et al. (2014) call a conduit.

In a moderated dyad a text stands between the two. A written text records what was said and leaves open how it was meant (Olson, 1994), and the reader cannot question the page. The writer therefore has to put into the text what a listener would have picked up from the occasion, and the reader has to work out intent from words alone. That pair of abilities is literacy. Organizations came to depend on it. Yates (1989) describes American firms between about 1850 and 1920 coming to be managed through memos, reports, forms, and files, and I infer that a firm managed in this way assumes its people can read and write. The inference is mine and not Yates's. Nor do I claim that one form replaced another or that the words arrived in this order; Wilkinson coined "oracy" in 1965 by analogy with the older "literacy." The claim is that organizations first relied on people who could talk, then also on people who could handle text, and are now beginning to rely on people who can work in a triad.

Texts are not inert, and the sorting does not need them to be. Cooren (2004) has shown that reports, contracts, and work orders "can be said to be performing something" (p. 373); Kuhn (2008) builds a theory of the firm on authoritative texts; and Lehtinen and Pälli (2021) show an appraisal form "presenting demands for the participants" (p. 47) in a performance interview. I accept all of this. The line I draw is about who holds the verdict. An appraisal form presents demands, but the manager fills it in, signs it, can be asked why, and answers for the rating. The form shapes the judgment and a person makes it. Replace the form with a system that computes the rating from what both of them entered, and the verdict has moved to a third. The same practice has passed from one form to the other.

In a triad, then, the intermediary *evaluates* both people, in that it produces a verdict on each one's part in their joint work, and *binds* both, in that the verdict has consequences that the two cannot set aside by agreeing between themselves. A host and a guest who review each other on a platform that publishes both reviews to later users are in a triad. So are two litigants before a court. The next section says what separates these two cases.

## What Sets the Algorithmic Third Apart

A third that evaluates and binds two parties is an old arrangement. Courts, arbitrators, and referees all do it. Litigants have always had to interpret a verdict, address the bench and the opponent in one filing, and follow rules that change. The comparison helps the argument in two ways. It shows that the ability to work in such a triad is real and distinct, since societies made a profession of it. And it shows what institutions do when they do not expect ordinary people to have that ability: they publish the rule, require reasons, allow appeal, and supply counsel. Students of online dispute resolution saw early that software could join such a proceeding: Katsh and Rifkin (2001) called the technology a fourth party, seated beside the disputants and a human neutral, and in their account its role is disclosed.

The intermediary in the arrangements this paper concerns has four further features, and each one removes something a court provides. First, its rule is *hidden*. Rahman (2021) describes workers under criteria they could not see, and Hinds and von Krogh (2024) expect that a generative system "may ultimately obscure managers' and employees' visibility into the basis on which the AI is making decisions" (p. 4). Second, it is *unanswerable*: it gives no reasons, there is no one to ask, and a complaint goes back into the system that produced the result. In a validated questionnaire on workers' competence with algorithms, the item on unfair results asks whether the worker can "use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing)" (Zhou et al., 2025, p. 8). Third, it *changes without notice*. Glaser et al. (2021) trace how algorithms evolve as they move across settings, Bailey et al. (2022) describe emerging technologies as "by design, always changing and adapting" (p. 1), and Hinds and von Krogh (2024) write that the functions of generative systems, "rather than being fixed," are "likely to evolve and expand as the AI learns" (p. 4). Fourth, it *takes the two people's dealings with each other as its evidence*. A court hears each party in a forum set apart from their dealings. A platform scores the reviews and ratings that are those dealings.

Behind these four lies a fifth fact that shapes the rest: the intermediary's authority was not conferred by the two people, and its operator declines the role. Schoeneborn et al. (2026) observe that digital platforms "rather strategically downplay their collective actorhood status to avoid accountability" (p. 13), and Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) write that accountability on platforms is "distributed, deflected, and denied" (p. 14). Selznick (1949) defined co-optation as "absorbing new elements into the leadership or policy-determining structure of an organization" (p. 13) and added that what is shared is "the responsibility for power rather than power itself" (p. 14). Participants on a platform carry the responsibility without the seat. Their remedy is to leave, and for the marketplace sellers Curchod et al. (2020) studied, rebuilding a reputation elsewhere was too costly to count as a remedy.

Algorithmacy is therefore not confined to software, and I do not define it by the technology. A blind review with a hidden scoring scheme has most of the features, and I expect an author in that position to need the same ability. I name the ability for algorithmic systems for the reason that literacy is named for letters though it applies to any text: that is where the form has become general. In a court the four features are removed by design and a professional stands in for the litigant. Under an algorithmic intermediary all four hold at once, for people with no counsel, as an everyday condition of their work.

A construct needs stated limits, and a single clear exception counts heavily against one (Suddaby, 2010). Because the features vary independently, the need for the ability varies with them, and the account can be wrong. Where the rule is published and results come with reasons, I expect interpreting to reduce to ordinary reading. Where the intermediary does not read the two people's dealings, specifying intent reduces to ordinary writing. Where the rule is stable, keeping track is finished once the rule is learned. Where the intermediary binds only one of the two, the triad is lopsided: the bound person needs the ability, and the other person is, for her, part of the intermediary's evidence. And where there is no second person, as when someone uses a chatbot, the arrangement is a dyad with a machine, and the accounts built on the first picture cover it.

## Algorithmacy

I define algorithmacy as the ability to do interdependent work with another person through an intermediary that evaluates and binds them both by a rule that is hidden, unanswerable, and changing, and that takes their dealings with each other as its evidence. By *ability* I mean what a person can reliably do in a kind of situation and has learned mainly by doing it. The definition follows Sandberg's (2000) argument that competence at work lies in how a person understands the work and not in a list of attributes, and Jablin and Sias's (2001) treatment of communication competence as situated. It separates algorithmacy from a trait, from an organizational capability (Stelmaszak et al., 2026), and from knowledge about a technology (Long & Magerko, 2020).

### The Participant's Position

The participant does not lack information. She has the result, she has what the counterpart said and did, and she has her own record. Her difficulty is that several explanations fit what she has and she has no channel in which to settle among them. Daft and Lengel (1986) separated these two conditions: uncertainty is the absence of information, and equivocality is the presence of information that supports conflicting readings. More data cures the first and does not cure the second. Weick et al. (2005) would call her situation one for sensemaking, which "involves turning circumstances into a situation that is comprehended explicitly in words and that serves as a springboard into action" (p. 409). What the triad adds to the general case is a specific source of equivocality—a third whose part in a result cannot be separated from the counterpart's part—and the absence of anyone to ask.

That position costs the two people the conditions on which their coordination rested. Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) define coordination as "the process of interaction that integrates a collective set of interdependent tasks" (p. 463) and identify three integrating conditions: accountability, predictability, and common understanding. Each feature of the intermediary undoes one. Accountability goes because the verdict comes from a party that gives no reasons and presents itself as no party at all. Predictability goes because the rule changes without notice. Common understanding goes because the two people's dealings are also evidence, so neither can take what the other says at face value. The last point has experimental support. Bolton et al. (2013) found that when each side of a transaction rates the other, the expectation of a return rating distorts what each reports. Fradkin et al. (2021) found, in an experiment on a rental platform, that hiding each review until both were submitted reduced retaliation and reciprocation and lowered ratings. Before the change, what each person said about the other had been written with an eye to what the other might say back.

The three parts of algorithmacy answer these three losses. Table 1 sets out the correspondence, and Figure 1 shows the parts as a cycle. Each part is a partial recovery: a participant cannot restore accountability, predictability, or common understanding by herself, but she can rebuild a working version of each, enough to act on. The hidden rule is a condition of all three parts and does not distinguish among them.

**Table 1.** *The three parts of algorithmacy*

| Part | Feature of the intermediary that calls for it | Condition it helps recover | How a person fails at it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interpreting | The rule is hidden and unanswerable | Accountability | Attributing every result to the rule, or every result to the counterpart |
| Specifying intent | The intermediary takes the two people's dealings as evidence | Common understanding | Writing for the intermediary only, or for the counterpart only |
| Keeping track | The rule changes without notice | Predictability | Acting on an understanding of the rule that no longer holds |

### Interpreting

Interpreting is working out whether a result came from the rule, from the counterpart, or from both. A seller on a marketplace whose standing drops holds one buyer's rating, processed by a rule she cannot see (Curchod et al., 2020). If the buyer judged the transaction poorly, she should change how she deals with buyers. If the buyer was satisfied and the rule pushed the result down, changing how she deals with buyers is wasted effort and may make things worse. The two readings call for different acts, which is why the attribution matters.

A participant interprets by setting the result beside what the counterpart visibly did, and by comparing across counterparts and across time. Research on communication shows that this kind of attribution is not automatic, though it shows it in dyads and not in triads. Sundar and Nass (2000) found that people rated the same content differently according to the source they were told it came from. Hohenstein and Jung (2020) found that suggested replies raised people's trust in their chat partner and, when a conversation went badly, moved blame from the partner to the system. Sundar and Kim (2019) found that people told a chat agent was a machine reported more willingness to give it a credit-card number than people told it was a human. People in these studies assigned credit, blame, and trust between a person and a machine in patterned ways that the content alone does not explain, and Heiland (2025) reports workers forming false theories of an algorithm because they could not see it. I expect the same tendency in a triad, where the stakes are higher and the machine's share is harder to see, but no study I know of tests it there.

### Specifying Intent

Specifying intent is making one's meaning reach the counterpart and the intermediary at once. Any writer addresses a reader she cannot see and has to imagine (Litt, 2012; Ong, 1975). The participant addresses two readers who want different things. The counterpart needs to understand what she means and what to do about it. The intermediary processes what she writes by a rule she cannot see, and she has to put her meaning in a form that survives that processing. Two people ordinarily check their understanding through small signals back and forth (Clark & Brennan, 1991); a machine holds a thin record of what a person meant, and the person does the repair (Suchman, 2007). Viduchinsky (2026) has named the problem from the user's side, describing a "dual-audience dilemma" (p. 1) in which users of dating applications perform for people and for the algorithm together.

A person can fail at this part in two directions. To illustrate with an invented case: an employee revises her self-assessment with a language model until an automated scorer rates it highly, and it now tells her manager nothing he can use. Another employee writes her manager a candid note about a problem on the team, and the system files it as a complaint against her. The first wrote for the intermediary alone and the second for the counterpart alone. Bucher et al. (2021) describe a version of the first failure among platform workers who, to keep the algorithm satisfied, curtailed what they said to clients.

### Keeping Track

Keeping track is noticing that the rule has changed and telling that change apart from a change in the counterpart. What a participant has learned about the rule holds only while the rule holds, and a change is not announced; she detects it from its effects. Research on social media has described much of this. Users form folk theories of how a feed works and adjust what they post to them (Bucher, 2017; Cotter, 2019; DeVito et al., 2018); DeVito (2021) followed 25 LGBTQ+ users of one platform as they revised such theories after the platform changed, and proposed that the revising could be taught; and Meng (2026) writes of creators' "algorithmic adaptability" (p. 1171). In organizations, Orlikowski and Scott (2014) compared two schemes for valuing hotels, one of them produced online by consumers, and found the production of valuations reconfiguring the everyday practices of the hotels being evaluated (see also Scott & Orlikowski, 2012).

What the triad adds is a second thing that can change. When a way of working that used to succeed stops succeeding, either the rule moved or the counterparts did, and the participant has to separate the two. Rahman (2021) found that workers who faced criteria that shifted unpredictably either experimented, at risk to their standing, or cut back their activity to protect it.

### How the Parts Fit Together

The three parts depend on one another, and the dependence shows when one is removed. Without specifying intent, interpreting is a private diagnosis that changes nothing. Without interpreting, specifying intent is formatting. Without keeping track, the other two run on an understanding that has expired. The parts form a cycle: an interpretation shapes the participant's next act toward the counterpart and the intermediary, and the next result tests whether the rule is still the one she had worked out. Whether telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart is a fourth part, and not a piece of interpreting and of keeping track, is a question I leave open.

A person acquires the ability by taking turns at this cycle, and turns are necessary without being sufficient. Jiang and Sinchaisri (2025) found a learning curve across more than a million orders on one delivery platform. Rahman (2021) found that how workers responded to an opaque evaluation depended also on how much they relied on the platform and on whether they had suffered a setback. Help from others has limits. Lee et al. (2015) and Yao et al. (2021) found that workers' forums passed on what experience could teach and failed where only the platform had the answer or where sharing meant losing an advantage. I therefore expect people in the same formal position to differ in algorithmacy, by the turns they have had, by what a mistake would cost them, and by whether someone interprets for them. This is an expectation. No one has measured it.

The two ways of failing at the ability correspond to the two pictures in the Introduction. A participant who treats the triad as a dyad with the system explains every result by the rule and treats the counterpart as noise. A participant who treats it as a moderated dyad reads the intermediary's verdict as the counterpart's opinion. In each case one corner of the triad stands in for the whole.

## Where the Account Applies

How far the account reaches depends on which arrangements have the features, and the published record is uneven. I take the cases in order of fit.

Two-sided reputation systems fit best. On the rental platform Fradkin et al. (2021) studied, host and guest each review the other, the reviews are published to later users, and the platform sets when and how each becomes visible. Both people are evaluated, both carry the result into their next transaction, and what they say to each other is the evidence. The study concerns platform design and reports nothing about what hosts or guests are able to do, so it establishes the arrangement and not the ability.

Marketplaces and hotel rankings are lopsided triads. On the marketplace Curchod et al. (2020) studied, the rule binds sellers and leaves buyers nearly free. On travel platforms, hotels are ranked from reviews "aggregated into rankings via the platforms' proprietary algorithms" (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023, p. 3), written by anonymous users who "may or may not have experienced" the hotel "in person" (p. 8); the hotel is bound and the reviewer is not (see also Karunakaran et al., 2022). In both settings the account applies to the bound party. The seller and the hotelier need to interpret, to address customer and platform at once, and to keep track. The buyer and the reviewer do not.

Evaluation by generative AI is a case that may arrive. Hinds and von Krogh (2024) offer what they call "an illustration as well as a thought experiment" (p. 6), in which a manager uses generative AI to evaluate employees, new relations "may cascade" among "managers, GenAI, and employees" (p. 4), and "the process for getting there is opaque" (p. 6). They report no data, and I use their case as they do. If it comes about, the employee will be bound. Whether the manager is bound is doubtful: in one experiment, managers were less willing to use an algorithm when it advised a low rating and more willing when they could adjust it (Lin et al., 2026). Where AI feedback has been tried, disclosing its source changed how employees performed, less so for those with longer tenure (Tong et al., 2021). The operator here is the firm that employs both, so the intermediary is closer to one party than to the other.

Two arrangements that resemble these do not qualify. A planning simulation that forecasts outcomes for planners and the public neither evaluates nor binds them. Leonardi and Leavell (2026) show something else with it: where planners presented detailed, animated output, 83% of stakeholders' coded statements about what the model predicted were absolute, against 9% where planners presented charts and called the model "just one tool of many" (p. 526; figures at p. 535), and the authors found "17 mentions of unique lawsuits" (p. 537) against the first organization and none against the second. They name the stakeholders' illusion "artificial certainty" and write that it is "not an inherent property of AI tools but emerges from the ways in which process experts position and deploy them" (p. 538). The finding bears on what an expert who stands between a system and other people can do, and I use it below for that. A person using a chatbot is the other arrangement that does not qualify, for the reason already given.

The upshot is a limit and a prediction. An intermediary that binds both parties equally is less common in the published record than one that binds a single side. The usual case is the lopsided triad, and in it the demand for algorithmacy falls on one side of the relation.

## Discussion

### What the Account Adds to Existing Constructs

Several constructs lie close to algorithmacy, and the differences can be stated on two dimensions: how many parties the construct covers, and whether it describes an ability or a response. Algorithmic competency (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019), measured by Zhou et al. (2025) as workers' "understanding of platform algorithms that assign and evaluate their work and their ability to adapt to and navigate those algorithms" (p. 2), is an ability defined over two parties (see also Hong et al., 2026). AI literacy and algorithmic literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025) are bodies of knowledge about a system; Long and Magerko's list includes a competency on the human role in AI, but none on working with a second person through the system. Reviews of this literature now frame it as learning from experience (Gagrčin et al., 2026), Hargittai (2002) showed long ago that experience predicts online skill, and Hargittai et al. (2020) have since asked how skill with algorithms can be measured at all. Reactivity (Espeland & Sauder, 2007; Rahman, 2021) describes how people respond to being measured and does not say what they are able to do. Gig literacies (Sutherland et al., 2020) come nearest, since they include work on the relationship with a client.

Field studies supply most of the observations the account organizes. Workers craft encounters with customers in pursuit of ratings (Cameron, 2022), manage their scores (Bellesia et al., 2023), and comply in advance with what they think the algorithm wants (Bucher et al., 2021). Clients and contractors repair misaligned expectations through a platform's tools (Rahman & Valentine, 2021), and workers learn from repeated interaction to change how they behave toward the algorithm (Möhlmann et al., 2021). Two of the three parts have names already: Viduchinsky's (2026) dual-audience dilemma is specifying intent seen from one setting, and Meng's (2026) algorithmic adaptability is keeping track seen from another. The account's addition is to define one ability over the three parties, to say which feature of the intermediary calls for each part, and to say when each part should not be needed.

### The Intermediary as a Party

Scholars who study technology as entangled with practice will object that I have set the algorithm apart as an entity. Orlikowski and Scott (2008, 2023) argue against treating technology as a discrete thing, Faraj and Pachidi (2021) argue "against viewing technology as an entity that is separate, exogenous, or causal" (p. 1), Glaser et al. (2021) treat algorithms as assemblages that change as they travel, and Alaimo and Kallinikos (2021) show categories made from data reordering an organization and its surroundings. I do not dispute this as an account of how such systems come to be and to act. My claim is about the participant's position. From where she stands, a result arrives that she cannot trace, from a source she cannot question, and she must act on it with another person. Calling that source a party describes what she faces. It makes no claim that the technology exists apart from the people and practices that produce it. Newlands (2021) makes a similar move for surveillance, which she notes has been understood as a dyad of observer and observed and now includes cases where "the observer and decision-maker can be a non-human agent" (p. 719).

The account also carries a risk that should be stated. Elish (2019) describes how a person placed beside an automated system "bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilities" (p. 40) when it fails. Algorithmacy may work as a private repair for a public defect. The better participants become at interpreting, specifying intent, and keeping track, the less the opacity of the rule shows in results, and the less pressure there may be to change it. I offer this as a possibility that follows from the account and not as a finding.

### Three Courses for Organizations

An organization that places such a system between its people has three courses, and the scope conditions say what each does. It can disclose the rule and give reasons, which should reduce the need for interpreting and for keeping track. It can place a person between the system and those it judges. Waardenburg et al. (2022) followed police intelligence officers who brokered an opaque predictive model to its users, and the planners in Leonardi and Leavell's (2026) second organization did similar work when they presented the model as one aid among several. Such a broker interprets on others' behalf, with the hazard Waardenburg et al. record, that the brokers ended by substituting their own judgment for the model's. Or the organization can see that its people acquire the ability, which leaves the rule as it is and moves the cost of its opacity onto them. Installing a system is none of these: Christin (2017) found algorithmic tools adopted on paper in newsrooms and courts and ignored or worked around in practice.

## Research Agenda and Conclusion

The account raises questions of theory before questions of measurement. The first is whether the lopsided triad and the two-sided triad are one form or two. I have treated them as one form with a feature that varies, but a relation in which only one party is bound may be closer to Simmel's third position, in which the third divides in order to rule, than to arbitration. The second concerns the operator. When the operator employs both parties, the intermediary may be better understood as an instrument of hierarchy than as a third, and the conditions under which an employer's system counts as a party need stating.

Other questions are empirical. Are the three parts distinct when measured, and is telling a change in the rule from a change in the counterpart a fourth? Does algorithmacy explain outcomes once dependence on the system and prior setbacks are taken into account, as Rahman's (2021) findings require? Does it differ in practice from general digital skill and from skill at communicating? The argument here is that it should, and Krakowski et al. (2023) give an indirect reason to think so: in their study of chess, capabilities at playing with a machine were "unrelated, or even negatively related, to traditional capabilities" (p. 1425). Finally, which of the three courses do organizations take, and does the need for the ability fall where the scope conditions say it should?

The triadic view of platforms describes a form of organizing. This paper has described what that form asks of a person inside it who has work to do with someone else. The ability has three parts because the intermediary takes three things away, and it is needed to the degree that the intermediary's rule is hidden, unanswerable, changing, and fed by the two people's own dealings. Where organizations remove those features, the need falls. Where they do not, they are relying on an ability that they have neither named nor checked that their people have.
