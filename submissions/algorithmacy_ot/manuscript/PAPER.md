## Introduction

Organizations fail to adopt and understand the tsunami of new platforms and services enabled by generative AI. While global leaders promote these technologies, quieter, (sociomaterial?) displacements of institutional values, norms, and rules are what actually reconfigure how organizations perform (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023 - OT). Focusing on those quieter shifts allows us to recognize how generative AI sits inside the work and reshapes it, functioning more as - but not quite - "counterparts" in a system, implementation, and use (Anthony et al., 2023 - OS). Hoping to install a plug-and-play intelligence ignores that organizing now depends on relations among humans and algorithmic black boxes (Stelmaszak et al., 2025 - JMS) which reduce visibility and control (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024 - OT) while paradoxically manufacturing a false sense of epistemological security (Leonardi & Leavell, 2026 - OS). While the academic debate has clearly moved past the surface of “tooling,” it still leaves open how someone coordinates through a cooptive, opaque, adaptive intermediary.

(That said, if it were only a matter of how, this proposal wouldn’t be so interesting.  In this case, however, the how I propose also requires that we refactor the why.)

Organizations have been doing the equivalent of giving books to people who lack literacy. We heap algorithmic management systems on people who lack algorithmacy, or the sensibility to navigate opaque triadic coordinative cooptation. As traditional firm boundaries dissolve, these algorithmic intermediaries operate with gradual *organizationality*, functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood (Schoeneborn et al., 2026 - OT) that co-opt (Stark, 2024 - OT), ie evaluate and bind. Building on the OT/OS discussion and exploring other popular models including Human-Machine Communication and AI Literacy, I argue that we are mast-strapping ourselves trying to preserve a “literacy”-based understanding. We do this by either setting aside the third actor, treating the mediator as a passive channel, or relying on a neo-Quinean set-theoretic dyadic ontology that is entirely blind to the emergent, binding power of the triad.

Oracy is the sensibility to navigate direct dyadic forms through speaking and hearing (or feeling and tapping). Literacy is the sensibility necessary for the competency to read and write texts that moderate chained or linked dyads. Algorithmacy is the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads. Drawing on recent views of imagination as a relational capacity enacted in the encounter between bodies, materials, and circumstances (Porsfelt et al., 2026 - OT), algorithmacy can be seen as a situated engagement with the unseen. As a sensibility like literacy and oracy, it operates though the competencies and capacities to: 1) interpret a variable counterpart’s intentions from an otherwise innocuous output; 2) specify intent so meaning survives the opaque, adaptive mediator; and 3) keep track as governance radically, repeatedly, and suddenly shifts. We cannot read our way out of or into this, just as we cannot—or should not—listen to a book.

## The Triadic Blind Spot: From Dyadic Relations to Coordinative Co-optation

Recent work in *Organization Theory* has powerfully articulated how algorithmic systems quietly displace established institutional norms (Orlikowski & Scott, 2023) and introduce novel forms of governance. Rather than fitting into classical archetypes of hierarchy, market, or network, digital platforms govern through "coordinative co-optation" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024)—deploying algorithms to match unchosen parties, evaluate their exchanges, and unilaterally bind them to outcomes. As traditional firm boundaries dissolve, these algorithmic intermediaries increasingly operate with "gradual organizationality" (Schoeneborn et al., 2026), functioning as distinct social addresses with collective actorhood. Yet, while OT has successfully mapped this macro-level shift in structural control, our micro-foundational models of how individuals actually execute work within these systems remain trapped in a theoretical bottleneck: a persistent, set-theoretic dyadic ontology.

When attempting to explain individual capacity within algorithmic management, scholarship frequently reduces the encounter to a two-party relationship. For instance, arguing that organizing capability arises strictly within "human–algorithm relations" (Stelmaszak et al., 2025) correctly elevates the machine to an active counterpart, but it inadvertently erases the interdependent human peer on the other side of the screen. This dyadic trap is pervasive across adjacent fields attempting to measure digital capacity. Frameworks like Human-Machine Communication (HMC) and AI Literacy meticulously document how individuals decode interfaces or build rapport with artificial agents, but they treat the algorithm as the final conversational partner, pushing the human counterpart entirely out of the analytical frame. Conversely, traditional Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC) and AI-Mediated Communication models analyze the two human peers, but reduce the technology to a passive conduit or a partisan delegate, ignoring its autonomous evaluative power. None of these extant models capture the actual structural reality of platform work: an individual coordinating with an interdependent human counterpart through an opaque intermediary that evaluates and binds them both.

To accurately theorize human action within coordinative co-optation, organizational theory must shift from a dyad to a triad. As Simmel (1902) demonstrated, moving from two actors to three fundamentally alters the social form rather than simply adding a member; the tie between the first two actors is no longer direct, but routed through a third entity operating with interests and rules of its own. On digital platforms, the algorithmic intermediary occupies this third position not as a passive channel, but as an active, binding arbitrator.

Furthermore, as generative AI accelerates the "progressive encapsulation" of these technologies (Hinds & von Krogh, 2024), this triadic arbitrator becomes increasingly opaque and adaptive. Navigating this arrangement cannot be solved by traditional "literacy" or simple algorithmic competency, because the user is not merely reading a static text or operating an isolated tool. Drawing on recent views of imagination as a relational capacity enacted in the encounter between bodies, materials, and circumstances (Porsfelt et al., 2026), navigating the algorithmic triad must be understood as a situated engagement with the unseen. It requires a distinct sensibility to decipher the hidden rules of the triadic gatekeeper while simultaneously reaching the human peer.
