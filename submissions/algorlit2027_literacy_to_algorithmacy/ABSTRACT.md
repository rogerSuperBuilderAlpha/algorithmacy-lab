**Title:** After Algorithmic Literacy: Coordinating With Another Person Through an Algorithm

## Abstract
**Objectives.** Algorithmic literacy has become the field's name for what people need in algorithmic environments. This paper asks whether that name fits a common situation it was not built for. In that situation, two people coordinate through a system that evaluates both of them: a host and a guest under a booking platform's ranking, or a seller and a buyer under a marketplace's ratings. It also asks what the history of literacy suggests about promising competence as a remedy.

**Methodology.** The paper is a conceptual analysis in two parts. First, it reads the definitions collected in the field's two integrative reviews, one of 50 articles and one of 169 studies, together with the leading conceptual and measurement proposals. For each definition it records who the parties are and what the person is said to know or do. Second, it compares this pattern with historical scholarship on print-literacy campaigns and with a recent critique of AI-literacy initiatives.

**Results.** In every definition examined, the relation is between one user and one system. The dimensions describe a person's awareness of algorithms, knowledge of how they work, critical evaluation of them, and ways of coping with or influencing them. None describes a second person on the other side of the system. Yet much algorithmic power runs between people. A host's ranking moves with guests' reviews, and a guest's choice moves with the ranking. The historical comparison shows a second pattern. Literacy campaigns promised that individual competence would bring advancement, and literacy was often more valuable to the society's goals than to most individuals. AI-literacy initiatives use literacy as a metaphor and repeat this promise.

**Contribution.** The paper proposes a structural distinction. Oracy names competence in direct exchange, and literacy names competence with a text that a human still interprets and judges. Algorithmacy names competence in coordinating with another person through a system that evaluates both. It has three practices: interpreting an ambiguous result to tell the counterpart's part from the system's, specifying intent so the counterpart can read it through the system, and tracking rule changes the operator does not announce. For algorithmic-literacy research, the contribution is a new object of study, the counterpart. For literacy education, it is a caution: knowing how the system works does not give a person standing in front of it, so programmes should not promise that it will.

**Keywords:** algorithmic literacy; algorithmacy; platforms; literacy myth; coordination
