Decision Experience Design in Physical Space: Algorithmic Intermediation, Material Enforcement, and the Limits of Choice Architecture

An algorithmic match calculated on remote servers produces immediate physical obligations on city streets. In commercial ride-hailing, two individuals converge on a specific curb under parameters determined by an automated system. The passenger follows a screen coordinate, while the driver follows a navigation system that locks out alternative routing software. Neither participant negotiated the price, selected the exact boarding location, or established the cancellation penalties (Cameron, 2024; Möhlmann et al., 2021). The platform ingests telemetry from both participants, computes the dispatch, and transfers the frictions of that match directly onto human bodies navigating physical streets (Cameron, 2024; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016).

[[DIAGRAM:curbside]]

Classical behavioral economics models environmental influence through static physical structures. In the canonical cafeteria experiment, a food director places fresh fruit at eye level and relocates desserts to less accessible corners (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). The cafeteria displays an inert, material architecture that affects every patron identically. The serving counter collects no data from diner trays, alters no layouts in response to approaching individuals, and mediates no bilateral exchange between counterparties (Hollands et al., 2017).

Decision Experience Design defines an interactive system wherein an adaptive, opaque algorithmic intermediary simultaneously reads multiple parties and commits binding determinations that neither party fully controls. While software interfaces frame users as disembodied operators viewing screens, corporate strategies in retail, logistics, and hospitality increasingly deploy computational intermediaries into physical space under the commercial label of the "phygital".

Physical decision experience design exists only within narrow structural boundaries. Most hybrid environments remain classical choice architecture, governed by the traditional principles of environmental psychology and servicescape design. True physical decision experience design occurs exclusively when an adaptive intermediary ingests data from multiple human parties and enforces an outcome that those parties must execute with their physical bodies. The physical environment serves as the operational site where algorithmic determinations harden into material constraints, where refusal incurs concrete monetary or social costs, and where organizational systems displace administrative accountability onto frontline personnel.

1. The Operational Marks of Decision Experience Design

Evaluating whether a physical or hybrid space belongs to Decision Experience Design requires four operational criteria. These criteria separate genuine multi-party algorithmic intermediation from static choice architecture and direct corporate administration.

+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                              THE FOUR MARKS OF DECISION EXPERIENCE DESIGN                        |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Mark 1: Distinct Intermediary  | Operates as an administratively distinct third party between    |
|                                 | counterparties who hold divergent interests in the outcome.     |
+---------------------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Mark 2: Bilateral Telemetry    | Actively ingests operational data from both counterparties      |
|                                 | during the encounter rather than relying on historical records. |
+---------------------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Mark 3: Mutual Binding         | Commits an outcome that neither party can unilaterally or      |
|                                 | costlessly undo within the physical environment.                |
+---------------------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Mark 4: Dynamic Rule Drift     | Adjusts matching parameters, weights, or prices across          |
|                                 | encounters rather than presenting a fixed, permanent rule.       |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

The first mark requires an administratively or economically distinct third party standing between counterparties who hold divergent interests. An employer directing an employee or a retailer selling to a customer represents a direct dyadic hierarchy, not an intermediated transaction. The intermediary must function as an external arbiter regulating the interaction.

The second mark requires active, real-time sensing of both parties during the encounter. An environmental system that reads historical demographic aggregates or monitors only one participant fails this standard. The intermediary must ingest operational data from both sides of the exchange as the interaction unfolds.

The third mark requires a binding determination that neither party can unilaterally or costlessly alter. If either participant can walk away without financial, legal, or physical penalties, the system functions as a advisory recommendation rather than an administrative ruling. The determination restricts subsequent human action.

The fourth mark requires dynamic rule adaptation across encounters. Systems that enforce permanent, immutable rules function as static physical barriers or simple digital access controls. Decision Experience Design requires operational rules, dispatch algorithms, or pricing formulas that drift dynamically based on broader environmental conditions or machine learning retraining cycles.

The curbside ride-hail pickup demonstrates all four marks. The platform operates as an economically distinct intermediary between an independent contractor and a consumer (Cameron, 2024). The application ingests continuous spatial coordinates, cancellation histories, and acceptance velocities from both parties (Möhlmann et al., 2021). The computed match binds the driver to proprietary navigation and a concealed fare, while binding the rider to upfront pricing and financial penalties for cancellation (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). The pricing multipliers and dispatch matrices adapt continuously across shifts and geographic sectors (Lee et al., 2015).

The behavioral cafeteria satisfies none of these operational marks. The dining facility features no third-party intermediary; the catering administration designs the serving space directly (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). The food line reads no individual tray weights, heart rates, or payment capacities during selection (Hollands et al., 2017). The display places no binding contractual obligation upon the diner, who can bypass apples to reach cakes at zero monetary penalty (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). The food placement remains stationary throughout the meal service, applying an unchanging geometry to every patron (Hollands et al., 2017). The cafeteria represents pure choice architecture.

2. Theoretical Deficits in Phygital Research

Marketing scholarship imported the term "phygital" directly from corporate advertising campaigns without establishing rigorous sociological or economic foundations (Johnson & Barlow, 2021; Mele et al., 2023). The construct represents an ambiguous conceptual category utilized primarily to describe commercial retail strategies (Mele et al., 2023). Systematic reviews indicate that empirical studies deploy the term without operational consensus, oscillating between ubiquitous computing descriptions and consumer experience frameworks (Johnson & Barlow, 2021; Mele et al., 2023).

Marketing theorists argue that physical and digital interactions have fused into an indivisible continuum, attributing autonomous agency to hybrid environments (Mele & Russo-Spena, 2025). This scholarship builds on philosophical concepts of hyperconnected environments to declare the boundary between online and offline behavior obsolete (Floridi, 2015; Mele & Russo-Spena, 2025). From this perspective, physical and computational spaces form an integrated network where technologies co-create economic value alongside human participants (Mele & Russo-Spena, 2025).

This theoretical perspective prioritizes the systematic elimination of transaction friction. Marketing literature characterizes the ideal customer journey as a continuous flow that removes conscious cognitive deliberation (Batat, 2024; Johnson & Barlow, 2021). Automated checkouts, mobile payments, and sensor-driven access eliminate tangible payment interactions, encouraging impulse expenditures by detaching consumption from conscious financial evaluation (Johnson & Barlow, 2021). Friction operates within this discourse as an operational failure requiring technological removal.

|                            SEAMLESS DESIGNS VERSUS CONSTRUCTIVE FRICTION                         |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Commercial "Seamless" Model     | Conceals pricing mechanisms, automates payment extraction,    |
|                                  | accelerates checkout velocity, and removes cognitive pauses.  |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Constructive Friction Model     | Introduces explicit operational verifications, preserves human|
|  (Padigar et al., 2025)          | dignity, prevents automated steering, and exposes authority.  |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

A parallel body of research demonstrates that removing friction compromises consumer autonomy (Padigar et al., 2025). Frictionless payment designs cause involuntary spending and permit automated commercial software to steer users toward unfavorable financial commitments (Padigar et al., 2025). Automated retail environments that eliminate human workers generate consumer apprehension, transforming routine transactions into encounters with opaque surveillance architectures (Gustafsson, 2026). Introducing intentional operational pauses preserves relational trust and consumer agency during service delivery (Pusceddu et al., 2025).

Empirical inquiries identify systemic structural problems within automated physical environments. Service providers engineer digital technology captivity by dismantling non-digital access methods, forcing consumers onto mobile applications to obtain admission to transit hubs, stadiums, and food markets (Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). Coercive service design systematically eliminates human-staffed checkout counters to enforce digital compliance (Lee & Kim, 2026). Computational environments enforce algorithmic rules that users cannot dispute or modify (Shabnam et al., 2026). Retail automations erase service employees entirely or demote them to diagnostic monitors charged with maintaining sensor machinery (Gustafsson, 2026; Mulcahy et al., 2026; Pusceddu et al., 2025).

The central analytical failure of phygital scholarship lies in its exclusive reliance on dyadic models. Marketing research models an individual consumer moving through a corporate sales funnel, evaluating single-user perceptions of brand consistency and convenience (Batat, 2024; Johnson & Barlow, 2021). The field offers no conceptual model of an algorithmic system that ingests telemetric streams from two distinct human parties and executes a binding settlement between them.

Phygital researchers propose solutions rooted strictly in information legibility: posting instructional signage, publishing privacy notifications, and updating screen layouts (Shabnam et al., 2026). These informational disclosures fail because human autonomy inside computational systems depends on operational control rather than cognitive transparency. Reading a static disclosure notification provides a user with zero operational capacity to alter an automated administrative determination.

3. Structural Predecessors: Static Environments, Physical Artifacts, and Built Scripts

Physical environments have shaped human economic behavior through spatial design for decades. Marketing literature defined "atmospherics" as the purposeful structuring of retail space to stimulate emotional responses that increase sales probabilities (Kotler, 1973). Atmospherics manipulates sensory dimensions, including lighting, background acoustics, aromas, and spatial flow (Kotler, 1973). The framework assumes an autonomous consumer who browses a fixed physical setting and retains the unrestricted legal right to leave without financial penalties (Kotler, 1973). Atmospherics alters sensory perception, but it cannot alter legal terms or record individual biometric states.

The servicescape framework expanded environmental analysis by modeling how physical space governs both customers and service employees (Bitner, 1992). Physical service environments accommodate conflicting operational priorities: layouts optimized for employee processing speeds frequently undermine customer physical comfort, and designs oriented toward leisure degrade employee labor efficiency (Bitner, 1992). The architecture of the servicescape settles this conflict through physical walls, fixed counter heights, and built walkways (Bitner, 1992).

+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Atmospherics (Kotler, 1973)     | Fixed sensory ambiance; assumes costless consumer exit;       |
|                                  | single-party orientation; no individual telemetry.             |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Servicescapes (Bitner, 1992)    | Two-party physical conflict (worker vs. patron) settled at    |
|                                  | design time; rigid physical partitions; completely static.     |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Prescriptive Objects            | Mechanical delegation; physical enforcement of civic scripts; |
|  (Latour, 1992; Akrich, 1987)    | uncalibrated resistance; fixed ratios without computation.     |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Decision Experience Design      | Dynamic algorithmic intermediary; real-time bilateral         |
|  (Contemporary DXD)              | telemetry; mutual binding enforcement; shifting parameters.   |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Servicescapes manage two distinct human populations, but they settle operational tensions once during architectural design (Bitner, 1992). The reception desk, retail display, or office partition remains anchored to the floor until physical remodeling takes place (Bitner, 1992). Classical physical design does not assume a lone actor; it assumes multiple human actors whose competing priorities are constrained by an unmoving material room (Bitner, 1992).

Public policy typologies for behavioral interventions formalize this architectural stability. The TIPPME framework classifies environmental nudges, such as item placement and container sizing, by explicitly excluding systems that tailor options to specific individuals or ingest personalized transactional histories (Hollands et al., 2017). Behavioral choice architecture restricts its scope to fixed environments that apply identical choices to all participants (Hollands et al., 2017).

Choice architects frequently introduce structural nudges without deliberate intent (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). Facility managers who specify door widths and warehouse workers who load shelves alter behavioral choices without conceptualizing their tasks as psychological engineering (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). The fundamental distinction between choice architecture and Decision Experience Design does not hinge on the conscious intent of the architect; it hinges on whether the intermediating architecture remains static or adapts dynamically based on real-time data.

Science and technology studies confirm that non-computational artifacts enforce social obligations through material construction (Latour, 1992). Organizations delegate human administrative tasks to mechanical objects: a hydraulic spring replaces a door attendant to keep conditioned air indoors, an ignition chime enforces seatbelt use, and a concrete speed bump compels vehicular deceleration (Latour, 1992). The mechanical artifact enforces an administrative script by physically obstructing non-compliant actions (Latour, 1992).

These non-digital devices lack the core properties of Decision Experience Design. The speed bump and the hydraulic door-closer execute mechanical resistance uniformly across every user (Latour, 1992). The concrete mound cannot read vehicle registration plates to modify its elevation, nor can the door hinge assess the physical strength of an approaching pedestrian to alter its resistance (Latour, 1992). The artifact carries a rigid social script, but its operational behavior remains completely static.

Mechanical meters historically functioned as physical arbiters between institutional suppliers and domestic consumers (Akrich, 1987). The electric utility meter in Abidjan mediated the relationship between a state utility vulnerable to revenue fraud and consumers vulnerable to fabricated billing claims (Akrich, 1987). The mechanical device required reciprocal participation: electricity had to cross the internal coils to turn the recording dials (Akrich, 1987). The physical device was an independent arbiter whose measurement bound both parties in administrative law (Akrich, 1987). The mechanical meter held entirely still: its internal gears operated on an immutable gear ratio determined by electromagnetism, incapable of dynamic algorithmic recalculation across time (Akrich, 1987).

Built architectural forms cannot enforce governance in the absence of administrative legal authority (Joerges, 1999). The claim that low stone overpasses on Long Island parkways served as architectural barriers to exclude low-income bus riders from public beaches conflates design intent with institutional power (Joerges, 1999; Winner, 1980). Administrative regulations explicitly banned commercial buses from all New York parkways, low bridge clearances conformed to standard highway safety engineering practices of the era, and public transit buses traveled to Jones Beach via surface roads (Joerges, 1999). Physical structures do not govern on their own; institutional regulations, legal police powers, and property rights dictate behavioral outcomes (Joerges, 1999).

4. Human-Computer Interaction Foundations: Seams, Invisibility, and Repair

Ubiquitous computing research rejected the pursuit of cosmetic seamlessness early in its development. Pervasive computation integrates microprocessors into ordinary physical artifacts (Weiser, 1991). Physical environments introduce radical infrastructural fragmentation, including dropped wireless connections, degraded sensors, and hardware misalignments (Weiser, 1994). Rather than concealing operational faults behind artificial interfaces, systems must present their structural joints openly to human users through seamful design (Weiser, 1994).

+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                            SEAMFUL DESIGN VERSUS SEAMLESS CONCEALMENT                            |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Seamful Paradigm                | Highlights communication boundaries, positioning errors, and  |
|  (Chalmers, 2003; Weiser, 1994)  | infrastructural limits, enabling users to exploit edges.      |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Seamless Concealment            | Fabricates frictionless continuity, conceals system failures, |
|  (Bell & Dourish, 2007)          | hides authority shifts, and eliminates opportunities to repair|
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Exposing infrastructural seams creates direct operational affordances for human users (Chalmers, 2003; Chalmers & Galani, 2004). Real-world computational architectures exhibit unavoidable material edges: radio frequencies fluctuate, satellite positioning drifts inside buildings, and battery voltages drop under load (Chalmers, 2003). In mixed-reality games, players quickly treated satellite signal shadows cast by tall buildings as tactical hiding locations rather than network errors (Chalmers, 2003). Physical and technical seams are not system pathologies; they represent the concrete boundaries of computational environments (Chalmers & Galani, 2004).

The corporate vision of ubiquitous computing functions as an ungrounded technical fantasy (Bell & Dourish, 2007). Deployed computing infrastructure remains messy, incomplete, and dependent on legacy physical systems that resist clean corporate control (Bell & Dourish, 2007). Modern retail and marketing frameworks reproduce this seamless fantasy by treating operational friction as an intolerable defect. In pursuing frictionless environments, commercial developers systematically conceal the physical points where computational control extracts economic value.

Interactive machines perceive human activities through narrow sensor apertures (Suchman, 1985). In observational studies of automated photocopiers, the computational system understood human action only through internal microswitches, projecting complex user behavior onto fixed procedural programs (Suchman, 1985). The machine cannot perceive the situated physical reality of the human actor.

Resolving this sensory divide requires immediate, tangible mechanisms for operational repair rather than abstract system disclosures (Suchman, 1985). When an automated system malfunctions, users do not benefit from abstract explanations of internal machine logic; they require physical levers, paper-release doors, and manual abort controls to correct errors and restore operation (Suchman, 1985). Agency within physical computational environments requires tangible tools to halt, contest, and correct algorithmic operations during execution. Algorithmic literacy represents an operational capacity to act rather than a passive comprehension of system code.

Seamful design provides direct operational affordances rather than informational disclosures (Chalmers, 2003). Modern explainable artificial intelligence literature routinely reduces system transparency to informational text boxes and advisory dashboards (Ehsan et al., 2024). Seamful interaction focuses on structuring operational controls so that material and software discontinuities can be detected and navigated during action (Chalmers, 2003; Chalmers & Galani, 2004).

5. Comparative Evaluation Across the Four Marks

Evaluating physical and hybrid arrangements across the four operational marks distinguishes genuine Decision Experience Design from standard choice architecture and bureaucratic organizational control.

[[TABLE]]

5.1 Ride-Hail Kerbside Matching

Ride-hail pickups satisfy all four operational marks of physical Decision Experience Design. The platform operates as an economically independent entity arbitrating between two participants with opposing incentives (Cameron, 2024). The software continuously tracks driver acceleration rates, GPS points, acceptance statistics, passenger locations, and account ratings (Möhlmann et al., 2021). The match locks both participants into binding physical obligations: the driver must navigate an unrevealed route for a predetermined fee under timer constraints, while the passenger incurs fees for failing to reach the pickup coordinate within three minutes (Cameron, 2024; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). Dispatch algorithms, surge factors, and incentive structures drift continuously across unannounced software builds (Lee et al., 2015). Driver and passenger physically execute a match computed by an algorithmic intermediary.

5.2 The Airport Biometric Gate

International border boarding gates deploy the Traveler Verification Service (TVS) to automate identity verification (CBP, 2018; TSA, 2026). The federal government serves as a third-party intermediary arbitrating access between commercial air carriers and passengers (Adey, 2004). The system ingests commercial flight manifests from the airline while simultaneously recording live facial biometric geometry from the passenger at the turnstile (CBP, 2018; TSA, 2026). The determination commits an absolute outcome: biometric verification opens the physical boarding gate, whereas a system non-match blocks boarding and alerts enforcement officers (Adey, 2004; Kitchin & Dodge, 2009). The system updates facial gallery subsets and passenger risk scores for every departing flight (Adey, 2004; TSA, 2026).

The airport gate reveals an operational hybrid: the algorithm generates a determination, but human agents commit the physical outcome. Border personnel and gate agents routinely override false rejections, verify secondary physical documents, and negotiate system discrepancies (Kitchin & Dodge, 2009).

The biometric gate illustrates how physical systems inflate exit costs. Federal statutory rules permit United States citizens to opt out of commercial biometric scans, but field audits reveal that mandated notification signage remains obscure, missing, or obscured from passenger sightlines (Gambler, 2022). Travelers attempting to exercise opt-out rights routinely encounter threats of denied boarding, severe delays, or punitive secondary interrogations by border guards (DHS, 2025). The physical environment renders nominal legal opt-out rights prohibitively costly.

5.3 Warehouse Pick Paths and Rate Systems

Automated fulfillment centers direct warehouse workers along software-optimized routes, indicating specific item locations and displaying countdown timers for item extraction (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; Delfanti, 2021). Wearable scanners record physical movements and calculate task execution speeds in real time (Cheon & Erickson, 2025). If a worker remains inactive for more than several minutes, the platform logs "Time Off Task," accumulating records that trigger automated termination notices (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; Gutelius & Theodore, 2019).

The warehouse rate system does not satisfy all four marks of Decision Experience Design. The platform belongs entirely to the corporate employer rather than an independent third party. Frontline supervisors function merely as diagnostic investigators summoned to confirm or excuse automated algorithmic penalties (Cheon & Erickson, 2025). Telemetric sensing remains entirely one-sided: the scanner monitors the worker's physical body, while the consumer remains an unmonitored order record in a database. The determination binds the worker under threat of discharge but imposes zero physical obligations on the purchasing consumer. Picking quotas fluctuate dynamically based on seasonal logistics velocity and opaque software revisions (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; U.S. Senate HELP Committee, 2024). The warehouse represents asymmetric algorithmic control rather than multi-party decision design.

The warehouse illustrates how algorithmic metrics manifest as physical constraints. State statutory regulations restrict these physical manifestations: California Assembly Bill 701 (2021) and the New York Warehouse Worker Protection Act (2022) require employers to provide written quota descriptions and prohibit performance algorithms from penalizing workers for taking bathroom breaks. Regulatory authorities in France reduced corporate fines while ruling that continuous real-time scanner metrics violate European privacy laws by transforming ordinary physical movements into permanent disciplinary evidence (Conseil d'État, 2025).

5.4 Hotel Housekeeping Dispatch

Hospitality facilities deploy mobile workforce management platforms to direct room cleaning sequences (Spektor et al., 2023). Hotel attendants receive shifting cleaning assignments on handheld screens (Spektor et al., 2023). When guests request expedited check-ins, the software reorganizes the schedule, forcing attendants to maneuver linen carts between distant floors rather than cleaning rooms in geographical sequence (Spektor et al., 2023). Attendants cannot decline platform orders without facing workplace discipline (Spektor et al., 2023). The system operates as an asymmetric corporate tool: it records the employee's labor and responds to guest requests, binding the worker's physical exertion while providing the guest with seamless service (Spektor et al., 2023).

5.5 Tracked Casino Floors

Casinos track patron activities through magnetic loyalty cards and network-connected gaming terminals (Schüll, 2005, 2012). The system records bet frequency, wagering size, button actuation speed, and total losses (Schüll, 2005). Modern slot platforms dynamically modify payout pacing, visual assets, and game volatility based on real-time biometric and behavioral data, while floor managers adjust spatial banks using real-time foot-traffic maps (Schüll, 2012).

The casino floor does not qualify as Decision Experience Design. The house owns the entire apparatus, acting as an integrated commercial predator rather than an independent arbiter. The system ingests telemetry exclusively from the gambler, altering odds to maximize house revenue. Casino tracking represents sensor-augmented choice architecture designed for unilateral value extraction.

5.6 SFpark: The Declared Operating Envelope

The SFpark municipal parking system deployed roadway sensors to monitor block-by-block vehicle occupancy, adjusting street meter rates to maintain target vacancy levels of one to two spaces per block (Pierce & Shoup, 2013). The parking system alters pricing based on aggregate behavioral demand, but it operates through a published, predictable schedule (Pierce & Shoup, 2013). Rates shift within pre-announced ranges across six-week evaluation cycles (Pierce & Shoup, 2013).

|                            THE DECLARED OPERATING ENVELOPE (SFpark)                              |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Occupancy Telemetry       ==>  Aggregated over 6-week cycles across city blocks.                |
|  Adjustment Schedule       ==>  Published, pre-announced intervals (no intraday volatility).     |

SFpark illustrates a declared operating envelope in municipal infrastructure. The operational rules shift, but that adaptation remains bounded, public, and slow enough for human drivers to assess and navigate deliberately (Pierce & Shoup, 2013). The system lacks a multi-party intermediary and binds patrons only through standard municipal parking ordinances.

5.7 App-Required Physical Access and Cashless Counters

Automated convenience stores requiring mobile application check-ins and retail establishments that reject cash transactions enforce digital compliance at the physical boundary. The underlying logic remains static: software checks credentials at the doorway and applies an unchanging rule to all visitors. The system adapts nothing in real time.

These spaces generate severe digital technology captivity (Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). Consumers who lack smartphones, active cellular data plans, or digital financial instruments face exclusion from purchasing food and essential merchandise (Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). Municipalities and state legislatures in Massachusetts and New York intervened by mandating cash acceptance at physical storefronts, blocking corporations from turning digital payment credentials into prerequisites for physical retail participation.

5.8 Electronic Shelf Labels

Federal regulators and legislative inquiries express concern that digital shelf displays enable surveillance pricing, where grocery stores alter food costs using facial scanners, personal loyalty histories, or local weather conditions (FTC, 2024, 2025; New York State, 2025; Warren & Casey, 2024). New York enacted statutory requirements compelling retailers to post disclosures whenever in-store prices rely on personal data algorithms (New York State, 2025).

Empirical deployments diverge sharply from these surveillance capabilities. Retailers install electronic shelf labels to reduce the labor costs of manually replacing paper tags and to implement scheduled price adjustments across entire store inventories (Stamatopoulos et al., 2021). Documented commercial deployments in the United States do not read individual shoppers standing before shelves to deliver individualized prices. Electronic shelf tags represent unactivated computational infrastructure rather than functioning Decision Experience Design.

5.9 Hostile Built Architecture

Anti-vagrancy spikes, divided public benches, and sloped architectural ledges enforce social exclusion through physical geometry (Petty, 2016; Rosenberger, 2023). Hostile architecture satisfies none of the marks of Decision Experience Design. It incorporates no sensors, executes no algorithmic calculations, and mediates no bilateral transaction. It represents unilateral physical coercion operating through non-digital materials (Petty, 2016).

6. Structural Evaluation of the Theoretical Model

The empirical evidence clarifies which configurations of physical decision systems survive analytical scrutiny.

+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                            EVALUATION OF THE FOUR THEORETICAL FORMS                              |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Form 1: Algorithmic Intermediary in Physical Space  | VALID, but restricted to transit, border  |
|                                                      | security, and gig logistics.              |
+------------------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
|  Form 2: Mechanical Devices as Independent Arbiters  | REFUTED. Mechanical tools lack adaptive   |
|                                                      | capacity; humans represent bureaucracy.   |
+------------------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
|  Form 3: Physical Systems as Choice Architecture     | BASELINE REALITY. Most hybrid spaces are  |
|                                                      | static servicescapes or access controls.  |
+------------------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+
|  Form 4: Physical Space as Enforcement Site          | EMPIRICALLY CONFIRMED. Material bodies    |
|                                                      | absorb the structural costs of systems.   |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Form 1 claims that algorithmic intermediaries reach directly into physical space to bind participants. The evidence validates this claim within a circumscribed operational domain. Ride-hail dispatch and biometric border checkpoints demonstrate that automated intermediaries ingest bilateral data and commit outcomes that participants must execute with their physical bodies (Adey, 2004; Cameron, 2024). Outside transit logistics and state security operations, commercial environments satisfying all four marks remain virtually non-existent.

Form 2 claims that non-digital physical mechanisms or human actors function as independent intermediaries. The evidence refutes this claim. Mechanical artifacts enforce behavioral scripts, but their material components remain static across interactions (Akrich, 1987; Latour, 1992). Human gatekeepers adapt rules and assess individuals, but categorizing human personnel as algorithmic intermediaries collapses Decision Experience Design into general organizational sociology.

Form 3 claims that physical designs function as classical choice architecture. This claim describes the baseline structural reality of modern commerce. Most hybrid retail deployments, automated payment terminals, and ambient service environments incorporate zero real-time individual telemetry. They enforce static corporate rules uniformly across visitors, operating as traditional servicescapes and behavioral choice architectures (Bitner, 1992; Kotler, 1973; Thaler & Sunstein, 2008).

Form 4 claims that the physical layer serves as the operational site of enforcement and exit pricing. This claim receives overwhelming empirical confirmation. Physical environments represent the material boundary where computational systems enforce compliance: where turnstiles lock, where delivery drivers risk physical collisions to beat countdown clocks, and where individuals without digital credentials face physical exclusion (Cameron, 2024; Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). This reality applies equally to non-computational systems. The material world operates as the site where digital platforms cash their structural checks, forcing human bodies to absorb the frictions of automated models.

7. Operational Design Principles for Physical Computational Systems

Reforming computational deployments in physical environments requires concrete design standards that preserve human operational agency.

|                            DESIGN PRINCIPLES FOR PHYSICAL COMPUTATION                            |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  1. Structural Refusal           | Provide viable, unpenalized non-digital pathways for all      |
|                                  | basic services; eliminate coercive compliance gateways.       |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  2. Declared Operating Envelopes | Publish pricing boundaries and enforce predictable temporal   |
|                                  | intervals; eliminate opaque intraday algorithmic drift.       |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  3. Conspicuous Commitment       | Provide legible transactional acknowledgments at points of    |
|                                  | exchange; eliminate covert sensor-driven billing.             |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+

7.1 Structural Refusal in Built Space

Digital systems permit refusal through software actions, such as closing browser windows or clicking cancellation links. Refusal in physical space requires viable, unpriced non-digital operational pathways. Biometric border checkpoints and automated retail stores fail to provide legitimate exit options when non-digital alternatives trigger interrogations, delays, or outright exclusion from essential services (DHS, 2025; Gambler, 2022; Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). Physical environments must provide non-digital alternatives that impose zero financial penalties, social stigmatization, or procedural delays.

7.2 The Declared Operating Envelope

Dynamic pricing algorithms in public facilities must publish operational parameters and adhere to predictable evaluation timelines. Systems must not alter operating rules unpredictably through hidden automated recalculations. Mimicking the municipal architecture of SFpark, systems must bound algorithmic adaptations within published ranges and slow operational changes down to intervals that human cognitive faculties can evaluate deliberately (Pierce & Shoup, 2013).

7.3 Conspicuous Commitment Over Seamless Extraction

Corporate design prioritizes seamless interaction to conceal the exact moments when financial extraction occurs. Consumers reject invisible tracking architectures, favoring visible verification mechanisms. In grocery environments, retail corporations retreated from automated ceiling camera tracking after shoppers rejected invisible billing systems. Shoppers preferred cart-mounted terminals that display running financial tallies and confirm individual item registrations as goods enter the basket. Consumers require explicit, legible confirmations when economic or legal commitments occur.

7.4 The Principle of the Accountable Human

Software systems routinely route human grievances into automated form dead-ends and customer service chatbots. In physical facilities, platforms station retail clerks and security personnel who possess zero organizational authority to alter automated scores, forcing them to absorb user distress on behalf of absent software engineers.

+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        THE STRUCTURAL SHIFT TO ADMINISTRATIVE HUMAN PRESENCE                     |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  The Human Shock Absorber Model   | Low-wage staff possess zero system discretion, enforce       |
|  (Current Standard Deployment)   | opaque machine outputs, and absorb public frustration.        |
+----------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------+
|  The Accountable Human Model     | Physically present personnel possess verified administrative  |
|  (Required Ethical Standard)     | authority to override machine flags and resolve grievances.   |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Physical computational systems must ban this buffer model. Wherever an algorithmic ruling binds a human body in physical space, the operating entity must station a physically present employee holding explicit institutional authority to grant exceptions, reverse algorithmic determinations, and formally answer for the system's operational outputs.

7.5 Unaddressed Research Domains

Academic literature exhibits two foundational omissions. Empirical researchers have never measured algorithmic literacy as a distinct cognitive capability that moderates decision success within physical environments. Scholarship offers extensive surveys measuring consumer acceptance and perceived convenience, but provides zero empirical evaluations of the cognitive skills required to navigate adaptive physical spaces.

Users possess no technological counter-delegation mechanisms in physical environments. Gig delivery workers and retail consumers enter automated physical encounters relying solely on sensory observation. Corporate platforms deploy real-time telemetry architectures, dynamic pricing engines, and predictive behavioral models to optimize every second of the interaction. Countering this structural imbalance requires building user-facing technologies that automate counter-monitoring, audit environmental metrics, and protect human autonomy in physical encounters.

8. Testable Propositions and Methodological Boundaries

The theoretical arguments advanced here yield four testable empirical propositions:

1. Physical service environments that dynamically read individual biometric or transactional streams to modify access or pricing produce higher rates of user entrapment and perceived helplessness than static arrangements that impose identical economic costs.
2. Adaptive computational environments that operate within bounded, published operating envelopes produce higher user decision calibration and lower economic error rates than systems executing unannounced, real-time algorithmic adjustments at the same price variance.
3. Automated physical security checkpoints staffed by personnel with formal authority to grant administrative overrides resolve contested flags with lower rates of service denial than checkpoints where personnel are limited to enforcing machine outputs.
4. Field studies that track customer and employee telemetric streams simultaneously will show that algorithmic systems executing bilateral binding determinations at the point of encounter occur in a small minority of commercial service spaces.

This analysis operates under specific empirical constraints. Proprietary corporate source code and private algorithm weights remain inaccessible behind trade secret protections, forcing researchers to evaluate commercial deployments via regulatory filings, court records, and field observations (California, 2021; FTC, 2025; New York State, 2022). Future fieldwork inspecting live dispatch software and industrial sensory networks will refine the operational boundaries of this taxonomy.

The physical environment does not dissolve computational power; it exposes and anchors that power. When algorithmic systems extend into built environments, they produce concrete physical junctions where human bodies must resolve the friction of automated calculations. Designing these systems with operational integrity requires abandoning the corporate ideal of seamlessness, exposing material boundaries, and ensuring that institutional authority remains visible, contestable, and accountable to human judgment.
