# F: The casino floor, the queue, the airport and hostile architecture as designed decision environments

*Facet F of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat F of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Four designed physical settings where Part 4's hardest question bites: the casino floor, the waiting line, the airport and the anti-homeless bench. Form 2 of the Part 4 README says a purely physical or human arrangement can itself be an intermediary; form 3 says physical design is only choice architecture. These four settings test which of them read the people inside them, bind two parties and change in response, and which hold still. The facet also tests whether Part 3's effect-based definition of a dark pattern ("presents one thing and delivers another to the provider's benefit and at the user's cost") already has a physical counterpart with its own name. The research questions:

- F1. The casino: what Schüll says is designed (floor, machine, mathematics), what the casino reads about the player, whether the environment adapts to the individual, when tracking began, and whether the casino is an early physical/digital hybrid.
- F2. Queues: how operations and service research treats the wait as a designed experience, who decides order, and what changes when the queue becomes virtual and is sold in tiers.
- F3. The airport: how scholars describe it as a space that sorts and commits people, and whether participation is mandatory in the series' sense.
- F4. Hostile or defensive architecture: what it is, who the parties are, whether intent is part of its definition, and whether anyone extends "dark pattern" off the screen (search logged).
- F5. For each setting in its pre-digital and digital form, which of the four marks it carries: a distinct intermediary; it reads both parties; it binds both; it does not hold still.

`schull2005digital` | Schüll, N. D. (2005). Digital gambling: The coincidence of desire and design. *The ANNALS of the American Academy of Political and Social Science*, 597(1), 65–81.
- *Record:* Crossref 10.1177/0002716204270435 (journal-article, issued 2005-01, vol. 597 iss. 1, pp. 65–81, author Schull). OpenAlex W2108743797. No `update-to`.
- *Read status:* Full text, author copy of the publisher's typeset version (running heads and folios 65–81 present), `natashadowschull.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/journalart-Schull-ANNALS-digital-gambling-coincidence-desire-design.pdf`, 17 pages.
- *What it argues:* The gaming industry "has established itself as an engine for experimentation and innovation with emergent digital capabilities, producing military-grade surveillance networks, sophisticated systems of accounting, and sleek gaming machinery" (p. 66). What is designed, in Schüll's account, falls into three layers. First, time and ergonomics on the floor: Cummings's "gaming productivity refers to wagering action (play) per patron per interval" (quoted p. 66); the switch from pull-handle to push-button doubles games per hour (p. 67); seat heights and screens that "slant at 38 degrees" reduce fatigue (p. 68); "noise cancellation technology to remove 'destructive interference' coming from the outside world (Kranes 2000, 33)" (p. 68); "Computerized menus allow players to key orders into a pad of coded choices, directly transmitting specific wants ... to employees in the proximity who wear 'vibrating call devices'" (p. 68). Second, the game mathematics: designers select payout schedules for player types, "matching math with markets, player types with schedule types" (pp. 70–71); one informant's patent would lower the payout rate when a progressive meter climbs, and "Although his technology is not yet able to tailor itself to the gaming patterns of particular players by selectively altering payout to retain their play, its innovation is to operationalize the recognition that motivations change in relation to a game's stakes" (p. 72). Third, what the machine reads about the player: gambling machines "function not only as individual play boxes but as 'electronic surveillance devices' (Casino Gaming Magazine 1990, 6) whose internal monitors track a player's game preferences, wins and losses, number of coins played per game, number of games played per minute, number of minutes or hours of play, number of visits to the casino, number of drinks consumed, and more. Individual machines perform these recording functions within a larger network of units controlled by a central computer. Originally engineered to track player behavior inside just one casino, digital tracking technologies embedded in games now track behavior across diverse consumer spaces by linking machines in taverns, supermarkets, pharmacies, and convenience stores" (p. 72). Note 5 (p. 79) quotes Klein 2002 on tracking services that supply "player photos, electronic signature identification, messages for players on screens". The environment does adapt to the individual at the machine level: Silicon Gaming's video poker has a "dynamic play rate"; "If you play slow, it deals slow. When you go so fast [imitates rapid pressing on buttons], the game detects it and adapts" (p. 70), and Schüll concludes that "digital machines adapt themselves to the unique speed of each player" (p. 70). Players seek isolation from human staff: "Rocky said, 'I didn't want to have a human interface'" (p. 73). The article closes on the convergence: "As the gap between desire and design grows smaller, the two appear to coincide" (p. 76).
- *Bearing:* The machine reads the player (mark 2, one side only) and does not hold still (mark 4) at the level of pace and, in the patent, payout. There is no distinct intermediary: the house designs, reads and pays. The casino in 2005 is a two-party arrangement with an adaptive, reading instrument on one side. This supports form 4 (the floor is where the house's rulings become money and time) and complicates form 1 (adaptivity in physical space predates the web platform). It refutes a strong form 3 for the digital casino: the machine is not a static menu.

`schull2012touchpoint` | Schüll, N. D. (2012). The touch-point collective: Crowd contouring on the casino floor. *Limn*, 2 ("Clouds and Crowds").
- *Record:* No article DOI. The *Limn* page (`limn.press/articles/the-touch-point-collective-crowd-contouring-on-the-casino-floor/`) gives issue 2, 2012, ISSN 2831-9699, and a journal-level DOI 10.70312/LIMN. Listed on the author's site as *LIMN* 2, 2012.
- *Read status:* Full text, author copy (`natashadowschull.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/journalart-NDS-TouchpointCollective.pdf`, 7 PDF pages, no folios; I locate by paragraph, counting the opening italic paragraph as 1). The piece ends: "[This piece draws on a chapter of the book, Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas, 2012, Princeton University Press]."
- *What it argues:* On priority: "Many surveillance techniques first applied in casinos were only later adapted to other domains—airports, financial trading floors, shopping malls, banks, and government agencies" (para. 1). On what is read and since when: "Nearly 70 percent of casino patrons in the United States participate in so-called loyalty programs, using player cards to gamble rather than coins, paper money, or tickets. While their participation grants them redeemable points based on the volume of their play, it grants casinos a wealth of information. Casino player tracking systems, inspired by airline and credit card reward programs in the mid-1980s, record the value of each bet gamblers make, their wins and losses, the rate at which they push slot machine buttons, and what drinks and meals they purchase" (para. 3). Harrah's "parses its market into ninety different segments and addresses each with a unique marketing scheme" (para. 3). On the pre-tracking floor: "In the past, strategic adjustments to casino layouts, game selections, or marketing campaigns were based either on trial and error or on projective modeling techniques such as 'stochastic migration'" run on observers' notes (para. 4); now "'We've got 20,000 [behavioral] models per second, streaming off the floor,' remarks Javier Saenz, vice president of network systems at IGT" (para. 4). Compudigm's seePOWER renders heat maps of time played by demographic; one animation showed young women moving away from a bank of machines each evening when men left a showroom, and "Casino managers responded by creating 'a whole new protective area for those women' containing the machines they liked to play" (para. 6). The data cloud "had rendered visible the fleeting, real-time contours of a behavioral group whose constituents ... were likely unaware of their kinship" (para. 7). "Outside maps" plot carded play against home addresses by hour (para. 8). On adaptation: server-based gaming "allows casinos to flexibly react to unanticipated preference groups in real time, switching out game configurations (i.e theme, denomination, payout rate) to match player predilections as they emerge and shift" so that "the casino becomes 'dynamically responsive' to the affective and behavioral contingencies of its player markets (Green 2007:34). Knowledge and intervention are intimately linked in the creation of touch-point collectives" (para. 11).
- *Bearing:* This is the source that answers the gate question from a full read: the floor reads bet size, wins and losses, button-press rate, purchases, time on device, visits, seat position by time of day, demographic segment and home address, and it changes layout, game configuration, denomination and payout in response. Marks 2 (one side) and 4 are carried. Mark 1 is carried only if the tracking vendor (IGT, Bally, Compudigm) counts as a distinct intermediary between house and player; Schüll treats the casino as the actor. Mark 3 is not carried: the player is bound by outcomes and offers, the house binds itself to nothing. The dating (mid-1980s, modelled on airline and credit-card programmes) makes the casino an early physical/digital hybrid, which complicates form 1's wording "reaching into physical space": the reading started on a floor.

## (c) Synthesis

### F1. The casino

1. What is designed, in Schüll's account, is three layers: the floor's time-and-ergonomics regime (push buttons, seat heights, 38-degree screens, noise cancellation, in-seat service ordering), the game's mathematics (payout schedules matched to player types), and the recording apparatus inside the machine (`schull2005digital`, pp. 66–72).
2. What the casino reads about the player, from sources read in full: game preferences, wins and losses, coins per game, games per minute, minutes or hours of play, visits, drinks consumed (`schull2005digital`, p. 72); with the loyalty card, the value of each bet, wins and losses, the rate of button pushes, meals and drinks purchased; the data are cut into ninety segments and mapped to seat position by hour and to home address (`schull2012touchpoint`, paras. 3, 6, 8). Nearly 70 per cent of US patrons carry the card (para. 3).
3. The environment adapts at two levels. The machine adapts its dealing speed to each player (`schull2005digital`, p. 70). Server-based gaming lets the casino "switch out game configurations (i.e theme, denomination, payout rate) to match player predilections as they emerge and shift" (`schull2012touchpoint`, para. 11), and managers re-zone the floor from heat maps (para. 6). Per-player payout tailoring is described as a patent not yet realised (`schull2005digital`, p. 72).
4. When tracking started: player-tracking systems date from the mid-1980s and were modelled on airline and credit-card reward programmes; before them, layout changes rested on observers' notes and "stochastic migration" simulations (`schull2012touchpoint`, paras. 3–4).
5. Is the casino an early physical/digital hybrid? Yes, on Schüll's own claim that casino surveillance techniques were "later adapted to other domains—airports, financial trading floors, shopping malls" (`schull2012touchpoint`, para. 1), and on her 2005 dating of networked machine tracking to the 1990 trade press (p. 72). The pre-tracking floor (Friedman's machine-dominant layout against Kranes's playground) is a static design evaluated for its effect on mood (`finlay2006physical`, abstract only).

### F2. Queues

6. The field treats the wait as a designed experience from Maister (1985) on: perception and expectation are managed, and the unfair wait is the one that violates FIFO (`maister1985psychology`; `larson1987perspectives`, abstract). Who decides order is the provider, through a human — maître d', triage nurse, gate agent, line manager — who reads the party and the provider's state and commits a place (`maister1985psychology`, "People Want to Get Started"; `norman2008psychology`, p. 13).
7. When the queue becomes virtual, three things change. The queuer loses the reading of the line that the field says matters (`norman2008psychology`, p. 14; `zhou2003looking` and `buell2020lastplace`, abstracts). The system reads the guest's ticket for identity and eligibility and commits a return window (`norman2008psychology`, pp. 14–15). The industry describes this as making the wait "invisible" (`dickson2005managing`, abstract).
8. When the queue is sold in tiers, the allocation becomes a two-sided determination: what the operator grants one class it takes from the other, and no rule satisfies both (`alexander2012priority`, abstract); the regular queuer judges the express line unfair (`althenayyan2024notall`, abstract); the operator prices against overbuying (`frazelle2024paid`, abstract); and park managers say express-pass revenue rises with the regular wait and that prices are raised "season after season" so the priority line stays shorter (`hernandez2016priority`, p. 44). Maister's 1985 advice to hide premium servers from regular customers (`maister1985psychology`, "Unfair Waits") and Norman's 2008 advice to admit priority through "special unmarked doors" (p. 8) show the concealment of the tier is a design norm older than the software.

### F3. The airport

9. Scholars describe the airport as a filter that sorts mobility into speeds (`adey2004secured`, pp. 507–508, 512). Architecture did this first by "cutting up the open flow of space" (p. 505); profiling, biometrics and algorithmic CCTV now do it on data the passenger cannot see or correct (pp. 506–508). The sort is multi-party: airline records, state databases and vendor systems feed a determination the airline must enforce at the gate (`kitchin2009airport`, ms p. 6).
10. Whether the commitment is the algorithm's or a human's is contested inside the sources. Kitchin and Dodge find that "if the code 'fails' passage is halted" (ms p. 2) and that the passenger is "initially ... denied the 'right to fly'" when unrecognised (ms p. 6), but also that "Code ... is not law by itself", that gatekeepers "interpret the results, and negotiates with a passenger(s)" (ms pp. 3–4), and that officials sometimes override the whole system by eye (ms p. 15). The booth's geometry makes the asymmetry: the passenger cannot see the screen (ms pp. 13–14).
11. Is participation mandatory in the series' sense? For international movement, yes: Fuller and Harley's "discourse of exception" ("Only at the airport", as quoted in `kitchin2009airport`, ms p. 1), Salter's confessionary complex (`salter2007governmentalities`, abstract) and Adey's selectee who cannot shed the label (p. 506) describe an arrangement with no exit short of not travelling. Exit from the slow line is priced: Privium at 90 euros (`adey2004secured`, p. 507).

### F4. Hostile or defensive architecture

12. What it is: structures installed in spaces of public use "to render them unusable in certain ways or by certain groups" (`petty2016london`, p. 68); designs "closing off usages of objects or areas of public space in ways that target vulnerable populations" (`rosenberger2023classification`, p. 55); "silent agents" that manage behaviour without officials present (`savic2014unpleasant`, p. 1). The parties are the decision-maker (politician, landlord, shop owner: `definelicht2017hostile`, p. 28) and the targeted body; the object is one party's instrument.
13. Is intent part of the definition? The field is split and moving. Petty (2016, p. 68) and Chellew (2019, p. 19) define by intention. De Fine Licht (2017, pp. 34–35) treats the non-culpable purchaser as blameless; by 2021 he allows hostility by effect without intent (p. 5). Rosenberger (2017, ch. 3; 2023, pp. 56–57) rejects designer intent as the locus and distributes responsibility across an assemblage. Albertsen and Knight (2025, pp. 4–5) propose a definition by disadvantage alone, with no intent condition. Molnar and de Fine Licht (2025, abstract) write "deliberate or incidental". The trajectory is the one Part 3 took for dark patterns, from intent to effect, and it is not finished.
14. Does the literature already describe hostile architecture in dark-pattern terms? Not by that name. I found no work in OpenAlex or Consensus that calls a bench, a spike, a queue or a floor plan a "dark pattern" (rows 26–27). What exists is (a) Jensen's separate coinage "dark design" for exclusionary urban objects (`jensen2023bench`, abstract; `jensen2017dark`, not obtained); (b) Purohit et al.'s audit of a kiosk as a "hybrid physical-digital" interface whose room amplifies the screen's patterns (`purohit2026deception`, §2.3); (c) Krauß et al.'s "physical/virtual barriers" in XR (abstract). The physical counterpart of Part 3's effect test exists under other names: Petty's hidden coercive function inside a "more socially palatable" one (p. 76), Rosenberger's "conspicuousness" variable (2023, p. 63) and his "a kind of sign" (p. 64), Norman's line bent to look short ("Deceitful? Yes", p. 7). None of these authors connects to Brignull or Gray.

Reading of the table. Only the digital airport carries all four marks, and even there the commitment runs through a human gatekeeper (`kitchin2009airport`). The digital casino and the digital queue carry M2 (one side), M3 (queue only) and M4 but fail M1 because the provider owns the reading instrument; they are two-party arrangements with an adaptive instrument on one side. Hostile architecture in any form fails M1, M3 and M4 and reads no one; it is form 3's coercive cousin. The pre-digital cases that carry M2 and M3 do so through a human reader, which is the ruling the author still has to make.

### Evidence against the thesis

- Form 1 ("the same algorithmic intermediary reaching into physical space") is contradicted on dating by Schüll: the reading of bodies on a floor began in casinos in the mid-1980s, modelled on airline and credit-card schemes, and casino techniques moved to airports and malls later (`schull2012touchpoint`, paras. 1, 3). The phygital did not arrive from the web.
- Form 2 ("a purely physical arrangement can itself be an intermediary") is not supported by any hostile-architecture source read: Petty, Savičić and Savić, Rosenberger and de Fine Licht describe one party's instrument that reads no one and holds still. Where a physical arrangement reads and binds both parties pre-digitally, a human does the reading (Maister's maître d', Adey's and Kitchin and Dodge's officer).
- Mark 3 at the airport is weaker than Part 1's wording: Kitchin and Dodge show the determination is made "by a person interfacing with a computer and often in negotiation with the passenger" (ms p. 13) and sometimes by eye alone (ms p. 15). The algorithm proposes; a human commits. Part 4 cannot say the physical layer merely records the algorithm's ruling.
- The dark-pattern counterpart: the hostile-design literature already has effect-based definitions (Albertsen and Knight 2025; Rosenberger 2023) and a hidden-function concept (Petty 2016, p. 76; Rosenberger 2023, p. 63), so Part 4 would not be first to name the physical dark pattern; it would be renaming something the planning and philosophy literatures call inconspicuous hostile design, unpleasant design or dark design.
- De Fine Licht (2017, p. 30) places mild defensive measures inside Thaler and Sunstein's nudge. The physical literature itself files part of its subject under choice architecture, which supports form 3 against form 2.

### Gaps

- I found no work in OpenAlex or Consensus that applies the term "dark pattern" to a bench, spike, queue layout, casino floor or airport (rows 26–27). The nearest is a 2026 workshop preprint on a fast-food kiosk (`purohit2026deception`).
- I found no source that documents a third-party queue vendor allocating places between a venue and its customers; the tiered-queue literature treats the operator as allocator.
- I found no full-text source on the casino host or pit boss as a reader of players; Schüll's articles describe machines and software.
- Schüll's design chapters remain unread (section d). Adey 2008 and Amoore 2006, the two Elsevier papers most directly about the terminal as a calculative architecture and the biometric border, were not obtained.
- The question whether a human reader (maître d', gate agent, immigration officer, pit boss) counts as an intermediary is not addressed by any source read; the sources describe the human, they do not theorise the role in Part 1's terms.
