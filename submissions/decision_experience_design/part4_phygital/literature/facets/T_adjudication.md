# T: Adjudication — the four marks applied to the cases

*Facet T of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (argument facet, local session, model Fable), from facets A–G and X. It adds no source. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet does.** It takes the four marks the README draws from Part 1, adds Part 3's test at the match, and applies them to fifteen physical or phygital arrangements that facets A–G and X document. Every fact below is a facet's fact, cited by facet letter, bib key and the facet's own locator; where a facet read a source only at abstract level, the adjudication that rests on it is marked provisional. The facet does not decide which of Part 4's four forms the author should claim. It reports which cases carry which marks, where the marks fail to sort a case, and where the result collides with a sentence already in the series. Sentences that go beyond what a facet says are marked **[inference]**.

**The test, in the series' own words.** Part 1 (the author's live text, `published/2026-09-30_decision-experience-design.md`) defines the object at line 34: DXD "must address situations where choices are brokered through an adaptive, opaque algorithmic intermediary that reads multiple parties simultaneously and commits binding determinations that neither party fully controls." Line 42 gives the two-sided case: "A platform objective governs an adaptive intermediary, which continually ingests behavioral telemetry from multiple sides of an ecosystem—such as workers and consumers on an on-demand labor platform—and issues policy-bound determinations that govern both parties at once." Line 48 names the predecessor's premises: "a visible human designer, a static menu, and a single chooser." Line 116 restates the competence: "coordination mediated by an opaque, adaptive intermediary that reads multiple parties and commits binding decisions." Line 118 gives the fourth mark its wording: "Traditional media hold still; an algorithmic intermediary does not." Line 140: "A transparent triad remains a triad." Line 146: "offering a costless exit path makes the parties substitutable, collapsing an irreducible triadic mediator back into a separable, manageable dyad." Part 3's test at the match (`part3_prediction_markets/ARGUMENT.md`, section C, form 2, Claude-drafted): "a trade is coordination when each side leaves with what it came for, whatever happens next; it is a bet when what each side came for is decided later by an event neither controls, and only one of them can have it." Part 3's P4 (Claude-drafted) adds the choice/decision line: a decision "has to consider coordination through a third party"; `DRAFT.md` l. 59 defines a choice as "a selection among options whose joint outcome is fixed before the person selects" and a decision as "an act whose quality depends on the joint outcome for the parties the intermediary coordinates, and which the intermediary then commits."

From these I take four marks and one test. The operational readings are mine, and each goes past Part 1's words at the point stated.

- **M1, a distinct intermediary.** Part 1 says "intermediary" and "neither party fully controls." I read this as a third thing standing between two parties who each have an interest in the outcome, not one party's instrument. **[inference]** I apply it in two readings throughout, because the facets split on it: a *strict* reading, under which the thing must be economically distinct from both parties (the platform is neither driver nor rider; the state's matching service is neither carrier nor traveller); and a *functional* reading, under which a thing one party owns still counts if both parties face it in the encounter and neither can override it there. Section 4 says what each reading does to Part 1's own cases.
- **M2, it reads both parties.** Part 1 says "reads multiple parties simultaneously." **[inference]** I require that both parties be read *in the encounter*, not at design time through research, and I distinguish reading a party from reading a party's record (a manifest, an order). Where only a record is read I say "partly."
- **M3, it binds both.** Part 1 says "binding determinations that neither party fully controls" and "govern both parties at once." **[inference]** I count a party as bound when it cannot costlessly undo the committed outcome. A provider that binds itself only by its own published rule or terms is marked "by rule"; a provider whose terms disclaim its own obligation is marked "not bound."
- **M4, it does not hold still.** Part 1 contrasts media that "hold still" with an intermediary that does not, and names "adaptivity means rules drift across retraining cycles" (l. 118). **[inference]** I read "does not hold still" as adaptation per person or per moment, within or between encounters, as against a fixed rule applied to everyone. Where the adaptation is slow, published and aggregate (a price step every six weeks for a whole block), I mark "partly" and flag it as a tempo question for the author (section 4, strain 2).
- **The test at the match.** Applied only where there is a match, that is, a moment at which the arrangement commits an outcome for two parties at once. Where there is no such moment (a bench, a shelf, a quota met over a shift), I write "no match" rather than force a verdict.

Evidence grade follows the facets' own legends: *full* (full text, any version), *partial* (named sections), *abstract* (abstract only), with facet G's document tiers (T1 statute, regulation, court or regulator; T2 company document; T3 agency technical report; T4 peer-reviewed study) where a case rests on documents. Confidence is mine: high where every mark rests on a full-text or T1–T3 source; medium where one mark rests on inference or a T2 page; low or provisional where a load-bearing source is abstract-only or not obtained.

## 1. The cases

### a. Ride-hail pickup at the kerb (and the delivery courier at the counter)

**What happens physically.** The driver's phone buzzes with a match and 15 seconds to accept; the destination and fare are hidden until acceptance (E `cameron2024making` p. 476; E `rosenblat2016algorithmic` p. 3762). At the kerb a timer runs (60 seconds to 10 minutes by ride type) before the driver may mark a no-show (Cameron p. 477); in shared rides the pickup order is locked (p. 477); on UberPOOL the navigation "comes directly as their own system, you cannot use any other" (E `mohlmann2021algorithmic` p. 2009). The rider walks to the pin the app chose and meets the driver there: "The app took me to the back ... she was in the front and it was really cold ... destroyed me on the ratings" (Cameron p. 486). For couriers the ruling is a four-minute ping with no decline button (E `griesbach2019algorithmic` p. 11) and an address released only with the bag in hand (E `veen2020platform` p. 398).

**M1: yes, on both readings.** The app is owned by the platform, which is neither the driver nor the rider; Cameron calls the work process "a three-way interaction between the driver, the rider, and the app" (p. 468). Facet E marks this the gate case for a digital ruling becoming a physical fact with both parties bound (E (d)).
**M2: yes.** Driver GPS, acceptance and cancellation rates, telemetry; rider location, rating and destination, the last hidden from the driver until a loyalty tier is earned (Cameron p. 489; E table row 1).
**M3: yes.** The driver is bound to the wait timer, the locked route and a blind fare; the rider to the pickup pin, an upfront price "whatever the route is" (Möhlmann p. 2008), a cancellation fee, and to being logged off for repeated cancelling (Möhlmann p. 2007); a low-rated passenger "risk[s] rejection by drivers" (E `lee2015working` p. 1605). For delivery M3 holds weakly on the customer side: the customer rates and is not rated (Griesbach p. 7; Veen p. 397), and facet E records that whether the delivery customer is bound at all "is not shown" (E `veen2020platform` bearing).
**M4: yes.** "Over the seven years of this study, the algorithmic management system frequently changed" (Cameron p. 488); Uber "constantly updated the system in order to make it more difficult for drivers to game" (Möhlmann p. 2013); surge "changed too rapidly and unexpectedly to utilize" (Lee p. 1607); pay structures "pivot" (Griesbach pp. 7–8).

**Test at the match.** The rider leaves the match with a ride at a known price; the driver leaves with a fare it did not see before accepting (Rosenblat & Stark p. 3762). **[inference]** Both have what they came for at the fill in the sense Part 3 means: no event after the match decides which of them gets it. The driver's complaint is about the terms being blind, not about the outcome being contingent. Passes, with the blind fare recorded as a disclosure defect rather than a bet.

**Forms supported.** Form 1 and form 4 at full strength (E's own verdict on Cameron, Möhlmann and Rosenblat & Stark). Two qualifications from facet E: the ruling is delivered on a phone, so "the physical facts ... follow from it rather than replacing it" (E `mohlmann2021algorithmic` bearing); and exit is priced *gradually*, through Cameron's "programmed leniency" (p. 488) with a cliff only at signal falsification (p. 492), so Part 1's "structural refusal" would have to be distinguished from a tolerance the platform grants and can withdraw (E, Evidence against, item 5). Human hands also appear: shopper support staff "badger" couriers (Griesbach p. 11), and delivery thresholds are "adjusted on a local basis" by managers (Veen p. 399).

**Evidence.** Cameron 2024, Möhlmann et al. 2021, Rosenblat & Stark 2016, Lee et al. 2015, Griesbach et al. 2019, Veen et al. 2020: all full text, publisher versions (E). **Confidence: high.** This is the one case in the fifteen on which the facets agree that all four marks are carried without an interpretive stretch.

### b. The warehouse pick path and rate system

**What happens physically.** The scanner shows the next item, the shelf and "the time the picker has to complete the task—often a minute or so" (E `delfanti2021machinic`, "Pick"); the system "calculates a rate" from scan-to-scan intervals (E `cheon2025fulfillment` CSCW228:10); more than 3–5 minutes of inactivity is Time Off Task, and "if more than 2 hours of TOT are recorded, this error would bring on an 'instant termination'" (CSCW228:13–14). Follow-me robots set walking pace and a patented wristband would guide the hand (E `gutelius2019future` pp. 34, 54, 58). In the French warehouse a "stow machine gun" flag fires when fewer than 1.25 seconds separate two scans and an idle flag after ten consecutive minutes (G `conseildetat2025amazon` paras. 5–6). California defines the reading as "time categorized as performing tasks or not performing tasks" (G `california2021ab701` §2100(e)(1)).

**M1: strict no; functional partly.** The scanner software is distinct from the worker, but the second party at the other end of the ruling is "the customer's order, present only as the pick path and the volume" (E `cheon2025fulfillment` bearing), and the system's owner is the employer. **[inference]** On the strict reading the system is the employer's instrument. On the functional reading it stands between worker and manager: the manager is the "context prober" who clears or confirms a TOT (CSCW228:13), and "Managers end up being the only source of unpredictability for workers" (CSCW228:19).
**M2: partly.** The worker is read continuously; the customer is read as an order (E, Evidence against, item 1). No second human party is read in the encounter.
**M3: one side.** Binding on the worker (feedback session, contract non-renewal, termination: Delfanti "Management"; Cheon CSCW228:14; Gutelius p. 55). The customer is "not bound" (E table). On the French record the flag "leads to a conversation, not an automatic sanction" (G `conseildetat2025amazon` bearing), and the worker must "faire valoir certains motifs pouvant relever de leur vie privée" to a superior (para. 7).
**M4: yes.** Rates rise to 420 units an hour at peak (Cheon CSCW228:9); Amazon's own definition of time off task moved from five minutes of unplanned time in 2020 to one cumulative hour of a 30-minute-threshold metric by 2024 (G `help2024amazon` p. 30 and n. 108); thresholds "are parameters" (G C-W2 bearing). New York's statute answers with a two-business-day notice of any quota change (G `newyork2022wwpa` §781).

**Test at the match.** No match. The worker's result is decided over a shift and then by a manager reading the number; there is no moment at which two parties leave with anything.

**Forms supported.** Form 4 strongly: "the floor is where the number becomes a firing" (E `cheon2025fulfillment` bearing), and the statutes legislate the surface (a written quota before it may bind, G `california2021ab701` §§2101–2102). Form 1 weakly: facet E's own summary is that "Form 1's distinct intermediary is, on the floor, an algorithm plus a person" (E, Evidence against, item 2). Delfanti's thesis cuts against a pure form 1 by name: "it is futile to analyze the technical organization of the labor process without studying the authoritarian nature of work" ("Theorizing Amazon labor"). A correction facet G insists on: the Conseil d'État reversed the CNIL on all three scanner indicators and cut the fine to €15 million; what stands is the 31-day retention finding and the video-surveillance breaches (G C24). Part 4 may not cite the January 2024 finding as settled.

**Evidence.** Cheon & Erickson 2025 (full text, author preprint with ACM pagination), Delfanti 2021 *NMS* (full text, preprint, located by section), Gutelius & Theodore 2019 (full text, grey literature), Delfanti's book (partial, chapter 1 only; the scanner chapter unread); California statute (full, T1), New York statute (partial, T1 via mirror), HELP report (partial, T1), Conseil d'État (partial, T1), CNIL decision (not obtained). **Confidence: high on the facts; medium on the classification**, because M1 turns on the author's ruling about ownership.

### c. The hotel: face or app check-in, the digital key, the housekeeping app

Three arrangements, adjudicated separately.

**c1. Facial-recognition check-in.** The kiosk reads the ID card then the face through paired spoken instructions; "Only in 79 cases of these 361 (22%) do the guests get recognized during the first cycle" (E `greiffenhagen2023work` PDF p. 11); failure after three cycles resets to the ID step (PDF p. 10); staff helped in about 70 of 361 cases (PDF p. 11 n. 3). **M1: no.** The kiosk is the hotel's instrument and reads one party against an identity database; the hotel is not read (E bearing). **M2: no.** **M3: one side**: refusal costs the whole check-in; "Hotel: nothing" (E table). **M4: no**: "nothing suggests the system changes over the fifteen months" (E bearing). **Match:** no match; a verification. **Form:** facet E's phrase is "form 4 without form 1's intermediary: a static verifier whose physical layer carries all the cost." **[inference]** On the marks this is form 3 with a biometric rule, and it is the richest case in the corpus for Part 1's three operations being done with the body (E E4). Evidence: Greiffenhagen et al. 2023 full text (author preprint); Xu & Greiffenhagen 2024 abstract only. Confidence high for the marks.

**c2. The digital key.** Marriott's terms: "Upon receiving a digital key, the first night's charges will be applied to your credit card on file"; liability for "inability to use, a digital key" is disclaimed; beacon location tracking is opt-in and separate (G `marriott2026digitalentry` clauses 1, 11, 12). **M1–M4: no.** "a digital key is a static credential; the terms describe no adaptive rule" (G bearing); the exit (a front desk) is "undocumented, not absent." **Form 3** (G's classification). Evidence: T2 company page, full text; Hilton not obtained. Confidence medium (one T2 source).

**c3. The housekeeping app ("UpKeep").** Before the app, attendants had a paper list and chose their order; now "GRAs' ability to control their own task flow ... depends on the configuration of UpKeep within their hotel properties" (E `spektor2023designing` p. 628); assignments outside the daily station mean "more physical labor" (p. 632); a late-entered assignment makes the attendant "get in trouble" for time she never had (p. 631); "GRAs could not realistically assert in-app rejections without fear of termination" (p. 630). **M1: strict no; functional partly.** The app is the property's instrument, but it stands between attendant and supervisor, and the guest enters it as a code ("Guest Declined Service", p. 629). **M2: partly.** The attendant is read (completion, timestamps, location); the guest is read as declined service and occupancy, "but binds only indirectly" (E bearing). **M3: one side, plus.** The attendant is bound; the guest only via points for declining service (p. 626). **M4: yes, within the property's hand.** Rush rooms reorder the list; "self-sequencing did not need to be built from scratch – they existed within UpKeep already, but simply weren't turned on in most properties" (p. 634). **Match:** no match between guest and attendant; the attendant's result is decided later by a supervisor reading a timestamp. **Form 4**, with E's finding that the "declared operating envelope" is set by the property, not the vendor or the worker, and that the one artefact that would constrain the ruling (a greyed-out "assign room" button past the union maximum, Fig. 6, p. 633) "exists in Figma." Evidence: Spektor et al. 2023 DIS, full text; Jianu 2025 abstract only. Confidence high for the marks, medium for the guest's side.

### d. The airport biometric gate

**What happens physically.** A camera at the boarding gate or the document-checker podium; a designated queue with a sign saying the photograph is optional and that a decliner "will be directed to standard screening processing" (G `tsa2026pia046e` p. 6). CBP builds a gallery per flight from the carrier's manifest and matches the face to it (G `cbp2018pia056` pp. 1–11; G C3). GAO found a sign at Las Vegas stating a 14-day retention a year after the rule had changed to 12 hours, signs "not consistently posted", and signs telling decliners to "see a CBP officer or a gate agent" when "CBP officers are typically not present at airport gates" (G `gao2022facialtestimony` p. 11). The 2025 rule records that citizens "have alleged that they have been referred to secondary inspection or told they would not be able to board because they declined biometrics", and removes the opt-out for non-citizens (G `dhs2025biometricrule`, section 6 and abstract).

**M1: yes, on both readings.** The Traveler Verification Service is the state's, not the carrier's and not the traveller's; Adey describes screening systems that "virtually sort passengers' information but also work to materially sort passenger's mobility" (F `adey2004secured` pp. 507–508); facet F's table gives the digital airport "Yes" on M1 (F F5).
**M2: yes, with one half read as a record.** The traveller's face and documents; the carrier's manifest and check-in opt-in flag (G C3). **[inference]** The carrier is read as a list, not as a party with a live interest at the gate; I mark M2 yes because the determination depends on both inputs, and note the record/party distinction in section 4.
**M3: yes, with a human at the point of commitment.** The match "admits you to the lane" (G bearing); "SSSS" on a boarding pass and the denied "right to fly" bind the airline (F `kitchin2009airport` ms pp. 6, 11). But Kitchin and Dodge insist that "the decision is one made by a person interfacing with a computer and often in negotiation with the passenger" (ms p. 13), and that at intra-EU borders "one of the most coded of processes becomes almost entirely uncoded by manual override" (ms p. 15). Facet F's verdict: "The algorithm proposes; a human commits."
**M4: yes, on profiles; still at the podium.** Profiles are secret and a "selectee" cannot shed the label (Adey p. 506); the gallery is rebuilt per flight; the rule at the podium "is the same for everyone that day" (G `tsa2026pia046e` bearing). Fuller and Harley's "metastability" is quoted at second hand (F `fuller2004aviopolis` via Kitchin and Dodge ms p. 4).

**Test at the match.** The matched traveller boards; the carrier boards a verified passenger. **[inference]** Both leave with what they came for. The decliner's result is decided later, by an officer who may not be there (GAO p. 11), at a price the regulator itself records (secondary inspection, threatened denial). The match test therefore passes for the compliant and fails for the refuser, which is the pattern form 4 predicts: exit is priced at the gate.

**Forms supported.** Form 1 and form 4 together; facet F calls Adey "the strongest support in this facet for form 1 and form 4 together", and facet G calls the GAO testimony its "best evidence on G6 and G7": a declared envelope on a sign that had drifted from the system's behaviour and an answerer who was absent. The strain is M3: the physical gate is where a human commits what the algorithm proposes (section 4, strain 3). Participation is mandatory in Part 2's sense for international travel (F F3 para. 11).

**Evidence.** CBP PIA-056, TSA PIA-046(e), GAO-22-106154, DHS final rule 2025: all partial reads of T1/T3 documents (G); Adey 2004 full text; Kitchin & Dodge 2009 full text, author manuscript (F); Adey 2008 and Amoore 2006 not obtained; Salter 2007 abstract. **Confidence: high** on documents and marks; the scholarship is 2004–2009 and describes an earlier generation of systems than the 2022–2025 documents, a gap no facet closes.

### e. The electronic shelf label and in-store pricing

**What happens physically.** A price on a shelf label that the FTC's staff say "could occur" at ecommerce frequency: "For companies that operate an ecommerce website or use electronic shelf labels, price changes could occur with similar frequency" (G `ftc2025staffperspective` PDF p. 4). Senators asked Kroger "Has Kroger ever used EDGE to change the price of an item more than one time within the same day?" and "Will customers have the option to opt out of the collection of personal data through ESL platforms?" (G `warren2024kroger` p. 4); no primary copy of the reply exists (G `kroger2024reply`). New York requires, "on, at, or near" the price and in the same medium, the sentence "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA" where personal data set it (G `newyork2025algorithmicpricing` §349-a(1)(b), (2)).

**M1: strict partly; functional partly.** The FTC's object is a third party between retailer and shopper (Revionics, PROS, Bloomreach: G `ftc2024orders`), so a distinct firm exists; but it is the retailer's vendor. **[inference]** This is the one physical case where a strict M1 might pass on vendor independence while the functional reading fails, because the shopper never faces the vendor.
**M2: partly, and not observed.** The tools read the retailer's loyalty and kiosk data (G `ftc2025staffperspective` PDF pp. 7, 9); "No document I opened shows an ESL deployment that reads the person in front of it" (G C6). Facet B's abstract-only studies point the other way: shoppers "largely unaware" and fairness perceptions unchanged (B `garaus2016shoppers`, abstract); one retailer installed ESLs with "no dynamic pricing" to signal consistency (B `boden2020effect`, abstract); ESLs "do not replace paper prices" (B `soutjis2017ethnography`, abstract).
**M3: shopper only.** The posted price is the price; the retailer sets it (G table).
**M4: capacity yes; observed partly.** Stamatopoulos et al. find "more and smaller price changes ... mostly price decreases, and they were dispersed in time" (G and B, abstract only).

**Test at the match.** The shopper pays the posted price and takes the good; passes as a transaction. **[inference]** If the price were set from the shopper's own data, the match would still clear; what changes is who set the terms, which the match test does not see. The statute's remedy is disclosure of cause, which Part 1 rules is awareness, not agency (G C18).

**Forms supported.** Form 1 "on the regulator's description of capacity, not on any observed deployment" (G, classification); form 3 as the observed practice (B B4 para. 16: "a capability the literature can see and a practice it mostly does not observe"). **Provisional.** Every peer-reviewed deployment study here is abstract-only (Stamatopoulos, Garaus, Boden, Soutjis in B and G), and Soutjis's ethnography, the one that would answer the shelf question directly, was not obtained by either facet. **Confidence: low–medium.**

### f. The casino floor with player tracking

**What happens physically.** A loyalty card inserted instead of coins; "Nearly 70 percent of casino patrons in the United States participate" (F `schull2012touchpoint` para. 3). The machine tracks "a player's game preferences, wins and losses, number of coins played per game, number of games played per minute, number of minutes or hours of play, number of visits to the casino, number of drinks consumed, and more" (F `schull2005digital` p. 72). Video poker adapts its dealing speed: "If you play slow, it deals slow" (p. 70). Server-based gaming lets the house switch "game configurations (i.e theme, denomination, payout rate) to match player predilections as they emerge and shift" (F `schull2012touchpoint` para. 11); managers re-zone the floor from heat maps (para. 6). Per-player payout tailoring is a patent "not yet able to tailor itself" (F `schull2005digital` p. 72). The UK code requires licensees to identify and interact with customers at risk, by "observation or loyalty carded play" (G `ukgc2026guidancepremises`).

**M1: no, on both readings.** "the house designs, reads, and pays" (F `schull2005digital` bearing); M1 is carried "only if the tracking vendor (IGT, Bally, Compudigm) counts as a distinct intermediary", and Schüll treats the casino as the actor (F `schull2012touchpoint` bearing).
**M2: one side.** The player is read in depth; "the player reads nothing of the house" (F F5 table).
**M3: one side.** "the player is bound by outcomes and offers; the house binds itself to nothing" (F F5).
**M4: yes, strongly.** Per-player dealing speed; real-time game configuration; floor re-zoning (F F1 para. 3). Facet F dates tracking to the mid-1980s, "inspired by airline and credit card reward programs" (F `schull2012touchpoint` para. 3).

**Test at the match.** A machine spin is Part 3's own object: the player's result is decided after the stake by an event the player does not control, and the house holds the edge. **[inference]** Fails as a bet, by Part 3's definition, before any question about the floor arises. The design question Schüll raises is about time on device, not about the match.

**Forms supported.** Form 4 ("the floor is where the house's rulings become money and time", F `schull2005digital` bearing); refutes a strong form 3 for the digital casino ("the machine is not a static menu"); complicates form 1 on dating, since "the reading started on a floor" and casino techniques were "later adapted to other domains—airports, financial trading floors, shopping malls" (F `schull2012touchpoint` para. 1). The UKGC guidance is form 2 "at the level of rule": a human reader with a "day diary with flags" doing the same duty as the carded system (G `ukgc2026guidancepremises` bearing).

**Evidence.** Schüll 2005 and 2012 *Limn*, full text (author copies); *Addiction by Design* design chapters not obtained (F's gate met "only at the downgraded level the brief allows"); Finlay et al. 2006 abstract; UKGC code full, guidance partial (T1); Nevada Regulation 5 partial (T1; the money ledger, not the tracking screen). **Confidence: high** for the marks; the pit boss and casino host as human readers are undocumented in any full text (F Gaps).

### g. The virtual queue sold in tiers, against the ordinary physical queue

**What happens physically.** In the ordinary line the queuer sees the line and the people behind; downward comparison improves affect and reduces reneging (F `zhou2003looking`, abstract); FIFO is the field's definition of justice (F `larson1987perspectives`, abstract). Order is decided by the provider's employee: the maître d', the triage nurse, the gate agent who takes the ticket so that "I have been asked to give up control of the situation" (F `maister1985psychology`, "Anxiety Makes Waits Seem Longer"). In the virtual queue the guest "tap[s] in using a MagicBand" in an arrival window; passes are "nonrefundable and nontransferable" while entitlements are "not guaranteed" and "subject to change or closure without notice or liability"; "traditional standby lines are still available" (G `disney2026lightninglane`). Norman: FastPass "doesn't let people go ahead of the line: it is a guaranteed service time", eligibility enforced by reading the park ticket (F `norman2008psychology` pp. 14–15), and the virtual queue "eliminate[s] the feedback that comes from being able to observe the length of a line" (p. 14). Park managers: "The sale of express products is directly proportional to waiting times"; prices rise "season after season" so the priority line stays shorter than the regular one (F `hernandez2016priority` p. 44).

*Ordinary physical queue.* **M1: no** (the line organises itself; where a human orders it, the reader is the provider's employee). **M2: no, or human** (the maître d' reads the party and the tables). **M3: yes** (the committed order binds every waiting party). **M4: no** (the line holds still; the human reader adjusts). Facet F's table gives the pre-digital queue M2 and M3 "via a person" and M1 "No" (F F5).

*Tiered virtual queue.* **M1: strict no; functional partly.** "the operator runs the system (third-party queue vendors not sourced)" (F F5). **[inference]** The allocation does stand between two parties with opposed interests, the payer and the non-payer, which is the one physical case in which a provider's instrument adjudicates between two *customers*; but the provider profits from the adjudication. **M2: one side, by machine.** The ticket is read for identity and eligibility; wristbands gather behaviour; the operator reads its own availability (F F5; G table). **M3: yes, asymmetric.** Return windows and classes bind payer and non-payer; Alexander et al. find that justice (FIFO) for one class is inequity for the other and no rule satisfies both (F `alexander2012priority`, abstract); Liu et al. find a priority option raises the regular queue's wait (F `liu2023customer`, abstract); the operator binds itself to nothing (G C8). **M4: partly.** Prices "vary by date"; the gate rule holds still for the day (G C12); seasonal repricing (Hernandez-Maskivker p. 44).

**Test at the match.** The payer gets a shorter line (not guaranteed); the non-payer's wait lengthens. **[inference]** One party's result comes at the other's expense, by the queue literature's own finding; and Frazelle and Katok find a revenue-maximising provider can set a priority fee higher than rational behaviour would allow because subjects overbuy (F `frazelle2024paid`, abstract). Fails Part 3's test in the way a bet does, except that the loser here is a bystander who bought nothing. The maître d' who seats by party size passes the match test only if the rule is accepted, which is Maister's whole advice: "the service provider must make vigorous efforts to ensure that these rules match with the customer's sense of equity" (F `maister1985psychology`, "Unfair Waits").

**Forms supported.** Form 4 strongly ("exit from the wait is priced, the price moves with load, and the operator's revenue rises with the length of the regular wait", F `hernandez2016priority` bearing); form 2 in its human variant for the 1985 queue (F `maister1985psychology` bearing); not form 1 unless M1 is read functionally. ### i. Hostile architecture: the bench, the spikes

### m. A human reader: the maître d', the gate agent, the front-desk clerk quoting an electronically set rate

## 3. What the four forms can each claim

### Form 1: the phygital is the same algorithmic intermediary reaching into physical space

**Supporting cases.** Ride-hail (a) at full strength; the airport gate (d) with a human at commitment; the warehouse (b) and the housekeeping app (c3) on M1, M4 and the worker's side of M3; SFpark (l1) and Singapore ERP (l2) as adaptive public pricing that reads the aggregate, not the person; the shelf label (e) as regulator-described capacity. **Cases that cut against it.** The second party is often not bound: the warehouse customer is an order, the kiosk's hotel is not read, the delivery customer rates and is not rated (E, Evidence against, item 1). A human hand prices exit in most physical settings (E item 2; F on Kitchin and Dodge). The intermediary does not need physical space: Rahman's freelancing platform "carries all four marks with no kerb and no clock" (E `rahman2021invisible` bearing), and Gandini says the platform is the point of production "irrespective of 'where' exactly the work is done" (E `gandini2019labour` PDF p. 12). The dating is wrong as worded: the casino floor read bodies from the mid-1980s and casino techniques moved to airports and malls later (F `schull2012touchpoint` paras. 1, 3), and Kimes's 1989 desk quoted electronically set rates (X). The phygital frontline is where the least deciding happens in tourism's own survey (E `tussyadiah2020review` ms pp. 12–14), and the shelf that can move mostly has not (B B4 para. 16). Barlow and Johnson state the static-physical/adaptive-digital contrast in a January 2026 commentary (A para. 18; X `barlow2026phygital`, abstract only).

**Narrowest version the evidence supports.** **[inference]** *Where a platform that is neither party reads both parties and commits a match, the physical layer is where that match is honoured by two bodies at once, under a clock, during the task.* This holds for ride-hail and, with a human at the gate, for airport screening. It does not hold for the warehouse, the hotel, the shelf or the casino as documented, and it cannot claim priority on the domain (Weiser 1991; Kitchin 2014; Lessig 2006; Greenberg 2014, all in X) or on the contrast (Barlow and Johnson 2026). What facet E adds to "reaching into physical space" is Wood et al.'s distinction: where the ruling has a physical layer, "control acts during the work and the sanction is exit" (E `wood2019good` bearing), against online work where "control operated at the end of the labour process" (p. 64).

### Form 2: a purely physical or human arrangement can itself be an intermediary

**Supporting cases.** Akrich's meter (j2): a physical arbiter between producer and consumer that needs "l'accord des deux" and is removed if either defaults (C §26). The 1985 queue and the pre-digital airport, *if a human reader counts* (m1, m2; F F5 table rows for the pre-digital queue and airport). The UKGC's floor staff with a "day diary with flags", performing the same reading duty the carded system performs (G `ukgc2026guidancepremises` bearing). Facet A's Pusceddu and Gustafsson cases, "human-plus-system; neither paper frames it so" (A). Bitner's servicescape as the one two-party model of designed physical space, in which the designer "settles between two users (P3)" (B `bitner1992servicescapes` bearing).

**Cases that cut against it.** No hostile-architecture source read supports it: "Petty, Savičić and Savić, Rosenberger and de Fine Licht describe one party's instrument that reads no one and holds still" (F, Evidence against). Latour's artifacts "bind only by force or by prescribed competence" and act for an absent author (C). Bitner's settlement "is made once, by one of the parties to it" (B B1 para. 3). The meter does not read both parties and does not adapt; "The 'does not hold still' mark has no pre-digital physical case in this facet" (C, Evidence against). Facet X's gap statement stands after this adjudication: "No source read documents a physical arrangement that reads two parties and binds both while adapting at run time without a digital system behind it" (X Gaps). Where a pre-digital arrangement reads and binds both, "a human does the reading" (F, Evidence against).

**Narrowest version the evidence supports.** **[inference]** *A physical device can hold a two-party contract and enforce it on both (the meter), and a human reader inside a physical arrangement can read both parties, commit an order that binds both and adjust it (the maître d', the officer); but no documented arrangement without a digital system does all four at once, and whether the human cases count at all is the author's ruling, not the evidence's.* If the author rules that a human reader counts, form 2 is well sourced and Part 1's lines 34, 42, 48 and 118 need rewriting (X X3). If the author rules that only an algorithm counts, form 2 has one case (the meter) that carries two marks. Form 2 as stated in the README, "a purely physical or human arrangement can itself be an intermediary", has no case that carries all four marks under either ruling.

### Form 3: physical design is only choice architecture and stays among the predecessors

**Supporting cases.** The cafeteria and shelf (h), by the predecessor's own definition: Hollands's typology requires interventions "not targeted or tailored to specific individuals" and excludes the machine that reads purchases (B `hollands2017tippme`, "Focus"). Bitner and Kotler (B B1, B2). Hostile architecture (i), which de Fine Licht himself places inside Thaler and Sunstein's nudge (F `definelicht2017hostile` p. 30). The digital key (c2), the cashless counter (o), the face kiosk on its marks (c1), the station (k2) on its marks, Latour's artifacts (j1), the SST line (E `meuter2000self`: "choice architecture with a keypad"). The phygital frameworks' default frame, in which a firm designs and one customer journeys (A A2 para. 5; A, "What Part 4 can claim", form 3), and Lee and Kim calling the SST-only store a "choice architecture" outright (A). The observed shelf (e).

**Cases that cut against it.** Anything that carries M4: the tracked casino floor is "not a static menu" (F); Wirtz's service robot "decides and adapts, which lifts it out of the SST's 'holds still' column" (E `wirtz2018brave` bearing); Grant et al. call for "AI-driven nudging" and name "hybrid nudges" that are "modified in response to consumer behaviors" (X `grant2025nudging` p. 4; B); Szocs et al. ask for atmospherics "personalized and customizable to suit individual consumer tastes" (B `szocs2023store`, abstract). The predecessor's own taxonomists put the tailored, interactive case outside choice architecture (B B4 para. 14), so form 3 cannot absorb cases that pass M2 and M4 without contradicting the predecessor.

**Narrowest version the evidence supports.** **[inference]** *Physical arrangements that read no one in the encounter and apply a fixed rule to everyone are choice architecture by the predecessor's own definition, whether or not a chip is in them; this covers most of the physical and phygital design the facets document, including the face kiosk, the digital key, the cashless counter and the station floor.* Form 3 is the default verdict, and it is defeated only by M2 and M4 together. The visible-designer premise does not belong to the predecessor (B, Evidence against), so form 3's boundary should be drawn at tailoring and interaction, where Hollands draws it, not at visibility.

### Form 4: the physical layer is where the algorithm's ruling becomes a fact and where exit is priced

**Supporting cases.** The most of any form. Ride-hail (Cameron's kerb scene, p. 486); the warehouse ("the floor is where the number becomes a firing"); the airport (GAO's absent officer; DHS's priced opt-out); the face kiosk (refusal costs the whole check-in); the housekeeping corridor; the tracked casino floor; the tiered queue (Hernandez-Maskivker p. 44); app-required access (Wilson-Nash §5.2, "the consumer cannot walk away"); the cashierless store (customers demanded a visible running commitment); Latour's "dire necessity" and toll-booth door; Chalmers's GPS shadows deciding the game (D `chalmers2003wearable`); Suchman's copier glass (D); Purohit's finding that the room amplifies the kiosk's patterns (F, X `purohit2026deception`, §"System level"). Facet A: form 4 is "the best-evidenced form."

**Cases that cut against it.** Gandini: the platform is the point of production regardless of place (E). Rahman: a four-mark intermediary with no physical layer (E). Joerges: built spaces "represent control rights" and "Only rarely and in the most trivial senses can one show that such constraints are coupled to building form" (C `joerges1999politics` p. 20), so the physical layer may be where a ruling is displayed and authorised, not where it binds. Zac et al.: a physical step before commitment *reduces* dark-pattern effectiveness, so the physical layer can be a brake on the ruling (X `zac2025dark`, abstract). And the decoupling: five of the fourteen supporting rows (c1, k1, k2, n, o) have no adaptive intermediary at all. Facet A states the consequence: "If form 4 is Part 4's strongest claim, it holds for arrangements that fail the 'does not hold still' mark, which weakens the link between form 4 and DXD's defining structure" (A para. 20).

**Narrowest version the evidence supports.** **[inference]** *Where a ruling, algorithmic or not, must be honoured by a body in a place under a clock, the place is where the ruling's cost falls and where leaving has a price; and where the ruling is algorithmic, the place is also where a human is or is not present to answer for it.* The second clause is what distinguishes the algorithmic cases: the absent officer (GAO), the manager's colour bar (HELP), the context prober (Cheon), the "try the other machine" worker (Greiffenhagen). Form 4 as the README words it ("the algorithm's ruling") is narrower than the evidence, which supports the claim for rulings in general; form 4 as a claim about DXD in particular needs the second clause or it ranges over form 3.

## 4. Strains on the test

1. **M1 and ownership, and what it does to Part 1's own cases.** On the strict reading, a thing one party owns is that party's instrument, and the casino, the warehouse, the hotel, the shelf, the theme park, the parking meter and every hostile object fail M1. On the functional reading, several of them pass. The strict reading is the one Part 1's wording invites ("neither party fully controls", l. 34), and it fits Part 1's own best example, the labour platform between "workers and consumers" (l. 42), where the platform is a third economic actor. It does not fit Part 1's other examples. The curated feed (Eslami et al., l. 96), e-commerce dark patterns (Mathur et al., l. 40) and "ad-driven network[s]" (l. 64) are arrangements in which the platform is one of the two parties and owns the instrument. **[inference]** If Part 4 applies strict M1 to the casino, it disqualifies Part 1's feed and e-commerce cases by the same stroke; if it applies functional M1 to keep those, the casino and the tiered queue come in. The author has to pick, and section 6 puts the question.

2. **"Holds still" is a matter of tempo and scope, not kind.** SFpark adapts every six weeks, by $0.25, by block, in public (G). Disney's prices "vary by date" while the gate rule holds still for the day (G C12). Kimes's 1989 rate changed "by date and by customer type" (X). Schüll's video poker adapts dealing speed within the hand (F). Cameron's rules changed over seven years (E). Part 1's wording, "rules drift across retraining cycles" (l. 118), does not say how fast or how fine. Facet X's X7 asks for a threshold: "per-person, run-time, unannounced" (X X7 rating). The two sources that draw a line draw it at tailoring and interaction, not tempo: Hollands excludes interventions "intended or enabled to vary dependent on their characteristics or responses" (B `hollands2017tippme`, "Focus"); Grant separates static cues from nudges "modified in response to consumer behaviors" (X `grant2025nudging` p. 4). **[inference]** Under a per-person threshold SFpark, Disney and Kimes fail M4 and the casino passes; under a run-time threshold SFpark fails and Disney's upstream allocation passes; under an unannounced threshold SFpark fails (published) and the warehouse passes (the TOT definition moved without a public clock, G C11). The adjudication cannot set the threshold; it can show that SFpark is the case Part 1's bounded-envelope remedy (l. 148) describes, so a threshold that excludes SFpark excludes the remedy from the discipline.

3. **The intermediary is an algorithm plus a person.** Facet E's summary sentence is the strain: "Form 1's distinct intermediary is, on the floor, an algorithm plus a person" (E, Evidence against, item 2). The warehouse manager clears or confirms a TOT (Cheon CSCW228:13); Veen's thresholds are adjusted "on a local basis" (p. 399); the immigration officer commits what the code proposes (Kitchin and Dodge ms p. 13); the remote concierge is the adaptive layer in the phygital store (A para. 21); the hotel worker says "try the other machine" (Greiffenhagen PDF p. 17). The marks assume a single thing that reads, binds and changes. In most physical cases the reading is the machine's and the binding is a person's, or the binding is the machine's and the pricing of exit is a person's (Cameron's "programmed leniency", p. 488). This is the same open question as the human-reader ruling seen from the other side: if the human is outside the intermediary, M3 fails in (b) and partly in (d); if inside, form 2's human cases come in.

4. **"Reads both parties" versus "reads a record" versus "reads at design time."** The airport reads the carrier's manifest; the warehouse reads the customer's order; Bitner's designer read customers and employees from research and "postdesign evaluation" (B `bitner1992servicescapes` p. 69); Gibson's reciprocal affordances and Norman's social signifiers mean a static setting already shows each party the other's traces (C C4 para. 4). Facet C puts the needed distinction: "The thesis's 'reads both parties' has to be distinguished from 'shows each party the other'" (C, Evidence against). **[inference]** I have applied "in the encounter" and "party, not record" as the line, and marked the manifest and the order "partly." Part 1 does not draw this line, and Mulcahy et al. report that customer and employee are "rarely examined together" in phygital research (A para. 22), so M2 has no phygital evidence base either way.

5. **The test at the match does not apply where there is no match.** Nine rows are "n/a": the warehouse, the kiosk, the housekeeping corridor, the cafeteria, the bench, Latour's artifacts, app-required access, the station. In those the ruling is a gate, a verification or a standard met over time, and nothing is committed for two parties at one moment. Where the test does apply it sorts cases the marks do not: it passes ride-hail, SFpark and the meter; it fails the casino (a bet) and the tiered queue (one class pays in time for the other's purchase). **[inference]** The match test is therefore an ethical test on a subset of DXD cases, not a mark of DXD; Part 3 built it for exchanges, and Part 4's physical cases are mostly not exchanges.

6. **Form 4 comes apart from the marks.** The face kiosk, app-required access, the station, the cashierless store and the cashless counter all price exit at a physical point and none passes M2 or M4. Facet B says of Gare du Nord that it "shows that mandatory participation and sorting do not need the algorithm" (B B4 para. 18); facet A says the same of Wilson-Nash and Lee and Kim (A para. 20). If form 4 is Part 4's claim, it is a claim about where rulings bind and what leaving costs, and it is true of choice architecture too. The link to DXD then has to be made by the second clause in section 3 (who is present to answer), not by the marks.

7. **Exit is priced gradually, and sometimes designed in.** Cameron's "programmed leniency" means "all of their counter-measures were allowable within the system" (p. 488) with a cliff at geo-spoofing (p. 492); Disney keeps a free standby line; the airport keeps an opt-out that the regulator records as priced in practice. Part 1's line 146 treats exit as binary (costless or not). Facet E: "Part 1's 'structural refusal' would have to be distinguished from a tolerance the platform grants and can withdraw" (E, Evidence against, item 5).

8. **Two facets disagree about three sources, and the disagreement changes a verdict's grade.** Wilson-Nash: A read the version of record in full; E lists it abstract-only. Baron 2019: B read the HAL manuscript in full under "Baron"; F has it abstract-only under "Baron-Yellès". Bitner 1992 and Kotler 1973: B read both in full from course mirrors; X records both as not reached and rates X1 "strong on scope" from the abstract. I have followed the facet that read the source, and the grades in the table reflect that. The lead should reconcile the read-status tables before the bibliography is merged.

9. **Dating cuts against "reaching into."** Form 1's wording implies the algorithm came first and the floor second. Schüll's casino read bodies on a floor from the mid-1980s and exported the techniques (F `schull2012touchpoint` paras. 1, 3); yield management reached hotel desks by 1989 (X); Greenberg et al. applied "dark patterns" to proxemic sensing in 2014 with captivity as the first pattern (X `greenberg2014dark`, §1). The physical adaptive case is older than the web platform. Form 1 can be stated without the direction of travel; it cannot be stated as a history.

10. **Facet X's lines X3 and X7, and how far this adjudication answers them.** *X3* (Part 1 contradicts Part 4) is the dilemma: "if every physical case in Part 4 has an algorithm behind it, Part 4 repeats Part 1 (line 34) with a door attached; if a case with no algorithm counts, lines 34, 42, 48 and 118 are repealed" (X X3). The adjudication answers it this far: the only cases carrying four marks have an algorithm behind them (a, d), so a four-mark Part 4 is Part 1 with a door attached, and what the door adds is Wood et al.'s "during the task" and a second body; the no-algorithm cases that carry marks do so through a human reader (m1, m2) or carry two marks (the meter), so the repeal is needed only if the author rules humans in. The adjudication does *not* answer X3's second half: it cannot tell the author whether to rule humans in. It also finds that X's own proposed exit, form 4, "holds for arrangements that fail the 'does not hold still' mark" (A para. 20), so form 4 does not escape the dilemma without the "who answers" clause. *X7* (the holds-still criterion fails) is answered in part: Hollands and Grant draw the line inside the nudge literature at tailoring, so the criterion is not empty (X's own counter-evidence); but the adjudication confirms X7's demand for a threshold and shows three cases (SFpark, Disney, Kimes) that fall on different sides under different thresholds. Neither line is closed.

11. **The second party is read and not bound, or bound and not read.** The delivery customer rates and cannot be rated (Griesbach p. 7); the guest is a code (Spektor p. 629); the carrier is a manifest. Part 1's "govern both parties at once" (l. 42) is true of ride-hail and, with a human, of the airport, and of nothing else physical in the corpus. **[inference]** Part 4 should expect to say "binds both" of two cases and "binds one, reads the other as a record" of the rest.

## 5. Collisions with the series' earlier parts

Part 1 is the author's live text. Parts 2 and 3 and `ARGUMENT.md` are Claude-drafted for the author. Each item quotes the sentence, states the conflict, and leaves it.

1. **Part 1 l. 48 (author): "Contemporary computing has dismantled the classical premises of choice architecture: a visible human designer, a static menu, and a single chooser."** Also l. 42: "an identifiable human designer deploying a fixed interface or default menu to steer an isolated chooser." Facet B, from the Introduction to *Nudge*: Carolyn "is a figment of our imagination", "many real people turn out to be choice architects, most without realizing it" (p. 3), and "some nudges are unintentional" (p. 10); Thorndike's rearrangement was unannounced; Rozin's and Dayan's nudges are "usually unnoticed" and "often hardly noticed" (B B3 para. 11). Conflict: visibility is not a premise of choice architecture in its source; "identifiable" (l. 42) survives, "visible" (l. 48) does not. Facet B's recommendation is to drop "visible" or defend it as Part 1's requirement. Not resolved here.

2. **Part 1 l. 34 (author): "In choice architecture, the environment is arranged by an identifiable human designer." and l. 48: "a single chooser."** Facet B: Bitner's servicescape "affects both consumers and employees" (p. 58) and its P3 anticipates "compatibility dilemmas" between them (p. 61). Conflict: the canonical model of designed physical space assumed two users and one designer, not one chooser; what it lacked was reading in the encounter and a committed outcome (B, Evidence against). Not resolved.

3. **Part 1 l. 152 (author): "seamful design reveals the gaps, joints, and transitions in a computational system, allowing users to understand its limits (Chalmers & Galani, 2004; Ehsan et al., 2024)."** Facet D: Chalmers's seams are "a physical limit of the system showing through in use" that users "exploit", and the gloss "allowing users to understand its limits" "fits Ehsan better than Chalmers" (D D2 para. 8); seamful design "is not a reaction against Weiser's ideal. Weiser called seamlessness misleading first" (D para. 29); Chalmers's cases were repaired "over a shared audio channel" between peers, and "Where an intermediary replaces that channel, the paper's own mechanism is missing" (D `chalmersgalani2004seamful` bearing). Conflict: Part 1 cites two senses of "seam" as one, and the sense it glosses is the disclosure sense the author has ruled is not the remedy. Not resolved.

4. **Part 2 `DRAFT.md` l. 101 (Claude-drafted): "Where participation is mandatory, a design that offers disclosure without predictability, a channel for intent, and standing fails the person it mediates"; l. 25: "The defensible claim is that exit is unavailable or ruinously priced."** Facet X's X5: Hirschman's paradigm of cheap exit is the shop (pp. 21, 33), so the obligation does not attach to the store or the restaurant. But facets A, B and G show priced exit in physical arrangements that have no intermediary at all: Wilson-Nash's turnstile and GP surgery (§3.4, §5.2), Baron's station (87–89% must pass), the cashless counter before the 2020 law. Conflict in both directions: Part 2's ground does not reach the voluntary physical venue, and it does reach physical arrangements Part 1 files under choice architecture. If Part 4 adopts form 4, it inherits a designer's obligation that Part 2 grounded in a condition the algorithm does not supply. Not resolved.

5. **Part 3 `DRAFT.md` l. 77 (Claude-drafted): "Part 2's argument, which grounded the designer's obligation in mandatory participation, does not reach a venue that nobody has to use"; `ARGUMENT.md` concession 7: "Part 2's mandatory-participation argument does not apply to a voluntary venue."** The casino and the theme park are voluntary venues, and both are form-4 cases in facet F (the floor as where rulings become money and time; the queue as where exit is priced). Conflict: by Part 3's concession, Part 2's obligation does not reach the two leisure cases Part 4 most wants; Part 4 would have to ground the designer's duty there in Part 1's principles alone, as Part 3 did for Kalshi. Not resolved.

6. **Part 1 l. 40 (author): "dark patterns—interface mechanisms intentionally engineered to subvert user intent for commercial extraction (Gray et al., 2018)."** Part 3's `ARGUMENT.md` (Claude-drafted) adopts an effect-based reading: "the ordinary rule against presenting one thing and selling another" (`DRAFT.md` l. 77) and concession 8, "the effect-based reading of 'dark pattern' [is] new to Part 3." Facet F finds the hostile-design literature making the same move: Rosenberger, "a particular object may enact a hostile effect even though nobody had intended for that outcome" (2023, p. 56); Albertsen and Knight's disadvantage-only definition; against Petty and Chellew, who keep intent. Conflict: Part 1 defines by intent, Part 3 by effect, and the physical literature Part 4 would cite is split the same way and has not settled it (F F4 para. 13). Not resolved; Part 4 cannot cite the hostile-design literature for either side without saying which.

7. **Part 1 l. 118 (author): "Traditional media hold still; an algorithmic intermediary does not."** Kimes 1989: a front desk quoting rates set electronically by date and segment (X `kimes1989basics` pp. 14–15); Schüll: per-player dealing speed and real-time floor reconfiguration (F). Conflict: physical arrangements that are not "algorithmic intermediaries" in Part 1's sense did not hold still decades before the phygital. Facet X's X7. The sentence survives only with the tempo or tailoring threshold of strain 2. Not resolved.

8. **Part 1 l. 146 (author): "offering a costless exit path makes the parties substitutable, collapsing an irreducible triadic mediator back into a separable, manageable dyad."** Cameron's drivers have cheap in-system exit ("programmed leniency", p. 488) and the arrangement carries all four marks; Disney keeps a free standby line and the allocation still binds the non-payer (F `alexander2012priority`, abstract). Conflict: a cheap exit at the option level does not collapse the arrangement in these cases, because the party who leaves still bears the ruling's effect (longer wait, lost early access). Part 1's sentence needs "exit from the coordination", not "exit from the option." Not resolved.

9. **Part 2 `DRAFT.md` l. 33 (Claude-drafted): "Nor is choice architecture 'passive and static,' as the pitch had it. Yeung's (2017) hypernudge is a choice architecture reconfigured continuously and per person from the target's own behavior."** Facet B: the predecessor's own taxonomists exclude the tailored, interactive case from choice architecture (Hollands 2013, Discussion; TIPPME, "Focus"). Conflict: Part 2 concedes that choice architecture need not hold still, which collides with Part 1 l. 42 and l. 48 and with form 3's boundary as Hollands draws it. Either the hypernudge is choice architecture (Part 2, Yeung) and form 3 has no boundary at M4, or it is not (Hollands) and Part 2's sentence is wrong. Not resolved.

10. **Part 2 `DRAFT.md` l. 99 (Claude-drafted): Akrich's "script", Latour's "Prescription is the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms" (reprint p. 157), and Verbeek's speed bump, cited for "why the designer, and not the user, carries the duty."** Facet C: Latour's artifacts bind one party for an absent author and read no one (C C1 para. 6); Akrich's meter is the one two-party device, and Part 2 does not cite it. Conflict: Part 2 has already filed the mundane artifact under designer-to-user ethics; if Part 4 claims form 2 through Latour, it contradicts Part 2's use of the same texts, and if it claims it through Akrich's meter, it uses a passage Part 2 did not. Also, facet C records that Akrich 1987 (n. 1), Latour 1988 (p. 302) and Lessig 2006 (p. 128) repeat the Moses-bridges story that Joerges contests (C C3 para. 3); Part 4 must not repeat it. Not resolved.

11. **Part 1 l. 42 (author): "workers and consumers on an on-demand labor platform ... determinations that govern both parties at once."** Facet E confirms this for ride-hail and finds it false for delivery on the consumer side ("Customer: not bound", Veen table row) and for the warehouse (customer as order). No conflict with the sentence as written; a conflict with any generalisation of it to platform work as a class.

## 6. Questions only the author can settle

1. **Does a human reader count as the intermediary?** *Yes:* form 2 gains the maître d' (m1), the immigration officer (m2), the pit staff and the UKGC's floor staff; the pre-digital airport carries four marks with M1 partial; Part 1's lines 34, 42, 48 and 118 need rewriting and the 1985 queue becomes DXD. *No:* form 2 has one pre-digital case (the meter) carrying two marks; the warehouse (b) has no binding determination until a manager makes one, and the airport (d) carries M3 only at an unattended gate; form 1's "algorithm's ruling" holds only where no human intervenes, which facet E finds rare. *A middle rule* (the human counts when reading from a screen the party cannot see, as in Kitchin and Dodge ms pp. 13–14): keeps (b) and (d), excludes the maître d', and makes the asymmetry of information, not the species of the reader, the criterion. This is the ruling every other question depends on.

2. **Does M1 require independence from the provider?** *Strict:* two physical cases pass (a, d); the casino, the shelf, the queue, the warehouse, the hotel and SFpark are provider's instruments; Part 1's feed and e-commerce cases fail too. *Functional:* the casino, the tiered queue, the warehouse and the housekeeping app come in on M1 and are then sorted by M2 and M3; Part 1's cases stay. *Vendor rule* (the pricing or tracking vendor counts): the shelf and the casino pass M1 and nothing else changes.

3. **What is M4's threshold?** *Per person:* the casino and ride-hail pass; SFpark, Disney and Kimes fail; the shelf passes only on capacity. *Run-time:* adds Disney's upstream allocation. *Unannounced:* adds the warehouse and excludes SFpark, which is the case Part 1's bounded-envelope principle describes. *Tailoring and interaction (Hollands's line):* the predecessor's own boundary, which places the shelf-that-reads and the robot outside choice architecture and SFpark inside it.

4. **Is form 4 a claim about DXD or about physical design?** *About DXD:* it needs the "who is present to answer" clause and covers (a), (b), (c3), (d), (f), (g2), (l); the kiosk, the turnstile, the station and the cash counter fall out. *About physical design:* it covers all fourteen rows, contradicts nothing, and is Lessig's "architecture is a kind of law" (X `lessig2006code` p. 77) and Joerges's objection to it (C) under a new name.

5. **Does reading a party's record count as reading the party?** *Yes:* the airport's manifest and the warehouse's order satisfy M2, and (b) rises to three marks. *No:* only ride-hail reads both parties in the encounter; M2 becomes the rarest mark.

6. **Is the test at the match part of the definition of a DXD case or a separate test on the cases that have a match?** *Part of the definition:* the casino and the tiered queue are excluded as bets, and the nine "n/a" rows have no standing. *Separate:* the marks sort the cases and the match test grades the subset that are exchanges, which is how Part 3 used it.

7. **Which of Part 1's wordings is the author prepared to amend?** Facets B, D and X each find a sentence in the live text that the sources do not support: "visible human designer" (l. 48), the single-sense gloss of seamful design (l. 152), "Traditional media hold still" (l. 118) without a threshold, and "costless exit ... collapsing" (l. 146) read at the option level. These are the author's words and only the author can decide whether Part 4 corrects them, restates them with a threshold, or leaves them and narrows Part 4's claims to fit.
