# G: Cases and facts — documented physical decision points in retail, hospitality, transport and the workplace

*Facet G of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat G of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Part 4 needs decision points a reader can picture, each documented from a primary source rather than from news coverage. This facet builds that case file across the author's four domains (retail; hospitality and leisure; transport; warehouse and workplace) and answers the same seven questions for each case: G1, what the decision point is physically; G2, who the parties are and what the system reads from each; G3, what the system commits and whether it binds both sides; G4, whether the rule holds still or adapts; G5, where the exit is and what it costs; G6, what the physical surface tells the person and whether that is a disclosure or a statement of what the system will and will not do; G7, whether failure is conspicuous and who is there to answer. Section (c) closes with one table (case × domain × seven answers × best tier × confidence) and a short classification against the four marks.

**Source tiers** (stated on every entry, following Part 3 facets K and D): T1 statute, regulation, court opinion or filing, regulator order or report, legislature report or oversight letter; T2 the company's own document (terms, policy, help page, blog post, filing); T3 government or standards-body technical or audit report, official dataset; T4 peer-reviewed study of the specific deployment; T5 journalism or advocacy (leads only; no fact rests on a T5 source alone).

**Read status legend.** Full text, publisher version / Full text, author preprint / Full text, reprint / Partial / Abstract only / Not obtained. Quotations appear only in entries marked full text or partial, and each carries the page or section of the copy read. Every document below was opened on 2026-10-04; where a company page is the source, the entry quotes the sentence and notes that such pages change. PDF page numbers are the physical page of the file I read; where the file carries a printed folio I give both.

### G-R2. Retail: cashless-only venues and the duty to accept cash

`nyc2020locallaw34` | City of New York (2020). Local Law No. 34 of 2020, *A local law to amend the administrative code of the city of New York, in relation to prohibiting food stores and retail establishments from refusing to accept payment in cash* (Administrative Code §20-840). Passed by the Council 2020-01-23; returned unsigned by the Mayor 2020-02-24.
- *Record:* No DOI. PDF at `https://intro.nyc/local-laws/2020-34` (certified copy, 4 pp.). Tier T1.
- *Read status:* full text, read 2026-10-04.
- *What it says:* §20-840(b): "It shall be unlawful for a food store or a retail establishment to refuse to accept payment in cash from consumers," except bills above $20 and telephone, mail or internet transactions not completed on the premises (p. 1). (c): no higher price for cash payers. (d): civil penalty "not more than $1000 for the first violation" and "$1500 for each subsequent violation" (p. 2). (e)(1): the subchapter does not apply to an establishment "that provides a device on premises that converts cash, without charging a fee or requiring a minimum deposit amount greater than one dollar, into a prepaid card" (p. 2). (e)(4): "In the event that such device malfunctions, the food store or retail establishment where such device is located shall accept payment in cash from consumers throughout the time period in which such device does not function ... Such food store or retail establishment shall place a conspicuous sign on or immediately adjacent to such device indicating that such food store or retail establishment is required by law to accept cash if such device malfunctions, and that consumers may report any violation to 311" (p. 2). §2: effective 270 days after it becomes law (p. 2).
- *Bearing:* **refutes form 1 for this case and supports form 3 with a twist.** A cashless counter is a static rule set by an identifiable owner; nothing adapts and nothing reads the customer. The law restores a costless exit from the digital payment layer by statute, and the one adaptive-looking element, the cash-to-card kiosk, is bound by a sign that states what the establishment will do when the machine fails. That sign is a statement of the system's limits, not a disclosure of its rule: it names the failure mode and the remedy.

`nycdca2020cashlessrule` | New York City Department of Consumer Affairs (2020). *Notice of adoption of rules to implement Local Law 34 of 2020* (6 RCNY §5-280, §6-72). Proposed 2020-06-30; hearing 2020-08-03.
- *Record:* No DOI. `https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/DCA-Cashless-Establishments-Final.pdf` (3 pp.). Tier T1 (regulation).
- *Read status:* full text, read 2026-10-04.
- *What it says:* "The intent of LL 34 is to ensure that all New Yorkers, including those who are unbanked or underbanked, can make retail and food purchases using cash" (p. 1). §5-280(a) creates "a presumption that a food store or retail establishment is in violation ... if such food store or retail establishment displays a sign representing that it refuses to accept payment in cash from consumers, or if any employee or agent ... represents that it refuses to accept payment in cash" (p. 2). ### G-W1. Warehouse and workplace: quotas and work-speed data

`california2021ab701` | California (2021). Labor Code §§2100–2112, "Warehouse distribution centers" (Part 8.6, added by Stats. 2021, ch. 197, §3, AB 701; effective 2022-01-01).
- *Record:* No DOI. `https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=LAB&division=2.&title=&part=8.6.&chapter=&article=`. Tier T1 (statute).
- *Read status:* full text, read 2026-10-04.
- *What it says:* §2100(e)(1): "'Employee work speed data' means information an employer collects, stores, analyzes, or interprets relating to an individual employee's performance of a quota, including, but not limited to, quantities of tasks performed, quantities of items or materials handled or produced, rates or speeds of tasks performed, measurements or metrics of employee performance - *Record:* No DOI. Read on the public.law mirror, `https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._labor_law_section_780` through `_783`, which cites nysenate.gov "(updated Jun. 23, 2023)". Tier T1 (statute), via mirror (row 26).

`conseildetat2025amazon` | Conseil d'État (2025, December 23). *Société Amazon France Logistique*, no. 492830, 10e et 9e chambres réunies, ECLI:FR:CECHR:2025:492830.20251223 (inédit au recueil Lebon).

### G-W3. Workplace: the EU rule on automated monitoring of platform work

`eu2024platformwork` | European Parliament and Council (2024, October 23). Directive (EU) 2024/2831 on improving working conditions in platform work. *Official Journal of the European Union*, L series, 2024/2831, 11.11.2024.
- *Record:* CELEX 32024L2831; no DOI. Read from the Wayback copy of the EUR-Lex HTML, `http://web.archive.org/web/2025id_/https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024L2831` (EUR-Lex itself returned empty bodies, row 29). Tier T1.
- *Read status:* partial (Articles 2, 9, 10, 11 and 29 and the title), read 2026-10-04.
- *What it says:* Article 2(1)(a): a "digital labour platform" provides a service that "involves, as a necessary and essential component, the organisation of work performed by individuals in return for payment, irrespective of whether that work is performed online or in a certain location" and "involves the use of automated monitoring systems or automated decision-making systems". - *Bearing:* the directive reaches physical work ("in a certain location") and legislates the author's principles one by one: a declared envelope (Article 9), a human who can override (Article 10), and a person who answers (Article 11). It bears on form 4 as the EU's statement of where the ruling must be made answerable.

## (c) Synthesis

Claims are numbered C1–C24. Each ties to a bib key and a locator. The seven questions (G1–G7) are answered case by case in the table at the end.

### G1. What the decision point is, physically

C1. Six decision points are documented at the level of the body and the surface: a palm hovered over a reader (`amazon2023amazonone`; `amazon2023motiondismiss`, p. 1 n. 3); a face in front of a camera in a designated queue (`tsa2026pia046e`, p. 6) or at a boarding gate (`cbp2018pia056`, p. 10); a wrist or card tapped at a Lightning Lane entrance (`disney2026lightninglane`, "simply tap in using a MagicBand"); a kerbside meter whose rate the city changes in $0.25 steps (`sfmta2014sfparkevaluation`, p. 8); a handheld scanner whose silence for ten minutes raises a flag (`conseildetat2025amazon`, para. 6); and a shelf label whose price can change at ecommerce frequency (`ftc2025staffperspective`, PDF p. 4).

C2. Two decision points are documented only as rules about a surface: a sign at every customer entrance, 8.5 by 11 inches (`nycdcwp2021biometricrule`, p. 1), and a sign beside a cash-to-card kiosk stating that cash must be accepted when the machine fails (`nyc2020locallaw34`, §20-840(e)(4), p. 2).

### G2. The parties and what the system reads from each

C3. The airport systems read two parties at once: the traveller's face and the carrier's manifest, which CBP uses to build a gallery per flight (`cbp2018pia056`, pp. 1–11; `tsa2026pia046e`, p. 1). The TSA variant also reads the airline's check-in record for an opt-in flag (`tsa2026pia046e`, p. 1).

C4. The warehouse systems read the worker's scans and the task's expected rhythm: 1.25 seconds between scans, ten minutes of silence (`conseildetat2025amazon`, paras. 5–6); California's statute names the reading as "time categorized as performing tasks or not performing tasks" (`california2021ab701`, §2100(e)(1)).

C5. SFpark reads the block, not the driver: in-ground sensors report occupancy, and the price answers the aggregate (`sfmta2014sfparkevaluation`, p. 4; `pierce2013prices`, p. 6). It is the only case here in which the intermediary does not read the individual.

C6. For the shelf label, what is read from the shopper is contested. The FTC's staff found tools fed by loyalty and in-store kiosk behaviour (`ftc2025staffperspective`, PDF pp. 7, 9); the senators alleged cameras and facial recognition on the shelf (`warren2024kroger`, p. 3, resting on T5/T2 footnotes); the retailer's denial exists only in T5 (`kroger2024reply`). No document I opened shows an ESL deployment that reads the person in front of it.

### G3. What the system commits and whether it binds both

C7. The cashierless store's commitment is a charge to the card presented at entry, made on exit without a checkout (`amazon2023motiondismiss`, p. 1). The company's own retreat from the format in its grocery stores rests on shoppers' wish to see the running total before it is committed (`amazon2026justwalkout`).

C8. Disney binds the guest and not itself: passes are "nonrefundable and nontransferable" while entitlements are "not guaranteed" and "subject to change or closure without notice or liability" (`disney2026lightninglane`, fine print). Marriott disclaims liability for a guest's "inability to use, a digital key" (`marriott2026digitalentry`, clause 12).

C9. The public systems bind themselves by rule: SFpark's step rule and floor (`sfmta2014sfparkevaluation`, p. 8); CBP's 12-hour deletion for citizens (`cbp2018pia056`, p. 11; `dhs2025biometricrule`, U.S.-citizen section); California's bar on adverse action for an undisclosed quota (`california2021ab701`, §2102).

### G4. Hold still or adapt

C10. The clearest adapters adapt slowly and in public: SFpark changed on-street rates ten times from the start of pricing to June 2013 (`sfmta2014sfparkevaluation`, p. 4), "about once every six weeks" (`pierce2013prices`, p. 6).

C11. The warehouse rule moved without a public clock: Amazon's "time off task" definition went from five minutes of unplanned time in 2020 to one cumulative hour of a 30-minute-threshold metric by 2024 (`help2024amazon`, p. 30 and n. 108). New York's statute answers with a two-business-day notice for any quota change (`newyork2022wwpa`, §781).

C12. Disney's gate rule holds still for the day while prices "vary by date and theme park or attraction" (`disney2026lightninglane`). The adaptation is upstream of the physical decision point.

C13. The cash law describes a system that never adapts: a cashless counter is a fixed policy, and the law fixes the exit in return (`nyc2020locallaw34`, §20-840(b)).

### G5. Where the exit is and what it costs

C14. The best-documented exit is the airport opt-out, and the documents price it. CBP says a citizen "may request alternative processing" (`cbp2018pia056`, p. 10); GAO found the sign points to "a CBP officer or a gate agent" when "CBP officers are typically not present at airport gates" and that the sign "do[es] not state what those alternatives are or the consequences" (`gao2022facialtestimony`, p. 11); DHS's 2025 rule concedes that citizens "have alleged that they have been referred to secondary inspection or told they would not be able to board because they declined biometrics" (`dhs2025biometricrule`, section 6). For non-citizens the rule removes the exit (`dhs2025biometricrule`, abstract).

C15. The theme park keeps a free exit priced in time: "traditional standby lines are still available" (`disney2026lightninglane`). The cash law makes the exit free by statute and penalises its removal at $1,000 then $1,500 (`nyc2020locallaw34`, §20-840(d)).

C16. The warehouse offers no exit from the system, only from an undisclosed or unlawful quota (`california2021ab701`, §§2101–2102; `newyork2022wwpa`, §782), plus a right to one's own data within 21 days (`california2021ab701`, §2104). The EU directive adds a right to an explanation and a named contact person, and a review within two weeks (`eu2024platformwork`, Art. 11).

C17. For the shelf label the exit is unknown: the senators asked "Will customers have the option to opt out of the collection of personal data through ESL platforms?" (`warren2024kroger`, p. 4, Q9) and no primary answer was found.

### G6. What the surface says, and whether it is a disclosure or a statement of limits

C18. Three laws legislate the surface. New York's §349-a puts the sentence "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA" "on, at, or near" the price, in the same medium (`newyork2025algorithmicpricing`, §349-a(1)(b), (2)): a disclosure of cause, not a statement of rule or limit. NYC's biometric law puts a sign at every entrance saying that collection happens (`nyc2021locallaw3`, §22-1202(a); `nycdcwp2021biometricrule`, p. 1): a disclosure. NYC's cash law requires a sign stating what the establishment will do when the kiosk fails (`nyc2020locallaw34`, §20-840(e)(4)): a statement of limits and remedy.

C19. The TSA sign is the one operator surface documented as stating both the rule and the fallback: the photo "is optional" and "alternative procedures are available" (`tsa2026pia046e`, p. 6). The CBP sign, as audited, stated a retention period the agency had changed a year earlier (`gao2022facialtestimony`, p. 11).

C20. SFpark's surface is the meter rate itself, and the rule behind it is published (`sfmta2014sfparkevaluation`, p. 8). The warehouse worker's surface, on the committee's record, is a manager's screen, not the worker's (`help2024amazon`, p. 30 n. 109).

### G7. Is failure conspicuous, and who answers

C21. The airport case documents an absent answerer: the sign names an officer who is not at the gate (`gao2022facialtestimony`, p. 11). The theme park names a telephone number (`disney2026lightninglane`). Marriott's terms name no one and disclaim the failure (`marriott2026digitalentry`, clause 12). The cash law names 311 on the sign (`nyc2020locallaw34`, §20-840(e)(4)).

C22. In the warehouse the person who answers is the one who asks: after a flag, employees may have to "faire valoir certains motifs pouvant relever de leur vie privée" to their superiors (`conseildetat2025amazon`, para. 7); managers "use it to monitor workers throughout their shifts" (`help2024amazon`, p. 30). The EU directive inverts this by naming a contact person the worker may question (`eu2024platformwork`, Art. 11(1)).

### Evidence against the thesis

C23. Against form 1 for retail: the one peer-reviewed ESL deployment study whose abstract I read finds more, smaller, mostly downward price changes (`stamatopoulos2021menu`, abstract), and no document I opened shows a shelf label that reads the shopper in front of it (C6). The regulator's statement is about what tools "could" do (`ftc2025staffperspective`, PDF p. 4). The cashless counter and the digital room key are static arrangements with an identifiable designer and no reader (C13; `marriott2026digitalentry`), which is Part 1's description of choice architecture, form 3.

C24. Against the strongest reading of the Amazon France case: the Conseil d'État reversed the CNIL on all three scanner indicators and cut the fine to €15 million (`conseildetat2025amazon`, paras. 5–8, Art. 1er). Part 4 may not cite the January 2024 finding as settled law. What survives is narrower and still useful: a 31-day store of second-level productivity data was held disproportionate (paras. 10–11).

### Gaps

- I found no primary document (T1–T3) showing an electronic-shelf-label deployment that changes price per shopper or per moment; the shelf-label case rests on a regulator's statement of capacity, a legislator's questions and a disclosure statute. Soutjis et al. (2017) may close G1 and G6 for the shelf; it was not obtained.
- I found no regulator or court document on Disney's Lightning Lane, on hotel mobile keys, or on casino player-tracking displays (Nevada's regulation covers the money ledger, not the tracking screen).
- I did not search for fair-workweek statutes, restaurant QR-only ordering documents, airport ride-hail zone rules or road-pricing operator rules (budget). The Platform Work Directive stands in for algorithmic scheduling at the level of rule, not deployment.
- The CNIL decision itself was not read; its content is known only through the Conseil d'État's account.
- Kroger's reply to the senators exists only in T5 accounts.

### Classification against the four marks

**Clearest instances of an algorithm's ruling arriving as a physical fact.** Airport facial comparison (the gate opens or you are sent to a person who may not be there); warehouse work-speed systems in the United States and France (a scanner's silence becomes a manager's question); SFpark (the meter rate is the ruling, though it is a ruling about the block); the cashierless store (the charge is made as you cross the threshold). The shelf label belongs here on the regulator's description of capacity, not on any observed deployment.

**Physical arrangements with no adaptive system behind them.** The cashless counter and the statutory duty to accept cash; the digital room key as a credential. Both have an identifiable designer, a static rule and a single chooser: Part 1's choice architecture. The casino floor under UKGC guidance is a documented human intermediary (staff observation, a day diary with flags) performing the same reading duty as the carded system, which is evidence for form 2 at the level of rule.

**Not classified.** Lightning Lane: adaptive upstream (price by date, allocation by availability), static at the gate, with a free exit; it carries the marks in the app and not at the turnstile. Casino player tracking as deployed: the duty and the ledger are documented, the system is not. The Platform Work Directive: a rule about the marks, not an arrangement that carries them.
