# B: Servicescape, atmospherics and physical choice architecture

*Facet B of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat B of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Part 1 places choice architecture among DXD's predecessors and describes it as having "a visible human designer, a static menu, and a single chooser". This facet tests that description against the literature on designed physical environments: Bitner's servicescape, Kotler's atmospherics, the physical nudge studies that Thaler and Sunstein's cafeteria made canonical, the public-health typologies that organise them, and the retail literature on omnichannel stores and electronic shelf labels. It asks where the physical shelf stopped holding still, and whether anyone in these literatures noticed. The research questions:

- **B1.** What does Bitner's servicescape model say the designed environment does to customers and employees, and who is the designer? Does it model one party or two?
- **B2.** What did Kotler claim for atmospherics, and is it the first statement of physical choice architecture?
- **B3.** Which of Part 1's three premises (visible designer, static menu, single chooser) do physical nudges keep? Is exit cheap?
- **B4.** When the price label goes electronic and the layout responds to data, which premise fails first, and does the retail literature notice?
- **B5.** Do servicescape's successors (social, e-, smart) add technology or a second party?

A sixth task, set by the lead: check the body text of servicescape and atmospherics papers for "nudge" and "choice architecture", which abstract search cannot see. The result is in section (c), under B6.

### B1. The gate items

`bitner1992servicescapes` | Bitner, M. J. (1992). Servicescapes: The impact of physical surroundings on customers and employees. *Journal of Marketing*, 56(2), 57–71.
- *Record:* Crossref, DOI 10.1177/002224299205600205 (journal-article, issued April 1992, 56(2), 57–71; no `update-to`). OpenAlex W4249135598. JSTOR stable URL 1252042 on the copy read.
- *Read status:* **Full text, reprint** (JSTOR page scan with text layer, 16 pp., mirrored at `https://eli.johogo.com/Class/jm-1992.pdf`; identity on p. 1: JSTOR header "Vol. 56, No. 2 (Apr., 1992), pp. 57-71"; running folios "Servicescapes / 57" to "/ 71" on every page). Two further mirrors (AUEB e-class, 16 pp.; sampleassignments.com, 24 pp. reflowed) carry the same text. Read in full, including references.
- *What it argues:* Bitner opens from a management problem: "Managers continually plan, build, change, and control an organization's physical surroundings, but frequently the impact of a specific design or design change on ultimate users of the facility is not fully understood" (p. 57). Her complaint about the two prior literatures is that each sees one party: organisational-behaviour research studies employees and "The customer is left out of that research stream, however, just as the employee typically is ignored in the limited atmospherics research in marketing (e.g., Donovan and Rossiter 1982; Kotler 1973; Milliman 1982, 1986)" (pp. 57–58). The remedy is a framework in which "the built environment (i.e., the manmade, physical surroundings as opposed to the natural or social environment), or what is referred to here as the 'servicescape,' affects both consumers and employees in service organizations" (p. 58), because "the organization's environment should support the needs and preferences of both service employees and customers simultaneously" (p. 58). The typology (Figure 1, p. 59) sorts service organisations by "who is performing actions within the servicescape—the customer, or the employee, or both" into self-service, interpersonal and remote services, and by lean versus elaborate complexity; "The relative level of involvement of customers and employees determines whose needs should be consulted in the design of the environment" (p. 58). The framework (Figure 2, p. 60) runs from three controllable dimensions (ambient conditions; space/function; signs, symbols and artifacts) through a holistically "perceived servicescape" to cognitive, emotional and physiological responses, moderated by traits and situation, to approach/avoidance behaviour and social interaction. The designer is the firm: the dimensions are "all of the objective physical factors that can be controlled by the firm to enhance (or constrain) employee and customer actions" (p. 65), and "the first step in the purposeful design of the servicescape is to identify desirable customer and/or employee behaviors and the strategic goals that the organization hopes to advance through its physical facility" (p. 62). The two users can want incompatible things: P3 reads "Optimal design for encouraging employee (customer) approach behavior may be incompatible with the design required to meet customer (employee) needs and/or facilitate positive employee-customer interactions" (p. 61), and "the strategist must understand the plans and goals of all participants and anticipate compatibility dilemmas in designing the servicescape" (p. 62). Behaviour change through cues is taken as given: "In actual service settings, examples of environmental cues being used to change behavior are abundant. At one 7-11 store, the owners played 'elevator music' to drive away a youthful market segment that was detracting from the store's image" (p. 60), and "In many service situations, a firm may want to ensure a particular progression of events (i.e., a 'standard script') and limit the duration of the service" (p. 61). Control appears as a response variable: P8, "Perceptions of greater personal control in the servicescape increase pleasure" (p. 63). Meaning is not fully under the designer's hand: symbolic communication "may be intentionally conveyed or it may be accidental, it may be subject to multiple interpretations, and it may have intended and unintended consequences" (p. 67). The managerial section names three roles for the servicescape, package, facilitator and differentiator (p. 67), and notes that "in most organizations, environmental management is a separate function performed by persons with titles such as 'environmental space manager,' 'facility planner,' and 'facility manager'", with "environmental decisions ... made routinely without much attention to the impact on employee (or consumer) behavior" (pp. 67–68). Nowhere does the environment respond to the user within the encounter; the only feedback is managerial redesign, "postdesign evaluation" (p. 69).
- *Bearing:* **supports form 3 and complicates form 2.** Marks: no distinct intermediary (the firm designs and is a party); the designer reads two parties, but at design time and from research, not in the encounter; the servicescape constrains and facilitates rather than committing a determination; it holds still until managers rebuild it. Against Part 1's wording: the one canonical model of designed physical space already models two parties, so "a single chooser" is not what the physical-design literature assumed; what it assumed was a single designer who settles between two users (P3). That is a pre-digital settlement without a third party, which is the strongest foothold for form 2 in this facet, and it is weak: the settlement is made once, by one of the parties.

`kotler1973atmospherics` | Kotler, P. (1973). Atmospherics as a marketing tool. *Journal of Retailing*, 49(4), 48–64.
- *Record:* OpenAlex W54930839 ("Atmospherics as a Marketing Tool", Philip Kotler, *Journal of Retailing* vol. 49, pp. 48–64, no DOI, 2,403 citations; OpenAlex year 1974). Not in Crossref (the journal's early volumes are not registered). Northwestern Scholars and the Kellogg faculty record list it as 1973, pp. 48–64, without volume or issue. The scan itself carries the issue header "Volume 49 Number 4 Winter 1973-1974" (p. 48) and folios 48 through 64, which settles the record: 49(4), Winter 1973–74, pp. 48–64; the conventional year 1973 is the cover year.
- *Read status:* **Full text, reprint** (image scan of the journal pages, 17 pp., mirrored at `http://belzludovic.free.fr/nolwenn/Kotler - Atmospherics as a marketing tool (cité 171) - 1973.pdf`; footer "Copyright © 2001 All Rights Reserved" marks it as a 2001 database digitisation). No text layer; OCR'd with `ocrmypdf --force-ocr` (5,736 words). Identity on p. 1: issue header, title, "PHILIP KOTLER, Harold T. Martin Professor of Marketing, Northwestern University". Read in full. OCR noise is confined to the copyright footers; quotations below were checked against the page images.
- *What it argues:* The claim is that the place of purchase is part of the product and sometimes outranks it: "In some cases, the place, more specifically the atmosphere of the place, is more influential than the product itself in the purchase decision. In some cases, the atmosphere is the primary product" (p. 48). The definition: "We shall use the term atmospherics to describe the conscious designing of space to create certain effects in buyers. More specifically, atmospherics is the effort to design buying environments to produce specific emotional effects in the buyer that enhance his purchase probability" (p. 50), with a footnote extending it to "the deliberate design of 'negatmospheres' to discourage demand as part of a 'demarketing' program" (p. 50, n. 2). Kotler separates designer from perceiver: "An important distinction exists between the intended atmosphere and the perceived atmosphere. The intended atmosphere is the set of sensory qualities that the designer of the artificial environment sought to imbue in the space" (p. 51), and perception varies by culture and person (pp. 51–52). Proposition 1 bounds the tool: "Atmospherics is a relevant marketing tool mainly in situations (a) where the product is purchased or consumed; and (b) where the seller has design options" (p. 52); "It is not a meaningful tool in mail, phone, or door-to-door selling" (p. 52). It matters most where exit is easy: "Retailers tend to give the most thought to atmospherics because family buyers tend to have great choice in where they buy and atmosphere is part of the total product" (p. 52), and it grows with competition (Proposition 2, p. 52) and with small product and price differences (Proposition 3, p. 53). The mechanism (Figure 1, p. 54) is a four-step causal chain from sensory qualities of the space, through the buyer's selective perception, to a modified "information and affective state", to purchase probability; atmosphere works as an "attention-creating", "message-creating" and "affect-creating medium" (p. 54), and "plays the role of a very specific situational factor helping to convert behavioral intentions into actual buying behavior. People walk around with many wants and buying intentions that don't materialize, until situational factors, such as motivating atmospheres, tip the scale in favor of purchase" (p. 54). The examples are affective priming (shoe stores, furniture showrooms, model homes, airlines, psychiatrists' offices, advertising agencies, pp. 55–61). Two examples concern arrangement rather than mood. Kotler quotes Raiffa's (1968) account of a bargain basement whose staff "took the blouses out of their boxes, threw them on the counter, and very methodically mixed them up" so that the resulting crowd "like a magnet, lured other bargain hunters into the melee", and asks "Is this atmosphere coincidental or calculated?" (p. 57); and antique dealers practise "organized chaos" so that the buyer "ends up buying something that he regards as a value. Little does he know that the whole atmosphere has been arranged to create a sense of hidden treasures" (p. 57). Planning is a management sequence (target audience, what it seeks, which variables, competition, p. 61); "Responsibility for atmosphere planning and monitoring should be invested in a specific member of the management team" (p. 62); designs "should be pretested on a sample of potential customers through a scale model or set of drawings" and "periodically reevaluated", because "Atmospheres exhibit a strong wear-out effect over time" (p. 62). The closing research agenda ends on the ethical question: "What are the social and ethical implications of man's growing power to create atmospheres to motivate purchase? What objections or defenses does the buyer have against 'subliminal' persuasion?" (p. 64). The last paragraph fixes the concept: "Until recently, atmospheres developed casually or organically. Atmospherics, however, is the conscious planning of atmospheres to contribute to the buyer's purchasing propensity" (p. 64).
- *Bearing:* **supports form 3.** One designer (the seller's management), one party read (the buyer), a static design with a wear-out curve, and a mechanism that works on affect and attention rather than on the structure of the option set. Exit is not only cheap, it is the condition under which the tool matters (Proposition 1(b), Proposition 2). On B2: this is the first statement of atmospherics as a marketing variable, but it is not the first statement of physical choice architecture. Kotler's own evidence that arrangement steers choice is borrowed (Raiffa 1968) and treated as a curiosity of "calculated" disorder; the structure/affect distinction that choice architecture rests on is absent. His p. 64 question is Part 2's question asked 35 years early, and the "intended versus perceived atmosphere" distinction (p. 51) is a seam in Part 1's sense.

### B2. The cafeteria and the physical nudge studies

`thaler2008nudge` | Thaler, R. H., & Sunstein, C. R. (2008). *Nudge: Improving decisions about health, wealth, and happiness*. New Haven: Yale University Press. Introduction, pp. 1–14.
- *Record:* Internet Archive catalogue records `nudgeimprovingde0000thal` (Penguin 2009, ISBN 9780143115267) and `nudgeimprovingde0000thal_n8s9` (2008); both lending-restricted. Cited in this form by Hollands et al. (2013, ref. 3) and Rozin et al. (2011).
- *Read status:* **Full text of the Introduction (book pp. 1–14), course-hosted copy** (`https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/Nudge%20Intro%20Ch1.pdf`, 38 pp., PDF title "Thaler Richard. H. Sunstein Cass R.. Biases and Blunders Chapter 1 In Nudge Improving..."; printed folios 1–14 for the Introduction). Chapter 1 is in the copy but was not read. The series had not opened the book before this session (Part 1 F2 relies on Thaler 2018).
- *What it says:* The book opens on the cafeteria. "A friend of yours, Carolyn, is the director of food services for a large city school system" who, with a consultant "who has worked with supermarket chains", runs experiments: "Without changing any menus, they would run some experiments in her schools to determine whether the way the food is displayed and arranged might influence the choices kids make ... In some schools the French fries, but in others the carrot sticks, were at eye level ... Simply by rearranging the cafeteria, Carolyn was able to increase or decrease the consumption of many food items by as much as 25 percent" (p. 1). "In a cafeteria, it is impossible to avoid some way of organizing food" (p. 2). "Carolyn is what we will be calling a choice architect. A choice architect has the responsibility for organizing the context in which people make decisions. Although Carolyn is a figment of our imagination, many real people turn out to be choice architects, most without realizing it" (p. 3). "A crucial parallel is that there is no such thing as a 'neutral' design" (p. 3). "a choice architect like Carolyn must choose a particular arrangement of the food options at lunch, and by so doing she can influence what people eat. She can nudge" (p. 4). The definition: "A nudge, as we will use the term, is any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives. To count as a mere nudge, the intervention must be easy and cheap to avoid. Nudges are not mandates. Putting the fruit at eye level counts as a nudge. Banning junk food does not" (p. 6). A footnote concedes that placing fruit at eye level and candy "in a more obscure place" imposes cognitive costs: "Nudges count as such, and qualify as libertarian paternalism, only if any costs are low" (p. 8, n.). "It is true, of course, that some nudges are unintentional" (p. 10). "Freedom to choose is the best safeguard against bad choice architecture" (p. 11).
- *Bearing:* **complicates Part 1's description of its own predecessor.** The cafeteria keeps a static menu ("Without changing any menus") and a single chooser, so two of the three premises hold at the source. The third does not: the designer is a "figment", choice architects act "most without realizing it", and "some nudges are unintentional". Visibility is not a premise of choice architecture; it is Part 1's addition. On exit: "easy and cheap to avoid" is defined at the level of the option (reach past the carrots), not the venue; the choosers are schoolchildren in a cafeteria they must use. The result section states the design logic: "The station operators are seeking to use aural, visual or digital messages to modulate the decisions of pedestrians, in order to encourage without forcing, to influence without pushing, and gently to prompt people, through the appeal of brands, or emulation of the flow, to move in the right direction, or to buy at the right time" ("New spatial patterns for the deployment of nudging techniques"); "the station space – a space both fragmented and filled – nudges the customer through a series of enticements"; "The act of buying no longer entails a break or a detour, but has arguably become a simple gesture of acceptance of the logic of streamed movement"; from the mezzanine restaurant "the traveller unconsciously consents to buy a more expensive sandwich" ("Making stations profitable begins with improving their visual qualities"). Between 87 and 89% of users of the four rail services pass through the concourse to connect; biometric passport gates and face recognition were added for "fluidity" (mezzanine section).
- *Bearing:* **the strongest case in this facet for form 4, and a complication for form 3.** The station is a place the traveller cannot avoid (87–89% connect through it); the designer is a state railway operator acting on observation and segmentation; the arrangement sorts travellers by class and dwell time and prices the sandwich by the view; the paper's own verbs are "modulate the decisions", "unconsciously consents". Marks: no algorithmic intermediary yet (the monitors display, the operator decides), one party read (by periodic observation, not in real time), no binding determination, and a design that was rebuilt once over seven years. ## (c) Synthesis

### B1. Bitner: two parties, one designer, a model that holds still

1. Bitner's servicescape is a two-party model of users and a one-party model of design. The framework exists because atmospherics research "ignored" the employee and organisational-behaviour research "left out" the customer (`bitner1992servicescapes`, pp. 57–58); the typology's first axis is who acts in the space, "the customer, or the employee, or both" (p. 58).
2. The designer is the firm, acting through managers, strategists, architects and facility planners (pp. 62, 65, 67–68). The two users are read at design time, through research and "postdesign evaluation" (p. 69), never within the encounter. The environment "does not directly cause people to behave in certain ways" (p. 62); it is perceived, moderated by traits and purposes, and then acted on.
3. The model already contains a settlement between parties: P3 says the design that suits the employee may not suit the customer (p. 61), and the strategist must "anticipate compatibility dilemmas" (p. 62). The settlement is made once, by one of the parties to it. That is the limit of form 2 in this literature: a physical arrangement can adjudicate between two parties, but Bitner gives no case in which anything other than the firm does the adjudicating.
4. Nothing in the framework moves. The dimensions are "objective physical factors that can be controlled by the firm" (p. 65); meaning can escape control ("intentionally conveyed or ... accidental", p. 67), but the shelf, the layout and the signs stay where the firm put them until it rebuilds them.

### B2. Kotler: the first statement of atmospherics, not of choice architecture

5. Kotler's definition is explicit about intent and about the mechanism: "the conscious designing of space to create certain effects in buyers", through "specific emotional effects in the buyer that enhance his purchase probability" (`kotler1973atmospherics`, p. 50). The chain runs through affect, attention and message (pp. 53–54), not through the structure of the option set.
6. The tool is defined for one party and for conditions of cheap exit. Proposition 1 requires that "the seller has design options"; Propositions 2 and 3 make the tool matter more as competition rises and as product and price differences fall, because "family buyers tend to have great choice in where they buy" (p. 52). Atmospherics presupposes that the buyer can walk out; it is a device for when walking out costs nothing.
7. It is not the first statement of physical choice architecture. The two examples of arrangement steering choice (Raiffa's mixed-up blouses; the antique dealer's "organized chaos", p. 57) are borrowed and marvelled at; the paper has no concept of the default, the order or the position as a choice-structuring variable. Kotler's own history (p. 49) and closing line ("Until recently, atmospheres developed casually or organically", p. 64) place the practice before the paper. What is first in 1973 is the claim that the atmosphere is a marketing variable to be planned, monitored and "periodically reevaluated" (p. 62), and the ethical question about "man's growing power to create atmospheres to motivate purchase" (p. 64).
8. Kotler holds still by design: atmospheres have "a strong wear-out effect over time" and are refreshed by management (p. 62). The intended/perceived distinction (p. 51) is the one place where the model admits that the design and the experience of it are different objects.

### B3. Physical nudges keep two of Part 1's three premises, and exit is cheap only at the option level

9. Static menu: kept at the source. Thaler and Sunstein's cafeteria works "Without changing any menus" (`thaler2008nudge`, p. 1); Rozin et al. define the nudge as working "without altering the choice set" (`rozin2011nudge`, p. 325); Dayan and Bar-Hillel as changes "that do not affect the choice set itself" (`dayan2011nudge`, p. 333). Hollands et al. and TIPPME require the intervention to be standardised for all recipients (`hollands2013altering`, Discussion; `hollands2017tippme`, "Focus").
10. Single chooser: kept. Every study reads one population of choosers; the counter staff appear only as a source of error (`rozin2011nudge`, p. 328 n. 3) or as the excluded "cafeteria worker" who would make the intervention interactive (`hollands2017tippme`, "Focus").
11. Visible designer: not kept, and not claimed by the source. Thaler and Sunstein's architect is "a figment", most architects act "without realizing it", and "some nudges are unintentional" (`thaler2008nudge`, pp. 3, 10). Rozin's and Dayan's nudges are "usually unnoticed" and "often hardly noticed"; Thorndike's rearrangement was made over a weekend and not advertised (`thorndike2012labeling`, Methods). Part 1's "visible human designer" describes the regulator's nudge unit, not the cafeteria. The design-ethics line (Verbeek, Latour) owned by facet C is where the question of a designer visible to the chooser belongs.
12. Exit: cheap at the option, not at the venue. Thaler and Sunstein define the nudge as "easy and cheap to avoid" and concede that eye-level placement imposes cognitive costs that must stay "low" (`thaler2008nudge`, pp. 6, 8 n.). The cost in the field is ten inches of reach or a pair of tongs (`rozin2011nudge`, pp. 326, 329). The venues are a school cafeteria, a hospital staff cafeteria and a hospital cafeteria, where the chooser must eat; none of the studies prices venue exit, and Bucher et al. set the freedom-of-choice question aside as ethics (`bucher2016nudging`, p. 2261). Part 2's exit test is therefore unasked in this literature, and Baron's station (claim 17) is the only case here where the venue itself cannot be avoided.
13. Effect sizes are real and modest: 8–16% in Rozin, "up to twice as popular" for menu edges in Dayan, 25.8% for bottled water in Thorndike, and the corrected food-domain meta-estimate of d = 0.65 in Mertens et al. with the publication-bias dispute recorded in F2 (`mertens2022effectiveness`).

### B4. The electronic shelf: the menu fails first, and the retail literature notices the capability, not the problem

14. The public-health taxonomists drew Part 1's line before Part 1 did. TIPPME excludes interventions "designed to be interactive or tailored", and names the excluded case as "a computer-based system providing personalised nutritional guidance based on food purchasing patterns" (`hollands2017tippme`, "Focus"). An arrangement that reads the shopper and changes for her is, by the predecessor's own definition, not a choice-architecture intervention. This is the sharpest support in the facet for form 3 as a bounded class and, by the same stroke, for the claim that whatever lies past the line is something else.
15. Of the three premises, the static menu fails first when the label goes electronic. Stamatopoulos et al. find "more and smaller price changes", "dispersed in time", after ESL adoption (`stamatopoulos2021menu`, abstract). Verhoef et al. describe assortments integrated across channels and a store that tracks the shopper by Wi-Fi (`verhoef2015multichannel`, pp. 175–176). The single chooser begins to fail with showrooming (p. 175). The visible designer survives longest: every retail paper names the retailer as the agent.
16. The shelf did not move as far as the technology allows. Soutjis et al. find ESLs "resisted" and coexisting with paper (`soutjis2017ethnography`); Boden et al. study a retailer that installed ESLs with "no dynamic pricing" to signal price consistency across channels (`boden2020effect`); Garaus et al. find shoppers "largely unaware" of what ESLs do and fairness perceptions unchanged (`garaus2016shoppers`). Grant et al. find traditional labels as effective as technology nudges (`grant2025nudging`). The adaptive shelf is a capability the literature can see and a practice it mostly does not observe; the one documented change is in the frequency of price moves, not in who or what decides them.
17. Does the retail literature notice? It notices the capability as management. Verhoef et al. define omnichannel as management "in such a way that ... performance over channels is optimized" and ask only whether "shoppers [should] control this integration" (pp. 176, 179). Szocs et al. tell managers to move from "one-size-fits-all" to atmospherics "personalized and customizable to suit individual consumer tastes" (`szocs2023store`, abstract), which is a call to make the physical environment stop holding still, written without any term for what that does to the chooser's standing. Grant et al. call for "AI-driven nudging" and tailoring (`grant2025nudging`, Discussion). None of these papers uses "choice architecture" to describe the retailer's own position, and none asks who is bound by a shelf that reads.
18. Baron's Gare du Nord is the case where the physical layer prices exit and sorts people, with no algorithm. The operator "modulate[s] the decisions of pedestrians", the traveller "unconsciously consents", and 87–89% of users must pass through (`baron2019designing`, results and mezzanine sections). The design was rebuilt once in seven years from in-situ counts; it holds still between rebuilds. That supports form 4 directly: a ruling about who shops where and at what price became a built fact, and Part 1's principles (a costless way out; conspicuous failure) are kept or defeated in concrete. It also shows that mandatory participation and sorting do not need the algorithm, which is a point against treating form 1 as the only route to Part 2's obligation.

### B5. The successors add technology; only the smart servicescape adds a layer that reads and acts

19. The social servicescape adds other customers as stimuli (`tombs2003social`); the expanded servicescape adds social, symbolic and natural stimuli that are "often managerially uncontrollable" (`rosenbaum2011expanded`). Neither adds an intermediary; the second adds doubt about the designer's control.
20. The e-servicescape moves Bitner's cue model to the web page and keeps its stimulus–response structure (`harris2010online`). It adds technology without changing who decides.
21. The smart servicescape adds a layer with Part 1's marks. Roy et al. place "perceived interactivity and perceived personalisation" among the dimensions (`roy2019rise`). Kang, Han and Kwon define an "imperceptible Datascape (ie, computing intelligence, databases, and communication networks)" whose elements the resident cannot perceive "unless they are intentionally visualized", which tracks people by sensors and acts through "actuators ... to perform curated service actions decided from computing intelligence", inside "a complex adaptive system" (`kang2019ecological`, Abstract; "Smart Servicescape Wheel"; "Hardware Components"). Nanu puts AI "as a core dimension" inside Bitner's three (`nanu2025redefining`); Yin et al. add technology as a third participant in rapport with employees and customers (`yin2023intelligent`); Kandampully et al. call technology "an important partner for the firm" (`kandampully2022linking`). Fariño Espitia, Rosenbaum and Contreras-Ramírez's 261-paper review ends with six dimensions including the technological and a "multi-stakeholder lens" of consumers, employees and communities (`farino2025reimagining`).
22. So the answer to B5 is: the successors add technology first (e-servicescape, 2009–2010), then an imperceptible deciding layer (smart servicescape, 2017–2019), and only then AI as a named participant (2022–2025). The second party was there from Bitner; what the successors add is a third thing in the room. The vocabulary for form 1 now exists inside the servicescape line, mostly for homes and hotels, mostly at abstract level in this facet, and nowhere joined to "choice architecture".

### B6. Body-text check: do servicescape or atmospherics papers say "nudge" or "choice architecture"?

23. In the full texts obtained, no servicescape or atmospherics paper uses either term. `bitner1992servicescapes`: zero occurrences of "nudge", "choice architecture" or "default"; three citations of Kotler 1973. `kotler1973atmospherics`: zero; the nearest is "'subliminal' persuasion" (p. 64). `verhoef2015multichannel`: zero. The abstracts of `turley2000atmospheric`, `mari2013servicescape`, `rosenbaum2011expanded`, `roggeveen2020dast` and `farino2025reimagining` carry neither term; their bodies were not obtained.
24. The citation runs one way. TIPPME's reference list contains Bitner (1992) and Turley and Milliman (2000), and its text sets previous environmental mappings aside as "insufficiently detailed, not systematically developed and assessed, or are not applied to consumptive health-related behaviours" (`hollands2017tippme`, "Previous attempts"); the pairing of these two references with that sentence is likely but unverified from the unnumbered manuscript list. Bucher et al. say the marketing shelf literature exists and was not searched (`bucher2016nudging`, p. 2261). I found no servicescape or atmospherics paper, in the texts and abstracts above, that cites Thaler and Sunstein, Hollands or TIPPME.
25. Two crossovers exist outside marketing. Baron (2019), in a planning journal, uses "servicescape" and "nudge" in the same analysis and attributes both to the station operator's practice (`baron2019designing`). Lee and Chu (2023), in an interior-design journal, import nudge theory into "retail space branding" as a designer's checklist (`lee2023nudge`, abstract). Z's row 14 found six OpenAlex hits for servicescape + nudge, including Kim (2016) on restaurant atmospherics, which I did not open.

### Evidence against the thesis

- Against form 1 as a description of the physical store: the ESL studies show an electronic label that coexists with paper (`soutjis2017ethnography`), is installed without dynamic pricing (`boden2020effect`), and leaves shopper fairness perceptions unchanged (`garaus2016shoppers`). The shelf that can move mostly has not.
- Against form 1 as a scientific category: the field's own typology excludes tailored and interactive interventions (`hollands2013altering`; `hollands2017tippme`). If Part 4 calls an adaptive shelf "choice architecture", the taxonomists of choice architecture will say it is not.
- Against form 2: the one two-party physical model is designed by one of the parties and holds still (`bitner1992servicescapes`); Baron's station shows sorting and priced exit without any party reading another in real time.
- Against Part 1's own wording (and so a correction Part 4 inherits): "a visible human designer" is not a premise of choice architecture in its source. Thaler and Sunstein's architect is a figment who acts unknowingly and sometimes unintentionally (`thaler2008nudge`, pp. 3, 10), and the field studies conceal the change (`thorndike2012labeling`). If Part 4 restates the predecessor, it should say "an identifiable designer" (Part 1's other phrase) and drop "visible", or defend visibility as Part 1's requirement rather than the predecessor's property.
- Against the claim that reading two parties is distinctive of the algorithmic intermediary: Bitner read customers and employees in 1992 (claim 1). What is distinctive is reading them in the encounter and committing an outcome, which no physical source here does.
- Against treating technology as the hinge: Grant et al. find traditional labels as effective as technology nudges and AR novelty "insufficient" (`grant2025nudging`); Rozin's six-month study finds no adaptation to a static arrangement (`rozin2011nudge`, p. 328).
