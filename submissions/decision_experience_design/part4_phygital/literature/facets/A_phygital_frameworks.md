# A: Phygital frameworks — what the researchers of hybrid physical/digital experience say

*Facet A of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat A of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The essay opens from the problems that researchers of the "phygital" raise. This facet supplies what those researchers say, read from their own text where their text could be reached. Five questions. A1: what the phygital framework papers define as "phygital", where the term came from, and which problems they name (fluidity and seamlessness, the journey, the employee's role, vulnerability, inclusion). A2: whether any phygital paper models an intermediary that reads two parties and commits an outcome for both, or whether all of them model one customer facing a designed environment. A3: whether phygital researchers treat friction as a defect to remove, and whether anyone defends it. A4: inclusion and captivity: who is shut out or compelled when the physical service requires the digital layer, and what exit costs. A5: what reviews of the term say about its theory.

**Gate result, stated first.** The two Batat framework papers (PH-CX, *Journal of Strategic Marketing*; PSR, *Journal of Services Marketing*) stayed at abstract level after every open route I could find (section (d) and (e) list them). By the brief's own criterion this facet fails its gate on those two items. Wilson-Nash and others ("No App, No Entry") was read in full from the Wiley version of record. What this facet can say about PH-CX and PSR rests on their abstracts and on verbatim restatements inside five full-text papers that quote or paraphrase them (Alexander and Varley 2025; Batat's own 2024 editorial; Mameli and others 2026; Zheng and others 2025; Shabnam and others 2026). Those restatements are marked as secondary wherever they appear.

`batat2024phcx` | Batat, W. (2024). What does phygital really mean? A conceptual introduction to the phygital customer experience (PH-CX) framework. *Journal of Strategic Marketing*, 32(8), 1220–1243.
- *Record:* Crossref (title, author, journal, volume, issue, pages; published online 2022-04-05; print 2024-11-16). DOI 10.1080/0965254X.2022.2059775. No update-to, no relation field. The version of record is the 2024 issue; the 2022 date is online-first. OpenAlex W4225832948 lists a HAL notice (hal-04444804) with no file.
- *Read status:* **Abstract only.** T&F abstract page (`tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0965254X.2022.2059775`, "Get Access") and Crossref. Tried: Unpaywall (closed), OpenAlex locations (HAL notice only), Semantic Scholar (no PDF), HAL API (notice), Google Scholar all five versions (two RePEc metadata records, two EBSCO links behind institutional login, which I did not use), ResearchGate (request-only), the author's site (publisher link only), curl to T&F (challenge page). A library copy of *Journal of Strategic Marketing* 32(8) would close this.
- *What the abstract says:* the construct "lacks an academic conceptualization"; existing studies define it "in regards to the marketing channel approach" (multichannel, cross-channel, omnichannel), which "limits its application"; phygital should instead be "a comprehensive framework for better managing customer experiences" that "guarantees a continuum in value delivery by connecting offline and online offerings"; PH-CX "examines the dynamics created by customers' shifting from physical to digital settings or vice versa" and names "driving forces, connectors, and pillars"; the design is done "by fluidifying the journeys of customers from online to offline and inversely". (These are the abstract's own words, which the brief permits as the abstract's content; no body text is claimed.)
- *Secondary restatements in full texts I read:* Alexander and Varley (2025, p. 3) quote the body definition as Batat (2022:10): "a holistic and integrative ecosystem that adopts a consumer standpoint as a starting point and integrates a combination of physical, human, digital, and media content elements, platforms, technologies and extended realities; the goal of phygital is to offer unique and compelling CX that guarantee a coherent continuum in the delivery process of consumer value provided from digital to physical and vice versa". Batat's own 2024 editorial (§1) restates it as "a human-centric holistic ecosystem that blends physical, human, digital and various other elements from a consumer perspective. Its aim is to ensure a seamless delivery of value from digital to physical realms, and vice versa." Zheng and others (2025, §2) restate it as "a holistic, integrative ecosystem designed to facilitate seamless connections between physical and digital realms". Mameli and others (2026, Page 3–4 of 31) report that Batat "distinguishes physical, digital and phygital experiences by defining phygital as the third domain of customer experience". Gustafsson (2026, §2.2) names the components as "connectors (human, digital, physical, media) and pillars". None of these is a substitute for the primary text; the page "10" in Alexander's citation is from the 2022 online-first pagination (pp. 1–24), not the issue.
- *Bearing:* On the abstract and the restatements, PH-CX models one customer whose journey a firm "fluidifies" across settings: a designed environment, a single chooser, a visible designer. That is Part 1's choice-architecture predecessor, so it supports form 3 for Batat's own framing. The "continuum" and "seamless delivery" language is the seamlessness ideal the author's seamful-design principle opposes. Marks: no distinct intermediary is named; the customer is read (consumer standpoint) but no second party; nothing binds; "does not hold still" is not claimed. Whether the body says more about algorithms or employees is unknown.

`batat2026psr` | Batat, W. (2026). Phygital service research (PSR): Advancing FSR and TSR toward human-first experience design in hybrid physical-digital ecosystems. *Journal of Services Marketing*, 40(4), 505–518.
- *Record:* Crossref (online 2025-12-09; print 2026-05-07; 40(4) 505–518). DOI 10.1108/JSM-09-2025-0600. No corrections. Crossref carries the structured abstract.
- *Read status:* **Abstract only.** Emerald abstract page ("You do not currently have access to this content"), Crossref, ResearchGate 398494572 ("No full-text available"), Google Scholar three versions (Emerald twice, a ScienceDirect abstract mirror). Unpaywall closed; OpenAlex no repository copy. Emerald permits author-accepted manuscripts in repositories, but I found none (Lyon 2 / HAL hold no record for this paper). A library copy would close it.
- *What the abstract says:* PSR "extends Foundational Service Research (FSR) and Transformative Service Research (TSR) toward human-first experience design"; it "emphasizes hybrid embodiment, contextual fluidity, multidimensional entanglement, holistic innovation and systemic impact"; it reframes "service experiences and value creation as fluid, dynamically poly-constructed, ethically and technologically mediated, and contextually adaptive"; it proposes "a human-first logic grounded in 'phygital phenomenology' by integrating technology without compromising experiential authenticity"; and it introduces "phyginography" as a method.
- *Secondary restatements:* Shabnam and others (2026, §1) read PSR as "a third wave of service research" and say that it "acknowledges technological mediation but emphasises experiential design over causal explanation" (§2.2); their Table 2 states that PSR "recognises technology as shaping agency in hybrid ecosystems" but does not "explain how algorithmic systems autonomously redirect, constrain or reconfigure agency through decision automation and behavioural nudging". Gustafsson (2026, §1) reads PSR as viewing "the store not merely as a channel, but as a holistic ecosystem where value is poly-created by multiple actors (customers, algorithms, remote staff)".
- *Bearing:* The abstract's "technologically mediated" and "contextually adaptive" wording comes nearer to the intermediary than PH-CX does, and Gustafsson's gloss names algorithms as co-creators. But the two full-text papers that build on PSR both say it stops at experience design and does not model algorithmic decision rights (Shabnam, Table 2 rows 1 and 3). On that secondary evidence PSR supports form 1 in vocabulary and leaves the marks (reads both parties, binds both) unmodelled. Primary confirmation is missing.

`wilsonnash2026captivity` | Wilson-Nash, C., Angell, R., Qiu, Y., Pavlopoulou, I., & Kolyperas, D. (2026). No App, No Entry: Conceptualizing digital technology captivity in service access. *Psychology & Marketing*, 43(5), 974–985.
- *Record:* Crossref (five authors; online 2025-12-30; print May 2026; 43(5) 974–985; CC BY 4.0). DOI 10.1002/mar.70098. No corrections. The hospitality card dates it 2025 (online); the version of record is the 2026 issue.
- *Read status:* **Full text, publisher version** (Wiley HTML version of record, `onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.70098`, read in Chrome; the HTML carries section numbers, not page numbers). The Southampton eprint (Unpaywall) sits behind an Anubis challenge and was not used.
- *What it argues:* Digital Technology Captivity (DTC) is "the condition in which consumers feel constrained when services move to digital access, and, after evaluating the situation through primary and secondary appraisal, perceive that they have few resources and options. This state produces stress through felt entrapment, as consumers are compelled to adopt adaptive or maladaptive coping strategies" (§2). It is "Triggered by a shift in the access environment—rather than the service experience itself" (§1). Against the neighbouring constructs: "Where digital exclusion typically captures non-access and unengagement reflects withdrawal or resistance, DTC centers on coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable" (§2.1). Against service captivity: "Consumers are therefore not captive to a single service provider but are captive to a system that requires digital skills and/or devices to participate" (§2.2); in the low-service-captivity, high-DTC cell of their 2×2, "choice exists on a surface level (e.g., many providers), but the access barrier is the same" (§2.2); the high/high cell is "the double bind" (§2.2). Four exemplars (Table 1): UEFA mobile-only tickets ("PDFs or printouts are refused"), UK app-first banking, QR-only restaurant menus and app-only deals, and Amazon Go/Fresh, where "Shoppers who lack smartphones, therefore, face outright exclusion or enforced adoption and subsequent data-harvesting lock-in" (Table 1, D). The trigger types are "substitution (analogue access replaced by digital), co-existence (analogue retained but downgraded and more difficult to access), liquid digital (constant updating), or a move to digital-only (systemic) access" (§3.1). Exit: maladaptive coping means "neglecting, abandoning, and distancing oneself from the service category altogether … which may carry a personal cost and loss (e.g., avoiding GP surgery because bookings are mandated to be online)" (§3.4). "While exclusion might mean simply walking away and foregoing a service, captivity is different: the consumer cannot walk away" (§5.2); "digital convenience for some can mean digital coercion for others" (§5.2). Susceptibility is read through intersectionality (§4.1); mitigation through "warm experts" (§4.2) and service design that reduces cognitive load, keeps humans on hand and preserves autonomy "by allowing consumers to choose modes of access (e.g., digital, analogue), control the pacing of adoption, and better understand the reasons for the change" (§4.3).
- *Bearing:* Strong support for form 4: the physical door, turnstile or counter is where a digital rule becomes a fact and where exit is priced (GP visits forgone, deals missed, venues barred). It complicates form 1: DTC needs no adaptive algorithm; a fixed "app required" rule produces it. Marks carried by the arrangements described: a gatekeeping layer distinct from the service (yes); it reads the customer's device and account (yes); it binds the customer (yes) but not a second party (the paper has no worker); "liquid digital (constant updating)" is one of four triggers (§3.1), so "does not hold still" is present but optional. The paper's own remedies are design-side (alternative channels, human support, pacing), which sit beside Part 1's structural-refusal principle; its "better understand the reasons for the change" is a legibility remedy, which the author has ruled is awareness, not agency.

## (c) Synthesis

### A1. Definitions, origin, and the problems named

1. **Origin.** Two reviews disagree. Mameli and others (2026, Page 3) say the term was "Coined by the Australian marketing agency Momentum in 2013" and "did not appear in academia until very recently". Mele and others' (2023) own Appendix 1 lists Nakazawa and Tokuda's 2007 "Phygital map" (a computer-science workshop paper) as the first of forty-two items and sets the review window at 2007–2022 (§2). The practitioner-coinage story is therefore incomplete: an engineering use predates it by six years. Both say the marketing literature took the word from practice (Mele §1: "used mainly by practitioners").
2. **Batat's definition** reaches Part 4 only at second hand (gate failed). Alexander and Varley (2025, p. 3) quote it as an "ecosystem that adopts a consumer standpoint" whose "goal … is to offer unique and compelling CX that guarantee a coherent continuum in the delivery process of consumer value provided from digital to physical and vice versa"; Batat's 2024 editorial (§1) restates the aim as "a seamless delivery of value". The PH-CX abstract itself speaks of "fluidifying the journeys". The construct is defined by its goal, and the goal is seamlessness.
3. **Mele's definition** is an assemblage: "not simply the combination of physical and digital, but as a nexus, assemblage, or hybrid" that is "still an ontological 'mess'" (2023, §4); in 2025 (§6) the same authors call it "an assemblage or hybrid where materiality, location and temporality meet immateriality, dislocation and timelessness".
4. **Problems named, in the frameworks' own words.** *Fluidity and seamlessness* are the aim, not the problem (Batat editorial §1; Mele 2023 §3.2.2; Johnson and Barlow 2021 §1, §4.3; Mameli 2026 Page 12, 25; Zheng 2025 §2); only Batat's editorial calls "conceptual fluidity" a challenge (§4), and only for method. *The journey* is the unit of analysis for Mele and Russo-Spena (2022, abstract) and the "decision" stage of Mele's ADO model (2023, §3.2.2). *The employee's role* is named as a gap (Mameli Page 26; Mulcahy 2026 abstract; De Vos 2026 abstract) and as the store's irreplaceable value (Alexander and Varley p. 8). *Vulnerability* appears in Batat 2025 (binge delivery, abstract), PHIVE's "value destruction" axis (abstract), Wilson-Nash (§2), Lee and Kim (§1), Kim and others (abstract). *Inclusion* is "the least prominent feature" of 84 industry cases (Mameli Page 20) and the explicit subject of Wilson-Nash, Lee and Kim, Gustafsson (Table 6) and Pusceddu (ms p. 9–10).

### A2. Does any phygital paper model an intermediary that reads two parties and binds both?

5. **No full-text phygital paper I read models that.** The frames are: firm designs, one customer journeys (Batat PH-CX abstract; Mele 2023 ADO; Johnson and Barlow 2021; Alexander and Varley 2025; Mameli 2026; Zheng 2025; Suh 2026 abstract). The gap searches (rows 18, 21) found nothing in Consensus or OpenAlex either.
6. **Three papers come near.** Shabnam and others (2026, §1) name "algorithmic mediation of agency", "digitally enforced rule structures" that are "non-negotiable and often opaque", and "non-human interpretive labour", and say prior frameworks "do not explain how algorithmic systems autonomously redirect, constrain or reconfigure agency" (Table 2). Gustafsson (2026, §1) names "customers, algorithms, remote staff" as co-creators and records operators changing hours in response to incidents (§3.2), so both sides are bound in her data though not in her model. Roederer and others (2026, abstract) say "AI increasingly acts as a third agent … transforming customer and employee dynamics". Mele and Russo-Spena (2022, abstract) describe "systems of insights and systems of engagement", a read-and-act layer for one party.
7. **The mediator distinction.** Mele and Russo-Spena (2025, §2) import Latour's split between an intermediary ("once the input is specified, the output is known") and a mediator, which "can modify what it transports … so the output cannot be anticipated solely from the input". Part 1's "adaptive, opaque algorithmic intermediary" is, in this vocabulary, a mediator. The phygital literature has the term available and does not apply it to a two-sided match.

### A3. Friction: defect or defended?

8. **Inside the phygital frameworks, friction is a defect.** Mele and others (2023, §3.2.2): phygital tools help "alleviate some of the most frustrating elements of the customer decision process". Johnson and Barlow (2021, §4.2.1) make the removal of payment friction the mechanism of the "phygital advantage": "no conscious attention to the payment process is required at all", and "the more abstract and unfamiliar the payment system … the more freely the consumer spends". Shabnam and others (2026, §1) describe the mainstream as "seamless channel integration and engagement optimisation". Mameli (Page 12) makes seamlessness the criterion that separates phygital from hybrid.
9. **Friction is defended beside, not inside, the phygital literature.** Padigar, Li and Manjunath (2025) define friction neutrally (§2.1), keep "constructive friction" in payment and consent because "when payment becomes easy, it may prompt customers to overspend" and "algorithms may provide output to nudge consumers into purchase decisions that may not work in their best interests" (§3.2), and find that firms "have been mindlessly removing constructive friction" (§5.3). Sheahan and others (2024, abstract) do the same in HCI. Neither uses the word phygital.
10. **Pushback inside the field takes other forms.** Sabri and others (2026, abstract) find "phygital overload". Gustafsson (2026, §5) finds that "removing staff increases the need for human signals" and that nudges without trust are "perceived as surveillance rather than support" (§5.1). Pusceddu and others' decompressive strategy (ms p. 11–12) adds human slowness on purpose. Mieli and others (2024, "Paths") find that optimised routes "reduce the liminality of the tourist experience". None of these defends friction as a design value; they record what its removal costs.
11. **Bearing on the author's rulings.** Padigar's remedy for commercially aligned assistants is that consumers "understand the mechanisms" (§4.3.2); Shabnam's P1a is transparency with override; Wilson-Nash's §4.3 asks providers to help consumers "better understand the reasons for the change". Each is a legibility remedy. Under the author's ruling these are evidence about where predictability fails, not the remedy Part 4 proposes. Padigar's "constructive friction" and Gustafsson's "alternative entry (credit card vs. app)" (Table 6) are closer to structural refusal and seamful design.

### A4. Inclusion and captivity

12. **Who is shut out.** Older customers (Pusceddu ms p. 9: 60+ hostility; Gustafsson §4.7.3: "older adults need 3-4 visits or they quit"; Lee and Kim: 389 Koreans aged 65+; Wilson-Nash §4.1); people without smartphones, bank accounts or current devices (Wilson-Nash Table 1: UEFA tickets, Amazon Go; Gustafsson §2.5: BankID and app to open the door); rural users whose fallback is distant (Gustafsson §4.7.1: "help is 30 min away"). Mameli (Page 20) finds accessibility the least-built feature across 84 cases.
13. **What compels.** Wilson-Nash name four triggers, "substitution … co-existence … liquid digital (constant updating) … digital-only" (§3.1); Lee and Kim define coercive service design as "the systematic withdrawal of viable human-staffed alternatives" (§2.1). Both treat the compulsion as structural, "a structural condition rather than an individual disposition" (Lee and Kim, abstract).
14. **What exit costs.** Wilson-Nash: "captivity is different: the consumer cannot walk away" (§5.2); abandonment "may carry a personal cost and loss (e.g., avoiding GP surgery because bookings are mandated to be online)" (§3.4); in the systemic cell "the access barrier is the same" across providers (§2.2). Lee and Kim: exit exists for consumers but not employees ("Unlike employees, consumers are not institutionally bound", §2.1), and older consumers use it by avoidance. Kim and others (abstract): compulsion becomes unpaid "shadow work". Gustafsson's netnography: "You're basically working for them" (§4.2).
15. **Where the rule becomes a fact.** The turnstile (UEFA), the store door (Amazon Go, BankID), the counter that is gone (Lee and Kim §2.1; Shabnam P3's civil servants), the kiosk. All are physical. This is form 4 described by the phygital literature's neighbours, mostly without an adaptive algorithm.

### A5. What the reviews say about the theory

16. Mele and others (2023): "used mainly by practitioners", "lacks a clear definition", "Vagueness in using the term characterises most of the studies" (§1, §3.1), "an ontological 'mess' … not developed … scant empirical and theoretical research" (§4). Johnson and Barlow (2021, §3): "not yet subject to systematic study and therefore lacks a coherent 'literature'". Batat's own abstract (2024): "the construct lacks an academic conceptualization". Del Vecchio and others (2023, abstract): "fragmented and far from a full understanding". Brochado and others (2026, abstract): "fundamentally consumer-centred and technology-enabled". Shabnam and others (2026, §1): "largely focused on descriptive and experiential dimensions". Klaus (2024) asks in his title whether the construct is the emperor's new clothes. Mameli (2026, Page 3) grants it only "very recent" academic standing.
17. The verdict across six reviews is consistent: the term is practitioner-born (with one engineering antecedent), definitionally loose, consumer-centred and thin on theory. Part 4 can say so with these citations and need not infer it.

### What Part 4 can claim, case by case

- **Form 1 (same algorithmic intermediary reaching into physical space):** supported in vocabulary by Shabnam (decision authority "embedded in code", non-negotiable rules), Mele 2025 (contexts that are "endlessly responsive and personalised"), Mieli 2024 (the dynamic map versus the static one), Johnson and Barlow (the smart cooler), Roederer (AI as "third agent"). Not one of them models the two-sided binding match. Form 1 is available as a reading of these cases; it is not already stated as such.
- **Form 2 (a human arrangement as intermediary):** one candidate: Pusceddu's staff reading hostile customers and adjusting the experience (ms p. 11–12); Gustafsson's remote concierge. Both are human-plus-system; neither paper frames it so.
- **Form 3 (physical design stays choice architecture):** the default frame of PH-CX, Mele's ADO model, Mameli, Alexander and Varley, Suh. Lee and Kim call the SST-only store a "choice architecture" outright (§1). Mele's "smart nudging" (2021, abstract) bridges the two literatures under choice architecture's name.
- **Form 4 (the physical layer is where the ruling becomes a fact and exit is priced):** the best-evidenced form. Wilson-Nash, Lee and Kim, Gustafsson, Kim and others, Pusceddu, Zheng's "assists or restricts" self-location space. Most of these arrangements hold still; the price of exit does not need an adaptive layer.

### Evidence against the thesis

18. **Barlow and Johnson (2026, abstract)** already state the contrast Part 4's test rests on: "Physical retail … remains static and non-adaptive. … phygital design offers a synthesis: adaptive responsiveness within shared physical space", and call it "market-shaping infrastructure". Their reference list reaches for Thaler and Sunstein and for Hanson and Kysar on manipulation. If the body develops the point, Part 4's fourth mark is a restatement. Abstract only; a library copy is needed before Part 4 claims priority.
19. **Shabnam and others (2026)** already name "algorithmic mediation of agency" and "digitally enforced rules" as the discontinuities phygital service research must model, with propositions. Part 4's form 1 is pre-stated in service research vocabulary; what remains open there is the two-party binding, which they do not model.
