# X: The counter-case against "physical decision experience design"

*Facet X of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat X of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The author will claim that DXD "is not only a software or digital concept" and that physical decision experience design exists and is visible in the phygital literature. This facet is opposing counsel. It states, at full strength, the published case that the claim is wrong, trivial, already made, or inconsistent with Parts 1 to 3, and rates each line. It does not answer the lines; other facets and the author do. Seven lines came from the brief and one novelty search was added:

- X1. Physical decision design is servicescape and choice architecture under a new label.
- X2. "Phygital" is a trade coinage that its own field calls under-theorised.
- X3. Part 1 defines DXD so that Part 4 must either repeat it or repeal it.
- X4. Ubiquitous computing, smart-city critique, science and technology studies and law already said it; Barlow and Johnson 2026 already draw the central contrast.
- X5. Exit is cheap in physical space, so Part 2's ground for the designer's obligation does not reach it.
- X6. Static physical design works, so the adaptive intermediary is not the interesting case offline.
- X7. The "holds still" criterion fails: shelves, prices and clerks have adapted to data for decades.
- X8 (novelty search). Dark patterns, sludge and administrative burden already cover physical and hybrid settings.

Where a line quotes Parts 2 and 3 or `ARGUMENT.md`, those texts are Claude-drafted for the author and are marked as such. Part 1 is the author's live text.

### X2. "Phygital" is a trade coinage with thin theory

`mele2023phygital` | Mele, C., Russo Spena, T., Marzullo, M., & Di Bernardo, I. (2023). The phygital transformation: A systematic review and a research agenda. *Italian Journal of Marketing*, 2023(3), 323–349.
- *Record:* Crossref, doi:10.1007/s43039-023-00070-7; OpenAlex (abstract). Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar list the Springer PDF as CC-BY open access.
- *Read status:* **Abstract only** (OpenAlex abstract). The open-access PDF is behind a Springer JavaScript challenge on every route tried (curl with and without a cookie jar through the identity provider; WebFetch on the article and PDF URLs; fatcat; OpenAIRE; the authors' institutional repository, which has the record and no file). **Gate item not reached.**
- *What the abstract states:* the label "phygital" (physical plus digital) describes how companies engage customers through smart technologies; the term is used mainly by practitioners, and research into its theoretical development is lacking; the authors review the literature systematically, identify four aspects (objects and applications; context, meaning space and place; customer journey; shopping or retail experience), and interpret the field with an antecedents–decisions–outcomes model; they propose research avenues "to develop the phygital construct".
- *Bearing:* **the strongest published form of X2**, at abstract strength: the field's own systematic review says the term is practitioner-led and theoretically undeveloped. Its four aspects are objects, places, journeys and shopping, none of which is an intermediary, so at this level the phygital literature does not describe marks 1 to 4. Supports form 3 against form 1.

`batat2022phygital` | Batat, W. (2022/2024). What does phygital really mean? A conceptual introduction to the phygital customer experience (PH-CX) framework. *Journal of Strategic Marketing*, 32(8), 1220–1243.
- *Record:* Crossref, doi:10.1080/0965254X.2022.2059775 (issued 2022-04-05 online; the issue is 32(8), 2024). HAL record hal-04444804 (abstract, no file).
- *Read status:* **Abstract only** (HAL abstract). T&F returned 403 on HTML and PDF.

`mele2024agencement` | Mele, C., & Russo-Spena, T. (2024/2025). Agencement of onlife and phygital: Smart tech–enabled value co-creation practices. *Journal of Service Management*, 36(2), 217–240.

- *Read status:* **Full text, publisher version** (Emerald PDF, 14,038 words, saved through WebFetch; pagination 217–240 confirmed on the pages).
- *What it argues:* the paper opens on Floridi's "Are you online or offline?" and the answer "We are both" (p. 217). ## (c) Synthesis

### X1. Servicescape and choice architecture renamed

1. Marketing has had a construct for the designed physical environment's effect on behaviour since Kotler 1973 and Bitner 1992; Bitner's framework covers customers and employees (abstract, `bitner1992servicescapes`), and the construct absorbed the social environment by 2011 (`rosenbaum2011expanded`, abstract), reached 188 papers by 2013 (`mari2013servicescape`, abstract), crossed to online settings by 2010 (`harris2010online`, title) and to IoT-sensed "smart servicescapes" by 2019 (`kang2019smart`, full text). Behavioural science has a typology for physical choice architecture (`hollands2017tippme`, p. 4) and the grocery literature has "hybrid nudges" for the adaptive in-store case (`grant2025nudging`, p. 5).
2. Part 1 itself concedes the point: environments in which people decide have been designed "for over seven decades … under different banners" (line 20), and fixed arrangements are filed under choice architecture (line 40).
3. **Rating: strong on scope, moderate on the decisive question.** The literatures cover two parties and technology. None of the sources read treats the environment as a third party that reads both sides and binds them. Bitner's title word is "surroundings"; Hollands excludes the interactive and tailored; Kang's smart servicescape is an environment with a datascape, not an agent. X1 therefore forces the essay to concede that the physical setting as such is servicescape, and it leaves open whether a physical arrangement can be an intermediary (form 2).
4. *Gate note:* neither Bitner 1992 nor Kotler 1973 was read in full. X1's strongest form rests on Bitner's abstract and later papers' descriptions. A library copy of Bitner would settle whether the 1992 framework already treats the environment as dynamic or as technology-bearing.

### X2. "Phygital" is a trade coinage with thin theory

1. The field's own systematic review says the term is used mainly by practitioners and that its theoretical development is lacking (`mele2023phygital`, abstract); the construct's main proponent opens by saying it lacks an academic conceptualisation (`batat2022phygital`, abstract); the scholarly literature begins in 2017 and numbers about 105 papers by 2025 (`brochado2026phygital`, abstract); an advertising agency claims the 2007 coinage and the attribution to a named person is unverified (`momentum2024phygital`, web; row 7).
2. The term's leading theorists deny the physical/digital distinction the essay's title presupposes: "The traditional dichotomies such as those between real and digital or human and machine are no longer clearly sustainable" (`mele2024agencement`, p. 224), following Floridi, for whom "it is no longer sensible to ask whether one may be online or offline" (`floridi2015onlife`, p. 1). The postdigital literature says the same of its own boundary and concedes its concept is "hard to define" (`jandric2018postdigital`, p. 4).
3. **Rating: strong as to the term, weak as to the thesis.** An essay that borrows "phygital" inherits a construct its field calls undeveloped and a boundary its theorists call dissolved. But the same sources help the essay: Mele and Russo-Spena ascribe "intelligence and autonomy" to phygital materiality and "automated" agency to the arrangement (p. 217), which is marks 1 and 4 in the phygital field's own words; Floridi's fourth transformation is "the primacy of interactions, processes and networks" over stand-alone things (p. 4). The line forces the essay to concede that it is supplying the theory the phygital field lacks, not drawing on one, and to state its physical/digital distinction as a design question (where a ruling is enacted and where exit is priced) rather than an ontological one.
4. *Gate note:* Mele 2023 was not read in full. Its four aspects (objects, places, journeys, shopping) come from the abstract. The full text might contain the "intermediary" language that its sequel (Mele 2024) does; it might not.

### X3. Part 1 contradicts it

1. Part 1 defines DXD by an adaptive, opaque algorithmic intermediary (lines 22, 34, 116) and places "the environment arranged by an identifiable human designer" among the predecessors (lines 34, 42, 48, 156). It says "Traditional media hold still; an algorithmic intermediary does not" (line 118).
2. The dilemma: if Part 4's physical cases all have an algorithm behind them, Part 4 is Part 1 with a door attached and the word "physical" names a delivery channel; if a case with no algorithm counts (a turnstile, a shelf, a hotel desk in 1989), lines 34, 42, 48 and 118 are repealed and every item in Hollands's typology qualifies.
3. Parts 2 and 3 (Claude-drafted) sharpen it: Part 2 grounds the designer's obligation in exit that is "unavailable or ruinously priced" (line 25) and has already conceded that choice architecture is not "passive and static" (line 33); Part 3 says the mandatory-participation argument "does not reach a venue that nobody has to use" (line 77) and supplies the figure a critic will reuse: "To the bet it is a cashier" (line 73).
4. **Rating: strong.** This is the line the author cannot cite around, because the texts are the series' own. It forces an explicit statement of which of forms 1 to 4 Part 4 claims and a rule for when a physical arrangement meets mark 4. Two things in Part 1 soften it: line 146 (a costless exit collapses the triad) gives the essay a test it can run on physical cases, and line 152 shows the series already borrows from physical-space computing.

### X4. Others already said it

1. Ubiquitous computing (`weiser1991computer`), smart-city critique (`kitchin2014realtime`, pp. 1, 12; `mattern2017city`, pp. 1, 9, 14), science and technology studies (`latour1992missing`, reprint pp. 154–157) and law (`lessig2006code`, pp. 77, 94, 123, 128) have each treated computing's move into physical space, or the physical artifact as a regulator, for decades. Part 2 already uses Latour's prescription and Verbeek's speed bump (line 99); Part 1 already uses ubicomp's seamful design (line 152).
2. Barlow and Johnson 2026 state the essay's contrast in a published commentary nine months before Part 4: physical retail "remains static and non-adaptive", digital retail personalises, and phygital design is "adaptive responsiveness within shared physical space" (`barlow2026phygital`, abstract).
3. **Rating: strong as priority on the domain and the contrast; moderate as priority on the triad.** None of these sources models a third party that reads and binds two human parties. Latour's spring presupposes a user but reads no second party and does not retrain; Lessig's architecture constrains one dot; Greenberg's proxemic systems are dyadic; Barlow's abstract frames personalisation against collective coordination. The essay must concede the domain and the contrast and must show the two-sided, binding, adaptive case in physical space to have anything left.

### X5. Exit is cheap in physical space

1. Hirschman's paradigm of cheap exit is the competitive shop (pp. 21, 33); Part 2 grounds the obligation in the opposite condition (lines 25, 27, 101); Part 3 concedes the obligation "does not reach a venue that nobody has to use" (line 77); Part 1 says a costless exit collapses the triad (line 146). A physical step before commitment reduces dark-pattern effectiveness (`zac2025dark`, abstract).
2. **Rating: moderate.** It holds for the store and the restaurant and fails for the cases facets A, E and G are likely to bring: the gate with no app (Wilson-Nash 2025), the welfare office (Bagger et al. 2023, Part 2 line 27), the captive urinal or ATM (`greenberg2014dark`), the warehouse floor. The line forces the essay to sort physical cases by exit price rather than claim them as a class, which is form 4's own logic.

### X6. Static physical design works and is harmless

1. Food choice is the domain with the largest and least bias-contaminated nudge effects (F2's reading of `mertens2022effectiveness` and `maier2022noevidence`); the grocery literature treats adaptive in-store nudges as "hybrid nudges", a continuation of nudging (`grant2025nudging`, p. 5).
2. **Rating: weak.** Part 1 (line 44) already rests on the bias corrections that shrink the whole literature to d = 0.01–0.08 (`szaszi2022noreason`, F2), so the essay cannot be threatened by nudge effect sizes it has itself discounted. What survives is the observation that the one surviving domain is physical and static, which supports form 3 for food and nothing else.

### X7. The "holds still" criterion fails

1. Hotel front desks quoted rates set by electronic systems that read segmented demand and changed by date in 1989 (`kimes1989basics`, pp. 14–15); airlines began the practice in the 1960s (`smith1992yield`, abstract) and it spread to lodging, car rental, rail and cruise lines (`kimes1989yield`, abstract); shelf allocation was fitted to sales data by 1972 (`curhan1972shelf`, abstract) and is now re-optimised from real-time sensors (row 6). Part 2 has conceded that choice architecture need not hold still (line 33, Yeung's hypernudge).
2. **Rating: moderate.** The tempo point is well documented: adaptation at the physical point of sale is old. The sources also show its limit, which the counter-case must record: yield management reads the market and changes a price list, not the individual, and binds nothing but the offer; and both Hollands (p. 4: "not designed to be interactive or tailored to specific individuals") and Grant (p. 4: nudges "modified in response to consumer behaviors") draw the essay's line themselves. The line forces the essay to state mark 4 as a threshold (per-person, run-time, unannounced) and to apply it; it does not show the mark is empty.

### X8. Dark patterns, sludge and administrative burden already reach physical and hybrid settings

1. "Dark patterns" entered physical space in 2014 through proxemic displays, with captivity and priced exit as its first pattern (`greenberg2014dark`); the method reached home IoT devices in 2023 (`kowalczyk2023dark`, abstract) and a fast-food kiosk in 2026 (`purohit2026deception`), whose authors find the physical setting amplifies the screen's patterns. Sludge (`sunstein2019sludge`, Part I; `sunstein2020audits`, abstract), administrative burden (`herd2018administrative`, Introduction) and their comparison (`madsen2021burdens`, pp. 1, 4, 7, 14) already name designed friction on people who cannot exit, in paper and in-person encounters.
2. I found no work that uses the phrase "physical dark patterns" in OpenAlex (row 21), no audit of a shop floor without a screen in OpenAlex or Consensus (rows 4, 22, 23), and no application of "sludge" to retail in OpenAlex (row 24).
3. **Rating: moderate.** The vocabulary exists and the hybrid cases are published; the specifically triadic physical case is not. Part 4 can claim the two-sided adaptive case; it cannot claim to be first to see dark patterns, friction or priced exit in physical space.

### Evidence against the thesis (collected)

- Part 1's definition is algorithmic by its own words (lines 22, 34, 116, 118), and its predecessors include the environment "arranged by an identifiable human designer" (line 34).
- Barlow and Johnson 2026 publish the static-physical versus adaptive-digital contrast before Part 4 (abstract).
- Mele and Russo-Spena 2024 and Floridi 2015 deny the physical/digital line the title rests on (pp. 224; 1, 4).
- Latour 1992 and Lessig 2006 already treat physical artifacts as prescriptive regulators (reprint pp. 154–157; pp. 77, 123, 128), and Part 2 has already borrowed them (line 99).
- Hirschman 1970 makes the shop the paradigm of cheap exit (pp. 21, 33), and Part 3 concedes the obligation does not reach a voluntary venue (line 77).
- Kimes 1989 shows a physical desk delivering adaptive prices in 1989 (pp. 14–15).
- Greenberg et al. 2014 apply dark patterns to physical sensing twelve years before Part 4, including captivity (section 1).

### Evidence against the counter-case (what the essay can use, recorded because the brief requires it)

- Hollands et al. 2017 exclude the "interactive or tailored" from physical choice architecture (p. 4), and Grant et al. 2025 separate static cues from nudges "modified in response to consumer behaviors" (p. 4): the essay's line is already drawn inside the nudge literature.
- Mele and Russo-Spena 2024 ascribe "intelligence and autonomy" to phygital materiality (p. 217).
- Purohit et al. 2026 find that the physical setting amplifies interface dark patterns (section "System level"), which is form 4.
- Greenberg et al. 2014 and Herd and Moynihan 2018 document priced exit in physical settings (captivity; the citizen who cannot leave the state).
- None of the X4 sources models a two-sided, binding, adaptive arrangement; the triad remains unclaimed.

### Gaps

- Bitner 1992, Kotler 1973 and Mele et al. 2023 were not read in full (gate items). Barlow and Johnson 2026 and Batat 2022 are abstract only.
- No source read measures exit price in a physical retail or hospitality setting; X5 rests on Hirschman's paradigm and the series' own concessions.
- No source read documents a physical arrangement that reads two parties and binds both while adapting at run time without a digital system behind it. If facet G finds one, form 2 opens; if not, form 2 is empty and the counter-case's dilemma (X3) stands.
- The attribution of "phygital" to Chris Weil and a 2013 claim of rights rests on secondary web pages.

**The most damaging line.** X3, and specifically the dilemma built from Part 1's own lines 34, 42, 48 and 118 together with Part 3's concession at line 77. Every other line can be answered by a source the essay does not yet cite; this one is answered only by a ruling the author has not yet made. Part 1 defines the discipline by an adaptive algorithmic intermediary and places the human-arranged environment among its predecessors. Part 4 therefore has two exits and no third: it can claim that the phygital is the same intermediary reaching into physical space, in which case Barlow and Johnson 2026 own the contrast, Greenberg et al. 2014 own the first physical dark patterns, Kitchin 2014 and Lessig 2006 own the governance problem, and "physical DXD" names a delivery channel rather than a concept; or it can claim that a purely physical arrangement can itself be an intermediary, in which case it must say why Kimes's 1989 front desk, Latour's door-closer and Hollands's shelf are not already DXD, and Part 1's line 118 ("Traditional media hold still") has to be rewritten. The only way through is the one the brief's form 4 already names: treat the physical layer not as a second kind of DXD but as the place where an intermediary's ruling becomes a fact and where exit gets its price, and show at least one case in which that layer reads both parties and binds both. Nothing read for this facet shows that case yet, and the sources that come closest (Mele and Russo-Spena 2024 on autonomous phygital materiality; Purohit et al. 2026 on the setting amplifying the screen) are a conceptual paper and a one-walkthrough preprint.
