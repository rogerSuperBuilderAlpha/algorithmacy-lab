# C: Scripts, prescription and affordance; the physical artifact that acts on people

*Facet C of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat C of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Part 2 cited Latour, Akrich and Verbeek on "scripts" and "prescription" without a full read (Latour at reprint pagination, Akrich through a third party's notes). These are arguments about physical objects that act on people: a door-closer, a seat belt, a key, a speed bump, a lighting kit, an electricity meter. Part 4 needs to know whether any of these authors already describes a physical intermediary that binds two parties, or only an object that binds its user on an absent designer's behalf. The research questions:

- **C1.** Latour: what are delegation, prescription, the "prescribed user", antiprograms and circumscription? In the door-closer, seat-belt and Berliner-key cases, who are the parties, does the artifact bind one of them or both, and does it ever "read" anyone?
- **C2.** Akrich: what is a script and a de-scription, what happens when users do not take up the role, and what are her cases?
- **C3.** Winner and Lessig: architecture as politics and as regulation. Do they treat built form as static? Does Lessig's "architecture" modality anticipate adaptive physical regulation? What is the standing of the Moses-bridges story?
- **C4.** Affordance from Gibson through Norman to Gaver: what the concept claims, what Norman later corrected ("signifiers"), and whether affordance describes anything beyond one actor and one environment.
- **C5.** Verbeek and Dorrestijn: mediation and "moralizing technology", as far as full text was obtainable.

`latour1992missing` | Latour, B. (1992). Where are the missing masses? The sociology of a few mundane artifacts. In W. E. Bijker & J. Law (eds), *Shaping Technology/Building Society: Studies in Sociotechnical Change* (pp. 225–258). MIT Press.
- *Record:* No DOI. Volume record: Internet Archive `shapingtechnolog00wieb` (MIT Press, 1992, ISBN 0262023385 / 0262521946); the chapter's own reference list and the reprint's credit line give pp. 225–258. Reprinted in D. G. Johnson & J. M. Wetmore (eds), *Technology and Society* (MIT Press, 2009), ch. 10, pp. 151–180.
- *Read status:* **Full text, reprint** (`/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/dissertation/research/library/pdfs/latour1992.pdf`, 30 pp., reprint pages 151–180), read whole; **plus the 1992 MIT Press pagination** from the open Internet Archive scan `https://archive.org/download/manualzilla-id-5914639/5914639.pdf` (pp. 224–259), in which every passage below was located by string match. Locators are MIT pages [reprint pages].
- *What it argues:* The chapter opens on the seat belt that will not let the car start: "The program of action 'IF a car is moving, THEN the driver has a seat belt' is enforced. It has become logically—no, it has become sociologically—impossible to drive without wearing the belt" (p. 226 [152]). The missing mass of sociology is the nonhumans, "the hidden and despised social masses who make up our morality" (p. 227 [153]). **Delegation** is defined by the two-column ledger for the door: "I will define this transformation of a major effort into a minor one by the words displacement or translation or delegation or shifting" (pp. 228–229 [154]); the removal test follows: "every time you want to know what a nonhuman does, simply imagine what other humans or other nonhumans would have to do were this character not present" (p. 229 [155]). Delegation to a human groom fails on cost and reliability and keeps the "present tense"; delegation to hinges "creates clear-cut distinctions between production, installation, and maintenance" and "time is folded" (p. 231 [156]). **Prescription**: "An unskilled nonhuman groom thus presupposes a skilled human user. It is always a trade-off. I will call, after Madeleine Akrich's paper (Akrich 1992), the behavior imposed back onto the human by nonhuman delegates prescription. Prescription is the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms" (p. 232 [157]); "We have been able to delegate to nonhumans not only force as we have known it for centuries but also values, duties, and ethics" (p. 232 [157]). The hydraulic closer "slowly closed the main door of our house"; "My sociologist friends at the School of Mines call such a clever extraction an 'obligatory passage point,' which is a very fitting name for a door. No matter what you feel, think, or do, you have to leave a bit of your energy, literally, at the door. This is as clever as a toll booth" (p. 234 [158]). Prescriptions discriminate: "neither my little nephews nor my grandmother could get in unaided … To use Langdon Winner's classic motto (1980): Because of their prescriptions, these doors discriminate against very little and very old persons" (p. 234 [158–159]); and they can be opposed: "its delegated action may be opposed by a foot (salesman are said to be expert at this). The foot may in turn be delegated to a carpet" (p. 234 [159]). The groom is anthropomorphic in three senses: "first, it has been made by humans; second, it substitutes for the actions of people …; and third, it shapes human action by prescribing back what sort of people should pass through the door" (p. 235 [160]). The only sensing artifacts in the chapter are mentioned in passing: "those many other innovations that have endowed much more sophisticated doors with the ability to see you arrive in advance (electronic eyes), to ask for your identity (electronic passes), or to slam shut in case of danger" (p. 236 [160]), and red lights "sophisticated enough to integrate traffic flows through sensors" (p. 246 [168]). The **prescribed user**: "Nothing in a given scene can prevent the inscribed user or reader from behaving differently from what was expected … The user of the traffic light may well cross on the red … There might be an enormous gap between the prescribed user and the user-in-the-flesh" (p. 237 [161]); the groom "is very bad at doing anything to help people arrive there. After fifty centimeters, it is helpless" (p. 237 [161]). **Circumscription** is the photocopier's gradient of labels from "ADD PAPER" through "ADD TONER. SEE MANUAL, PAGE 30," "DON'T ADD THE TONER YOURSELF, CALL THE SECRETARY," to "DO NOT OPEN — DANGER, HIGH VOLTAGE, HEAT": "Each of these messages addresses a different audience, from the widest … to the narrowest" (p. 239 [161–162]); delegates are "lieutenants (from the French 'lieu' plus 'tenant,' i.e., holding the place of, for, someone else)" (p. 239 [162]). The chain from morality to force ends in "the road bumper, or a speed trap that we call in French 'un gendarme couché,' a laid policeman. It is impossible for us not to slow down, or else we break our suspension … It is the complete chain that makes up the missing masses, not either of its extremities" (p. 244 [166]). **Antiprograms**: the seat belt re-inscribes contradictory demands (firm and lax, fast to buckle and to unbuckle), and "The program of action is in practice the answer to an antiprogram against which the mechanism braces itself. Looking at the mechanism alone is like watching half the court during a tennis game" (p. 247 [168]). The **Berliner key** (pp. 252–254 [172–174]) orders "RELOCK THE DOOR BEHIND YOU"; the key cannot be withdrawn until the door is relocked: "This is a clever translation of a possible program relying on morality into a program relying on dire necessity: you might not want to relock the key, but you cannot do otherwise. The distance between morality and force is not as wide as moralists expect; or more exactly, clever engineers have made it smaller. There is a price to pay … you have to replace most of the locks in Berlin" (p. 253 [174]). Yet "a really bad guy may relock the door without closing it!" and "No artifact is idiot-proof because any artifact is only a portion of a program of action and of the fight necessary to win against many antiprograms" (p. 254 [174]). The notes fix the vocabulary: scripts, scenes, scenarios or setups "played by human or nonhuman actants" (n. 3, p. 255 [177]); de-scription as "the retrieval of the script from the situation" (n. 4, p. 255); prescription as "whatever a scene presupposes from its transcribed actors and authors (this is very much like 'role expectation' in sociology, except that it may be inscribed or encoded in the machine)" (n. 8, p. 256 [177]); Norman 1988's Gulf of Execution as the gap between inscribed and real users, with the remark that Norman "never considers the shaping of the artifact by the engineer themselves" (n. 14, p. 257 [177]); de-inscription and subscription (n. 15), pre-inscription as "all the work that has to be done upstream of the scene" (n. 16), circumscription as "the organization in the setting of its own limits" (n. 17), all p. 257 [177–178]; and why the groom struck: "the hydraulic piston did its job, but not the spring that collaborated with it" (n. 23, p. 258 [178]).
- *Bearing:* **Supports form 3 and complicates form 2; against form 1 on adaptivity.** Every artifact here is designed once by an absent author and acts on one party, the user, on that author's behalf: the enunciator "has disappeared from the scene" (see `latour1994mediation`). None binds the author. None reads the user except the belt's sensor and the sensor-fed red light, and both apply a fixed rule to the reading. The chapter is the strongest statement in the facet that physical artifacts bind only by force or by prescribed competence, that binding is never complete ("No artifact is idiot-proof"), and that the designer's reply to an antiprogram is a new fixed inscription, not a run-time adjustment. Marks: distinct intermediary, yes; reads both, no; binds both, no; holds still, yes (the belt's escalation from alarm to door-strap happens at the drawing board, between models). For form 4 it supplies the exact wording for "the ruling becomes a fact": morality translated into "dire necessity" (p. 253), and the toll-booth door that prices passage in energy (p. 234).

`akrich1987comment` | Akrich, M. (1987). Comment décrire les objets techniques ? *Techniques & Culture*, 9, 49–64. Reprinted in *Techniques & Culture*, 54–55 (2010), 205–219.
- *Record:* Crossref DOI 10.4000/tc.4999 (journal-article, issued 2010, vol. 54–55, pp. 205–219, author Madeleine Akrich); OpenEdition's editorial note: "in Techniques et culture 9, 1987 : 49-64". HAL `halshs-00005830` (1987, file `87T_C.pdf`, Anubis-walled).
- *Read status:* **Full text, publisher version** (OpenEdition reprint read through Chrome at `https://journals.openedition.org/tc/4999`; 44 numbered paragraphs, notes 1–6; the 1987 pagination is not marked on the page, so locators are OpenEdition paragraph numbers, which I give as §n).
- *What it argues:* Technical objects "ont un contenu politique au sens où ils constituent des éléments actifs d'organisation des relations des hommes entre eux et avec leur environnement" (§1); they "attribuent des rôles à certains types d'acteurs - humains et non-humains - en excluent d'autres, autorisent certains modes de relation entre ces différents acteurs" and "deviennent des médiateurs obligés dans toutes les relations que nous entretenons avec le « réel »" (§2). The object "se définit très exactement comme le rapport construit entre" the material device and its uses (§7); "l'objet technique est la mise en forme et la mesure d'un ensemble de relations entre des éléments tout à fait hétérogènes" (§5). The **script**: "Par la définition des caractéristiques de son objet, le concepteur avance un certain nombre d'hypothèses sur les éléments qui composent le monde dans lequel l'objet est destiné à s'insérer. Il propose un « script », un « scénario » qui se veut prédétermination des mises en scène que les utilisateurs sont appelés à imaginer à partir du dispositif technique et des prescriptions (notices, contrats, conseils.) qui l'accompagnent. Mais tant qu'il ne se présente pas d'acteurs pour incarner les rôles prévus par le concepteur (ou en inventer d'autres), son projet reste à l'état de chimère : seule la confrontation réalise ou ir-réalise l'objet technique" (§10). The **method**: "il nous faut sans arrêt effectuer l'aller-retour entre le concepteur et l'utilisateur, entre l'utilisateur-projet du concepteur et l'utilisateur réel, entre le monde inscrit dans l'objet et le monde décrit par son déplacement" (§11). The cases are physical and African. (1) The **photovoltaic lighting kit** (§§12–17), built for an aid agency (AFME) and French photocell makers: direct current with unmarked poles, fluorescent tubes unobtainable locally, sealed batteries, fixed wire lengths, non-standard plugs and no separate switch, each justified by the makers but together "procède par élimination et ne tolère qu'un utilisateur docile à l'exclusion de tout autre acteur" such as local technicians and traders (§15); the designers' client "n'est pas l'utilisateur mais l'AFME" (§14). The de-scription method appears here: "Ce n'est que dans la confrontation entre l'utilisateur réel et l'utilisateur projeté qu'apparaissent les connexions et l'interrupteur qui donnent une mesure du décalage entre eux deux" (§16). (2) The **generator** in Senegalese villages (§§18–22): the chassis and the fuel tank organise a micro-society of investors, owner-associations, member-users, renters and porters; the tank "mesure la proportionnalité entre l'utilisation du groupe et la dépense" and so makes renting possible (§21), where a photovoltaic panel "ne fournit aucune mesure susceptible d'être retraduite en termes socio-économiques" (§20). (3) The **electricity network and meter** in Côte d'Ivoire (§§23–30): the network is laid only over public space, so it makes individual citizens out of villagers and collects local taxes through the bill (§§24–25); "Le compteur matérialise dans la durée le contrat initial passé entre producteur et consommateur : si l'un des deux faillit à ses obligations, le compteur est soit inactif, soit déposé. Alors que chaque compteur a un effet de symétrisation sur la relation producteur-consommateur - il faut « l'accord » des deux pour le faire tourner -, l'ensemble des compteurs constitue un instrument fort de contrôle qui mesure la cohésion de l'édifice socio-technique" (§26); "Le compteur intervient comme arbitre et gestionnaire d'une relation quand il est considéré seul" (§30); the "compteurs russes" can be stopped by tapping and so fail to tell licit from illicit behaviour (§29). (4) The **charge regulator** on photovoltaic installations (§§31–34): three thresholds cut the panel or the load, so "l'utilisateur ne peut être trop gourmand ; et il ne peut espérer « racheter » ses excès de gourmandise par une abstinence prolongée. La sanction d'un comportement hors normes … est immédiate et abrupte" (§31); "La régulation s'avère constituer un système de dressage de l'utilisateur comportant sanction et récompense" (§32); the makers chose non-standard connections because "la trahison possible du dispositif de régulation … pourrait se retourner contre eux en accusateur" (§32). In French Polynesia an installer, tired of evening call-outs, wired a fuse in parallel so users could shunt the regulator until morning: "Le fusible marque la soumission de l'installateur à ses clients" (§33). (5) **Burkina Faso** (§§39–42): the economic service surveyed willingness to pay; the technical service drew the network the law and the terrain allowed, and demand followed the network. Conclusion: objects "préforment les relations entre les différents acteurs qu'ils suscitent et leur donnent ce qu'on pourrait appeler un contenu « moral » ; attribuant rôles et responsabilités, ils constituent en puissance des ressorts d'accusation" (§35); they "naturalisent, les dépolitisent" the orders they produce (§44). Note 1 repeats Winner's Moses story without qualification (see `joerges1999politics`).
- *Bearing:* **The facet's strongest support for form 2, with the limits stated.** The meter is a physical intermediary between two parties with opposed interests, it reads one of them (consumption), it binds both ("il faut « l'accord » des deux pour le faire tourner"; it is removed if either defaults), and Akrich calls it "arbitre et gestionnaire d'une relation". The regulator reads a state (battery voltage) and sanctions the user with a fixed three-threshold rule; its shunt fuse is a physical structural refusal installed by the party the rule had bound. Neither device adapts: the rule is set once, the meter can be tampered with, and the regulator's "trahison" is a fixed rule misfiring. Marks for the meter: distinct intermediary, yes; reads both, no (one side); binds both, yes; holds still, yes. For the kit and the generator the marks are Latour's: one party bound, nothing read, nothing moving. Against form 1's novelty claim: a pre-digital arrangement already "materializes the contract" and "measures" the relation; what it lacks is exactly the adaptive rule.

## (c) Synthesis

### C1. Latour: delegation, prescription, the prescribed user, antiprograms, circumscription

1. **Delegation** is the transfer of a task from a provisional carrier to a more durable one, measured by the two-column ledger and the removal test (`latour1992missing`, pp. 228–229 [154–155]; `latour1988mixing`, p. 299). The transfer "folds" time: once installed, a nonhuman delegate needs maintenance, not discipline (p. 231 [156]). That is the sense in which the physical layer holds still: the designer's work is "concentrated at the time of installation".
2. **Prescription** is what the delegate imposes back on the humans who meet it, "the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms" (p. 232 [157]), a formula Latour credits to Akrich in 1988 (p. 301). Prescriptions presuppose competences and so discriminate (p. 234 [158–159]). The note definition is wider than the body's: "whatever a scene presupposes from its transcribed actors and authors" (n. 8, p. 256), which includes the author, since a traffic light "presupposes that there is someone who has regulated the lights" (`latour1988mixing`, p. 306).
3. **The prescribed user** is the user the scene was built for; the user-in-the-flesh may ignore the scene, "cross on the red", or never arrive (p. 237 [161]). The gap is the designer's exposure, and pre-inscription (driving lessons, the map's "you are here") is the work done upstream to close it (n. 16, p. 257).
4. **Antiprograms** are the moves the mechanism braces against; the seat belt is the answer to "unbuckle it fast", "unlink the sensor", and each answer is a new re-inscription at the drawing board (pp. 226 [152], 247 [168]). "No artifact is idiot-proof" (p. 254 [174]).
5. **Circumscription** is the setup's drawing of its own limits and entry points; the photocopier's labels grade the audience by competence and the secretary's homemade label narrows it further (p. 239 [161–162]; n. 17, p. 257). A disclosure is itself a prescription about who may act.
6. **Parties and binding.** In every case the author is absent and unbound, and the artifact binds the user by force (the belt that straps you, the key that will not come out, the bump that breaks your suspension) or by presupposed skill (the slamming door). Latour never describes an artifact that binds the party who inscribed it. The one place where a device sits between two parties with opposed interests is Akrich's meter (C2), not Latour's door.
7. **Reading.** Latour's artifacts sense three things in passing: the buckle and door switches of the belt (p. 225–226 [151–152]), "electronic eyes" and "electronic passes" on sophisticated doors (p. 236 [160]; 1988, p. 303), and the sensor-fed red light (p. 246 [168]; 1988, p. 305). Each applies a fixed rule to a reading. The sleeping policeman reads nothing and is "there night and day" (`latour1994mediation`, p. 39).

### C2. Akrich: script, de-scription, non-uptake, cases

1. A **script** is the designer's set of hypotheses about the world the object will enter, "une prédétermination des mises en scène" that users are to play, accompanied by prescriptions (manuals, contracts, advice); it stays a chimera until actors take the roles or invent others (`akrich1987comment`, §10). The English chapter's "inscribing this vision of (or prediction about) the world in the technical content of the new object" is confirmed by phrase search (`akrich1992description`), but the body of the English text was not read, and the widely quoted "like a film script" sentence remains unverified.
2. **De-scription** is the analyst's reverse movement from the object to the script (`akrichlatour1992summary`, p. 259; `latour1992missing`, n. 4), and its method is the back-and-forth between projected and real user (§11). Akrich's own rule for when a script becomes visible: only "dans la confrontation entre l'utilisateur réel et l'utilisateur projeté" (§16), that is, at the mismatch.
3. **Non-uptake** has three outcomes in her cases: exclusion (the kit tolerates only a docile user and repels technicians, §15), reciprocal accusation between makers and users with the user "instrumentalisé" in the agency–industry relation (§35), and bricolage that re-inscribes the device (the installer's fuse, §33). Breakdown is "a test of the solidity of the sociotechnical network materialized by a technical object" (`akrich1992description`, n. 12, p. 224).
4. **Cases**: all physical, all African or Polynesian: lighting kit, generator, electricity network and meter, charge regulator, Burkina Faso network sizing. The meter and the regulator are the two that read a state and sanction; the meter is the one that binds two parties (C1 table).
5. What Akrich adds to Latour for Part 4: objects "donnent une mesure de ces relations, établissent des hiérarchies, définissent des normes" (§26); the physical device is where a contract between two parties is kept, counted and enforced, and where a tampered meter or a shunt fuse is the exit.

### C3. Winner and Lessig: architecture as politics and as regulation

1. Winner treats built form as a political founding that freezes: "the original flexibility vanishes for all practical purposes once the initial commitments are made" (`winner1980artifacts`, p. 128). The person's move is the adoption vote (p. 134). His politics are "arrangements of power and authority in human associations" (p. 123), hierarchies of persons, never a mechanism's reading of them.
2. Lessig's "architecture" modality is the physical burden a design imposes (`lessig2006code`, p. 124), fixed once built and "plastic" through law (p. 127). Chapter 7 contains no adaptive physical regulation: the locked car radio (p. 126) and the ignition lock (p. 130) are one rule, one user. The chapter's contribution to Part 4 is the transparency argument: indirection "misdirects responsibility" (p. 133), zoning makes segregation look like "choice" (p. 135), and "people experience these controls as nature" (p. 138).
3. **The Moses bridges are contested and Part 4 must not repeat them.** Joerges shows Caro's version rests on two late interviews (`joerges1999politics`, p. 8), that commercial traffic including buses was barred from every American parkway (pp. 12–13), and that the bridges were "found quite innocent" (p. 18). Akrich 1987 (n. 1), Latour 1988 (p. 302) and Lessig 2006 (p. 128) repeat the story uncorrected; Tromp et al. 2011 (n. 4) is the model of how to cite it (as a thought experiment, with the two 1999 replies). Woolgar and Cooper 1999 was not obtained.
4. Joerges's positive claim is the one to answer: built spaces "represent control rights", and "Only rarely and in the most trivial senses can one show that such constraints are coupled to building form" (p. 20). If he is right, the physical layer is where a ruling is displayed and authorized, not where it becomes a fact; the Berliner key (p. 253) and the meter (§26) are the counter-cases, where form does the binding.

### C4. Affordance from Gibson through Norman to Gaver

1. Gibson's affordance is a relation between animal and environment that "points both ways", exists whether or not perceived, and "does not change as the need of the observer changes" (`gibson1979ecological`, "Origin"). It extends to persons: buyer and seller afford each other reciprocally ("Other persons and animals"). So the concept reaches two actors, but as a direct relation with no third thing between them, and it is invariant by definition.
2. Norman's correction is twofold. In 1999 he says POET meant "perceived affordance", that on screens "the designer primarily can only control perceived affordances", and that most of what designers call affordances are "conventions, and feedback" (`norman1999affordance`). In 2008 he replaces affordances with "signifiers", including accidental and social ones (the empty platform, desire lines), and says that for "social structures, complex, intelligent products, and a heavy dominance of services … new principles are needed" (`norman2008signifiers`).
3. Gaver keeps Gibson's realism ("Affordances per se are independent of perception") and adds the design-relevant cases: false affordances (the pull handle on a locked door), hidden ones, sequential and nested ones, and sound as a carrier of "affordances for collaboration" (`gaver1991technology`, pp. 2–4).
4. For Part 4: affordance describes a static physical layer read by a person; a signifier describes a static trace of other persons read by a person. Neither describes a layer that reads the person. The term the phygital literature would need is the one Norman says is missing.

### C5. Verbeek and Dorrestijn

1. Only Dorrestijn and Verbeek 2013 and Tromp, Hekkert and Verbeek 2011 were read in full; the three Verbeek books and the 2006 article were not obtained this session (see B5).
2. Dorrestijn and Verbeek hold that mediation cannot be avoided, that the nudge's opt-out and persuasive technology's transparency are "paradoxical" as ethical foundations, and that freedom is a practice of appropriation: "Rather than designing possibilities to opt out, it is important to think about multiple ways to opt in" (`dorrestijn2013technology`, "Post-utopian Design"). Tromp et al. sort influence by force and salience into coercive, persuasive, seductive and decisive, and locate the category in the user's experience, not the product (`tromp2011design`, pp. 11–12).
3. Both texts are designer → user; neither reads two parties; both treat the artifact's rule as fixed. Their "decisive design", infrastructure that makes the desired behaviour the only possible one (p. 18), is the physical ruling-as-fact of form 4 with no algorithm behind it.

### Evidence against the thesis

- **A pre-digital two-party intermediary exists in the canon.** Akrich's electricity meter materializes the producer–consumer contract, needs both parties' "accord" to run, reads consumption, and is removed if either defaults (§26). If Part 4 claims the phygital as the first physical layer that binds two parties, this is the counter-example; it supports form 2 and weakens form 1's novelty.
- **Binding by form is contested.** Joerges: the built form "constrains" only rarely and trivially; what binds is authorization (p. 20). This cuts against form 4's "the physical layer is where the ruling becomes a fact".
- **The physical layer already carries other people.** Gibson's reciprocal affordances and Norman's social signifiers mean a static setting already presents the second party's traces to the first. The thesis's "reads both parties" has to be distinguished from "shows each party the other".
- **Structural refusal is questioned on principle.** Dorrestijn and Verbeek argue that the opt-out is not the ethical fundamental and that transparency does not restore freedom; their alternative (appropriation, "opt in") sits closer to the author's "not legibility" ruling than to Part 1's costless way out.
- **Nothing in these sources adapts.** Every artifact applies a fixed rule; the only movement is the designer's re-inscription between models (Latour) or the user's bricolage (Akrich). The "does not hold still" mark has no pre-digital physical case in this facet, which is evidence for form 1 and against form 2 on that mark.
