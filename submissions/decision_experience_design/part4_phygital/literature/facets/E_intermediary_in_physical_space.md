# E: The intermediary in physical space: algorithmic management, service-encounter technology and automated access

*Facet E of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat E of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Form 1 of Part 4's thesis says the phygital is the same algorithmic intermediary reaching into physical space; form 4 says the physical layer is where the intermediary's ruling becomes a fact and where exit is priced. This facet gathers the scholarly evidence on settings where an algorithm's determination is delivered to a body: a picker on a warehouse floor, a driver at a kerb, a courier at a restaurant counter, a guest at a hotel kiosk, a customer at a self-checkout. Five questions organise it. E1: in algorithmic management delivered physically (warehouse pick paths and rates, ride-hail dispatch and pickup, delivery routing, hotel housekeeping assignment), who are the two parties the system reads, what does it commit, and how does the worker meet the ruling physically? E2: how did service research model the move from a customer–employee dyad to a triad with technology (technology infusion, self-service technologies, Service Encounter 2.0, service robots), and does the technology in those models decide anything or only assist? E3: what does research on biometric and automated access (facial-recognition check-in, mobile keys, automated gates) report about refusal, failure and the employee's residual role? E4: are Part 1's three operations (interpreting the system, specifying intent to it, keeping track of its changes) observed in these physical settings, and what do people use in place of a screen? E5: two expected gaps, (a) evidence that a competence, as distinct from disclosure, moderates outcomes in physical service or work settings, and (b) any person-side agent or tool acting for the worker or customer against the system (counter-delegation) in a physical setting. The facet carries no named gate item, but it fails unless at least one full-text source shows a digital ruling becoming a physical fact with both parties bound; section (d) records that test.

### B1. Gate candidates: a digital ruling becoming a physical fact, both parties bound (E1)

`cameron2024making` | Cameron, L. D. (2024). The making of the "good bad" job: How algorithmic management manufactures consent through constant and confined choices. *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 69(2), 458–514.
- *Record:* Crossref 10.1177/00018392241236163; issued 2024-03-14, print 2024-06; no `update-to`. The `lima_pdw` card (full text) and the dissertation library card are leads; I re-read the text.
- *Read status:* Full text, publisher version. `pyphi-experiments/dissertation/research/library/pdfs/cameron2024.txt` (typeset Sage PDF, 57 pages = 458–514; running heads "Cameron / 459" and "514 / Administrative Science Quarterly 69 (2024)" confirm the offset). Read in full by a reader agent under my instructions; every quotation below re-matched by me against the file (row 22).
- *What it argues:* Seven years of ride-hail fieldwork (2016–2023), "participant observation (as both a driver and rider), longitudinal interviews (n = 136), social and print media, and official company materials" (p. 460), in 23 North American cities. The work cycle is a triad by construction: "The work process of a ride-hailing driver is based on a three-way interaction between the driver, the rider, and the app" (p. 468). The ruling arrives on the dashboard: "When the algorithmic management system completed a match, the driver's phone buzzed and displayed the distance to the pickup spot, rider's details (including rating), and demand-based incentive amount (if any). Drivers had up to 15 seconds to accept, and they were sanctioned if they did not accept a certain percentage of rides" (p. 476). After acceptance it governs the body in space: "Once the driver arrived, a timer appeared, dictating how long drivers must wait (60 seconds to 10 minutes based on ride type) before drivers could select 'no show' and receive a new ride" (p. 477); in shared rides, "Always follow the app's instructions. The route is built on efficiency, so the order of who is picked up and dropped off first varies from ride to ride" (p. 477, quoting company instructions). The rider is bound by the same ruling and meets its consequences at the kerb: a driver reports "The app took me to the back [of the pickup location], but she was in the front and it was really cold ... I said, 'Ma'am, I'm just going where the app sent me.' She ran in the back, got in the car, destroyed me on the ratings" (p. 486). On what is read from each side: the driver's location, acceptance rate, ride count and telemetry; the rider's location, rating and (hidden from the driver until a loyalty tier is earned) destination, since "being able to see a rider's destination before accepting a ride" is a privilege for drivers who keep "their acceptance (cancellation) rates high (low)" (p. 489). On what binds: "In short, riders could fire drivers" (p. 477); riders are themselves tracked ("The rider will see your car approaching on their app and your ETA", p. 477) and pay cancellation fees. On exit's price: ordinary refusal is cheap and "all of their counter-measures were allowable within the system (i.e., programmed leniency)" (p. 488); geo-spoofing, the one tactic that falsifies the signal the system reads, meets "immediate and permanent deactivation" (p. 492). On change: "Over the seven years of this study, the algorithmic management system frequently changed, and for the most part, workers were able to adjust" (p. 488). The construct is "choice-based consent" through "constant yet confined choices" (p. 478), and Cameron is explicit about standing withheld: workers "cannot organize ..., may not negotiate personally with managers ..., lack access to official voice channels ..., have minimal recourse against customer malfeasance ..., and have limited knowledge of the actual code" (p. 494). Counter-evidence she herself supplies: "workers' enjoyment is not faked" (p. 498); "I don't have a boss" was the commonest answer to what drivers liked (p. 468).
- *Bearing:* **Supports form 1 and form 4 at full strength.** The arrangement carries all four marks: a distinct intermediary (the app, which neither party controls), reading both parties (driver GPS and rates; rider location, rating and destination), binding both (the driver to the wait timer and route, the rider to the pickup point, the fare and the fee), and not holding still (seven years of rule changes that programmed deviance out). The kerb scene on p. 486 is the clearest single instance in this facet of a ruling becoming a physical fact for two bodies at once. One tension for Part 4: the "programmed leniency" finding means the physical layer is where exit is priced *gradually*, with a cliff only at signal falsification; Part 1's "structural refusal" principle would read Cameron's leniency as a design choice, not a right.

`mohlmann2021algorithmic` | Möhlmann, M., Zalmanson, L., Henfridsson, O., & Gregory, R. W. (2021). Algorithmic management of work on online labor platforms: When matching meets control. *MIS Quarterly*, 45(4), 1999–2022.
- *Record:* Crossref 10.25300/misq/2021/15333; no `update-to`. Cards in `lima_pdw` (full text) and `hospitality_phygital` (abstract) are leads.
- *Read status:* Full text, publisher version. `.../library/pdfs/mohlmann2021.txt` (25 PDF pages; pages 1–24 = printed 1999–2022, footers confirm; page 25 is an EBSCO notice). Read in full by a reader agent; quotations re-matched.
- *What it argues:* Uber drivers in New York and London, 2015–2019 (36 observed rides, 34 driver interviews, 1,092 UberPeople.net posts, 8 Uber staff interviews; Table 1, p. 2003). "We define algorithmic management as the large-scale collection and use of data on a platform to develop and improve learning algorithms that carry out coordination and control functions traditionally performed by managers" (p. 2001), split into *algorithmic matching* and *algorithmic control* (pp. 2005–2006). The ruling reaches the car as a rule with a physical penalty: an engineer explains, "if you don't accept three trips in a row, the app will just switch off for 15 minutes or whatever" (p. 2007); a driver on UberPOOL: "once you press the navigation, it comes directly as their own system, you cannot use any other... I have no choice; you're following it even if you get stuck in the traffic" (p. 2009). The rider is bound by upfront pricing, "whatever the route is that the driver takes, it should still be your price" (p. 2008), and can be removed by the same hand: "we would log off the rider or the driver if they don't except [sic] dispatches or they keep canceling on riders" (p. 2007). On decoding: driver communities "are not facilitated by Uber, but are created and maintained by the drivers themselves, beyond Uber's control" (p. 2016). The authors stress legal limits on control (an executive: "we can't tell them what to do", p. 2005) and that switching between platforms is "(now) generally tolerated" (p. 2013).
- *Bearing:* **Supports form 1 and form 4.** All four marks: the matching–control engine; reads driver (GPS, acceptance, movement after acceptance) and rider (request, rating, cancellation); binds both (locked POOL route and 15-minute shut-off for the driver; upfront price and log-off for the rider); and "constantly updated the system in order to make it more difficult for drivers to game" (p. 2013). The interplay model (p. 2015) is a usable formal statement of the triad for Part 4. Complication: the paper locates the ruling's physical delivery in a *phone*, which is a screen; the physical facts (the car stuck in traffic, the frozen POOL meter) follow from it rather than replacing it.

`cheon2025fulfillment` | Cheon, E., & Erickson, I. (2025). Fulfillment of the work games: Warehouse workers' experiences with algorithmic management. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 9(7), article CSCW228, 1–30.
- *Record:* Crossref 10.1145/3757409; issued 2025-10-16; CC BY 4.0; no `update-to`. The `org_frontier` card is a lead (it names only "Cheon and others"; Crossref gives both authors).
- *Read status:* Full text, author preprint (arXiv 2508.09438v1, which carries the ACM typeset layout and the article's own pagination CSCW228:1–30; I read it in full myself). `scratchpad/E/cheon2025.txt`. Page locators are the article's "CSCW228:n" folios.
- *What it argues:* Two years of ethnography with Amazon fulfillment-centre workers (three online communities; 16 interviews across 16 FCs in 12 US states; CSCW228:7–8). The "labor-tracking system" is an assemblage: "individual barcodes; handheld scanners that track workers' activities, speeds, and locations; workstation computers ...; monitors at workstations constantly displaying individual workers' performance; cameras ...; the 'A-to-Z' application ...; and clock-in/clock-out stations" (CSCW228:9–10). The ruling is a number on a screen and a path on the floor: "the labor tracking itself is it can tell whatever you're doing at any given moment. It can tell what item you've scanned, what shelf you've scanned, how long you're going between scanning items. So it calculates a rate" (P8, CSCW228:10); "if more than 2 hours of TOT are recorded, this error would bring on an 'instant termination'" (CSCW228:14). Workers describe the physical work as already virtual: "Even all those people in direct roles who are just packing packages, they are doing something in the system virtually" (P1, CSCW228:12). The second party is the customer's order, present only as the pick path and the volume; the paper's distinctive finding is the human manager as "context prober" who corrects the data: "when the tracking system detects more than 3 to 5 minutes of work inactivity (i.e., Time off Tasks (TOT)), the worker has to explain to their manager exactly what happened" (CSCW228:13), and "Managers end up being the only source of unpredictability for workers" (CSCW228:19). Resistance is "tricks": cherry-picking a better path by logging out ("if it put you on a pick path that was just too spread out ... people would log out of the scanner entirely", CSCW228:16), "virtual picking" with printed barcodes, scan-to-scan break stretching. The authors contrast FC workers with journalists and judges: "our workers, who lack access to the internal algorithmic systems they must utilize, attempt to deceive the system" (CSCW228:24). Little collective action was found (CSCW228:21).
- *Bearing:* **Supports form 1 and form 4, with a complication for the second mark.** The intermediary is distinct, adaptive (rates rise to 420 units/hour at peak, CSCW228:9) and binding on the worker's body (the path, the TOT clock, termination). But the customer is read only as aggregate demand; no named second party stands at the other end of the ruling, and a human manager sits between the number and the sanction. For Part 4 this is the warehouse version of form 4 (the floor is where the number becomes a firing) with the fourth mark present and the second mark weak. The "tricks" are the E4 evidence that interpreting and specifying happen through the scanner, the badge and the body rather than a screen of one's own.

- *Read status:* Full text, author preprint (the OSF deposit `osf.io/rckdh/`, 16 pp, whose cover line reads "New Media & Society, OnlineFirst: 1–17"; no printed pagination, so I locate by section). `scratchpad/E/delfanti2021.txt`. ## (c) Synthesis

### E1. Algorithmic management delivered physically: who is read, what is committed, how the body meets it

1. In ride-hail the ruling is a timed, blind match that two bodies then have to honour in one place. Cameron (2024, p. 476) and Rosenblat & Stark (2016, p. 3762) both record the 15-second window and the hidden destination; Cameron adds the wait timer at the kerb (p. 477) and the locked pickup order in shared rides (p. 477); Möhlmann et al. (2021, p. 2009) add the locked POOL route and the 15-minute shut-off for three refusals (p. 2007). The system reads the driver's GPS, acceptance and cancellation rates and telemetry, and the rider's location, rating and destination. It commits a match, a fare, a route and a wait. The rider is bound as well as read: to the pickup point the app chose (Cameron p. 486), to an upfront price whatever route is driven (Möhlmann p. 2008), to a cancellation fee, and to being logged off for repeated cancelling (Möhlmann p. 2007). Lee et al. (2015, p. 1605) supply the two-sided rating: a low-rated passenger is refused by drivers.
2. In food delivery the ruling is a sound and a counter. Griesbach et al. (2019, p. 11) describe the four-minute ping with no decline button; Veen et al. (2020, p. 396) the ten-second accept and the address released only after pickup (p. 398). The system reads courier GPS, accept/cancel and speed, restaurant readiness and ratings, and the customer's order, address, tip and rating (Veen p. 396; Griesbach pp. 6–7). It commits a single offer and a piece rate. The customer is read and binds the match only in one direction: a one- or two-star rating "permanently" blocks that courier from that customer (Griesbach p. 7), while the courier cannot rate the customer (p. 7). Veen et al. found Deliveroo used no customer rating at all (p. 397).
3. In the warehouse the ruling is a path and a rate, and the customer is present only as an order. Delfanti (2021, "Pick") traces the click to the scanner screen and the minute-scale task timer; Cheon & Erickson (2025, CSCW228:10, 14) give the scan-to-scan rate and the two-hour TOT termination; Gutelius & Theodore (2019, pp. 34, 54, 58) give the follow-me robot, the tethered cart and the hand-guiding wristband. No named second party meets the ruling. Instead a human manager sits between the number and the sanction: the "context prober" who clears or confirms a TOT (Cheon CSCW228:13, 19), the lead sent to tell a fast worker to speed up (Delfanti, "Management"), the manager alerted by a low scanner score (Kellogg et al. 2020, p. 378).
4. In hotel housekeeping the ruling is a room list on a handheld and a credit count; the guest enters as a declined-service code and as "green choice" rooms that shift labour to checkout (Spektor et al. 2023, pp. 626, 629). Refusal is unavailable in practice (p. 630). Jianu et al. (2025, abstract) describe managers "translating" the algorithm's decisions.
5. How the body meets the ruling: by driving to a pin, waiting out a timer, walking a path at a pace, lifting a cart to another floor, standing still for a camera. The common element across sectors is that the ruling arrives with a clock (15 seconds, 4 minutes, a minute per pick, 3–5 minutes of TOT) and that the clock is enforced on the body before any screen is consulted.

### E2. Service research's move from dyad to triad: does the technology decide?

1. The self-service line does not decide. Meuter et al. (2000, p. 50) define the SST as an interface that lets the customer "produce a service independent of direct service employee involvement"; the machine executes, and when it fails the customer goes back to a person (p. 59). The design-failure incidents (p. 56) show the SST enforcing rules it does not disclose, but the rules are fixed. This is form 3: choice architecture with a keypad.
2. The service-robot line gives the technology a deciding role but keeps the dyad. Wirtz et al. (2018, p. 909) define a robot as "autonomous and adaptable", connected to CRM and biometrics, able to personalise "on scale"; the paper's configurations are robot, human, or team (Figs. 3–6), and "triad" does not appear. The robot is the firm's agent, not an intermediary reading two parties. Its stated limit, that it "can usually not explain afterward why this solution is optimal" (p. 911), is Part 1's opacity mark; its stated inferiority, that a human may be seen "bending company rules to accommodate a customer" (p. 917), is the physical employee pricing exit locally.
3. The explicit triads (Larivière et al. 2017; van Doorn et al. 2017; van Doorn et al. 2023) could not be read. At abstract level the CAW framework's technology "supports" and "augments"; it does not commit. Whether any service-research triad models a technology that binds both parties is not shown by this facet.
4. Tussyadiah (2020, ms pp. 12–14) finds that the deciding systems in tourism sit upstream (marketing analytics, border risk scoring) while frontline robots "add on" to self-service. The physical frontline is where the least deciding happens in her survey.

### E3. Biometric and automated access: refusal, failure, the residual employee

1. The gate reads one party and binds that party's body. Greiffenhagen et al. (2023) record 412 attempts; 22% succeed on the first instruction cycle (PDF p. 11); failure resets the process (PDF p. 10); refusal is the whole check-in. The guest does preparatory and remedial work (repositioning, removing glasses, sending a roommate out of frame) without diagnostic feedback (PDF pp. 12–17, 23). Xu & Greiffenhagen (2024, abstract) extend this to 674 cases.
2. The employee's residual role is advisory and fallback. Staff intervened in about 70 of 361 recognitions, saying "take off your glasses", "step backward a bit", "try the other machine" (Greiffenhagen PDF pp. 11 n. 3, 17); unreadable ID cards went to manual check-in (PDF p. 10). Brady & Lin (2023, abstract) call the same work "regulatory ... the work that cannot be automated", for "passengers that self-service interfaces exclude". Meuter et al. (2000, p. 59) had already found that SST failure returns the customer to a person.
3. Refusal's price. Wilson-Nash et al. (2026, abstract) name the condition where the gate is the only entry "digital technology captivity": adapt under strain or abandon the service. Kim et al. (2026, abstract) model "forced use" as a driver of stress and resistance. The adoption studies (Xu et al. 2021; Boo & Chua 2022; Morosan & DeFranco 2016) ask whether guests accept the gate, not what the gate does when it says no.
4. Mobile keys and automated airport gates remain unread: Morosan & DeFranco's abstract is about apps, not doors; Oostveen (2014) was not obtained.

### E4. Interpreting, specifying intent, keeping track in physical settings

1. Interpreting happens without a screen of one's own and often yields false theories. Drivers infer from three nearer cars that "the system didn't coordinate the GPS correctly" (Lee 2015, p. 1606); shoppers infer "I trained the computer what I'll do" (Griesbach 2019, p. 6); guests at the kiosk "do not know what the problem is" and change how long they close their eyes (Greiffenhagen 2023, PDF pp. 17–20); couriers "develop false theories about the algorithms' mechanisms due to their opacity, which effectively disciplines their actions" (Heiland 2023, abstract). Delfanti's workers "can only guess and test in the wild" ("Introduction"). The instruments are forums (Rosenblat & Stark p. 3759; Möhlmann p. 2016; Cheon CSCW228:20), WhatsApp groups, a second phone running the rider app (Cameron pp. 479, 481), and the body itself.
2. Specifying intent is done with the body's position and the device's state. Drivers open the app two minutes before reaching a preferred spot, position the car "in non-approved spaces to avoid the waiting timer", set a destination filter that "doesn't work" (Cameron pp. 481, 490; Zhang 2023, p. 13); couriers tap "arrived at restaurant" from the corner (Perrig 2023, p. 725); pickers log out so the path is reassigned (Cheon CSCW228:16); guests step back from the kiosk and remove glasses before being asked (Greiffenhagen PDF pp. 12–14). The platform-side "Accept All Rides" button (Cameron p. 480) is specifying intent by abdication.
3. Keeping track of changes is forced and costly. New terms arrive as a login wall (Rosenblat & Stark p. 3764); pay structures "pivot" without notice (Griesbach p. 8); Uber "constantly updated the system in order to make it more difficult for drivers to game" (Möhlmann p. 2013); Cameron records seven years of changes that drivers "were able to adjust" to (p. 488); pickers watch a UPH number "constantly fluctuating" on the monitor (Cheon CSCW228:15). Rahman's five components (criteria, execution, magnitude, impact, who influences) name what has to be tracked (OnlineFirst p. 32).
4. All three operations are observed in every physical setting read at full text. What stands in for the screen is the clock, the body's position, the device's on/off state, the forum, a second phone, a dash cam, and a paper backup list (Spektor p. 624). Hong et al. (2026, abstract) have begun to name these as "algorithmic skills", including "awareness of algorithmic evolution".

### E5a. Competence as a moderator, distinct from disclosure

1. I found no study, in Consensus, OpenAlex or the full texts read, in which a measured competence predicts a decision outcome in a physical service or work setting over and above general ability or disclosure. What exists: trait moderators of satisfaction and attitude (technology readiness in Wang et al. 2017, abstract; self-efficacy and traits in Dabholkar & Bagozzi 2002 and Zhu et al. 2007, not obtained); perceived competence as a self-determination need predicting motivation among customers who struggle with SSTs (Hong & Ahn 2023, abstract); self-efficacy reducing role ambiguity among employees (Christ-Brendemühl & Schaarschmidt 2019, abstract); and qualitative typologies of "algorithmic skills" (Hong et al. 2026, abstract) and "algorithmic competencies" (Jarrahi & Sutherland 2019, cited in Cheon CSCW228:6, not opened).
2. The full texts supply observations that point the right way without measuring anything. Lee et al. (2015, p. 1609) found that drivers with detailed knowledge of the assignment algorithm "could create workarounds ... whereas people only with a general understanding of proximity-based assignment could not", and framed this as a case for transparency. Pidoux et al. (2024, PDF p. 11) found that drivers "already knew a lot about data when defined as 'working time'" and needed "technical capacity", not literacy, and that "transparency does not ensure accountability". Spektor et al. (2023, p. 626) note that their participants were "some of the most competent users of the technology" and that newer GRAs learned "tips and tricks of veteran peers" (p. 634). Zhang et al. (2023, p. 15) warn that their sample "may be more data literate ... than a standard driver". Greiffenhagen et al. (2023, PDF p. 23) show outcomes turning on "background knowledge about what interactional work to perform". Each is a difference in knowledge or skill that changes what the person could do; none is isolated from disclosure or tested against an outcome.
3. The gap is therefore open as stated. The numeracy analogy (a competence predicting decision quality over and above general ability) has no physical-setting counterpart in the located literature.

### E5b. Counter-delegation: a person-side agent or tool acting against the system in a physical setting

1. I found no deployed tool that acts for a worker or customer against the system's ruling at the point of delivery (the ping, the pick, the kiosk) in any full text read. What exists acts on the record after the fact: the Geneva digipower.academy calculation of unpaid time between rides, now used in court claims (Pidoux et al. 2024, PDF pp. 9–10); subject access requests as "sousveillance" (Perrig 2023, p. 727); FairFare's crowdsourced take-rate estimate for Colorado legislators (Calacci et al. 2026, abstract); the dash cam that reversed a fare cut (Rosenblat & Stark 2016, p. 3765); worker-built scripts and Turkopticon, all online (Kellogg et al. 2020, pp. 391–392).
2. The nearest physical-setting counter-agents are human: the roommate who tells the guest to take off her glasses (Greiffenhagen PDF p. 14); the hotel worker who says "try the other machine" (PDF p. 17); the friend who stows an item at your station to stop the TOT clock (Cheon CSCW228:17); the forum that tells a driver which passengers to refuse (Lee p. 1607). Devices used against the system are the second phone and the rider app (Cameron pp. 479, 487), GPS spoofers (Cameron p. 491; Veen p. 400; Cheon CSCW228:17) and printed barcodes (Cheon CSCW228:16), and these are met with deactivation or dismissal when detected.
3. One designed artefact would constrain the ruling rather than document it: Spektor et al.'s greyed-out "assign room" button when a supervisor exceeds the union's contractual maximum (2023, Fig. 6, p. 633). It exists in Figma. The one *configured* counter-rule found was the union-negotiated engineered labour standard (Gutelius & Theodore 2019, p. 28), which is bargaining, not an agent.
4. The structural limit on E5b in the full texts is stated by Do et al. (2024, p. 1:12): "conducting sousveillance is entirely impossible or impractical if it does not strictly follow gig platform policies on data collection", and by Cameron (p. 492): tactics that falsify the signal the system reads bring "immediate and permanent deactivation".

### Evidence against the thesis

1. The second party is often not bound. In the warehouse the customer is an order (Cheon; Delfanti; Gutelius & Theodore); at the kiosk the hotel is not read at all (Greiffenhagen). In food delivery the customer rates and is not rated (Griesbach p. 7; Veen p. 397). Only ride-hail shows two bodies bound by one ruling at one place.
2. A human hand prices exit in most physical settings. The warehouse manager clears or confirms the TOT (Cheon CSCW228:13), the lead delivers the feedback session (Delfanti), delivery thresholds are "adjusted on a local basis" by managers (Veen p. 399), shopper support "badger[s]" workers (Griesbach p. 11), Kellogg et al. insist "human curation remains essential" (pp. 388–389), and Veen et al. argue control is "more than algorithmic management" (p. 388). Form 1's distinct intermediary is, on the floor, an algorithm plus a person.
3. The intermediary does not need physical space. Rahman's platform carries all four marks with no kerb and no clock (2021), and Gandini says the platform is the point of production "irrespective of 'where' exactly the work is done" (PDF p. 12). Form 4 must therefore claim something narrower than "the physical layer is where the ruling becomes a fact": that the physical layer is where it becomes a fact *during the task* (Wood et al. 2019, p. 64) and *for a second body*.
4. The physical frontline is where the least deciding happens in service research's own survey (Tussyadiah 2020, ms pp. 12–14), and the SST line is form 3 (Meuter 2000). The phygital that researchers describe is more often a fixed interface than an adaptive ruling.
5. Leniency is designed in. Cameron's "programmed leniency" (p. 488) and Veen's adjustable thresholds mean exit is priced gradually; Part 1's "structural refusal" would have to be distinguished from a tolerance the platform grants and can withdraw.
6. Workers like it. "I don't have a boss" (Cameron p. 468; Möhlmann p. 2009; Griesbach p. 12) is the modal answer, and Cameron's consent is "not faked" (p. 498).

### Gaps

1. The service-research triads (Larivière 2017; van Doorn 2017, 2023; Bitner 2000) were not read; whether any models a binding technology is unknown.
2. No full text on mobile keys, automated airport gates (Oostveen 2014) or hotel housekeeping beyond Spektor was obtained; Jianu 2025 and Spektor's CSCW review are abstract only.
3. E5a: no measured-competence moderator in a physical setting (logged in rows 5, 13).
4. E5b: no deployed point-of-delivery counter-agent (logged in rows 5, 12); the Calacci & Pentland 2022 Shipt paper could not be resolved.
5. Delfanti's Chapter 2 ("Work hard"), where the scanner and the rate are treated, was not obtained.
6. Nothing on retail kiosks or electronic shelf labels was read here; facets B and G own those.
