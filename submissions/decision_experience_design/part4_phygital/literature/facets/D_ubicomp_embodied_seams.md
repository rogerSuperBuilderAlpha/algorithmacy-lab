# D: Ubiquitous computing, embodied interaction and the seams

*Facet D of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 4 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-04 (seat D of an eight-seat run, local session, model Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Part 1 tells a history in which the interface "continually moves outward from the physical hardware" (Grudin 1990) until the unit of design becomes the decision, and it cites seamful design (Chalmers & Galani 2004) without saying that the idea comes from ubiquitous computing. Phygital marketing now praises "seamless" journeys. This facet establishes what human–computer interaction learned when computing moved into rooms, objects and bodies, and whether "phygital seamlessness" repeats a position that field argued over twenty years ago. Five questions:

- **D1.** Weiser: what was the ubiquitous-computing ideal (invisibility, calm), and what exactly did Weiser say about seams? Is "beautiful seams" his phrase, and where is it documented?
- **D2.** Chalmers: what is a seam, what is seamful design, and what were the cases? Are Chalmers's seams (a) technical limits made visible so users can exploit them, (b) disclosures of how the system works, or (c) something else? Does the argument transfer when the thing with seams is a decision made through an intermediary?
- **D3.** Tangible and embodied interaction (Ishii & Ullmer; Hornecker & Buur; Dourish): how did HCI theorise computing's move into physical objects and settings, and does that work handle more than one user facing one system?
- **D4.** Suchman: plans versus situated action at the photocopier; what the machine can and cannot "read" of the person's situation.
- **D5.** Critiques of the seamless ideal (Bell & Dourish 2007; Rogers 2006; Greenfield 2006), and whether a human-factors or HCI literature uses the word "phygital".

### D1. Weiser and the ubiquitous-computing ideal (gate items 2 and 3)

`weiser1991computer` | Weiser, M. (1991). The computer for the 21st century. *Scientific American*, 265(3), 94–104.
- *Record:* Crossref 10.1038/scientificamerican0991-94 (journal-article, 1991-09, vol. 265 no. 3, pp. 94–104). No correction or retraction field.
- *Read status:* Full text, author preprint. The pre-publication draft headed "Scientific American Ubicomp Paper after Sci Am editing — one more final edit from me to go", `https://www.ics.uci.edu/~corps/phaseii/Weiser-Computer21stCentury-SciAm.pdf` (9 pp., unpaginated). I read the whole draft. The magazine text was not compared, so wording may differ.
- *What it argues:* "The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it" (para. 1). The model is writing: "The constant background presence of these products of 'literacy technology' does not require active attention, but the information to be conveyed is ready for use at a glance" (para. 2). Disappearance "is a fundamental consequence not of technology, but of human psychology. Whenever people learn something sufficiently well, they cease to be aware of it" (para. 5). The word "seamlessly" occurs once, as the ideal: "The idea of integrating computers seamlessly into the world at large runs counter to a number of present-day trends" (para. 6). "Beautiful seams" and "seamful" do not occur. Location and scale are the two design issues; active badges let the environment read people: "doors open only to the right badge wearer, rooms greet people by name, telephone calls can be automatically forwarded… appointment diaries write themselves" (para. 20). The retail anticipation is explicit: "'Who made that dress? Are there any more in the store? What was the name of the designer of that suit I liked last week?' The computing environment knows the suit you looked at for a long time last week because it knows both of your locations" (para. 70). The privacy paragraph: "hundreds of computers in every room, all capable of sensing people near them and linked by high-speed networks, have the potential to make totalitarianism up to now seem like sheerest anarchy" (para. 63); Weiser's remedy is cryptography and Jim Morris's rule, "build computer systems to have the same privacy safeguards as the real world, but no more" (para. 66). Sal's day is one worker in one environment; the only other party is a colleague on a shared board.
- *Bearing:* Supports form 1 as a 1991 forecast: the environment that reads a shopper's dwell time and location is the phygital intermediary described before the word existed. Marks carried by Weiser's own scenario: it reads one party (the badge wearer); it does not bind anyone; it does not hold still (it "adapts its behavior" to the room). Against the thesis: the paper's ideal is literacy, a channel one stops noticing, and that ideal assumes a stable technology; nothing in it is a two-party system with an objective.

### D2. Chalmers and seamful design (gate item 3)

`maccoll2002seamful` | MacColl, I., Chalmers, M., Rogers, Y., & Smith, H. (2002). Seamful ubiquity: Beyond seamless integration. *Proceedings of the UbiComp 2002 Workshop on Models and Concepts for Ubiquitous Computing*, Göteborg. Workshop paper, 4 pp.
- *Record:* No DOI. Semantic Scholar record (CorpusId 17362652, year 2002, 27 citations). The Eurowearable paper's ref. 10 and the 2003 workshop paper's ref. 5 cite it with this venue.
- *Read status:* Full text, author-hosted PDF, `https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~matthew/papers/ubicomp2002workshop.pdf` (4 pp., unpaginated; located by section).
- *What it argues:* The first seamful-design paper in the Equator line. It quotes Weiser's "A good tool is an invisible tool" (attributed to Weiser 1994 "The world is not a desktop") and then documents how the field translated invisibility into seamlessness, quoting IEEE Pervasive Computing ("combine these technologies into a seamless whole"), IBM and the Disappearing Computer initiative (sect. "Introduction"). "Weiser describes seamlessness as a misleading concept. In his invited talks to UIST94 [weiser94b] and USENIX95 [weiser95] he suggests that making things seamless amounts to making everything the same, and he advocates seamful systems (with beautiful seams) as a goal" (sect. "Introduction"; the reference list links "slides" for both talks). The mobile-phone example: cell handover is seamless, yet "mobile phones can be set to display the current cell… users can choose whether it is presented and, if so, what use to make of it (for example, seeking a stronger signal by moving to a physical location…)". The ultrasonic positioning case: "50% accuracy of 0.52m, and 95% accuracy of 1.83m"; four presentation policies, "pessimistic: only show information that is known to be correct; optimistic: show everything as if it were correct; cautious: explicitly present uncertainty; opportunistic: exploit uncertainty" (sect. "Uncertainty"). "Note that we do not claim that seamlessness is bad, rather that it is one end of a continuum". Design for appropriation: systems "overt, robust, flexible, simple and manipulable. By overt, we mean the underlying mechanisms of such systems are made visible, as a precondition for the other requirements" (sect. "Appropriation"), with the caveat that for children's educational systems "we may choose (or be required) to restrict or constrain the opportunities for appropriation."
- *Bearing:* Answers the author's D2 question at its origin: a seam here is a physical limit of sensing or coverage, presented honestly (a) rather than an account of system logic (b); the "overt mechanisms" clause in the appropriation section is the first step toward (b). Marks: a co-visiting system with three peers and no intermediary; it reads one party (the on-site visitor's position) and binds no one.

Opacity does the work: "it is precisely the complexity and opacity of the computer's inner workings that invites description in intentional terms… the computer generally is a 'black box' for most users… To refer to the behavior of the machine, then, one must speak of 'its' functionality. And once reified as an entity, the inclination to ascribe actions to the entity rather than to the parts is irresistable" (p. 13). The chapter already weighs disclosure as a design response: Fitter (1979) is quoted, "it becomes the responsibility of the systems designer to provide a language structure which will make apparent to the user the procedures on which it is based and will not lead him to expect from the computer unrealistic powers of inference" (p. 12), and Suchman adds, "In view of the tendency to ascribe full intelligence on the basis of partial evidence, one can argue that designers would do best to make available to the user just how the system is not like a participant in interaction" (p. 12). Weizenbaum on ELIZA: "A switch of objectives from the concealment to the revelation of misunderstanding is seen as a precondition to making an ELIZA-like program the basis for an effective natural language man-machine communication system" (quoted p. 19). Chapter 3 states the planning model, "plans are prerequisite to and prescribe action" (p. 21), and its failure: "plans are a constituent of practical action, but they are constituent as an artifact of our reasoning about action, not as the generative mechanism of action" (p. 28); the Garfinkel assignment shows that enumerating shared background "was not to describe some existing content, but to generate it" (p. 32). Chapter 4 gives the five propositions of situated action (pp. 35–36) and the map analogy: "The mapmaker assumes that in using the map to navigate the terrain, the traveler will have recourse not only to the map, but to the terrain itself… plans and goals do not provide the solution to the question of what determines rational action, they simply re-state the problem" (p. 46). It also quotes Dreyfus on the blind man's cane, "equipment that is ready-to-hand is invisible just when it is most genuinely appropriated" (p. 37), the same Heideggerian source as Weiser's invisibility. Chapter 7 defines the two situations: "the situation of the 'expert help system,' in contrast, comprises a plan for the use of the machine written by the designer and implemented as the program that determines the machine's behavior, and sensors that register changes to the machine's state, including some changes produced by the user's actions. The design plan defines what constitutes 'intelligible action' by the user insofar as the machine is concerned" (p. 77). Two design propositions, "A relevant response anticipates the other's actions" and "A relevant response is occasioned by the other's actions"; the second "is constrained by… limitations on the system's access to the actions taken, its ability to draw relevant inferences about their significance, and its ability to construct a relevant response" (p. 78). The strategy is "to provide the effect of B, through the use of A", and "the result of that strategy is an insensitivity to actual circumstances that is both the system's central resource, and its fundamental problem" (p. 78). What the machine cannot read: "the system can be engineered to provide the appropriate next instruction in spite of the fact that it does not actually have access to the history of the user's actions, or even to the presence or absence now of a document on the glass" (p. 86); a faulted page order is "a fault not available to the system, which has no access to the actual markings on the page" (p. 86); the users' situation "is available only through a history of which the system has no record, or through their reports and assertions about their situation, to which the system has no access" (p. 87); "the system's insensitivity to particulars of this user's situation is the limiting factor on its ability to assess the significance of her actions" (p. 120). Summary: "one way to characterize machines is by the severe constraints on their access to the evidential resources on which human communication of intent routinely relies" (p. 121); "breaches in understanding that for face-to-face interaction would be trivial in terms of detection and repair, become 'fatal' for human-machine communication" (p. 121). Conclusion: "the machine is mapping a restricted set of effects, left by the user's actions, onto a prescribed template of possible meanings. As a consequence of the asymmetrical access of user and machine to the situation of action, the ordinary collaborative resources of human interaction are unavailable. The real project of the designer of an interactive artifact, accordingly, is to engineer alternatives to interaction's situated properties" (p. 124); designers "substitute a description of the situation of action, for access to the actual situation. The user is left to contend with the disparity between the hypothetical sequence of events on which the design is based, and the action's actual course" (p. 124).
- *Bearing:* The deepest statement of what the machine reads: a template of detectable states standing in for the person's situation. It supports form 4 directly: the physical copier is where the plan meets the actual document, and the breakdowns ("false alarm", "garden path", p. 121–122) occur at the glass, where the ruling becomes a fact the user must repair. It supports form 1 by inversion: a phygital intermediary with badges and cameras widens the "restricted set of effects" but does not change their status as a template. Against the thesis in two ways. First, the arrangement carries one mark at most (it reads one party through a thin trace, binds nothing, holds still). Second, on the author's ruling: Suchman entertains disclosure ("make available to the user just how the system is not like a participant") in chapter 2 and then does not make it her remedy; her conclusion is that the designer must engineer alternatives to repair, which is a stronger position than "show how it works".

## (c) Synthesis

### D1. Weiser's ideal and the seams

1. Weiser's ideal was disappearance through learning, with writing as the model: "only when things disappear in this way are we freed to use them without thinking" (`weiser1991computer`, para. 5). It became "calm": technology that moves between the periphery and the centre of attention while "the individual, not the environment, must be in charge" (`weiserbrown1997calm`, sect. "Inner Office Windows").
2. Seamlessness was never Weiser's ideal. The 1991 article uses "seamlessly" once (para. 6) and nothing more; by November 1994 he listed "seamlessness" among the "deep problems" and devoted a slide to "Two misleading concepts": "making everything the same is easy; hard is letting everything be itself, with other things; goal: seamful systems, with beautiful seams" (`weiser1994invisible`, slides 2, 19; repeated word for word in `weiser1995ubiquitous`, slide 20, with "Unix is Ideal! Not seamless. No interface" on slide 21).
3. The phrase "beautiful seams" is therefore Weiser's and is documented in two primary slide decks he wrote (24 November 1994 and 17 January 1995), archived from his own ubiq.com pages. The dial-response card's verdict "unverifiable as a standalone citable source" is superseded; Part 4 can cite the talks directly with the Wayback URLs, and need not route the phrase through Chalmers or Inman and Ribes. What remains unverifiable is anything beyond the slide text (the spoken argument, the paint-tool example), which Chalmers marks "personal communication" (`chalmers2003wearable`, sect. 1).
4. Weiser's seams were infrastructural and were to be fed back to the person: bandwidth feedback, a "control panel for user monitoring of bandwidth", an "MPG meter", buttons coloured "according to power-cost of pressing it" (`weiser1994invisible`, slides 29, 31, 33). Seamfulness and invisibility were, for him, compatible: "literally visible, effectively invisible" (slide 9).
5. Weiser built the programme on Suchman: "anthropological studies of work life [Suchman 1985, Lave 1991] teach us that people primarily work in a world of shared situations and unexamined technological skills" (`weiser1993issues`, sect. 1), and he rejected the computer "idealized as an assistant" (`weiser1994invisible`, slide 17; `weiser1993issues`, para. 6). An algorithmic intermediary that acts for a party is the model he argued against.

### D2. Chalmers's seams, and whether they transfer

6. A seam in Chalmers is a physical limit of the system showing through in use: "uncertainty in sensing, limited coverage of communications infrastructure, and the transformations needed to share data between heterogeneous tools and media" (`chalmers2003wearable`, abstract); the measured cases are ultrasonic error of 0.52 m at 50% and 1.83 m at 95% (`maccoll2002seamful`, sect. "Uncertainty") and GPS error of 4–106 m, mean 12.4 m, in Sheffield streets (`chalmers2003wearable`, sect. "Uncertainty and heterogeneity").
7. Seamful design is "deliberately revealing seams to users, and taking advantage of features usually considered as negative or problematic" (`chalmers2003infrastructure`, abstract), with four presentation policies (pessimistic, optimistic, cautious, opportunistic) and the signal-strength map as the worked example (`maccoll2002seamful`; `chalmers2003wearable`, sect. "Seamful design").
8. Answer to the author's question: Chalmers's seams are (a), technical limits made visible so users can exploit them. Disclosure of how the system works (b) enters only in the "design for appropriation" sections, as a means to let users "understand or even change the tool" and eventually to expose "systems' internal models of user activity" (`chalmers2003infrastructure`, sect. "Design for appropriation"). The line then moves wholly to (b) in Ehsan et al., where a seam is a "mismatch… between assumptions made in designing and developing AI systems and the reality of their deployment contexts" (`ehsan2024seamful`, p. 5). Part 1's gloss, seams "allowing users to understand its limits", fits Ehsan better than Chalmers.
9. In every Chalmers case the seam was handled socially: Mack Room visitors "did build up a shared understanding of these 'seams' in the infrastructure" over a shared audio channel (`chalmersgalani2004seamful`, sect. "User experience of heterogeneity"). The argument transfers to a decision made through an intermediary only if the parties keep a direct channel; the papers supply no case where they do not.
10. The one adversarial case is CityWide: runners learned the GPS shadows and "waited for players to enter areas with higher GPS accuracy", which "significantly changed the balance of the game" (`chalmers2003wearable`, sect. "Uncertainty and heterogeneity"). A seam in the physical layer decided who won, which is form 4 in miniature, with a designer who later built the asymmetry into the rules of the next game (`chalmers2003infrastructure`, sect. "Initial work on seamful designs").
11. Chalmers sets a limit the essay should keep: appropriation "may be a bad design choice… where consistent interaction is desirable for legal, medical or educational reasons" (`chalmers2003wearable`, sect. "Conclusion"), and revealing seams can be "distracting and intrusive, or dangerous or counter–productive" (`chalmers2003infrastructure`, sect. "Conclusion").

### D3. Computing in objects and settings; more than one user

12. Tangible interaction's founding paper kept seamlessness as its goal: "Seamless coupling of everyday graspable objects… with the digital information that pertains to them" and "truly seamless, 'invisible' integration" (`ishiiullmer1997tangible`, pp. 2, 7). Within HCI the seamless ideal was held and contested in the same years; the phygital marketing literature's "seamless" is the Ishii position, and the Weiser–Chalmers position is the answer to it.
13. Multiple users enter tangible interaction as co-operators: two users may "cooperatively manipulat[e]" the map so that "there is no one locus of control" (`ishiiullmer1997tangible`, p. 5); Hornecker and Buur make the allocation of control a design theme, "Embodied constraints… ease some activities and limit others… Multiple access points distribute control, keep individuals from taking over control" (`horneckerbuur2006grip`, p. 5), and report a table so large that it was "physically impossible for one person to take over control of the entire board" (p. 7).
14. Dourish's own retrospective says the 2001 book meant to show that physically embodied computing "could be understood best in terms of existing analytic frameworks such as those of ethnomethodology" and that "desktop computing with mouse and keyboard is also 'embodied'" (`dourish2013epilogue`, paras. 4, 6). Dourish 2004 adds that context "is an achievement, rather than an observation" (`dourish2004context`, p. 5), so a sensing environment reads a representation fixed in advance under four assumptions it cannot discharge (pp. 3–4).
15. Distributed cognition takes a crew and its instruments as the unit (`hollan2000distributed`, p. 175): several people, one setting, no intermediary with interests.

### D4. What the machine can and cannot read

16. The machine's situation is "a plan for the use of the machine written by the designer and implemented as the program… and sensors that register changes to the machine's state" (`suchman1985plans`, p. 77); it has "no access to the history of the user's actions, or even to the presence or absence now of a document on the glass" (p. 86), nor to "their reports and assertions about their situation" (p. 87).
17. The general law: "the machine is mapping a restricted set of effects, left by the user's actions, onto a prescribed template of possible meanings… the ordinary collaborative resources of human interaction are unavailable" (p. 124). Breakdowns that face-to-face repair would catch become "'fatal'" (p. 121).
18. Suchman considered disclosure and set it aside: chapter 2 reports the recommendation to "make available to the user just how the system is not like a participant in interaction" (p. 12), but the conclusion asks the designer "to engineer alternatives to interaction's situated properties" (p. 124). This is the earliest statement in the facet of the author's own ruling that showing how the system works is not the remedy.
19. The copier is a physical setting where the plan's template meets the actual document; the "false alarm" and "garden path" (pp. 121–122) happen at the glass. That is form 4 at the scale of one user and one machine.

### D5. The seamless ideal criticised; "phygital" in human factors

20. By 2007 ubicomp's own critics called seamlessness "at best a misleading vision and at worst a downright dangerous one" and infrastructure "never seamless in the ways in which they are put to work" (`belldourish2007yesterdays`, sects. 1, 6). Phygital marketing's "seamless" and "fluid" journeys repeat the position Weiser rejected in 1994 and the field rejected in 2002–2007, under a new name and without citing the debate: OpenAlex finds 118 works joining "phygital" to "seamless" and one joining it to "seamful", a 2026 *Communications of the AIS* vision paper that I did not open (search log row 18).
21. Bell and Dourish's Singapore ERP is a phygital decision point described in 2007: variable prices on LED boards at the zone boundary, debit by smart card, drivers and taxi passengers deciding at "these transition points" (sect. 3). It is the facet's best candidate for forms 1 and 4 and needs primary documents (facet G).
22. Rogers 2006 and Greenfield 2006 exist as described but were not obtained.
23. "Phygital" has reached a human-factors venue once in what I found: Aristi et al. use it for workshops that combine maps and clay models with a digital memory layer that "does not determine what counts as valid knowledge" and was not yet built (`aristi2026distributed`, pp. 143–144). OpenAlex returns 13 works joining "phygital" with human factors, ergonomics or HCI terms, led by marketing and XR papers (row 18). I found no work in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar that connects "phygital" to Weiser, Chalmers or seamful design.

### Which forms the facet supports

24. **Form 1** (the same intermediary in physical space): supported as forecast by `weiser1991computer` (the store that "knows the suit you looked at") and as a case by the Singapore ERP in `belldourish2007yesterdays`; complicated by `suchman1985plans` and `dourish2004context`, which say the environment reads a template, not a situation.
25. **Form 2** (a physical or human arrangement as intermediary): weak support from `suchman1994categories` (The Coordinator) and `aristi2026distributed` (the trusted human intermediary); neither adapts or binds.
26. **Form 3** (physical design as choice architecture): supported by `ishiiullmer1997tangible` (mechanical constraints resolve ambiguity), `horneckerbuur2006grip` (layout allocates control) and `aristi2026distributed` (artifacts "channel outcomes"); all have a visible designer and hold still.
27. **Form 4** (the physical layer is where the ruling becomes fact and exit is priced): supported by `suchman1985plans` (the glass), `chalmers2003wearable` (GPS shadows decide the game) and the ERP boundary in `belldourish2007yesterdays`.

### Evidence against the thesis

28. No HCI source in this facet models an intermediary that reads two parties and binds both. The seamful literature's systems have no objective; its seams are honest gaps, and its remedy works through a direct channel between peers (`chalmersgalani2004seamful`). The claim that "HCI already answered phygital seamlessness" holds for the ideal of seamlessness and not for the decision structure Part 1 defines.
29. Part 1's framing needs one correction: seamful design is not a reaction against Weiser's ideal. Weiser called seamlessness misleading first (`weiser1994invisible`, slide 19); the Equator group's target was the field's reading of him, quoted from IEEE Pervasive Computing, IBM and the Disappearing Computer initiative (`maccoll2002seamful`, sect. "Introduction").
30. The seams lineage drifts from (a) toward (b) across 2002–2024. If Part 4 cites "seamful design" as a design principle it should say which sense it means, because Ehsan's sense is the disclosure the author has ruled out as a remedy.
31. Dourish's position that embodiment does not distinguish physical from screen interaction (`dourish2013epilogue`) cuts against treating the phygital as a new kind of interaction rather than a new reach of the same one.

### Gaps

32. I found no work in OpenAlex (row 18) that applies "seamful" to a marketplace, platform or algorithmic-management system that commits determinations between two parties; the 17 hits for the joined query concern online communities, location games, crisis communication and feed curation.
33. I found no work in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar that attributes "beautiful seams" to Weiser's talks other than Inman and Ribes 2019 and the Chalmers papers; the slide decks themselves are uncited in both databases.
34. Not obtained and material: Inman & Ribes 2019 (how they document the attribution and what they make of concealment); Rogers 2006; Barkhuus & Polichar 2011; Dourish 2001 chapters 4–6; Grudin 1990 body (to check Part 1's time scales and whether any stage is physical).
