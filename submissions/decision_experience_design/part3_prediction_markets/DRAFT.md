# Decision Experience Design, Part 3

*A bet is not a decision*

A prediction market exists to produce a number. Kalshi and Polymarket list a question, let people trade contracts that pay a dollar if the answer is yes, and publish the price as a probability. The promise is that the price tells the rest of us something true, and economists have defended that promise for thirty years. The product is something else. From July 2024 to April 2026, sports made up 80% of trading volume on Kalshi and 39% on Polymarket (Radde, 2026), and Apple's App Store lists the two apps as "Kalshi: Trade Football & more" and "Polymarket: Trade Live Sports" (Blockratize Inc., n.d.; Kalshi Inc., n.d.; both read 3 October 2026).

This essay argues that the distance between the promise and the product is a design failure of a familiar kind. Parts 1 and 2 of this series defined decision experience design as the design of the conditions under which a person decides through an intermediary, and they asked what such a design owes the person. Part 3 applies that standard to a product whose makers say it helps people decide. I argue that a prediction market shows its user coordination—a price that many people set together—and sells that user a bet, a contract whose outcome one side wins and the other loses. Showing the first while selling the second is what interface designers call a dark pattern, and it arose the way dark patterns usually arise: from a design its makers believed was helping.

The argument has to pass through the two people who have made the best case for these markets. Robin Hanson, the economist who designed the decision market, holds that speculative prices beat the institutions we now use to estimate uncertain things. Tarek Mansour, Kalshi's co-founder and chief executive, holds that an open exchange is a fairer place to trade on events than a sportsbook. I state both cases before answering them, and I concede what the evidence requires: the exchange form does differ from a sportsbook, sports prices are well calibrated, and the design Hanson specified passes the test this essay proposes. The platforms did not build it.

## Dark Patterns Without Villains

Part 1 described dark patterns as mechanisms "intentionally engineered to subvert user intent," and cited Gray, Kou, Battles, Hoggatt, and Toombs (2018). That description is narrower than its source. Gray and colleagues do write that "many of these dark patterns result from explicit, purposeful design intentions" (p. 3). They also ask the question this essay starts from: "Where along this trajectory does a pattern become dark, and with what level of intentionality? A design decision may have been made with good intentions for a specific audience, but resulted in manipulative outcomes when exposed to a broader audience" (p. 9). I correct Part 1 here. The field has not settled whether a dark pattern needs a villain, and the weight of the definitions is moving toward the answer that it does not.

That movement is documented. Mathur, Kshirsagar, and Mayer (2021) collected nineteen published definitions of the term and found that eight of them "do not address the role of user interface designers" at all (§2.1). California's privacy regulations go further and say that "a business's intent in designing the interface is not determinative in whether the user interface is a dark pattern, but a factor to be considered" (California Privacy Protection Agency, 2023, § 7004(c)). On this reading a dark pattern is identified by what the design does to the person's choice, and the designer's motive is evidence, not the test. I adopt that reading for the rest of the essay: a dark pattern is a design that presents one thing and delivers another to the provider's benefit and at the user's cost, whatever its makers meant.

The effect-based reading matters because the best-known cases began with stated good intentions. Leah Pearlman announced Facebook's Like button in 2009 as "an easy way to tell friends that you like what they're sharing," one that "makes room in the comments section for longer accolades" (Pearlman, 2009). Justin Rosenstein, the engineer who built it, later called likes "bright dings of pseudo-pleasure" and gave the general form: "It is very common for humans to develop things with the best of intentions and for them to have unintended, negative consequences" (Lewis, 2017). Advertising at Google has a sharper record. Before they had a business, Brin and Page (1998) predicted that "advertising funded search engines will be inherently biased towards the advertisers and away from the needs of the consumers" (Appendix A). They named the hazard and then built the business. These histories support a modest claim. The builders say they meant well, and nothing I found measures how often designers of harmful patterns do. What the histories show is that a good motive and a harmful structure can sit in the same product, and that the motive tells us little about the structure.

No source I found calls a market, as opposed to a button or a checkout page, a dark pattern. Mathur et al. (2019) crawled about 11,000 shopping sites and found dark patterns on 1,254 of them; the unit in that study and its successors is an interface element. To apply the term to the structure of a product is a step the literature has not taken, and this essay takes it. The step needs a product whose makers state a good aim, and the prediction market has two such makers on record.

## The Good the Markets Aim At

Hanson states the aim as an estimate that a decision-maker can use. His 1999 paper defines the instrument: "I suggest we consider decision markets, which are speculative markets focusing on particular decisions" (Hanson, 1999, p. 17). His example is a bill to allow concealed handguns. One market trades the murder rate if the bill passes, another trades the rate if it fails, and the gap between the two prices estimates the bill's effect. The reader of that gap is not a trader: "a state legislature might accept this estimate when considering whether or not to pass this hidden-gun bill" (p. 17). The design also names who pays. A sponsor can "directly subsidize a market," which "in effect creates an information prize offered to those who first make the market price better reflect relevant information," or a decision-maker can commit "to make a policy choice based on the market estimate" (p. 17). Hanson's claim is therefore conditional. Prices are informative when someone who wants the answer funds the market or binds a decision to it. He does not claim that any market on any topic tends toward the right decision, and of the political questions now trending on Kalshi and Polymarket he writes, "I don't have great confidence in that, or know who those people are" (Hanson, 2025).

Mansour states the aim as a fair venue. His contrast is with the sportsbook, and he draws it by structure: "The core differentiator with sports gambling is whether you are trading on an open, fair, liquid marketplace or in a closed forum system against the house that picks winners or losers. One of them you can win" (Raskin & Mansour, 2026). On an exchange the customers trade with each other and the operator takes a fee: "When somebody loses on Kalshi, they're losing it to someone else. And I take my 1% fee, regardless of who wins or loses" (Speros, 2026). He adds a claim about what the customer is looking at. "The charts are really the key component of our experience, which is showing this idea of open price discovery on any question about the future" (Speros, 2026). In Mansour's account the product is the price chart, and the trading is how the chart gets made.

Both aims are serious, and the two men disagree about how to reach them. Hanson wants the consumer of the information to pay for it. He describes the current arrangement with some distaste: "The customer is the trader who just pays a tax in order to be allowed to trade, and all the information that it's produced is a side effect that everybody can get for free. I would rather people who get the information pay for it" (Schechtman & Hanson, 2026). Mansour's business charges the trader. That disagreement over who pays returns in the last section, where it turns out to be the same question as what a decision market would have to look like.

## The Pitfall This Essay Sets Aside

The obvious objection to a betting product on every phone is that it teaches people to gamble on everything. I do not make that argument. No study measures harm among users of Kalshi or Polymarket, and the evidence from sports-betting apps reaches these platforms only by analogy. The harm is unmeasured, which is different from absent, and a later essay could take it up when the studies exist.

Hanson, for his part, defends the betting. "I don't personally mind people having fun knowingly betting on sports," he writes, and what he fears is "a new prudish temperance movement" that shuts down sports contracts and takes the markets he cares about with them (Hanson, 2025). The word that matters in his sentence is *knowingly*. A person who opens a sportsbook knows what it is. The question for decision experience design is narrower than whether betting is bad: it is what a person is led to believe they are doing when the app says they are trading on the future. That question needs a way to tell a bet from the thing it is presented as.

## Hedges and Bets

The law has distinguished the two for a long time, and the distinction turns on what a party holds before the trade. Stout (1999) reconstructs the old common-law rule: a contract for differences, settled in cash on a future price, was an unenforceable wager unless one party was protecting an interest they already had. Her analysis of trading between people who merely disagree about the future is blunt: "speculation on disagreement is a zero-sum game in which one player's gain is necessarily the other's loss," and once brokers are paid, "speculators' average returns become negative" (p. 745). Lockhart (2025) tests five ways of separating investing from sports betting—information-gathering, risk profile, time horizon, an underlying asset, and "extant versus created risk"—and concludes that "only the last principle sufficiently separates the two activities" (p. 2221). The Ninth Circuit applied the same test to Kalshi's sports contracts in August 2026: they "do not help institutions or investors hedge against risk; they create risk, largely for ordinary consumers, where none previously existed" (*KalshiEX LLC v. Assad*, 2026, p. 29).

Extant risk gives the distinction a form that a general reader can check at the moment of the trade. A trade is coordination when each side leaves with what it came for at the moment the intermediary matches them, whatever happens next. A miller who buys wheat futures has a known cost for next quarter's flour; the farmer on the other side has a known price for the crop; the speculator between them has a premium for carrying the risk. A trade is a bet when what each side came for is decided later, by an event neither controls, and only one of them can have it. I call this the test at the match, and the rest of the essay uses it.

Grain passes the test at the match, and Mansour is right to invoke it. "The law is not ducks," he says of the charge that Kalshi looks like gambling, "because if it was, then gambling in grain would still be regulated at the state level" (Speros, 2026). Working (1953), the economist who studied grain hedging most closely, found "a strong tendency for hedgers to prefer to use the exchange which has the largest volume of speculative trading" (p. 319). In positions reported for 29 September 2026, producers, merchants, processors, and users held 38.5% of all positions in Chicago corn futures (Commodity Futures Trading Commission, 2026). The speculator in that market is a third party that the hedgers need. Speculation there is one role in a three-role structure.

Currency markets pass the test only in part, and my own first statement of the contrast was too generous to them. The Bank for International Settlements (2025) reports that trading with non-financial customers was 443 billion of 9,595 billion dollars a day in April 2025, or 4.6% of turnover, down from 13.4% in 2010. Its analysts write that "trading for financial motives has come to dominate trading for the purpose of goods and services exchange" (Huang et al., 2025, p. 24). The retail end is worse. European regulators require brokers of leveraged retail contracts, currency contracts among them, to warn that "between 74-89% of retail investor accounts lose money" (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2018). So the claim cannot be that foreign exchange is coordination and prediction markets are bets. Two currency speculators facing each other are making a bet too. The claim that survives is about a class of participant. Grain and currency markets contain people whose trade offsets an exposure they already carry, and the markets were built around them. The comparison that holds is hedger-with-speculator against speculator-with-speculator, and it leads to a plain question about the event contract: who in that market holds the exposure?

## What Is on the Screen

Kalshi answered the exposure question itself, in a federal appeals court, ten weeks before it listed sports contracts. Defending its election contracts in November 2024, the company argued that Congress had meant to keep something else off the exchanges: "The classic example is a contract on the outcome of a sporting event; as the legislative history directly confirms, Congress did not want sports betting to be conducted on derivatives markets" (KalshiEX LLC, 2024, p. 41). Contracts relating to games, the brief continued, "are unlikely to serve any 'commercial or hedging interest'" (p. 45). That was the company's legal position at a date, and Mansour has not repeated it. In January 2025 Kalshi certified its first sports contracts to the regulator, and the certification places the "analysis of its risk mitigation and price basing utility" in "Confidential Appendices C, D, and E" (KalshiEX LLC, 2025, p. 2). The public record contains no account of who hedges a football game.

The scholars who defend these markets do not claim that anyone does. Macey and Enriques (2026) rest the whole case on information: "prediction markets are zero-sum. Every contract is a side bet; every dollar won is a dollar lost by a counterparty. The market as a whole creates no wealth for its participants. What it creates—the only thing it creates—is information" (p. 30). Hanson says the same of his own designs, which "usually have few 'hedging' traders" (Hanson, 2008, §IV). Defenders and critics agree, then, on the structure of the trade. A sports contract is a bet between two speculators, and by the test at the match it stays one however it is listed.

Recreation complicates that verdict, and the essay should give it its due. Two people who each want action on tonight's game do meet a need of each other's, and Hanson (2003) has always filed sports under that heading: "sports betting markets enhance entertainment from watching sporting events" (p. 107), he writes, in a list that gives hedging to commodity futures and information to no market yet. Hanson (1995) had already explained why betting volume gathers on games, observing that "most organized betting focuses on questions which, like sporting events, will become very clearly resolved in a fixed time," which is the property a bettor who wants a quick result is looking for. Arrow et al. (2008), the statement he signed with Kenneth Arrow and twenty others, kept that entertainment outside the instrument it defended, proposing a legal safe harbor for prediction markets that "would presumably not include contracts on the outcomes of sports events" (p. 878). Entertainment is a real need. It is also a different need from the one the words *prediction market* and *decision market* name, and the Ninth Circuit records which need the company advertises to: Kalshi "advertises itself as 'the first app for legal sports betting in all 50 states'" (*KalshiEX LLC v. Assad*, 2026, p. 14).

The sports bet costs its buyer money in a way the price chart does not show. Bürgi, Deng, and Whelan (2026) studied Kalshi's trades and found that "the average return on contracts for Makers was -9.64% while for Takers it was -31.46%." A maker posts an offer and waits; a taker accepts the price on the screen. The person who taps the button the app puts in front of them is the taker. An exchange has no house in Mansour's sense, and it still has a side of the trade that loses three times as much, and the interface decides which side a newcomer is on. The gap between what a market computes and what its screen asks for is the subject of the next section.

## A Jointly Set Price, a Two-Sided Bet

Parts 1 and 2 used *decision* for what a person does through an intermediary and never defined it against *choice*. Part 3 needs the definition, and I mark it as new. A choice is a selection among options whose joint outcome is fixed before the person selects: a bet is the limiting case, since the two sides' results always sum to zero less the fee. A decision is an act whose quality depends on the joint outcome for the parties the intermediary coordinates, and which the intermediary then commits. Part 2 called coordination triadic when "a third party reads both human parties and commits an outcome binding on both." By that definition an exchange's matching engine is a third party. It reads the buyer and the seller and binds them both.

The matching engine is why the simple version of this essay's claim—that a prediction market is really a two-party affair—is wrong, and why the claim has to be split. A market has two stages. At the first, many traders set a price together, and each of them reads it. That stage is three-party in the full sense: no trader's price is fixed by any one other trader, and the price belongs to all of them. At the second stage the contract settles. The game ends, the exchange relays the result, and each account is credited or debited according to the event alone. Nothing about my counterparty affects my result except that they were on the other side. The settlement stage factors into two separate outcomes, one win and one loss. The price is jointly set; the bet is two-sided.

That split locates the dark pattern exactly. The platforms present the first stage. Mansour says the charts are the product, and the chart is the jointly set price. The user who acts on the presentation, however, enters the second stage. The button buys a contract, the contract is a bet, and the bet is a choice in the sense just defined. A person who came for the probability, the thing the name promises, can read it on the public page without trading. A person who trades has stopped consuming the estimate and started funding it, and a person who takes the price on the screen does so on the worse side of the maker and taker gap. The design presents coordination and sells a wager, to the provider's benefit, since fees rise with volume, and at a measured cost to the user. By the effect-based reading adopted above, that is a dark pattern, and nobody needs to have intended it.

Evidence that users read the presentation the way the pattern predicts is thin, and I state it at its weight. Sah, Karduni, Markant, and Dou (2026) examined 360 posts containing market charts from the Reddit community r/Kalshi and report that users "struggle with probability information displayed." Theirs is one qualitative preprint. No experiment tests how a lay reader interprets a price of 70 cents, and Manski (2006) showed long ago that the inference is loose even in theory: a price of 0.75 is consistent with a mean belief anywhere "in the range (0.5625, 0.935)." Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2006) replied that under a broad class of models prices "are usually close to the mean beliefs of traders," and the reply is the standard one. For a design argument the dispute itself is the point. Economists need a model to read the number, and the app shows it to a newcomer as a percentage beside a buy button.

Kalshi's structure adds one more complication, which the Ninth Circuit noted while rejecting the company's claim to have no house. The court pointed to "the existence of Kalshi Trading, Kalshi's affiliate that acts as a 'significant player on the exchange,' largely as a market maker" (*KalshiEX LLC v. Assad*, 2026, p. 29 n.5). How much of the volume the affiliate takes is not public, and I did not open the archived help pages the court cites. Where the affiliate is the counterparty, even the first stage loses its three-party character for that trade, and the user faces a single informed opponent connected to the venue. The presentation remains the same. Three replies to this account are on the record, and the next section takes them in order of strength.

## Three Replies

**Hanson: there is no probability without the trade.** In an unsubsidised market the only people who pay to move the price are people who want to trade, and Hanson's theory says their motives do not need to be good ones: "in most markets the opportunity to win against such noise traders is the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders" (Hanson, 2006, preprint p. 12). On this view the bettor is the mechanism, and asking the user to trade is how the number gets made. The reply is strong, and its premise can be tested. Dana, Atanasov, Tetlock, and Mellers (2019) asked the same traders both to trade and to state their beliefs across 113 questions. The aggregated stated beliefs scored 0.210 on the Brier measure of error against 0.227 for market prices, a difference that was not significant, and combining beliefs with prices beat prices alone (p = 0.004). Stakes were not what made the number good in that study. An interface that asked people for a probability and scored them could produce the estimate without selling anyone a bet. Hanson himself left the interface open. His 2003 paper names "user interfaces" as "the main category of issues not dealt with" (p. 118), and in 2008 he expected that "the problem of understanding how to trade can be overcome with education and better trading interfaces" (§IV.A). He treated the screen as a detail to be engineered later. This essay treats it as the product, because it is what the user meets.

**Mansour: the exchange is the third party.** The exchange form is a real difference, and the evidence for it is good. Smith, Paton, and Vaughan Williams (2006) compared betting exchanges with bookmakers on British horse racing and found that exchanges brought "significant efficiency gains by lowering transaction costs for consumers" and showed "significantly lower market biases." A federal appeals court has accepted Kalshi's legal framing: the Third Circuit held in April 2026 that the company's "sports-related event contracts are swaps traded on a CFTC-licensed DCM" and that federal law preempts state gambling law (*KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty*, 2026). The Ninth Circuit reached the opposite result, and its phrase for Mansour's structural argument, "distinctions without differences," is a holding about a statutory word and should not be borrowed as a finding about design. I concede the form. What the form does not change is the second stage. A lower fee makes the bet cheaper, and an open order book makes it fairer, and neither turns the contract into something both sides leave with. The exchange is a third party to the price. To the bet it is a cashier.

**Hanson again: this is a hedging standard applied to an information market.** If coordination requires a hedger, every prediction market fails, including the ones Hanson designed, since he says they have few hedgers and work anyway. The reply forces a clarification that improves the argument. The party with a need in an information market is the reader of the price. Hanson's legislature needs the estimate to vote; his sponsor needs it enough to pay for it. A market built around that reader passes the test at the match, because the sponsor gets the estimate when the market forms a price, whatever the event turns out to be, and the traders get the sponsor's subsidy for improving it. The retail exchange has readers too. Federal Reserve staff have evaluated Kalshi's macroeconomic contracts and conclude that they "provide a high-frequency, continuously updated, distributionally rich benchmark that is valuable to both researchers and policymakers" (Diercks et al., 2026). Those readers pay nothing and are bound to nothing, and most of the volume is in sports. The standard this essay applies is therefore Hanson's own: a market is a decision instrument when someone with a decision is party to it.

Several concessions follow from these replies and belong in one place. Sports is the best-calibrated category on Polymarket, where longshot bets in crypto and politics lose and, in the authors' words, "Sports is the clearest exception" (Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe, 2026). The explicit cost of an exchange bet is lower than a bookmaker's. Mansour's grain analogy is right about grain. Hanson has not called either platform a disguise, and this essay does not put the charge in his mouth. And Part 2's argument, which grounded the designer's obligation in mandatory participation, does not reach a venue that nobody has to use; the obligation here comes from Part 1's design principles and from the ordinary rule against presenting one thing and selling another.

## The Market Hanson Designed

The decision market in Hanson's 1999 paper has three parties with something to do. A sponsor or legislature has a decision to make and wants an estimate. Traders have information and want to be paid for it. The market takes the traders' information and commits a price that the decision-maker has agreed to use. Each party leaves the match with what it came for: the sponsor has its estimate as soon as the price forms, and the traders are paid from the sponsor's subsidy for moving the price toward the truth, not from each other's losses. Hanson (2003) explains why the subsidy is necessary: "Once rational agents have hedged their risks regarding the events covered by an information market, they should not want to trade with each other" (p. 108). Without a sponsor, the trading has to be motivated by something else, and on the platforms the something else is the game.

The missing sponsor is where Hanson's complaint and this essay's meet. He says the trader "pays a tax" for information that others take free and that the consumers of information should pay instead. I have argued that the user is shown coordination and sold a bet. These are one observation from two sides. The retail exchange took the decision market's vocabulary of prices, probabilities, and forecasts, removed the party with the decision, and replaced that party's subsidy with the losses of people who came to bet on football. The price survived the substitution, which is why the charts still look like the thing the theory describes.

Part 1's design principles say what a product would do differently if it took the decision as its unit. It would declare an operating envelope for the price: what the number is, what model is needed to read it as a probability, and how thin the market behind it is. It would let a person take the estimate without taking a position, and would treat that as the main use and not a leak. It would elicit probabilities and score them, which the evidence suggests loses little accuracy, for users whose aim is to forecast. Where it sold bets it would call them bets, as a sportsbook does, and Kalshi's advertising shows that the company can use the word when it chooses. None of these changes requires abandoning markets. Each of them separates the two stages that the current design runs together.

## Propositions and Limitations

The argument yields propositions that evidence could refute.

1. Lay users shown a contract price as a percentage beside a trading button will describe the act of buying as forecasting or investing more often than users shown the same contract in a sportsbook layout, and will estimate their expected return as higher.
2. On a platform that offers both, users who state probabilities and are scored will be calibrated no worse than users who trade, at lower average loss. Dana et al. (2019) support the first half for forecasting-tournament participants; no study tests it on a retail platform.
3. The share of volume in which a position offsets an exposure the trader already holds is near zero for sports contracts and small for all event contracts. Disclosure of the confidential appendices to Kalshi's sports certifications would test this directly.
4. The gap between maker and taker returns narrows when the default order type is a limit order.

The limitations are plain. No study measures harm, comprehension, or learning among users of either platform; the one study of Kalshi's display is a qualitative preprint. The sports share of volume depends on the measure: 80% of Kalshi's volume on Pew's figures, and a range from two fifths to two thirds for Polymarket depending on how categories are assigned. The Ninth Circuit's statement that "over 90% of Kalshi's trades in 2025, representing 95% of its revenue, were sports related" rests on a record citation I did not trace. The law is unsettled, with one appeals court for Kalshi and two against as of this writing. The well-meant origin of the Like button and of search advertising rests on what their builders said. The definition of a decision against a choice, and the application of *dark pattern* to a market's structure, are this essay's own, and a reader may reject either without rejecting the evidence. Mansour's case is reconstructed from interviews and from Kalshi's filings, since he has published no scholarly statement of it; the filings are the company's and the interviews are his.

What the evidence does establish is narrower than the title and enough for the claim. The trade the platforms sell is a bet by every test the law and the economics offer, including the tests their defenders use. The price the platforms show is jointly made and useful to people who never trade. The design joins the two, and the person at the screen pays for the join.
