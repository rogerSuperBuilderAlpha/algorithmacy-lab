# K: Dark patterns and designer intent; the Like button and personalised advertising as histories

*Facet K of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 (seat K of a four-seat run, local session, model Fable). Nothing below is the author's text; the author has not reviewed it.*

What this facet covers. The author's premise P1 for Part 3 reads: "Dark patterns rarely occur because someone is trying to hurt people. Google and Facebook started out generally to do a good thing. But the things they thought were helping, showing personalized ad experiences and the Like button, turned on them." The live Part 1 defines dark patterns as "interface mechanisms intentionally engineered to subvert user intent for commercial extraction (Gray et al., 2018)". This facet asks four questions. K1: do the field's definitions of a dark pattern require designer intent, and how has that changed from 2010 to 2025? K2: which adjacent concepts (deceptive design, anti-patterns, asshole design, sludge, attention-capture damaging patterns, persuasive technology, hypernudge, online manipulation, structural characteristics) describe harm without malice? K3: what do the best obtainable sources say about who built the Like button and personalised advertising, what they said it was for, and what they said later? K4: has anyone called a market, trading product or gambling product, as a whole structure, a dark pattern? The synthesis in section (c) answers the five propositions the run asked for.

`brignull2010darkpatterns` | Brignull, H. (2010). *DarkPatterns.org: Naming and shaming sites that use "black hat, anti-usability" design patterns*. Website, launched 2010.
- *Record:* No DOI. Wayback Machine capture of 2010-07-28 14:15:25, `http://web.archive.org/web/20100728141525id_/http://darkpatterns.org:80/`. Footer: "Curated by Harry Brignull. Largely based on community input from this blog post and this discussion thread." Licensed CC BY-SA 3.0.
- *Read status:* full text (the home page of the archived site).
- *What it says:* "This pattern library is dedicated to Dark Patterns: user interfaces that have been designed to trick users into doing things they wouldn't otherwise have done, or to prevent users from doing the things they want to do. Normally when you think of 'bad design', you think of laziness or mistakes. These are known as design anti-patterns. Dark Patterns are different – they are not mistakes, they are carefully crafted with a solid understanding of human psychology, and they do not have the user's interests in mind." The 2010 type list on the page: Bait and Switch, Voice Theft, Sneaky credit card roll-over, Price Comparison Prevention, Sneak into basket, Anti-scan trick questions, Disguised Ads, Roach Motel, Price Sensitivity Targeting, Anti-privacy Zuckering, Forced Information Disclosure.
- *Bearing:* **refutes** P1 as a matter of the original definition. Intent is constitutive: a dark pattern is what an anti-pattern is not, and the difference is that it is "carefully crafted" and "not mistakes". Gray et al. (2018, p. 3) quote this passage; the Part 1 sentence inherits its intent clause from here, not from Gray.

### B2. Academic definitions, 2018 to 2025

`gray2018dark` | Gray, C. M., Kou, Y., Battles, B., Hoggatt, J., & Toombs, A. L. (2018). The dark (patterns) side of UX design. In *Proceedings of the 2018 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (Paper 534, pp. 1–14). ACM.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174108 (Crossref: proceedings-article, issued 2018-04-21, pp. 1–14). Existing card: `submissions/algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/gray2018.md` (S2-verified).
- *Read status:* full text, NSF Public Access Repository accepted manuscript, `https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10057203` (14 pp., folios "Paper 534 Page n"). Re-read this session for every passage on intent.
- *What it says on intent:* Abstract: dark patterns are the phenomenon "where user value is supplanted in favor of shareholder value". p. 1: "instances where designers use their knowledge of human behavior (e.g., psychology) and the desires of end users to implement deceptive functionality that is not in the user's best interest" (as the card records). p. 3, quoting Brignull: dark patterns are "a user interface that has been carefully crafted to trick users into doing things...they are not mistakes, they are carefully crafted with a solid understanding of human psychology, and they do not have the user's interests in mind", then: "However, this definition leaves many important and unanswered questions ... Can interactions that were not designed to trick the user later become dark patterns as use or technological infrastructure changes?" Same page: "While some instances of these behaviors are simply a result of poor design—a phenomenon known as 'anti-patterns' (c.f., [43])—many of these dark patterns result from explicit, purposeful design intentions." p. 8: "While not all interactions that take on these strategies are necessarily equally 'dark' in terms of design intent and motivation, they do have the potential to produce poor user outcomes, or force users to interact in ways that are out of alignment with their goals. Paradoxically, some instances of dark patterns test well from a usability perspective (e.g., forced action, nagging), but do so at the expense of user choice." p. 9: "This allows us to question: Where along this trajectory does a pattern become dark, and with what level of intentionality? A design decision may have been made with good intentions for a specific audience, but resulted in manipulative outcomes when exposed to a broader audience. How might we assess the 'dark' characteristics of the pattern or the designer employing the pattern? The aphorism Hanlon's Razor has been commonly shared in the UX design community as a means of separating malicious intent from lack of design skill; put simply, this means to 'never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity' [2]. This is also reflected in the practitioner differentiation between dark patterns (where malice is assumed) and anti-patterns (where lack of skill is at issue)." p. 9: the second dimension of responsibility concerns "whether a pattern must be intentionally applied in a malicious manner to be 'dark.' Are patterns consistently misunderstood by certain user groups in a harmful manner also considered dark? And is the designer culpable for this 'dark'ness, particularly after they are made aware of any harmful effects?" p. 9: "Existing codes of ethics do not generally anticipate deception, and instead tend to conflate designer intent and eventual use (Albrechtslund's [5] 'positivist problem')".
- *Bearing:* **complicates Part 1 and supports Part 3's question, though not its answer.** Gray et al. do write intent into their working definition ("designers use their knowledge ... to implement deceptive functionality") and say "many" dark patterns "result from explicit, purposeful design intentions". They also, in the same paper, name the case the author's premise describes ("good intentions for a specific audience, but resulted in manipulative outcomes when exposed to a broader audience"), pose "with what level of intentionality?" as an open question, and attribute the malice/skill split to practitioner folklore (Hanlon's razor), not to their own analysis. The paper does not resolve whether intent is required. See proposition (b).

`mathur2019dark` | Mathur, A., Acar, G., Friedman, M. J., Lucherini, E., Mayer, J., Chetty, M., & Narayanan, A. (2019). Dark patterns at scale: Findings from a crawl of 11K shopping websites. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 3*(CSCW), Article 81, 1–32.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1145/3359183 (Crossref: issued 2019-11-07, 3(CSCW), 1–32).
- *Read status:* full text, arXiv 1907.07032v2 (32 pp.; folios "81:n" match the ACM pagination).
- *What it says on intent:* p. 81:1 (abstract and opening): "Dark patterns are user interface design choices that benefit an online service by coercing, steering, or deceiving users into making unintended and potentially harmful decisions." p. 81:4 (Background), on Greenberg et al.: they "analyzed dark patterns and 'antipatterns'—interface designs with unintentional side-effects on user behavior".
- *Bearing:* supports an effect-and-benefit definition without a designer-intent clause. The elements are a benefit to the service and a mechanism acting on the user (coerce, steer, deceive); the designer's state of mind is not named. Mathur et al. (2021, Table 1) classify this definition accordingly (see next entry). The anti-pattern remark shows the field's standing contrast: unintended side-effects are anti-patterns.

`luguri2021shining` | Luguri, J., & Strahilevitz, L. J. (2021). Shining a light on dark patterns. *Journal of Legal Analysis, 13*(1), 43–109.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1093/jla/laaa006 (Crossref: issued 2021-01-01, 13(1), 43–109). SSRN 3431205 is the 2019 working paper. Facet B holds a partial read of Sections 3.1–3.2.
- *Read status:* partial (abstract from Crossref and Consensus; Introduction and Part 2 passages via WebFetch of the OUP HTML; page numbers as the extraction reported them, unverified against the PDF).
- *What it says on intent:* Abstract and Introduction (p. 43): "Dark patterns are user interfaces whose designers knowingly confuse users, make it difficult for users to express their actual preferences, or manipulate users into taking certain actions." Abstract: "Dark patterns are presumably proliferating because firms' proprietary A-B testing has revealed them to be profit maximizing." Part 2 (reported pp. 54–55): dark patterns "manipulate consumers by altering online choice architecture in ways that are designed to thwart users' preferences for objectionable ends", in contrast to nudges that "may use similar techniques to foster prosocial behavior like organ donation or retirement saving"; identifying one requires asking "whether [an interface] is the product of an intentional design choice, an accident, or an external constraint."
- *Bearing:* **refutes P1 on definition, supports it on mechanism.** The legal-economic definition keeps "knowingly" and "designed to thwart". The A/B-testing sentence, however, names a route by which a harmful pattern can be selected by a metric rather than by a person who means harm, which is the mechanism P1 needs. Isola (2025) classifies "the unintended outcomes of A/B testing" as what the intent-based literature calls poor design (see `isola2025systematic`).

## (c) Synthesis: five propositions

**(a) Does the field's definition of a dark pattern require intent, and how has that changed?** It did, and it is ceasing to. Brignull's 2010 page makes intent constitutive: dark patterns "are not mistakes, they are carefully crafted with a solid understanding of human psychology", and what is not crafted is an anti-pattern (`brignull2010darkpatterns`). The first academic generation kept that line. Gray et al. (2018, pp. 1, 3) have designers who "use their knowledge of human behavior ... to implement deceptive functionality" and say "many of these dark patterns result from explicit, purposeful design intentions"; Luguri and Strahilevitz (2021, p. 43) have "designers knowingly confuse users"; Fogg's captology, which Mathur et al. (2021, p. 5) and Gray et al. (2018, p. 9) both name as the ancestor of the pattern vocabulary, defines persuasion so that "not all behavior or attitude change is the result of persuasion" (`fogg1998persuasive`, p. 226). The census is Mathur, Kshirsagar and Mayer (2021, pp. 4–5): of 19 definitions to December 2020, eight state that designers deploy patterns intentionally and eight do not address the designer at all; their own 2019 definition, "design choices that benefit an online service by coercing, steering, or deceiving users", has no intent clause (`mathur2019dark`, p. 81:1). The turn came from regulators in 2020–2023: California's statute says "designed or manipulated with the substantial effect of" (`california2020cpra`); its regulation says a business's intent "is not determinative ... but a factor to be considered" (`cppa2023regs`, §7004(c)); the FTC describes "design practices that trick or manipulate users into making choices they would not otherwise have made" (`ftc2022dark`, p. 2); the DSA's recital 67 says "either on purpose or in effect" (`eu2022dsa`). HCI then followed the regulators: Gray et al. (2024, pp. 1–2) write "regardless of the designer's intent" and cite the CPRA, the DSA and the FTC for it. Legal scholarship has made the exclusion explicit: Isola and Esposito (2025) find intent constitutive in 36 of 116 documents and propose a definition, "the design of a digital choice environment that is capable of distorting user behaviour", that leaves it out, because "the defining characteristic of a dark pattern lies not in the subjective mindset of its creator, but in its observable effect on user choice" (thesis §2.6); Trzaskowski (2024, p. 12) tells lawmakers "to focus on the architecture rather than the intent of a trader"; Timms (2025, abstract) wants to "decouple an account from other salient concerns, such as intentionality". Two things have not changed. Brignull's own current definition still says "trick" (`brignull2026deceptivedesign`), and the price of dropping intent is named by Isola: it "removes a convenient means of distinguishing between dark patterns and what the literature sometimes calls poor design". Mathur et al. (2021, p. 19) say the same of the autonomy lens, which "sweeps broadly".

**(b) Does Gray et al. (2018) support the sentence Part 1 attributes to it?** In part, and the part it does not support is the part Part 3 needs. Part 1 writes "interface mechanisms intentionally engineered to subvert user intent for commercial extraction (Gray et al., 2018)". "Commercial extraction" is fair to the abstract's "user value is supplanted in favor of shareholder value". "Intentionally engineered" is fair to p. 1 ("designers use their knowledge ... to implement deceptive functionality") and to p. 3 ("many of these dark patterns result from explicit, purposeful design intentions"), though Gray et al. say "many", not all. "Subvert user intent" is not Gray's phrase; Mathur et al. (2021, p. 4) list "Subvert user intent" as an element of Brignull's, Bösch's and Waldman's definitions, not Gray's. More important, the same paper treats intent as an open question. On p. 3 it asks "Can interactions that were not designed to trick the user later become dark patterns as use or technological infrastructure changes?"; on p. 9 it asks "Where along this trajectory does a pattern become dark, and with what level of intentionality? A design decision may have been made with good intentions for a specific audience, but resulted in manipulative outcomes when exposed to a broader audience", and it files the malice/skill distinction under "the practitioner differentiation" by Hanlon's razor rather than endorsing it. So Part 1's sentence presents as settled what its source leaves open. The honest move for Part 3 is not to contradict Part 1 but to cite Gray et al. (2018, pp. 3 and 9) for the question and the regulators for the answer: the question Gray posed in 2018 was answered by the DSA and California in 2022–2023, and Gray et al. (2024) adopted the answer.

**(c) Which term or definition lets Part 3 make the well-meant-structure claim honestly?** Three routes are clean and three are closed. Closed: "persuasive technology" (intent by definition, `fogg1998persuasive`), "online manipulation" ("intentionally and covertly", `susser2019technology`, already Part 2's definition), and "asshole design" (coded by "malicious intent", `gray2020asshole`, p. 64). Clean, in order of fit. First, "dark pattern" in the regulatory sense, stated as such: the DSA's "either on purpose or in effect" and California's "intent ... is not determinative" are quotable, and Gray et al. (2024) give the HCI endorsement. The essay must then say it is using the 2022 regulatory definition and not Brignull's 2010 one, and it must accept Isola's cost: on this definition an honest mistake and a trick are both dark patterns, so "dark pattern" no longer says anything about motive, and P1's first sentence ("rarely occur because someone is trying to hurt people") becomes an empirical claim about designers that no source here measures, except Zhang-Kennedy et al. (2024, abstract, 23 interviews). Second, the gambling-studies vocabulary, which already handles products rather than screens: "structural characteristics" for the product (`newall2021structural`; `newall2025sludge`), "dark nudge" for the framing, with "presenting gambling as a fun activity" as Newall's own example, and "dark pattern" for the interface tier. Schüll's *Addiction by Design* is the model for saying that designers "don't even think about addiction" and that the harm comes from "problem machines, problem environments, and problem business practices" (sample p. 21). This is the register in which "sports betting dressed as decision markets" can be said without a claim about anyone's heart. Third, Caragay et al.'s (2024) expectation definition, "a design is dark when its concepts violate users' expectations, and benefit the application provider at the user's expense", which describes the Part 3 gap exactly (the user expects a probability; the concept delivered is a trade) and is explicitly "regardless of the intentions of the designer or programmer". "Sludge" fits only the withdrawal and account-closing frictions McGarrigle et al. catalogue; the Part 3 complaint is the absence of friction, which Pearlman herself names ("If things took a little longer, a little more effort", `luckerson2017ringer`). "Hypernudge" fits the adaptive layer (feeds, notifications) and Esposito and Maciel Cathoud Ferreira (2024) have already called "a digital interface with an addictive design" a "hyper-engaging dark pattern ... a form of hypernudge", which is the one dark-pattern-labelled whole-interface class found. Recommendation: use "dark pattern" in the DSA sense for the interface, "structural characteristics" or Schüll's "design" for the product, and drop the sentence about what designers are trying to do unless it is rewritten as "the record of what they said they were trying to do".

**(d) What do the Like-button and personalised-ads histories support, and not support?** The Like button supports "they said they meant well" at three tiers and does not support "the firm meant well". Pearlman's launch note (T1, 2009) frames the button as comment hygiene ("The aggregation of the sentiment 'I like this' makes room in the comments section for longer accolades"). Pearlman (T2, 2017) says "I was trying to solve what we called the redundant problem"; Rosenstein (T2, 2017) says "is there a way to increase positivity in the system?" and "had a lot of unintended consequences". Both now describe regret: "bright dings of pseudo-pleasure" (Rosenstein, `lewis2017guardian`), "eating bad potato chips" (Pearlman, `luckerson2017ringer`). But the Guardian's same paragraph has Rosenstein acknowledging that "Facebook harvested valuable data about the preferences of users that could be sold to advertisers", and Facebook's own press release of 6 November 2007, fifteen months before launch, had announced that "Social actions are powerful because they act as trusted referrals" (`zuckerberg2007ads`). The firm's advertising use of social actions was public before the button existed; the two scholarly histories found (Gerlitz and Helmond 2013; Halpin 2023, both abstract only) describe the Like as a tracking and data device from its export to the web in 2010. So the sentence "the Like button turned on them" is true of its builders' feelings and not shown of the company's plan. No causal study of the Like's effects on users was found (gap statement, B5). Personalised advertising supports "they said so" and complicates "they thought it was helping". For Google, the founders' 1998 appendix predicts that "advertising funded search engines will be inherently biased towards the advertisers and away from the needs of the consumers" (`brin1998anatomy`, Appendix A); six years later the S-1 letter claims the newspaper model solves it (`google2004s1`) and Brin tells Playboy "Our ads aren't distracting; they're helpful" (`google2004playboy`). The honest sentence is that Google's founders named the hazard before building the business they had warned against, and then said in filings that they had contained it. Zuboff (2015, p. 79) gives the "heroic exemplar ... then profit pressure ... then the ad model" narrative the author wants, sourced to three trade books; cite it as her argument. For Facebook, Beacon is the documented case: a founder's contemporaneous statement that the goal "was to build a simple product to let people share information across sites with their friends", an opt-out default that shared purchases without consent, and an apology within a month (`zuckerberg2007beacon`). That supports P1's shape for one feature, on the founder's word about his own motive. Across both histories the evidence is of two kinds, statements of purpose by the people who profited (T1–T3) and retrospective regret by employees (T2); neither shows what anyone believed, and the essay should write "said", not "thought".

**(e) Has anyone called a market or product structure, as opposed to an interface element, a dark pattern?** Not in any source read, and the gap search (log rows 21, 24, 25) found no paper that applies "dark pattern" to a prediction market or event-contract platform. The closest cases use other words. The Massachusetts Securities Division (2020, ¶¶40–53) pleaded Robinhood's whole engagement design as "gamification strategies ... to increase revenue by stimulating customers to interact with the application more frequently and thereby execute more trades", and never wrote "dark pattern". The SEC (2021) calls the layer "digital engagement practices". Law reviews call it "gamification" and "gamblification" (`petkov2024trading`; `packin2025hooked`). Finance measures "the app's unique features" as a set (`barber2022attention`). Gambling studies has the product-level term, "structural characteristics", and Newall (2025) files whole-product framing ("presenting gambling as a fun activity") under "dark nudge" while reserving "dark pattern" for user-interface features. On prediction markets, Packin and Rabinovitz (2026, Science) write "gambling-like design" and Johnson and Chan (2025) write that "functionally, these products resemble gambling"; both are abstract-only here. The only source found that attaches "dark pattern" to a whole interface rather than an element is Esposito and Maciel Cathoud Ferreira (2024), whose "hyper-engaging dark pattern is a digital interface with an addictive design", and even that is an interface, not a market. Mathur et al. (2021, pdf p. 13, per F2) remark that marketplaces "can manipulate both the sellers ... and the buyers", an aside. If Part 3 calls Kalshi or Polymarket a dark pattern as a structure, it is the first to do so in the literature searched, and the essay should say so, name the regulatory definition it relies on, and expect the objection Isola and Mathur both raise: without intent, the term covers everything that distorts choice, and the argument then rests entirely on showing the distortion, which is facets B and C's job.
