*Facet M of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 (seat M of the eight-seat run). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The case Tarek Mansour (co-founder and CEO of KalshiEX LLC) and Kalshi make for event contracts, reconstructed from the primary record: Kalshi's court filings and the opinions that answered them, Kalshi's CFTC filings, and Mansour's own words in long interviews. The brief set the standard: state his case at full strength, do not bend it toward the essay's thesis, do not strawman it. Section (c) does that in three strands, each as numbered premises and a conclusion, with three things kept apart in every row: what Mansour or Kalshi said (quoted from a document I opened), what scholarship says, and what I infer.

**Read-status legend.** *Opened in full* = I downloaded the document and read every page relevant to the argument; page locators are to the document's own pagination unless marked "PDF p." *Partial* = I opened the document and read the pages cited, not the whole. *Raw page* = I fetched the web page with curl, stripped the HTML, and copied the quotation from that text; a WebFetch extraction was used first and then checked against the raw text. *Not obtained* = I could not open it; nothing from it is quoted. *Secondary* = a report about the document, not the document. All access dates are 2026-10-03.

**Headline limitations.** (1) Mansour has given no congressional or agency testimony I could find, and I found no op-ed under his byline; his case lives in Kalshi's briefs (signed by counsel), in CFTC filings signed by Kalshi's Head of Markets, and in interviews. ### B1. KalshiEX LLC v. CFTC (D.D.C. No. 1:23-cv-03257; D.C. Cir. No. 24-5205)

`kalshi2023complaint` | KalshiEX LLC, Complaint, D.D.C. No. 1:23-cv-03257, Doc. 1, filed 2023-11-01 (27 pp).
- *Record:* https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.261465/gov.uscourts.dcd.261465.1.0_1.pdf
- *Read status:* opened in full.
- *What it argues:* The complaint opens the hedging case. ¶21 (p. 7): "Many derivatives contracts are tools to mitigate risk, including risk associated with the occurrence of events. Event contracts are the purest expression of that concept." ¶22 (p. 7): "Event contracts trade on a centralized exchange, similar to a stock market. Their prices are determined by market forces, not by the exchange." ¶3 (p. 2): "Uncertainty surrounding these events poses economic risk, no less than uncertainty over hurricanes, pandemics, or oil supply." ¶26 (p. 9) separates the instrument from insurance: "An event contract would instead hedge the diffuse risk of losses associated with a hurricane, including property damage, a decline in Airbnb revenues, and higher costs of food and water." ¶27 (p. 9) states the information strand: "their prices can be understood as a collective prediction by the market. The expectations and willingness of traders to put their money on the line form the price, which reflects the market's view of the odds that an event will occur." On "gaming", ¶67 (p. 20): "it refers to betting on games, and especially to betting on games of chance. An election is not a game at all, let alone the equivalent of bingo or roulette." ¶69 (p. 20): "every event contract by definition stakes money on a contingent event. As a result, under the Commission's reading of 'involve' and 'gaming,' no event contract would escape public-interest scrutiny."
- *Bearing:* the foundational statement of all three strands, written when Kalshi's product was elections, not sports.

`kalshi2024sjmemo` | KalshiEX LLC, Memorandum in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment, D.D.C. No. 1:23-cv-03257, Doc. 17-1, filed 2024-01-25 (55 pp; brief pagination cited).
- *Record:* https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.261465/gov.uscourts.dcd.261465.17.1.pdf
- *Read status:* opened in full for Introduction, Background A–B, Argument I.B, II.A, II.B, III.A–C (pp. 1–7, 20–27, 31–42); the remainder skimmed.
- *What it argues:* p. 1: "Similar to futures and other derivatives, these instruments are tools to hedge risks; they also harness the 'wisdom of crowds' to generate reliable predictive data." p. 4: "Event contract prices, like stock prices, are determined by market forces, not set by the exchange on which the contracts or stocks are traded. Traders arrive at those prices based on all available information at the time of the transaction. See AR 27–28, 1479. As a result, the prices of event contracts reflect the market's real-time probabilistic belief about whether the underlying event will occur." p. 5 concedes the speculation point and turns it: "Of course, not everyone who trades an event contract is hedging risk—some simply seek a return. That is true of all derivative markets; indeed, a robust market depends in part on the liquidity provided by speculators." p. 6 quotes Jason Furman from the record: "Congressional control impacts legislation, policy, and the business environment in ways that have direct economic consequence to businesses and workers. This risk is conceptually identical to climate risk, business interruption risk, and other similar risks that can and should be managed using the financial markets." The hedging theory is at p. 36: "The point of hedging is to mitigate risk, not (like insurance) to offset precise losses. And risk is all about uncertainty." On sports, pp. 22–23, Kalshi argued that "gaming" is not a null set because "according to the CFTC itself, the 'gaming' category encompasses contracts based on sporting events. See id. at 8. And contracts contingent on sports betting are not hypothetical. ... The only relevant legislative history, moreover, confirms that contracts on 'sporting events such as the Super Bowl, the Kentucky Derby, and Masters Golf Tournament' were precisely what Congress had in mind as 'gaming' contracts." p. 27: "Elections are not games. They are not remotely analogous to casino games, lotteries, bingo, or even sporting events. Elections, unlike 'games,' are not staged for entertainment or to facilitate speculation for sport."
- *Bearing:* carries the full economic-purpose argument (III.A) and the structural argument that hedging use is not the statutory test. The sports passages are the clearest statement of Kalshi's 2024 position that sports contracts are "gaming" contracts subject to public-interest review.

## (c) Reconstruction of the argument

Three strands. In each row: **Said** = Mansour or Kalshi, verbatim, with locator (all from documents opened above). **Scholarship** = peer-reviewed or working-paper literature the premise relies on or that bears on it; DOIs resolved on Crossref today unless marked. **Test** = evidence that would confirm or break the premise. Where I draw an inference it is marked *I infer*.

### Strand A: hedging and economic purpose

**A1. Event contracts are derivatives, and derivatives exist to transfer risk.**
- Said: "Many derivatives contracts are tools to mitigate risk, including risk associated with the occurrence of events. Event contracts are the purest expression of that concept." (Complaint ¶21, p. 7.) "Similar to futures and other derivatives, these instruments are tools to hedge risks" (SJ memo p. 1).
- Scholarship: Arrow (1964), "The Role of Securities in the Optimal Allocation of Risk-bearing", *Review of Economic Studies* 31: 91–96 (DOI 10.2307/2296188) is the theory of state-contingent claims a binary event contract instantiates; Kalshi's briefs do not cite it. The CFTC's own glossary (quoted in the D.D.C. opinion p. 2) says derivatives "provide a way to transfer market risk".
- Test: a definitional premise. It is true of the instrument class and says nothing about any particular contract's use. The CFTC's 2023 order attacked the particular contract (p. 18: payout "not tied in any way to actual or estimated losses incurred elsewhere").

**A2. Any event with economic consequences generates hedgeable risk; elections do, and (from 2025) so do sporting events.**
- Said: "Uncertainty surrounding these events poses economic risk, no less than uncertainty over hurricanes, pandemics, or oil supply." (Complaint ¶3, p. 2.) Furman via Kalshi: "This risk is conceptually identical to climate risk, business interruption risk, and other similar risks that can and should be managed using the financial markets." (SJ memo p. 6, quoting AR 1551.) For sports: "Sporting events likewise are associated with financial consequences—often significant ones—for stakeholders, including team sponsors, advertisers, television networks, franchises, local communities, and more. Sportsbooks have direct exposure to outcomes of sporting events, leading some to turn to Kalshi to 'hedge against volatility.'" (Kalshi 9th Cir. stay motion p. 12.) Mansour: "The second thing is, is this a real thing that's happening in the world, where some people may benefit from hedging?" (Uncapped.)
- Scholarship: for elections, Snowberg, Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2007), "Partisan Impacts on the Economy: Evidence from Prediction Markets and Close Elections", *QJE* 122(2): 807–829 (DOI 10.1162/qjec.122.2.807) and Knight (2006), *J. Public Econ.* 90: 751–773 (DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2005.06.003), both in Kalshi's record (SJ memo pp. 38–39 n.14), show equity prices move with electoral odds. For sports, Kalshi cites no scholarship; the one example is a sportsbook (Underdog) hedging its own book, from a trade-press item.
- Test: for sports, the premise would be supported by evidence that non-bookmaker commercial hedgers (franchises, broadcasters, local businesses) hold positions, or by the confidential risk-mitigation analysis in Appendices C–E of the self-certifications being made public. The Ninth Circuit's counter-claim is the hypothesis to beat: "Kalshi's sports event contracts do not help institutions or investors hedge against risk; they create risk, largely for ordinary consumers, where none previously existed" (Assad p. 29). *I infer* that Kalshi's own 2024 appellate brief is evidence against A2 for games: "contracts relating to games—again, activities conducted for diversion or amusement—are unlikely to serve any 'commercial or hedging interest'" (Appellee brief p. 45). Kalshi's footnote 13 (same page) is its reservation that the landscape changed after Murphy v. NCAA; it does not say the hedging interest changed.

**A3. Hedging means mitigating diffuse risk; it does not require, as insurance does, offsetting a precise loss. Binary payout and indirect transmission do not defeat it.**
- Said: "The point of hedging is to mitigate risk, not (like insurance) to offset precise losses. And risk is all about uncertainty." (SJ memo p. 36.) "An event contract would instead hedge the diffuse risk of losses associated with a hurricane" (Complaint ¶26, p. 9). Mansour: "Hedging is a little different from insurance. Insurance is usually regulated at the state level because there's also a house. So you go to an insurance company and they give you a price. Hedging is on the open market." (Uncapped.)
- Scholarship: the premise is textbook derivatives theory and needs no citation; the live question is empirical, whether anyone uses the contracts this way. The CFTC's 2023 order (p. 19) applied the pre-2000 "economic purpose" test ("more than an occasional basis"; "predominantly by market participants having a commercial or hedging interest"). The 2026 NPRM (p. 92) says the Commission "preliminarily does not believe that the Dodd-Frank Act reinstated the economic purpose test", and (p. 96) that "whether event contracts can be used directly for hedging is of limited importance in the public interest determination".
- Test: participant-level data on hedging versus speculative positions (the Commitments-of-Traders analogue), which Kalshi has not published. Mansour's "hurricane season" calls (Uncapped) are anecdote.

**A4. Speculators are necessary; a market that is mostly speculation is still a hedging market. The grain-futures precedent settles this.**
- Said: "not everyone who trades an event contract is hedging risk—some simply seek a return. That is true of all derivative markets; indeed, a robust market depends in part on the liquidity provided by speculators." (SJ memo p. 5.) "speculators provide necessary liquidity that hedgers cannot" (Appellee brief p. 56). Mansour: "When you look at the percentage of hedging versus speculation in any derivatives markets, like grain futures or commodity futures or stock options, it tends to be very tilted towards speculation. The reason you want speculation is you want liquidity." (Freakonomics.) "the one that legalized grain futures, the most boring financial market, the OG hedging market" (Uncapped; *I infer* the referent is Board of Trade v. Christie Grain & Stock Co., 198 U.S. 236 (1905); Mansour does not name the case, and I did not open it).
- Scholarship: Grossman and Stiglitz (1980), *AER* 70(3): 393–408, on why informed speculation is what makes prices informative (Crossref returned only secondary records for the title; DOI not resolved today, so this is cited from knowledge and flagged); Snowberg, Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2013), "Prediction Markets for Economic Forecasting", *Handbook of Economic Forecasting* 2: 657–687 (DOI 10.1016/B978-0-444-53683-9.00011-6), on liquidity and accuracy.
- Test: the premise is sound as far as it goes. What it cannot do is distinguish a hedging market with speculators from a speculation market with no hedgers; that requires A2/A3 evidence. *I infer* this is the load-bearing joint of Strand A: A4 is true of grain and is untested for sports.

**A5. The statute is categorical. The CEA presumptively permits event contracts; the CFTC may review only contracts that "involve" an enumerated activity; hedging use is irrelevant to that threshold.**
- Said: "Congress made the judgment that these derivatives should be presumptively permissible." (Complaint ¶5, p. 2.) "Hedging use is irrelevant to whether a contract falls into one of those enumerated categories at the first step." (Appellee brief p. 57.) Secondary (ANPRM letter via InGame): the "special rule does not require DCMs to prove a contract is in the public interest".
- Scholarship: a legal premise; the D.D.C. accepted it for elections (opinion p. 26). The Ninth and Sixth Circuits later held that sports contracts are not "swaps" at all, which sidesteps A5 (Assad p. 4; Orgel p. 3).
- Test: pending litigation (Fourth Circuit; possible certiorari) and the 2026 rulemaking.

**Conclusion A (Kalshi's).** Event contracts, including sports contracts, are legitimate derivatives whose listing the CFTC may not block absent an enumerated activity and a public-interest finding; they also serve the CEA's purposes of risk transfer and price discovery, though they need not prove it. *I infer* the strand is strong for elections and macro events (A2 has published evidence) and rests on A4 alone for sports, where Kalshi's own 2024 brief said the hedging interest is unlikely.

### Strand B: exchange versus sportsbook

**B1. On an exchange, traders contract with each other; the exchange is not a counterparty, and the market, not the house, sets the price.**
- Said: "event contracts do not reflect a 'bet' against the 'house.'" (NJ Complaint ¶21, p. 6.) "Traders do not bet against the house, but rather enter into contracts with other traders on an exchange." (Sottile decl. ¶27, p. 8.) "Their prices are determined by market forces, not by the exchange." (Complaint ¶22, p. 7.) Kalshi's brief as quoted by the Ninth Circuit: "Kalshi's DCM is not counterparty to any trade, does not set betting odds, does not profit when its customers lose, and has no incentives to favor itself at customers' expense." (Assad p. 29.) Kagan: "we don't set pricing. We don't set lines like a sportsbook does." (Freakonomics.)
- Scholarship: Levitt (2004), "Why are Gambling Markets Organised so Differently from Financial Markets?", *Economic Journal* 114(495): 223–246 (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2004.00207.x), is the canonical statement that bookmakers set prices and take positions while exchanges match orders, and that bookmakers can profit by exploiting bettor biases. Smith, Paton and Vaughan Williams (2006), "Market Efficiency in Person-to-Person Betting", *Economica* 73: 673–689 (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0335.2006.00518.x) and Franck, Verbeek and Nüesch (2010), *International Journal of Forecasting* 26: 448–459 (DOI 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2010.01.004) compare betting exchanges with bookmakers and find exchange prices at least as accurate and with less favourite-longshot bias. These papers support the structural distinction but were written about Betfair, a sports betting exchange: they show that an exchange can be a better betting venue, not that it is not a betting venue.
- Test: the Ninth Circuit's footnote: Kalshi Trading, "Kalshi's affiliate that acts as a 'significant player on the exchange,' largely as a market maker" (Assad p. 29 n.5), with the help-page reference removed after Nevada's brief. B1 is testable by disclosure of the affiliate's share of volume and its P&L. Also the D.C. Circuit's record that Kalshi allows "up to $100 million per contract" (stay opinion p. 6) and Mansour's own account that market makers "including Susquehanna" stand ready (secondary, CNBC 2024, not opened).

**B2. The exchange earns a fee independent of who wins; a sportsbook's revenue equals its customers' losses.**
- Said: "I take my 1% fee, regardless of who wins or loses." (Rotowire.) "Gambling is a business model where, like, the revenue of the company is equal to the customer's losses." (Sequoia.) "The losses of the customers are equal to the wins of the house, and vice versa." (Freakonomics.) "If your revenue equals your customers' losses, you have a different business model" (Boardroom).
- Scholarship: Levitt (2004) again on bookmaker economics. Whelan (2023), "On prices and returns in commercial prediction markets", *Quantitative Finance* (DOI 10.1080/14697688.2023.2257756; facet D, abstract only) shows a fee schedule itself produces a favourite-longshot pattern in post-fee returns. Kagan's "parametrically set, so lower fees at the tails, higher fees in the middle" (Freakonomics) matches the secondary fee formula in facet D (0.07 × C × (1 − C)).
- Test: true of the stated model. The test is whether the exchange's incentives under a volume fee differ in practice from a house's: a volume-fee venue also wants losers to keep trading. Mansour concedes the gap and offers conduct, not structure, to close it: "I have all the incentive in the world to basically throttle them" (Sequoia); "It's not my job to block or ban ... but to give them a lot of warning signs and stop marketing to them" (Freakonomics).

**B3. Because of B2, a sportsbook limits winners and courts losers; an exchange wants winners because they make prices accurate and supply volume.**
- Said: "Over time, what the house does is it figures out who are the big losers, and makes sure that they come back, and then figures out who are the winners, and make sure they don't come back." "Prediction markets, you want those guys. These are the people who will do a lot of volume, they're making the markets accurate" (Freakonomics). "If you're a winner on Kalshi over the last 10 to 12 months, you're still trading on Kalshi." "Could you go on DraftKings and make $30 million from them? It's impossible." (Rotowire.) NJ PI brief p. 8: "entering into event contracts with other sophisticated traders will ultimately net out."
- Scholarship: Shin (1991), "Optimal Betting Odds Against Insider Traders", *Economic Journal* 101: 1179–1185 (DOI 10.2307/2234434) models bookmakers protecting themselves against informed bettors, which is the theory behind limiting. On the exchange side, Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026, abstract) find "Makers — relatively well-informed traders who post offers — seek positive expected returns ... while Takers accept these offers", i.e. the exchange has winners and they are the liquidity suppliers. Kagan's "1 to 2.9" (Freakonomics) is Kalshi's own statement that about 26% of trades are profitable for the trader.
- Test: account-level retention of winners versus losers on Kalshi (the claim "you're still trading on Kalshi" is checkable from Kalshi's data and unverified). Also the NJ PI brief's citation, Shang, Duan and Lu (2021), *Journal of Economic Psychology* 84, is a paper about lay theories of gambling versus investment; it does not, from its title, establish that "tailored investment strategies on financial markets result in positive returns for all traders", and the claim as written is implausible for a zero-sum contract. (Not opened; flagged.)

**B4. The legal line follows structure, not subject matter: open, fair, liquid marketplace versus a closed system against a house. "The law is not ducks."**
- Said: "The core differentiator with sports gambling is whether you are trading on an open, fair, liquid marketplace or in a closed forum system against the house that picks winners or losers. One of them you can win." (Raskin.) "The law is not ducks. Because if it was, then gambling in grain would still be regulated at the state level." (Rotowire.) "there's a price discovery aspect to this where you're on an open, transparent exchange where people are trading against each other. That makes it a financial market." (Sequoia.)
- Scholarship: this is a legal and historical claim; the derivatives-versus-gambling line in US law is treated in the 2026 NPRM's history (pp. 21–33, skimmed) and in the courts' opinions. No peer-reviewed source is cited by Kalshi for it.
- Test: the courts split. Third Circuit (structure wins); Ninth and Sixth Circuits and Massachusetts (subject matter wins: "placing sports bets, even when called by another name, is still gambling", Assad p. 28). *I infer* that B4 is where Mansour's argument is strongest as policy and weakest as description of settled law: three of four appellate courts to rule on sports have rejected it, and the one that accepted it did so on preemption grounds without endorsing the structural claim.

**B5. The exchange's protections are the financial-market kind (KYC, surveillance, insider bans), and they work better than state gambling rules.**
- Said: "First, you have to K.Y.C. anyone that participate on the market. ... Number two is you run surveillance. ... Kalshi goes a step beyond that ... We proactively ban people from trading on things that they can have control over." (Freakonomics.) Self-certification Appendix B bars players, coaches, staff, league employees, owners and their households.
- Scholarship: Packin et al. (2026, SSRN 6913339; facet D, abstract only) argue the platforms act as "de facto regulators" of insider trading and that disclosure-centred approaches assume self-policing participants. Johnson et al. (2025), *Addiction* (DOI 10.1111/add.70272; facet D) note that state harm-minimisation rules do not apply to CFTC venues.
- Test: enforcement counts (Kalshi says it caught politicians trading their own races; the Oversight letter of 2026-05-22 cites four such cases) and the absence of self-exclusion and deposit-limit tools that state regimes require. Mansour's position is explicit that he does not block: "I believe in personal responsibility" (Freakonomics).

**Conclusion B (Mansour's).** Kalshi is a financial market because of its structure and incentives, not its subject matter; "it quacks like a duck" is answered by who sets the price and who profits from losses. *I infer* that B1–B3 are accurate descriptions of the mechanism as designed and that the untested claims are the behavioural ones: that winners stay, that losses "net out", that a fee venue does not want losers to keep losing.

### Strand C: price as public information

**C1. Prices aggregate dispersed information because traders with money at stake set them; the price is the market's real-time probability.**
- Said: "the prices of event contracts reflect the market's real-time probabilistic belief about whether the underlying event will occur." (SJ memo p. 4.) "a 'yes' contract that trades at 30 cents reflects that the market believes that there is a 30% chance of an earthquake this year." (NJ Complaint ¶23, p. 6.) "traders have a financial incentive to make the most accurate predictions based on the information they know." (Appellee brief p. 6.) Mansour: "Markets are a very good weighing function" (Uncapped, as extracted; the raw transcript has the passage at the "infinite markets" discussion).
- Scholarship: Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004), "Prediction Markets", *JEP* 18(2): 107–126 (DOI 10.1257/0895330041371321); Arrow et al. (2008), "The Promise of Prediction Markets", *Science* 320: 877–878 (DOI 10.1126/science.1157679); Hayek (1945) on prices as knowledge (the AER original did not resolve on Crossref today; reprints did). Against the strict reading: Manski (2006), "Interpreting the predictions of prediction markets", *Economics Letters* 91: 425–429 (DOI 10.1016/j.econlet.2006.01.004), shows the price of a binary contract identifies only an interval around the mean belief; Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2006), "Interpreting Prediction Market Prices as Probabilities", NBER w12200 (DOI 10.3386/w12200), show the gap is small under plausible risk aversion. Facet A covers these.
- Test: calibration studies. Berg, Nelson and Rietz (2008), *IJF* 24: 285–300 (DOI 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2008.03.007) for the IEM; Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026) for Kalshi: "informative ... but they display a clear favorite–longshot bias" (abstract). Diercks, Katz and Wright (2026) support the claim for macro contracts. *I infer* the premise holds on average and fails at the tails, which is where the "30 cents means 30%" sentence in Kalshi's complaint is literally false on Kalshi's own data (Bürgi et al.: "low-price contracts win far less often than required to break even").

**C2. Market prices beat polls and pundits; prices are "truth".**
- Said: "Kalshi is really the most accurate way to predict the future, which is pretty cool." (Freakonomics.) "Markets don't lie" (Boardroom). "Prediction markets are the new source of truth, free from bias." (Cointelegraph, 2024-10-20, quoting a post.) Appellee brief p. 2: election markets "have also become a ubiquitous market-based check on unreliable polling and misinformation." On the Fed: "The Fed itself is saying this is the best gauge we have on the economy." (Uncapped.)
- Scholarship: Berg et al. (2008) (IEM beat 964 polls 74% of the time at long horizons; facet A); Atanasov et al. (2017), *Management Science* (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2374; facet A) find well-aggregated polls beat markets. The FEDS paper's own abstract claims a "benchmark that is valuable to both researchers and policymakers", and its disclaimer says it "does not indicate concurrence ... by the Board of Governors". *I infer* that "the Fed itself is saying" misattributes staff research to the institution and "best gauge" is not in the abstract.
- Test: domain-specific calibration (Le 2026; facet D) and head-to-head accuracy against polls and models (Goel et al. 2010; facet A). "Truth" is not a testable predicate; accuracy is, and the record supports "informative" rather than "truth".

**C3. Skin in the game rewards research and calibrated judgment; the market is a public good that depolarises.**
- Said: "these calibrated, well-reasoned takes are not getting reward on social media, but they get reward in prediction markets." (Sequoia.) "They're rewarding someone going out there doing the research and doing the truth-seeking on behalf of society." (Uncapped.) "Maybe prediction markets can like help depolarize some of that. Like, climate change—real or not? Maybe let's ask a market." (Freakonomics.) Metric of success: "how often people quote a prediction market when thinking about the future versus quoting any other traditional source" (Raskin, as extracted by WebFetch; the raw text has Mansour's "one clean number" passage).
- Scholarship: Hanson (2013), "Shall We Vote on Values, But Bet on Beliefs?", *Journal of Political Philosophy* 21(2) (DOI 10.1111/jopp.12008), is the normative case for using markets to settle factual disputes; Hanson appears in the same Freakonomics episode. Rothschild and Sethi (2016), *Journal of Prediction Markets* 10(1): 1–29 (DOI 10.5750/jpm.v10i1.1179), document a 2012 manipulation attempt that failed, which both the D.C. Circuit (stay opinion pp. 13–14) and Kalshi use.
- Test: whether exposure to market prices changes beliefs or polarisation is an empirical question with, as far as facets A–C found, no field study. The premise is a hypothesis.

**C4. Pricing many questions lowers "entropy" and improves the pricing of traditional assets ("infinite markets").**
- Said: "Kevin Hassett wrote about this a few years ago, about this idea that as society gets more and more complicated, the entropy in the system goes up. ... Having prediction markets on all these different questions will enable us to have a better gauge, lower the entropy in those questions" (Freakonomics). "Prediction markets can price all these different factors that then feed into the stock price." (Uncapped.)
- Scholarship: this is Arrow (1964) complete markets in a new vocabulary, and Hanson (2003), "Combinatorial Information Market Design", *Information Systems Frontiers* 5(1): 107–119 (DOI 10.1023/A:1022058209073) on the design problem of many linked markets. Hassett's piece was not located and is a lead.
- Test: whether event-contract prices are used as inputs to asset pricing (the "price-basing" claim, SJ memo p. 37; the NPRM's version at pp. 94–96). Facet A found corporate and policy uses in the literature but no study of retail platform prices being used this way.

**C5. The sports share is an on-ramp, not the point; the non-sports share is growing faster.**
- Said: "Late last year, the numbers were generally 95% of the volume on sports. The number is much lower now." (Rotowire.) "I think the latest numbers I was looking at were basically at two-thirds, one-third now." (Raskin.) "I've always felt like sports would be a great sort of gateway into some of the other things." (Rotowire.) "sports are scheduled and organized well and put on a calendar. People are used to that." (Raskin.)
- Scholarship: none cited. The Rotowire article's own figures put sports and sports combos at $128.3 billion of $179.4 billion in 2026 to date (about 71%), consistent with "two-thirds" if the two-thirds is sports.
- Test: Kalshi's published category volumes. Mansour's own two figures (95% "late last year"; two-thirds/one-third now) are the best available first-party statements of the volume mix the essay's section 2 needs; both are oral and unaudited.

**Conclusion C (Mansour's).** The exchange's output is a public good, a price that is the best available estimate of an event's probability, and that good justifies the venue whatever the motives of the traders who produce it. *I infer* the strand is well supported for "informative" and unsupported for "truth"; and that its policy force depends on C5, since the public-information value of a table-tennis moneyline is not argued anywhere in the record.

### What I infer about the structure of the whole

Mansour's three strands are not independent. Strand B (structure) is offered as the sufficient condition for being a financial market; Strand A (hedging) is offered as the CEA's purpose but disclaimed as a test; Strand C (information) is offered as the social return. For elections and macro contracts all three have support in the record. For sports, Kalshi's own filings supply B only: the self-certifications keep the economic analysis confidential, the state complaints hedge earthquakes rather than games, the one sports-hedging example is a bookmaker, and the 2024 appellate brief said game contracts are "unlikely to serve any 'commercial or hedging interest.'" Mansour's interviews fill the gap with A4 (speculation is normal) and C5 (sports is a gateway). The essay should state this as what the record shows, not as a contradiction Mansour has conceded; his consistent position since 2025 is that structure, not subject, is the test, and the Third Circuit agreed with him on the law.
