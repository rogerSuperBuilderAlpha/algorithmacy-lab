# H: Robin Hanson's arguments on prediction and decision markets

*Facet H of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 (seat H of the eight-seat deep-research run). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

What this facet covers. Hanson's academic work on prediction markets, decision markets and market scoring rules, read in full text where a full text could be obtained, plus a separate tier of his recent public writing on Kalshi, Polymarket and sports betting. The reconstruction answers four questions. (a) What a decision market is, as distinct from a prediction market, in his terms. (b) Who pays for the information, and what he says about unsubsidised markets and the topics they gravitate to. (c) What he says about manipulation, thin markets and noise traders. (d) What his framework implies about a commercial market whose volume is mostly sports, and whether he has said so himself. The facet closes with the strongest reply his work offers to the essay's thesis.

`hanson1995gambling` | Hanson, R. (1995). *Could Gambling Save Science? Encouraging an Honest Consensus*. Social Epistemology, 9(1), 3-33.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1080/02691729508578768 (Crossref: vol 9 iss 1 pp 3-33, issued 1995-01). Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/gamble.html, which is the 1990 conference text ("To appear: Proc. Eighth Intl. Conf. on Risk and Gambling, London, July 1990"), the version the vita says "first appeared" in Eadington and Cornelius (1992). The journal pagination was not reached.
- *Read status:* full text, author pre-publication version; locators are the paper's all-caps section headings. Also read: *Reply to Comments* (Social Epistemology 9(1):45-48), https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/gamble-reply.html, full text.
- *What it argues:* Academia rewards "being popular, rather than being right" (abstract). The remedy is an "idea futures" market: "Imagine a betting pool or market on most disputed science questions, with the going odds available to the popular media, and treated socially as the current academic consensus" (THE PROPOSAL). Funding runs through subsidy: "Imagine that funding agencies subsidize pools on questions of interest to them" (THE PROPOSAL), and "By funding an automatic inventory-based [St] market-maker, which always offers to buy or sell at prices determined solely by its current inventory, one gives away money only to those who move the market price in the direction of its final verdict" (SOCIAL ATTITUDES). The paper then works through more than thirty objections, including thin markets, compulsive gambling, fools, wealth, manipulation, moral hazard and insider trading.
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports and complicates. Two passages bear directly on question (b). On which topics commercial bookmakers take: "English bookmakers perceive little demand for science bets, and so take them mainly to induce popular articles mentioning the going odds on unusual subjects [ShG]. This publicity brings in new clients, who may then switch to the 'real' betting on sports. Because of this, bookies prefer small bets on subjects 'in good taste' that anyone can understand, like UFOs, Yetis, and Moon landings. They avoid subjects that seem too esoteric for the general public, like the recent 'cold fusion' claims, and subjects that won't very clearly resolve themselves, as a judging industry has not yet evolved" (IF THIS IS SO GREAT, WHY HASN'T IT HAPPENED ALREADY?). On why betting clusters on sports: "Most organized betting focuses on questions which, like sporting events, will become very clearly resolved in a fixed time. This minimizes disputed verdicts and judging costs, and so it makes sense for risk and entertainment seeking bettors to focus on such subjects" (DOESN'T BETTING ONLY WORK FOR CLEAR CUT QUESTIONS LIKE HORSE RACES?). The compulsive-gambling section argues science questions "are generally too long term to be a problem, offering no more 'action' than long-term stock investments" (WHAT ABOUT COMPULSIVE GAMBLING?), a condition that fast-resolving sports contracts do not meet. The Reply adds that a subsidised market maker lets a researcher "expect to profit even if no one else ever traded in these markets" and calls the market-maker bankroll an "information prize".

`hanson1999decision` | Hanson, R. D. (1999). *Decision Markets*. IEEE Intelligent Systems, 14(3), 16-19.
- *Record:* The essay is part of the "Trends and Controversies" department article indexed by Crossref as 10.1109/5254.769877, "Building intelligent systems one e-citizen at a time", IEEE Intelligent Systems 14(3):16-20, with authors recorded as Hearst, "Hunson" [sic] and Stork (Crossref ISSN filter, search log row 5). Hanson's essay occupies pp. 16-19 under its own title and byline. Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/decisionmarkets.pdf (publisher scan, 5 pages).
- *Read status:* full text, publisher version; page locators are journal pages.
- *What it argues:* Speculative markets "pool the information that is known to diverse individuals into a common resource, and have many advantages over standard institutions for information aggregation, such as news media, peer review, trials, and opinion polls" (p. 16). "I suggest we consider decision markets, which are speculative markets focusing on particular decisions" (p. 17). The worked case is a hidden-gun bill: markets on the murder rate conditional on the bill passing and on it not passing; "comparing these two estimates tells you whether, and by how much, speculators expect this bill to increase or decrease the murder rate" (p. 17). The six design steps are: state the claim, pick a judge, pick the base asset, authorise a bank to split it into four contingent assets, create markets, and "decide how much to subsidize this market" (p. 17). On funding: "If interest in your topic is strong enough, simply creating these markets might induce people to trade in them. Failing that, sufficient interest might be induced if someone committed to make a policy choice based on the market estimate... You can also safely and directly subsidize a market to induce more participation. Doing this in effect creates an information prize offered to those who first make the market price better reflect relevant information" (p. 17). On thinness: "a subsidized market can function over any time period with only one trader" (p. 18). On legality: "Accepted functions of markets now include entertainment, capitalization, and hedging, but not information aggregation" (p. 18). The essay ends by recommending internal corporate markets as the "one plausible loophole" (p. 19).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports the essay's distinction between the price's consumer and its producer, since the reader of the price in the example is a state legislature that "might accept this estimate when considering whether or not to pass this hidden-gun bill" (p. 17), not a trader. It is also the source for the information-prize framing of subsidy.

`hanson2003combinatorial` | Hanson, R. (2003). *Combinatorial Information Market Design*. Information Systems Frontiers, 5(1), 107-119.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1023/A:1022058209073 (Crossref: vol 5 iss 1 pp 107-119, issued 2003-01). The vita and Hanson's own later citations give pp. 105-119; the publisher PDF header reads "Information Systems Frontiers 5:1, 107-119, 2003", so 107-119 is correct. Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/combobet.pdf (publisher layout).
- *Read status:* full text, publisher version; page locators are journal pages.
- *What it argues:* Information markets "were created not to entertain or to hedge risks but to aggregate information on particular topics of interest" (p. 107). Two problems "plague standard information markets" (abstract): the thin market problem ("traders must coordinate on the assets they will trade, even though there are an astronomical number of possible events", p. 107) and the irrational participation problem ("Once rational agents have hedged their risks regarding the events covered by an information market, they should not want to trade with each other", p. 108). Market scoring rules "become automated market makers in the thick market case and simple scoring rules in the thin market case" (abstract). The patron's cost is bounded: "the patron should set the initial report to his initial beliefs q, in which case his worst case expected loss is" b times the entropy of q (p. 112). Decision-contingent estimates "can give us relatively direct advice about what choices to make (Hanson, 1999)" (p. 108). The conclusion names the gap: "The main category of issues not dealt with in this paper is that of user interfaces" (p. 118).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports question (b): the design presupposes "patrons willing to pay a modest per-variable cost to induce information revelation" (p. 108), and it classifies sports betting among the entertainment exemptions: "sports betting markets enhance entertainment from watching sporting events" (p. 107). It also supplies the essay's hook that the designer of the mechanism flagged the interface as the unaddressed problem (p. 118).

`hanson2007logarithmic` | Hanson, R. (2007). *Logarithmic Market Scoring Rules for Modular Combinatorial Information Aggregation*. The Journal of Prediction Markets, 1(1), 3-15.
- *Record:* DOI 10.5750/jpm.v1i1.417. Year corrected from 2012 to 2007. Evidence: the vita lists "Journal of Prediction Markets 1(1):3-15, February, 2007"; the journal's own archive lists "Vol. 1 No. 1 (2007)" with the paper at pp. 3-15 (https://www.ubplj.org/index.php/jpm/issue/view/49); the Crossref record carries vol 1 iss 1 pp 3-15 but an issued date of 2012-12-13 and a created date of 2020-08-26, and the issue page shows "Published: 2012-12-19", which is when the back archive went online. The Crossref title is misspelled "LOGARITHMIC MARKETS CORING RULES". Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/mktscore.pdf, the January 2002 working paper (12 pages); the journal pagination was not reached.
- *Read status:* full text, author preprint (January 2002); locators are section names and preprint pages.
- *What it argues:* "In practice, scoring rules elicit good probability estimates from individuals, while betting markets elicit good consensus estimates from groups. Market scoring rules combine these features, eliciting estimates from individuals or groups, with groups costing no more than individuals" (abstract). "The cost to create a market scoring rule depends only on the informativeness of the last report given it, realtive [sic] to its starting position, and does not otherwise depend on how often or how many people use it. In contrast with a standard betting market, rational agents should expect a positive profit from participating, and do not need to find another person willing to make a matching bet" (Introduction, preprint p. 2). The market maker "has a zero bid-ask spread, at least for infinitesimal trades" (Market Scoring Rules, preprint p. 5). "A principal funding the scoring rule minimizes her expected payments by setting the initial report to her initial beliefs" (Costs, preprint p. 6). Theorems 1 and 2 show the logarithmic rule is the only rule under which a bet on A given B leaves p(B) unchanged (Modularity, preprint pp. 7-8).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports (b): the mechanism is built around a funder who pays a bounded, entropy-sized subsidy, which is the opposite of a fee-charging order book. It also supports the essay's contrast with Kalshi and Polymarket, whose continuous double auctions are what this design was meant to replace in thin markets.

`hanson2013vote` | Hanson, R. (2013). *Shall We Vote on Values, But Bet on Beliefs?*. Journal of Political Philosophy, 21(2), 151-178.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1111/jopp.12008 (Crossref: vol 21 iss 2 pp 151-178, 2013). Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/futarchy2013.pdf, the accepted manuscript (sections I-XIV, twenty-five numbered objections, endnotes i-lxi); journal pagination not reached.
- *Read status:* full text, author manuscript; locators are section numbers and objection numbers.
- *What it argues:* "we could 'vote on values, but bet on beliefs'" (Introduction). "The basic rule of government would be this: when speculative markets clearly estimate that a proposed policy would increase national welfare, that policy becomes law" (Introduction). Section IV gives the mechanics via called-off trades: "a market price of $0.60 can be interpreted as a consensus among traders that p(D) ≈ 60%" (§IV), and "Standard decision theory says to take the action associated with the highest action-conditional expected utility. When the decision market estimate of E[W|N] is clearly greater than its estimate of E[W|Q], speculators are saying they expect more national welfare, given this new policy" (§IV). Section VIII treats advisory markets as the "simplest way" but warns that "without a subsidy, too few may volunteer to pay to create such better info sources" and that in both simple approaches "there is a serious risk that the decision markets will be ignored" (§VIII). On manipulation: "Manipulators, who trade hoping to distort prices, are noise traders, since they trade for reasons other than asset value info. Thus adding manipulators to speculative markets doesn't reduce average price accuracy" (§II). On thinness: "A market known to influence important policies, however, simply cannot be very thin" (Objection 8). On accuracy: "My claim is not that betting prices are always more accurate than other sources, but that they are a robustly accurate public institution estimating policy-relevant topics" (§II). On the long-shot bias: "betting markets typically overestimate low probability, or 'long shot,' events, though with low transaction costs specialists usually eliminate this bias" (§II).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports the essay's reading that Hanson's decision market needs a decision-maker bound to the price (§VIII, §IX), which a retail exchange does not supply. Complicates the thesis by stating the strongest pro-market accuracy case and the "compare to institutions" standard.

`hanson2006information` | Hanson, R., Oprea, R., & Porter, D. (2006). *Information Aggregation and Manipulation in an Experimental Market*. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 60(4), 449-459.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2004.09.011 (Crossref: vol 60 iss 4 pp 449-459, 2006; authors Hanson, Oprea, Porter). Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/biastest.pdf, dated July 12, 2005 (15 pages).
- *Read status:* full text, author preprint; locators are section names and preprint pages.
- *What it argues:* Eight sessions of twelve undergraduates (96 subjects, September 2003 to April 2004) traded a common-value asset worth 0, 40 or 100 in a double auction; in the manipulation treatment half the subjects received two units per point of the period's median price (§2, preprint pp. 5-6). "Result 1 Manipulators submit higher bids than non-manipulators" (preprint p. 8). "Result 2 The presence of manipulators does not harm the information aggregation properties of trade; manipulation has no significant effect on the accuracy of price or the dependence of price on state" (preprint p. 9). "Result 3 Non-manipulators tend to accept contracts at lower prices in the MT treatment than in the RT treatment" (preprint p. 11; median accepted price 60.9 under RT against 55 under MT, p. 12). The authors caution: "Although these results are suggestive, they are by no means conclusive... Our design is specialized in that all participants know (i) that manipulators are present, (ii) how strong the incentive to manipulate is, and (iii) in what direction manipulators have incentives to push price" (Conclusion, preprint p. 13).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports the benign lab finding on manipulation and records its own scope limits. Aggregation itself was weak: "Aggregation is far from perfect; α is high and γ is a quarter of what it would be with perfect aggregation" (preprint p. 10).

`hanson2009manipulator` | Hanson, R., & Oprea, R. (2009). *A Manipulator Can Aid Prediction Market Accuracy*. Economica, 76(302), 304-314.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0335.2008.00734.x (Crossref: vol 76 iss 302 pp 304-314, 2009). Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/biashelp.pdf, dated December 2007 (19 pages).
- *Read status:* full text, author preprint.
- *What it argues:* "Prediction markets are low volume speculative markets whose prices offer informative forecasts on particular policy topics" (abstract). The model adapts a one-period Kyle market and adds a manipulator with a quadratic preference over the price: "when other traders are uncertain about the manipulator's target price, the mean target price has no effect on prices, and increases in the variance of the target price can increase average price accuracy, by increasing the returns to informed trading" (abstract). Theorem 2: "Changes in the mean manipulator bias w̄ have no effect whatsoever on prices. When the variance of manipulator bias Sw and bias clue error Sδ increase in the same proportion, trader asset clue variance S and price error Φ both decrease" (preprint p. 11). "Thus liquidity trading is not required to produce a prediction market equilibrium; a manipulator with unknown bias or any trader irrationality can substitute as a source of noise trading" (preprint p. 10). On noise traders: "a manipulative trader is in essence a 'noise' trader, in the sense that his trades are based on considerations other than his best estimate of asset value" (Introduction, preprint pp. 2-3). On limits: "The typical thinness of prediction markets, however, does seem to make it cheap for participants to mislead markets with their trades" (Introduction, preprint p. 2); "budget constraints are clearly an important feature of many real prediction markets... manipulation may be more feasible when the first group is less constrained than the second" (Discussion, preprint p. 14). Conclusion: "concerns about manipulators reducing average price accuracy may be overstated" (preprint p. 15).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports question (c). It is also the theory behind Hanson's claim that fun or noise trading raises accuracy, which the essay must answer when it treats sports volume as noise.

`hanson2006terrorism` | Hanson, R. (2006). *Designing Real Terrorism Futures*. Public Choice, 128(1-2), 257-274.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1007/s11127-006-9053-9 (Crossref: vol 128 iss 1-2 pp 257-274, 2006). Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/realterf.pdf, dated August 2005 (22 pages).
- *Read status:* full text, author preprint.
- *What it argues:* PAM "was not intended to forecast the details of terrorist attacks" (Introduction, preprint p. 2). The paper treats five design issues: combinatorics, manipulation, moral hazard, hiding prices and decision selection bias. On decision markets: "Prediction markets can forecast more than just possible events, like elections or terrorist attacks. Decision markets can also advise policy choices via decision-conditional outcome estimates (Hanson, 1999)" (Basic Terrorism Futures, preprint p. 8). On cost and topic: "Since the cost of creating a market is largely independent of the topic, the obvious place to look for high benefit-relative-to-cost applications is in advising our most important policy questions" (preprint p. 8). On the interface: "The first problem to confront would be a serious user-interface problem. Traders would need effective ways to browse the current market prices for these billions of possibilities" (Combinatorics, preprint p. 10). On manipulation: "unless the informed traders have very shallow pockets, or are prevented from trading as much as they want, manipulation efforts should on average make prediction markets more accurate. The key thing to understand is that all known speculative markets have a lot of 'noise trading,' and a manipulator is mainly just another kind of noise trader" (Manipulation, preprint p. 12); "Real markets, however, are full of fools, mental mistakes, and people trading for non-information reasons. In fact, in most markets the opportunity to win against such noise traders is the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders" (preprint p. 12). On harm versus error: "there is still a logical possibility that they might also increase the harm from price errors" (preprint p. 13). The PAM subsidy was $50,000 and "individual bets were to be limited to a few tens of dollars" (footnote 6, preprint p. 11). Decision selection bias: "decision-contingent prices can be misleading when speculators suspect that the decision maker may have relevant information that they do not" (preprint p. 18).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports (c) and (a). The explicit statement that noise traders are "the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders" is the passage the essay must confront if it treats sports bettors as noise.

`hanson2008insider` | Hanson, R. (2008). *Insider Trading and Prediction Markets*. Journal of Law, Economics and Policy, 4(2), 449-463.
- *Record:* No Crossref or OpenAlex record (search log rows 3 and 6). Volume, issue and pages are from the vita ("4(2):449-463, Spring 2008") and are unverified against a second database. Copy read: https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/insiderbet.pdf, dated November 2007 (15 pages; "First version January 2007").
- *Read status:* full text, author preprint; metadata unverified.
- *What it argues:* Insider trading laws "present a substantial barrier" to corporate prediction markets (§I). Section IV defines the two kinds of internal market the essay needs: play-money "morale markets" at firms "such as Google, where ordinary employees in their free time trade on topics that ordinary employees find fun and interesting. Higher management does not suggest topics for these markets, or pay attention to who wins and loses" (§IV), against "decision markets" where "Where management support is stronger, more serious 'decision markets' are being tried. These markets tend to be on topics chosen by management to be useful inputs to important decisions, and trading in them is often limited to a small group of managers or related experts" (§IV.A). On volume: "Prediction markets tend to be very low volume markets, as they usually have few 'hedging' traders. However, this does not prevent them from having informative prices. Prediction markets only need enough volume to induce a few people who have relevant information to trade and thereby reveal their information" (§IV). On motives: "They might expect to profit financially, either because they disagree or because someone has subsidized trading there. They might trade in order to express a point of view, to share their feelings with a larger community, or even to acquire the right to brag about their accuracy" (§IV). On the trading barrier: "People complain that it is hard for ordinary managers to understand how to trade, and that market prices distribute key information to an uncomfortably wide audience. The problem of understanding how to trade can be overcome with education and better trading interfaces" (§IV.A). Results of decision-market trials "have been mixed" (§IV.A).
- *Bearing on Part 3:* supports the essay strongly. Hanson here separates a market whose topics are chosen for fun (a morale market) from a decision market whose topics are chosen for a decision, and names "how to trade" as the barrier that "better trading interfaces" should remove. The thesis can cite his own category of fun-topic markets without bending him.

## (c) Argument reconstruction

Each claim carries a source key, a locator, and a short quotation copied from the text I read. Items marked **inference** are mine, not Hanson's.

### (a) What a decision market is, as distinct from a prediction market

1. A prediction market trades contracts on an uncertain event and its price is read as a probability. `arrow2008promise` p. 877: "Prediction markets are forums for trading contracts that yield payments based on the outcome of uncertain events." `hanson2013vote` §IV: "a market price of $0.60 can be interpreted as a consensus among traders that p(D) ≈ 60%."

2. A decision market is a set of such markets whose contracts are conditional on a decision, so that the difference between conditional prices estimates the decision's effect. `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "I suggest we consider decision markets, which are speculative markets focusing on particular decisions"; and, after the two conditional prices, "comparing these two estimates tells you whether, and by how much, speculators expect this bill to increase or decrease the murder rate." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 1: "Decision markets are information markets designed to inform a particular policy decision, by directly estimating relevant consequences of that decision." His 2026 one-line definition is "speculative markets that advise specific decisions by estimating decision-conditional outcomes" (OB 2026-04-25, NOT peer-reviewed tier).

3. The distinction is one of what is forecast, not of mechanism. `hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 8: "Prediction markets can forecast more than just possible events, like elections or terrorist attacks. Decision markets can also advise policy choices via decision-conditional outcome estimates (Hanson, 1999)."

4. The decision rule is standard decision theory applied to the conditional prices. `hanson2013vote` §IV: "Standard decision theory says to take the action associated with the highest action-conditional expected utility. When the decision market estimate of E[W|N] is clearly greater than its estimate of E[W|Q], speculators are saying they expect more national welfare, given this new policy." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 10: "These summary expectations are all that should matter, not the details that produce them."

5. A decision market has a consumer who is not a trader. `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "most nonexperts would accept this estimate as our 'best answer' or as a neutral 'consensus.' In particular, a state legislature might accept this estimate when considering whether or not to pass this hidden-gun bill."

6. A decision market has requirements a plain prediction market does not: distinct options, measured outcomes, decision-insider traders, a decision tied to the price, and a short decision window. `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint pp. 9-15 (the fifteen-item list). `hanson2013vote` §VIII: "To avoid such decision selection biases, it is sufficient either to have informed insiders trade in the markets, so that prices reflect what decision makers know, or to put market estimates directly in control of decisions, rather than being advisory."

7. Advisory decision markets that bind no one risk being ignored. `hanson2013vote` §VIII: "in both cases there is a serious risk that the decision markets will be ignored." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 16: "decision markets have not yet gained enough credibility in the public mind to be an attractive forum in which to argue for particular policy choices."

8. Within organisations Hanson separates fun-topic markets from decision markets. `hanson2008insider` §IV: morale markets are where "ordinary employees in their free time trade on topics that ordinary employees find fun and interesting. Higher management does not suggest topics for these markets, or pay attention to who wins and loses"; §IV.A: decision markets "tend to be on topics chosen by management to be useful inputs to important decisions."

### (b) Who pays for the information

9. Hanson's canonical funder is a patron who subsidises an automated market maker, which he calls an information prize. `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "You can also safely and directly subsidize a market to induce more participation. Doing this in effect creates an information prize offered to those who first make the market price better reflect relevant information." `hanson1995gambling` SOCIAL ATTITUDES: "one gives away money only to those who move the market price in the direction of its final verdict."

10. The subsidy is bounded and does not grow with the number of traders. `hanson2007logarithmic` Introduction, preprint p. 2: "The cost to create a market scoring rule depends only on the informativeness of the last report given it, realtive [sic] to its starting position, and does not otherwise depend on how often or how many people use it." `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 112: the patron's "worst case expected loss" is b times the entropy of his prior.

11. A second funder is a decision-maker who commits to act on the price. `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "sufficient interest might be induced if someone committed to make a policy choice based on the market estimate." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 12: "one can ensure market prices exist either via a large enough financial subsidy to fund an automated market maker, or via a large enough chance market prices would influence important decisions."

12. Without a subsidy, rational traders should not trade at all once hedged, so unsubsidised markets run on irrationality, signalling or fun. `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 108: "Once rational agents have hedged their risks regarding the events covered by an information market, they should not want to trade with each other... Now real markets do show surprisingly high levels of speculative activity, perhaps due to widespread irrationality, or to people using bets to signal their expertise to observers (as in 'bar bets')." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 12: traders "might trade for insurance, for fun and sport, or because of an irrational overconfidence in their opinions."

13. Hanson names the two business models explicitly and calls the fee-for-trading model "gamified" and a "mooch". OB 2012-07-19 (NOT peer-reviewed): "Intrade is a typical gamified market – you might pay Intrade a little to put up a question, and if traders like it they'll pay Intrade to answer your question. A market-maker-driven corporate prediction market ... is very different – to answer key business questions, firms pay lots to create markets, to fund employee participants, and to subsidize market makers." And: "It is only when you want people to answer your question for free that you'll have to piggyback on their having some other reason to trade."

14. Unsubsidised commercial betting gravitates to fast-resolving, easily understood topics, and he said so in 1990. `hanson1995gambling` DOESN'T BETTING ONLY WORK FOR CLEAR CUT QUESTIONS LIKE HORSE RACES?: "Most organized betting focuses on questions which, like sporting events, will become very clearly resolved in a fixed time. This minimizes disputed verdicts and judging costs, and so it makes sense for risk and entertainment seeking bettors to focus on such subjects." IF THIS IS SO GREAT, WHY HASN'T IT HAPPENED ALREADY?: bookmakers take odd bets for publicity that "brings in new clients, who may then switch to the 'real' betting on sports."

15. He classifies sports betting under the entertainment function, not the information function. `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 107: "sports betting markets enhance entertainment from watching sporting events." `hanson1999decision` p. 18: "Accepted functions of markets now include entertainment, capitalization, and hedging, but not information aggregation."

16. He has been sceptical of public-question markets sold to the public since at least 2017. OB 2017-09-19 (NOT peer-reviewed): "I'm not that optimistic about markets on broad public questions, sold to the public, relative to markets on specific organization questions, funded by those organizations. I'm skeptical that there is much public demand for betting on odd questions, or that such markets do much to promote the more promising organizational markets."

17. In 2026 he still describes the retail model as traders paying a tax and consumers getting the price free, and says he would rather the consumer paid. WhoWhatWhy transcript, 2026-05-08 (NOT peer-reviewed): "At the moment, financial markets are pretty much all. The customer is the trader who just pays a tax in order to be allowed to trade, and all the information that it's produced is a side effect that everybody can get for free. I would rather people who get the information pay for it."

### (c) Manipulation, thin markets and noise traders

18. A manipulator is a noise trader. `hanson2009manipulator` preprint pp. 2-3: "a manipulative trader is in essence a 'noise' trader, in the sense that his trades are based on considerations other than his best estimate of asset value."

19. Expected manipulation raises average accuracy because it raises the return to being informed. `hanson2009manipulator` abstract: "increases in the variance of the target price can increase average price accuracy, by increasing the returns to informed trading and thereby incentives for traders to become informed." `hanson2013vote` §II: "the most accurate real speculative markets tend to be those with the most noise trading."

20. The claim is ex ante, not ex post, and it carries stated conditions. `hanson2009manipulator` preprint p. 13: "Holding constant all other trading behavior, it is clear that ex post manipulation must be feasible... This does not, however, imply the feasibility of ex ante manipulation." `hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 12: "unless the informed traders have very shallow pockets, or are prevented from trading as much as they want, manipulation efforts should on average make prediction markets more accurate." `hanson2009manipulator` preprint p. 14: "manipulation may be more feasible when the first group is less constrained than the second."

21. The lab result is a null effect on accuracy with a documented offset by non-manipulators, and the authors call it inconclusive. `hanson2006information` preprint p. 9 (Result 2) and p. 13: "Although these results are suggestive, they are by no means conclusive."

22. Lower error does not guarantee lower harm. `hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 13: "there is still a logical possibility that they might also increase the harm from price errors." The fix is to trade on parameters "as close as possible to the actual decision parameters of interest" (preprint p. 14).

23. Thin markets are a coordination problem that the market scoring rule removes; a subsidised market can run with one trader. `hanson2003combinatorial` pp. 107-108 (thin market problem); `hanson1999decision` p. 18: "a subsidized market can function over any time period with only one trader."

24. Thinness is what makes cheap manipulation possible in the first place. `hanson2009manipulator` preprint p. 2: "The typical thinness of prediction markets, however, does seem to make it cheap for participants to mislead markets with their trades."

25. A market that steers an important decision cannot stay thin, because the stakes attract both sides. `hanson2013vote` Objection 8: "A market known to influence important policies, however, simply cannot be very thin."

26. Noise traders are also the subsidy in disguise. `hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 12: "in most markets the opportunity to win against such noise traders is the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders." OB 2012-07-19: "Noise traders are traders who subsidize your market for free, for reasons of their own, such as risk-hedging, idiocy, etc."

27. Accuracy rests on the best-informed, not the average, participant, so the typical trader "need not be informed or even rational." `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 11-12: "the accuracy of speculative markets depends more on the most informed participants; the typical person able to trade need not be informed or even rational."

28. The long-shot bias is a leverage and transaction-cost effect that combinatorial design can cut. `hanson2013vote` §II; `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 14; OB 2025-06-23 (NOT peer-reviewed): "in a combinatorial betting market everyone can bet up to the limits of their risk tolerance... and so there is no longer a leverage problem to bias the prices for long-shots."

### (d) What the framework implies about a commercial market whose volume is mostly sports

What he says.

29. He does not object to sports betting and fears the backlash more than the product. OB 2025-12-07: "I don't personally mind people having fun knowingly betting on sports, on actions that celebrities can influence, or on topics where insiders have big info advantages. But I see many people complaining about these things, and I fear a new prudish temperance movement may shut them down, and as a side effect shut down the more promising markets that I've envisioned." OB 2026-04-13: "Not my thing, but I usually don't mind others having fun via their things."

30. He values Kalshi and Polymarket chiefly as cost reducers for the markets he wants, and expresses low confidence that their current topics inform decisions. OB 2025-12-07: "The politics/policy questions on Polymarket and Kalshi trending now seem plausibly like topics where some people might find their estimate useful in making personal or collective choices. But I don't have great confidence in that, or know who those people are. However, if these systems continue to grow in size, and to attract users and competitors, they could lower the many costs of creating and managing such markets."

31. He lists fun as one of three market functions and separates it from the information function he cares about. OB 2026-04-29 (his CFTC comment): "prediction markets can serve many functions, such as moving and cutting risk, collecting and sharing information, and the fun of action and proving yourself in competition... I want these markets to grow toward that potential. While I personally don't mind people having fun, I see that others mind, and we might have to compromise there."

32. His own harm-benefit rule puts fast, easy, familiar topics on the harm side and long-term technical topics on the benefit side. OB 2025-10-09: "Gambling has more potential harms, at least to those who dislike it, when bets are easy to make, quickly resolved, on topics that ordinary people feel they understand... Gambling has more potential gains on topics that are more technical and take longer to resolve, and when it can serve substantial risk-hedging and information aggregation functions. Thus: allow betting on long-term technical topics that matter."

33. In 2008 he co-signed a proposal whose safe harbor excluded sports contracts, capped deposits near $2,000 a year, barred brokers and paid advisers, and required not-for-profit exchanges. `arrow2008promise` p. 878 (quoted in the entry). In 2017 he predicted sports would be a leading use of blockchain prediction markets and doubted it would count as a "legal laudible" application (OB 2017-09-19).

34. He has not said that Kalshi or Polymarket are sports betting in the dress of prediction markets, and he has not written about their interfaces. In the eighteen posts and the transcripts read, the nearest he comes is the three-function statement (claim 31) and the 2012 "mooch" distinction (claim 13). I found no sentence of his on bet slips, order types, price display or what a user is asked to do on either platform.

What I infer (marked as inference).

35. **Inference.** By his own taxonomy a sports-dominated exchange is an entertainment market with an information by-product, not a decision market: sports contracts fall under the entertainment function (claim 15), no decision-maker is bound to the price (claim 11), and the funder is the trader paying a fee rather than a patron paying an information prize (claims 13 and 17). On his 2006 requirements list (claim 6) such a market fails "decision-insider traders", "enough influence" over a decision, and the decision window; it is a prediction market by his definition, and only an advisory one.

36. **Inference.** His noise-trader theory cuts against the essay's instinct to call sports volume a distortion. Within a given market, more fun trading should raise accuracy (claims 19 and 26). Whether fun trading in sports markets subsidises accuracy in political or policy markets on the same exchange is a cross-market claim his model does not make; his model is single-asset (`hanson2009manipulator`, Model section).

37. **Inference.** His 1990 account of bookmakers predicts the volume mix the essay reports: odd questions as publicity, sports as the "real" betting (claim 14). He wrote this of William Hill, not of Kalshi, so the essay should cite it as a prediction his framework made, not as a judgment he has delivered on the platforms.

38. **Inference.** The design failure the essay names is one Hanson's own writing locates in two places he has not closed: the user interface, which he called "the main category of issues not dealt with" in 2003 (`hanson2003combinatorial` p. 118) and "a serious user-interface problem" in 2005 (`hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 10); and the trading-skill barrier, which he proposed to meet with "education and better trading interfaces" (`hanson2008insider` §IV.A). He treats the interface as a solvable engineering detail; the essay treats it as the product.

### The strongest reply Hanson's work offers to the thesis

The essay says the market computes a probability but the interface asks the user to trade, and calls the gap a design failure. Hanson's framework replies in four steps, each of which the essay must answer rather than bypass.

First, there is no probability without the trade. The price exists because someone is paid to move it, and in an unsubsidised market the only people who pay are traders who want to trade (claims 12 and 13). Asking the user to trade is the mechanism, not a costume. The fix in Hanson's framework is not a different display; it is a different funder: a patron or a decision-maker who pays an information prize so that the consumer of the probability never has to trade (claims 9 and 11). On his account Kalshi and Polymarket are missing a funder, not a screen.

Second, the consumer of the price was never meant to be the trader. His legislature reads the price and votes (claim 5); his CEO-dumping market binds a board (`hanson2013vote` §V). A retail trader who wants only the probability is, in his design, a consumer who has wandered into the producers' room. That supports the essay's observation but relocates the failure from the interface to the absence of any role for a non-trading consumer.

Third, fun traders are a feature. Noise trading is "the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders" (claim 26), and expected manipulation raises accuracy (claim 19). An essay that treats sports bettors as noise must say why their presence does not improve the political prices by his mechanism, or must show the cross-market claim fails (claim 36).

Fourth, compare institutions to institutions. OB 2017-08-05: "with each of his reasons, Thicke compares prediction markets to some ideal of perfection, instead of to the actual current institutions it is intended to supplement." Hanson would ask what instrument a lay user had before Kalshi for a probability on a policy event, and whether the bet slip is worse than that. The essay's answer, that the criterion is Part 1's decision-experience standard rather than relative accuracy, is a different standard, and should be stated as such.

Hanson also supplies the historical frame the thesis inverts. `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 23: "all of these products are ways to gamble, but, because they serve useful social functions, they have become politically and morally acceptable." The essay says the dress is the failure; Hanson's history says every financial product wore that dress until its function was accepted.

## (g) Synthesis for the essay

What Hanson's work settles. A decision market, in his terms, is a prediction market made conditional on a choice and read by a decision-maker who is bound, or at least committed, to act on the difference between conditional prices (claims 2, 4, 5, 11). Its funder is a patron who pays a bounded information prize through an automated market maker, or a decision-maker whose commitment makes the price worth moving (claims 9-11). Kalshi and Polymarket, by his own categories, are neither: their contracts are mostly unconditional, no one is bound to the price, and the trader pays a fee rather than a patron paying a subsidy (claims 13, 15, 17, 35). He classified sports contracts under the entertainment function in 2003 and co-signed a 2008 proposal that excluded them from the information-market safe harbor (claims 15, 33). He predicted in 1990 that commercial bookmakers would take odd questions as publicity and keep sports as the "real" betting (claim 14), and in 2017 that sports would lead blockchain prediction-market volume (claim 33).

What his work does not settle for the essay. He has not criticised the two platforms, their volume mix, or their interfaces; he defends sports betting as a legitimate pleasure and fears a backlash more than the product (claims 29-31, 34). His noise-trader theory says fun traders make a market more accurate, which the essay must answer if it treats sports bettors as a corruption of the instrument (claims 19, 26, 36). And he treats the interface as an unsolved engineering issue, flagged in 2003 and 2005 and assigned to "education and better trading interfaces" in 2008 (claim 38), where the essay treats it as the product's identity.

How the thesis can use him without bending him. The essay can say, with his words, that a decision market has a consumer who does not trade and a funder who is not the trader; that a market sold to traders for a fee is a different business, which he called "gamified" and a "mooch"; and that sports contracts belong, in his taxonomy, to entertainment. It cannot say that he regards Kalshi or Polymarket as a design failure, and it should report that his own harm-benefit rule (claim 32) would restrict, not what Kalshi sells, but what a prudish regulator should fear from it.
