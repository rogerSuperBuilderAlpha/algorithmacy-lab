# R: The replies Hanson and Mansour would make to the author's argument

*Facet R of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 (seat R of the four-seat run). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet is.** Opposing counsel. For each premise of the author's planned argument (P1–P8, C) it writes the strongest reply Robin Hanson and Tarek Mansour would make, bound to what facets H, M, X, L and D record them as having written or said. Every reply carries its evidence: the facet claim or premise number, the source key, the locator, and the quotation as the facet copies it. Where a reply is an inference from their stated positions and not something they said, it is labelled **inference** and the steps are shown. Where the record holds nothing, the entry says **no reply on the record**. I added nothing from memory and opened no source beyond the facets.

**Two gaps in the evidence base that shape this facet.** No facet mentions Forex, currency markets, or the words triad, dyad or counterpart. P4 and P6 therefore have no direct record to meet them; the replies there are inferences from what Hanson and Mansour say about hedgers, speculators and the consumer of a price. Seat T (the triad/dyad criterion) and seat F (who trades Forex and futures) own those gaps.

**The author's premises as given to me.** P1 dark patterns begin well-meant, the harm is structural. P2 Hanson and Mansour state the good the design aims at. P3 the gambling-addiction pitfall is not the main argument. P4 a decision, unlike a choice, has a counterpart coordinated through a third party (triadic coordination). P5 a prediction-market trade is zero-sum between two sides. P6 in Forex, participants get what they need through an algorithm; only speculators win or lose; speculation is not triadic. P7 sports contracts have no participant with a need; bridging "the hedge" to the betting pool does not supply triadic coordination. P8 the platforms present this dyad as a triad. C presenting a dyad as a triad is a dark pattern and poor decision experience design.

## (b) Replies by premise

### P1. Dark patterns usually begin as well-meant design; the harm is in the structure, not the motive

**Hanson would reply** that he has no quarrel with judging structure over motive, since institutional design is his subject, but that the structure must be judged against the structures people actually had and not against an ideal, and that a design charge still needs a harm. He has written that critics of prediction markets compare them "to some ideal of perfection, instead of to the actual current institutions it is intended to supplement," and his own regulatory standard is to approve "by default" and "only restrict them when we see clear evidence of harm." He would add that the structure the author attacks is one he named fourteen years ago: a "gamified market" in which traders pay the exchange to answer a question, which he distinguished from a subsidised decision market and called a "mooch." On his account, then, the structure is known, differs from his design, and has produced no measured harm. He would also turn the author's history against the thesis: every financial product began in this dress. "all of these products are ways to gamble, but, because they serve useful social functions, they have become politically and morally acceptable."

- Evidence. H, strongest-reply section, OB 2017-08-05 (not peer-reviewed): "with each of his reasons, Thicke compares prediction markets to some ideal of perfection, instead of to the actual current institutions it is intended to supplement." H (d) table, OB 2026-04-29 (his CFTC comment): "let's approve prediction markets by default, especially when they can inform topics that matter, and only restrict them when we see clear evidence of harm, applying similar standards and scrutiny as we do for other info institutions." H claim 13, OB 2012-07-19: "Intrade is a typical gamified market – you might pay Intrade a little to put up a question, and if traders like it they'll pay Intrade to answer your question." H entry `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 23: "all of these products are ways to gamble, but, because they serve useful social functions, they have become politically and morally acceptable." H (d), WhoWhatWhy 2026-05-08: "all the familiar financial markets you know of were once somewhere illegal as gambling."
- Status: direct on the standard and the history; the link to P1 specifically is mine.

**Mansour would reply** that structure is exactly his argument, and that the structure favours him. The author says look at the structure and not the motive; Mansour says the structure is an open order book in which the venue is not a counterparty and earns a fee whichever side wins, so the venue's incentive is volume and the perception of fairness, not customer losses. This is a structural claim, not an appeal to good intentions. The facet M test is where the author can press: a volume-fee venue also wants losers to keep trading, and Mansour closes that gap with conduct ("throttle them"), not with structure.

- Evidence. M B2, `mansour2026rotowire`: "I take my 1% fee, regardless of who wins or loses." M B2, `mansour2026sequoia`: "Gambling is a business model where, like, the revenue of the company is equal to the customer's losses." M entry `mansour2026uncapped`: "What is my incentive as a company? My company takes a small fee, a transaction fee. So what I want is volume. I want people to just trade more. I'm actually incentivized. If I want people to trade more, I want the platform to be fair, and perceived to be fair." M B2 test: "a volume-fee venue also wants losers to keep trading. Mansour concedes the gap and offers conduct, not structure, to close it: 'I have all the incentive in the world to basically throttle them' (Sequoia)."
- Status: direct.

### P2. Hanson and Mansour state the good the design aims at

**Hanson would reply** that P2 misstates his good in two ways. The good is not "accurate prices" but information that advises a decision, and he is explicit that his claim is not that prices are always more accurate than other sources. And the design he aims at has a consumer who pays for the information; the retail exchange, where "the customer is the trader who just pays a tax" and the information is a free side effect, is the model he would rather replace. He has also said he has no great confidence that the topics now trending on Kalshi and Polymarket inform anyone's decisions. So the author attributes to the platforms a good Hanson has already said they do not deliver, and then blames the platforms' design for not delivering it. Hanson would say: agreed, and the missing piece is a funder.

- Evidence. H (d), Freakonomics ep. 687: "according to our standard decision theory, information is valuable because it advises decisions." H entry `hanson2013vote` §II: "My claim is not that betting prices are always more accurate than other sources, but that they are a robustly accurate public institution estimating policy-relevant topics." H claim 17, WhoWhatWhy 2026-05-08: "The customer is the trader who just pays a tax in order to be allowed to trade, and all the information that it's produced is a side effect that everybody can get for free. I would rather people who get the information pay for it." H claim 30, OB 2025-12-07: "The politics/policy questions on Polymarket and Kalshi trending now seem plausibly like topics where some people might find their estimate useful in making personal or collective choices. But I don't have great confidence in that, or know who those people are." H claims 9 and 11 for the two funders.
- Status: direct.

**Mansour would reply** that "a well-structured venue in place of sloppy bets on real-world events" is not his statement of the good and reduces his case to one strand of three. The record has him arguing hedging (event contracts "are tools to hedge risks"; the hurricane buyer who wants to be paid "if the hurricane hits the town"), structure (an "open, fair, liquid marketplace"), and a public price ("one clean number"; "the most accurate way to predict the future"; a reward for "doing the research and doing the truth-seeking on behalf of society"). If the author states only the structural good, Mansour would say the essay has skipped the hedging and information strands that carry his regulatory case and his social claim.

- Evidence. M entry `mansour2026uncapped`: "I just want it so that if the hurricane hits the town, I get paid.' That's a hedge." M B4, `mansour2026raskin`: "whether you are trading on an open, fair, liquid marketplace or in a closed forum system against the house that picks winners or losers." M entry `mansour2026raskin`: "But now you have one clean number". M C2, `mansour2026freakonomics`: "Kalshi is really the most accurate way to predict the future, which is pretty cool." ### P4. Decision experience design aims at the best decision; a decision, unlike a choice, has a counterpart coordinated through a third party

**Hanson would reply** (inference) that he accepts the unit of analysis, since information for him has value only as it advises a decision, but that the author has the roles wrong. In his design the person who makes the decision does not trade: a legislature reads the conditional prices and votes. The trader supplies information; the trader's own trade is not "the decision" the market serves, and the typical trader "need not be informed or even rational." And his design does have a third party with a stake in the outcome: a patron who pays an information prize, or a decision-maker committed to act on the price. If a triad is what a decision requires, Hanson's decision market is triadic, and what the author has found is that Kalshi and Polymarket omit the third party he specified. He would say that supports his account of what is missing (a funder) and does not show that the form cannot have one.

- Steps. (1) H claim 4: "These summary expectations are all that should matter, not the details that produce them" (`hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 10). (2) H claim 5, `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "a state legislature might accept this estimate when considering whether or not to pass this hidden-gun bill." (3) H claim 27, `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint pp. 11–12: "the accuracy of speculative markets depends more on the most informed participants; the typical person able to trade need not be informed or even rational." (4) H claim 9, `hanson1999decision` p. 17: "Doing this in effect creates an information prize offered to those who first make the market price better reflect relevant information." (5) H claim 11, `hanson2006policyadvice` preprint p. 12: "one can ensure market prices exist either via a large enough financial subsidy to fund an automated market maker, or via a large enough chance market prices would influence important decisions." (6) H claim 35 (facet agent's inference) that by his taxonomy a sports-dominated fee exchange "is a prediction market by his definition, and only an advisory one." Therefore: the triad exists in his design, is absent on the platforms, and the absence is a missing funder rather than a property of prediction markets as such.
- Status: **inference**. Hanson has written nothing on triads or on the decision/choice distinction (H claim 34: "I found no sentence of his on bet slips, order types, price display or what a user is asked to do on either platform").

**Mansour: no reply on the record** on the decision/choice distinction or on triadic coordination. The nearest inference is that he would name the hedger as the counterpart with a need, coordinated through the exchange: "I don't want to deal with the insurance process. ... I just want it so that if the hurricane hits the town, I get paid.' That's a hedge" (M entry `mansour2026uncapped`). That answers P4 only for contracts that have hedgers, which is the question P7 opens.

### P5. A prediction-market trade is zero-sum between two sides: a plain win or lose

**Hanson would reply** that this is true of an unsubsidised betting market and that he said so first: once rational agents have hedged, "they should not want to trade with each other," and in most markets the lay trader "would be the fool" and should "just leave them alone." His own design is not zero-sum, because the market scoring rule's subsidy means "rational agents should expect a positive profit from participating." But he would then say the author has mistaken the mechanism for a defect. The zero-sum transfer from noise traders to informed traders is what pays the informed to reveal what they know: "the opportunity to win against such noise traders is the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders." And the trade is zero-sum only between the two traders; the price it produces is a public good that non-traders consume free, which facet X documents for Federal Reserve staff. Zero-sum at the trade, positive-sum at the price.

- Evidence. H claim 12, `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 108: "Once rational agents have hedged their risks regarding the events covered by an information market, they should not want to trade with each other." H (d), Freakonomics ep. 687: "In most markets, you'd be the fool. So you should not trade most markets, you should just leave them alone." H entry `hanson2007logarithmic` Introduction, preprint p. 2: "In contrast with a standard betting market, rational agents should expect a positive profit from participating, and do not need to find another person willing to make a matching bet." H claim 26, `hanson2006terrorism` preprint p. 12: "in most markets the opportunity to win against such noise traders is the main profit incentive that attracts informed traders." H claim 17 (quoted under P2): "all the information that it's produced is a side effect that everybody can get for free." X Line 4, `diercks2026kalshi`: "Federal Reserve staff read the displayed distribution without trading. This is the clearest documented case of non-trader use."
- Status: direct; the "zero-sum at the trade, positive-sum at the price" framing is my compression of claims 17 and 26.

**Mansour would reply** that he concedes the money transfer and denies that it is the relevant frame. He concedes it in his own words: "When somebody loses on Kalshi, they're losing it to someone else." He denies the frame on three grounds the record supplies: a hedger gains by reducing risk even when the contract pays nothing ("The point of hedging is to mitigate risk, not (like insurance) to offset precise losses"); speculators are the liquidity every derivatives market runs on; and the price is a public good. Where the author can press is Kalshi's New Jersey brief, which went further than Mansour does in interviews and claimed trading "will ultimately net out" with "positive returns for all traders," a claim facet M flags as implausible for a zero-sum contract and which Bürgi, Deng and Whelan's −20% average return contradicts. Stout's line in facet L is the author's doctrinal support: disagreement-based speculation "is a zero-sum game" that turns negative-sum once transaction costs enter.

- Evidence. M entry `mansour2026rotowire`: "I am a neutral platform. When somebody loses on Kalshi, they're losing it to someone else." M A3, `kalshi2024sjmemo` p. 36: "The point of hedging is to mitigate risk, not (like insurance) to offset precise losses. And risk is all about uncertainty." M A4, `kalshi2024sjmemo` p. 5: "a robust market depends in part on the liquidity provided by speculators." M entry `kalshi2025njpibrief` p. 8: "entering into event contracts with other sophisticated traders will ultimately net out. ... tailored investment strategies on financial markets result in positive returns for all traders." M B3 test: "the claim as written is implausible for a zero-sum contract." X entry `burgi2026makers`: "the average rate of return on Kalshi contracts is about minus 20%" (section 1). L (c)(ii), Stout (1999) p. 745: "speculation on disagreement is a zero-sum game in which one player's gain is necessarily the other's loss. Indeed, when HE [heterogeneous expectations] trading involves transaction costs, speculators' average returns become negative."
- Status: direct.

### P6. In Forex, participants normally get what they need while coordinating through an algorithm; only speculators win or lose; speculation is not a triadic activity

**Hanson would reply** (inference) that P6 states his position and then draws the wrong conclusion from it. He agrees that prediction markets, unlike Forex, have almost no hedgers, and he says this does not matter for the function he cares about: "Prediction markets tend to be very low volume markets, as they usually have few 'hedging' traders. However, this does not prevent them from having informative prices." The author's criterion for a triad is a participant with a need the market meets; in Forex that participant is the hedger. Hanson would say that is the hedging function, which he lists as one accepted function of markets alongside entertainment and capitalisation, and that information aggregation is a fourth, distinct function. By the author's criterion every prediction market fails, including Hanson's ideal, because in an information market the speculator is the producer and the only trader there is. Hanson would conclude that the author is judging an information market by a hedging standard, and that the "participant who gets what they need" in his design is the non-trading consumer of the price (the legislature of claim 5), who never enters the order book at all.

- Steps. (1) H entry `hanson2008insider` §IV: "Prediction markets tend to be very low volume markets, as they usually have few 'hedging' traders. However, this does not prevent them from having informative prices. Prediction markets only need enough volume to induce a few people who have relevant information to trade and thereby reveal their information." (2) H claim 15, `hanson1999decision` p. 18: "Accepted functions of markets now include entertainment, capitalization, and hedging, but not information aggregation." (3) H entry `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 107: information markets "were created not to entertain or to hedge risks but to aggregate information on particular topics of interest." (4) H claim 12 (the irrational participation problem): once hedged, rational agents "should not want to trade with each other," so an information market runs on speculation by construction. (5) H claim 5 (the legislature as consumer). Therefore: the hedger-need criterion excludes information markets as a class; the party who "gets what they need" is the price's consumer, not a trader.
- Status: **inference**. Hanson has written nothing on Forex in the texts facet H read. Note that step (2) is a statement about which functions the law accepted in 1999, not Hanson's own taxonomy; step (3) carries the taxonomy.

**Mansour would reply**, directly, that the Forex analogy is his and it cuts his way. All derivatives markets are mostly speculation, and the hedgers who "get what they need" get it because speculators take the other side: "you really don't get liquid, vibrant financial markets with real price discovery without speculation." So on the author's own description of Forex, speculators are a necessary part of the arrangement, not a parasite on it, and Mansour would say the same of Kalshi. He would then claim that Kalshi has hedgers too: the hurricane buyer, the portfolio holder worried about an election, and (for sports) sponsors, networks and a sportsbook hedging its own book. The author's reply is in the record: for sports, Kalshi's own 2024 brief and the Ninth Circuit (see P7).

- Evidence. M B4, `mansour2026raskin`: "Actually, most activity in most financial markets is speculation, right? And you really don't get liquid, vibrant financial markets with real price discovery without speculation. If it's just hedging by itself or capital allocation by itself, that doesn't build a real marketplace." M A4, `mansour2026freakonomics`: "When you look at the percentage of hedging versus speculation in any derivatives markets, like grain futures or commodity futures or stock options, it tends to be very tilted towards speculation. The reason you want speculation is you want liquidity." M entry `mansour2026uncapped`: "if you're worried about, for example, Republicans winning or Democrats winning, you can buy Republican or Democrats. If you think that's going to impact your portfolio, your hedge is going to basically compensate for that." M A2, `kalshi2025ca9staymotion` p. 12: "Sportsbooks have direct exposure to outcomes of sporting events, leading some to turn to Kalshi to 'hedge against volatility.'"
- Status: direct on speculation and derivatives; Mansour does not name Forex in the record, so the transfer to Forex is mine.

### P7. Sports contracts (most of the volume) have no participant with a need the market meets; bridging "the hedge" to the betting pool does not supply triadic coordination

**Hanson would reply** by conceding most of the premise and contesting its weight. He classified sports betting under the entertainment function in 2003, co-signed a 2008 safe-harbor proposal that "would presumably not include contracts on the outcomes of sports events," and his own harm-benefit rule puts fast, easy, familiar topics on the harm side. But he would say entertainment is a need, and a legitimate one: sports betting "complements those activities, making them more interesting, engaging, and better informed," and "the fun of action and proving yourself in competition" is one of three functions he lists. He would add that the sports price still informs, and facet X shows sports is the best-calibrated category on both platforms. On his taxonomy a sports-heavy exchange is an entertainment market with an information by-product, and he would ask why that is a design failure rather than a market serving one function while producing another. And he would repeat the comparison rule: compare Kalshi's sports market to DraftKings, the institution it replaces, where facet X finds the exchange cheaper and less biased.

H (d), OB 2026-04-13: "sports bets complements those activities, making them more interesting, engaging, and better informed" (grammar as the facet copies it). H claim 31, OB 2026-04-29: "prediction markets can serve many functions, such as moving and cutting risk, collecting and sharing information, and the fun of action and proving yourself in competition". The Ninth Circuit's sentence is the author's answer: the contracts "create risk, largely for ordinary consumers, where none previously existed."

### P8. The platforms present this dyad as a triad (peer-to-peer exchange, no house, "open, fair, liquid marketplace")

**Hanson would reply** (inference) that the peer-to-peer description is structurally accurate and that an accurate description is not a misrepresentation. He has himself distinguished the fee-taking exchange from the subsidised market and knows which one Kalshi is. If the author means that a third party with a stake in the decision is missing, Hanson agrees, and has said so in his own vocabulary: the missing party is the funder, "the customer is the trader who just pays a tax," and the consumer of the price never pays. But the platforms have not claimed to have a sponsor or a bound decision-maker, so he would not call the absence a disguise. He has written nothing about either platform's marketing or screens.

- Steps. (1) H claim 13 (the "gamified market" versus the market-maker-driven corporate market). (2) H claim 17 ("the trader who just pays a tax"). (3) H claim 34: "He has not said that Kalshi or Polymarket are sports betting in the dress of prediction markets, and he has not written about their interfaces." Therefore: he would grant the missing funder and deny the deception, for want of any claim by the platforms that a funder exists.
- Status: **inference**.

### C. Presenting a dyad as a triad is a dark pattern and poor decision experience design

**Hanson would reply** (inference) on three fronts. First, a dark pattern needs someone deceived, and he would ask who. ## (c) Where Hanson and Mansour would disagree about the author's argument

1. **Who should pay for the information.** Hanson wants the consumer of the price to pay (a patron's information prize or a decision-maker's commitment) and calls the trader-pays-a-fee model "gamified" and a "mooch." Mansour's business is that model: the trader pays a fee, the price is free, and the free price is his "top of funnel." On P2, P5 and P8 Hanson's position describes Kalshi's structure as the one he would rather replace. Sources: H claim 13 (OB 2012-07-19: "It is only when you want people to answer your question for free that you'll have to piggyback on their having some other reason to trade"); H claim 17 (WhoWhatWhy: "I would rather people who get the information pay for it"); M entry `mansour2026sequoia`: "the more liquid, the better the forecast, the more truthful my forecast, the more people look at it, the more my top of funnel increases."

2. **Whether hedging matters.** Mansour's Strand A leads with hedging and his regulatory case needs it, even after Kalshi demoted it to a factor the statute does not require (A5). Hanson says prediction markets "usually have few 'hedging' traders" and that this "does not prevent them from having informative prices"; information aggregation is for him a function distinct from hedging. On P6 and P7, Hanson would tell the author that the hedging test is the wrong test, and would tell Mansour the same. Sources: M A1–A3, A5 (`kalshi2024appelleebrief` p. 57: "Hedging use is irrelevant to whether a contract falls into one of those enumerated categories at the first step"); H entry `hanson2008insider` §IV; H entry `hanson2003combinatorial` p. 107.

3. **Whether sports volume matters.** Hanson classifies sports under entertainment, co-signed the 2008 exclusion of sports from the safe harbor, places fast and familiar topics on the harm side of his own rule, and predicted in 1990 that odd questions would be publicity for the "real" betting on sports. Mansour calls sports "a natural event," a gateway, and says non-sports growth "is going to eat into the sports very quickly." Hanson was sceptical in 2017 "that there is much public demand for betting on odd questions"; Mansour's growth claim depends on that demand. Sources: H claims 14, 15, 32, 33; H claim 16 (OB 2017-09-19); M C5 (`mansour2026raskin`: "It's going to eat into the sports very quickly"; `mansour2026rotowire`: "sports would be a great sort of gateway").

4. **What a decision market is.** Hanson's decision market is a set of contracts conditional on a decision, read by a decision-maker bound or committed to act on the gap between conditional prices. Mansour calls Hanson's work "foundational" (Freakonomics, per facet H) and lists almost no such contracts; Kalshi's product is unconditional event contracts. The author can use the gap: the platforms borrow the name of a design whose requirements (H claim 6, the fifteen-item list) they do not meet, and the man who wrote the requirements says so in his own taxonomy. Sources: H claims 2, 4, 6, 11; H (d) Freakonomics ep. 687 note that the page "quotes Kalshi's CEO Tarek Mansour calling Hanson's work 'foundational'"; ARGUMENT_MAP H1.

5. **"Truth."** Mansour: "Markets don't lie"; "the new source of truth, free from bias"; "the most accurate way to predict the future." Hanson: "My claim is not that betting prices are always more accurate than other sources"; "even thick speculative markets, full of lots of people trading lots of money, often have big info failures." Sources: M C2 (`mansour2026boardroom`; `mansour2024cointelegraph`; `mansour2026freakonomics`); H entry `hanson2013vote` §II; H (d) OB 2012-02-13.

6. **Who should trade.** Hanson: "In most markets, you'd be the fool. So you should not trade most markets, you should just leave them alone." Mansour: "Wall Street will always beat Main Street. ... The beauty of what we're building is that it's just not the case"; "Our best inflation forecaster is a random guy in Kansas." The author's user, the lay reader who wants a probability, is the person Hanson tells to stay out and Mansour invites in. Sources: H (d) Freakonomics ep. 687; M entry `mansour2026uncapped`; M entry `mansour2026national`.

7. **Freezing payouts.** On Kalshi's handling of the Khamenei market Hanson said the firm "is doing what it thinks wise to avoid that sort of backlash ... but it might not be for the best in terms of public information." A minor disagreement, but it shows Hanson judging Kalshi by the information standard and finding it short. Source: H (d), WhoWhatWhy 2026-05-08.

## (d) Ranking

### The three replies the essay cannot survive without answering

**R1. Hanson: there is no probability without the trade; the missing piece is a funder, not a screen.** (Bears on P5, P8, C.) In an unsubsidised market the only people who pay to move the price are traders; asking the user to trade is the mechanism; the fix in his framework is a patron or a bound decision-maker, and no display supplies one. If the essay calls the trade a costume, it must say how the number gets made without it. Sources: H claims 9, 11, 12, 13, 17, 26; H strongest-reply section, steps one and two.

**R2. Mansour: the exchange is the third party, and its structure differs from a sportsbook for the user.** (Bears on P8, C.) Traders contract with each other; the venue is not a counterparty; it earns a fee whichever side wins; and facet X confirms that the exchange form carries lower cost and less bias than a bookmaker on the Betfair precedent. The essay's claim that a dyad is "presented as" a triad therefore has to say what a third party must have that an exchange lacks, because the exchange is a third party by any ordinary reading. The Ninth Circuit's "distinctions without differences" is a holding about the statutory word "swap," not a finding about design, and the essay cannot borrow it as one. Sources: M B1–B3; X Line 1 and concession 2; M entry `ca92026assad` p. 29 and n.5.

**R3. Hanson: the author applies a hedging standard to an information market.** (Bears on P4, P6, P7.) Prediction markets "usually have few 'hedging' traders" and that "does not prevent them from having informative prices." If the triad criterion requires a participant with a hedger-type need, then every prediction market fails it, including Hanson's own, and the essay has defined its target out of existence rather than found a fault in it. The essay must either let the non-trading consumer of the price (Hanson's legislature; the Fed staff in facet X) count as the party with the need, or let the sponsor count, or explain why neither does. Sources: H entry `hanson2008insider` §IV; H claims 5, 12, 15; X Line 4.

## (e) What would answer the top three

**R1 (no probability without the trade; the missing funder).**
- In the facets: X Line 5 and hold 7. Stakes did not improve accuracy on sports (Servan-Schreiber et al. 2004) and stated beliefs matched or beat the same traders' prices (Dana et al. 2019). This supports a probability-elicitation interface that produces the number without the trading skill. X Line 4 and concession 4 let the essay narrow its claim to the trader's screen while conceding the price's public life. H claims 7 and 25 (advisory markets "risk being ignored"; a market that steers a decision "cannot be very thin") let the essay say the funder Hanson wants is also the one the platforms lack, so Hanson's reply is a restatement of the thesis in his vocabulary.
- Being gathered: seat T decides whether a sponsor or a bound decision-maker counts as the third party of a triad. If yes, Hanson's design is triadic and the platforms' is not, and R1 becomes an ally's correction rather than an objection.
- Does not exist: a field test of a subsidised, non-trading elicitation (a market scoring rule or a calibrated poll) against Kalshi on the same questions at scale. Gruca and Rietz (X) compare the IEM to Kalshi and find the same forecast, but the IEM is still a trading venue. Dana et al. is one tournament.

**R2 (the exchange is the third party; structure differs for the user).**
- In the facets: the Kalshi Trading affiliate (M entry `ca92026assad` p. 29 n.5; the perma.cc captures are on facet M's verify list and were not opened); the maker/taker asymmetry (X hold 8: −9.64% against −31.46%) showing that order defaults decide who pays; the explicit-cost gap, which X computes as narrower than the Betfair precedent (3.5 against 4.55 cents per dollar at even odds) and which widens at the price extremes; the unresolved question whether Kalshi restricts winners (X verify list). The Ninth Circuit's holding should be cited as law, not design.
- Being gathered: seat T must say what a third party in a triad must have (a stake in the decision's quality? independence from volume?), since Mansour's exchange has a stake in volume and an affiliated market maker. Seat F's account of who trades Forex and futures bears here too: if dealers and speculators dominate Forex, Mansour's analogy strengthens and P6's "participants get what they need" weakens.
- Does not exist: Kalshi Trading's share of volume and P&L; account-level data on whether winners stay (Mansour's "you're still trading on Kalshi"); any measurement of whether a volume-fee venue's retention practices differ from a sportsbook's.

**R3 (the hedging standard applied to an information market).**
- In the facets: Hanson's own requirements list (H claim 6: distinct options, measured outcomes, decision-insider traders, a decision tied to the price) is the test the platforms fail, and it is an information-market test, not a hedging test. H claim 5 and X Line 4 supply the non-trading consumer. The essay can say: the party with the need is the reader of the price, and Hanson's design gives that reader a role (a legislature, a board) where the platforms give the reader a bet slip. That meets R3 without invoking hedging.
- Being gathered: seat T, on whether the non-trading consumer or the sponsor can be the third party; seat F, on whether Forex hedgers are the model of a "participant who gets what they need" or a minority among dealers.
- Does not exist: any field study of how a non-trader reads a Kalshi or Polymarket price into a decision and what follows (OUTLINE section 11 lists this gap; X Line 4 confirms no study of lay non-trader comprehension). Sah et al. (2026) is the nearest and is qualitative.
