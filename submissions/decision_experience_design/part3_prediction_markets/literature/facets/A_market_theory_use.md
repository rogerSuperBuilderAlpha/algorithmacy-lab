# A: Prediction-market theory and decision use

*Facet A of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base ("Decision markets as poor decision experience design"). Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 at abstract level; verified against Crossref, OpenAlex and full texts in a second session the same day (local verification session, 2026-10-03). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

What this facet covers. The theory of what prediction markets are for (information aggregation and forecasting accuracy), the conditions under which a market price can be read as a probability (risk preferences, belief heterogeneity, favorite-longshot bias, fees, thin markets, manipulation), the evidence on how decision-makers use prices (corporate and policy use, futarchy and decision markets), and what a market asks a participant to do compared with what a decision-maker needs. The questions the synthesis answers are A1 to A3. A1: what does the literature claim prediction markets are for, and under what conditions does the price equal a probability? A2: is there evidence on how decision-makers actually use market prices as inputs to decisions? A3: what do the markets ask the participant to do (a trade, a position size, liquidity provision), versus what a decision-maker needs (a probability and a decision)?

`wolfers2004prediction` | Wolfers, J., & Zitzewitz, E. (2004). *Prediction Markets*. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 18(2), 107–126.
- *Record:* DOI 10.1257/0895330041371321 (Crossref: journal-article, American Economic Association, issued 2004-05-01; venue, volume, issue and pages now from the record, not inferred). Consensus: https://consensus.app/papers/details/81c556c00fee5b5dafe76399254cb419/?utm_source=claude_desktop
- *Read status:* full text, NBER Working Paper 10504 version (May 2004), https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10504/w10504.pdf. The AEA PDF returned 403. Page numbers below are the working paper's.
- *What it argues:* The abstract states the field's purpose: "simple markets can be used to aggregate disperse information into efficient forecasts of uncertain future events," and that market forecasts "outperform most moderately sophisticated benchmarks." The paper opens on DARPA's Policy Analysis Market (Section 1), reviews election, sports, Oscar and Hewlett-Packard markets (the HP internal market "produced more accurate forecasts of printer sales than" official forecasts, Section 3, citing Chen and Plott), lists contract designs that reveal a probability, a mean or a median (Section 2), and treats design issues (how buyers are matched, money versus play money, where markets fail: "attempts to set up markets on topics where" there is little information "barely exist", Section 5). The conclusion discusses decision markets and the "correlation from causation" problem.
- *Bearing:* supports the claim that the literature's stated purpose is forecasting by aggregation. The "carefully designed contracts" phrase in the abstract sets up the A3 contrast with the contracts a retail platform lists. *Change in verification:* venue confirmed; previously inferred.

## (c) Synthesis

### A1. What markets are for, and when the price is a probability

The literature's stated purpose is forecasting by information aggregation. Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004) state it for the field, Berg, Nelson and Rietz (2008) give the strongest field result (the Iowa market was closer to the outcome than 964 polls 74% of the time, in markets of 155 to 790 active traders), Rhode and Strumpf (2004) show a pre-polling betting market whose mid-October favourite won 11 of 15 elections, and Arrow et al. (2008) argue that the tool deserves fewer legal restrictions. The accuracy claim is conditional in the evidence itself. Atanasov et al. (2017, abstract only) found that last-day market prices beat the simple mean of poll forecasts but lost to team polls aggregated with weighting and recalibration. Goel et al. (2010) found the edge over polls and statistical models surprisingly small, in data that include a Las Vegas sports book. Atanasov et al. (2022, abstract only; full version 2025, not obtained) found a continuous double auction, the architecture of an order-book platform, underperformed a market-maker design in thin markets. Dudley and Magdaleno (2026, a preprint) found Polymarket forecasts for influenza and measles failed to beat standard benchmarks, and the best combination put zero weight on the markets.

The conditions under which the price equals a probability are narrower than the field's shorthand suggests. Manski (2006) shows that with risk-neutral price takers and heterogeneous beliefs, the price is the midpoint of an interval for the mean belief, (B², 2B − B²), and says nothing about dispersion; his own conclusion is that "direct measurement of expectations may be preferable to inference on expectations from prices." Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2006) answer that log utility makes price equal to mean belief exactly, that other plausible utility functions keep the gap "typically within a percentage point," and that Manski's case "is in fact a worst-case scenario"; Gjerstad (2004, abstract only) reaches the same conclusion. Ottaviani and Sørensen (2009; published 2015) show that limited budgets alone produce underreaction to information and a favorite-longshot pattern, and Gandhi and Serrano-Padial (2015) trace the pattern to a mix of 70% near-correct traders and 30% noise traders. Page and Clemen (2013) add a time-to-expiry bias that works through abstention by traders who discount, Whelan (2023) shows that the fees of a commercial market, written about Kalshi, push low-probability contract prices off the true probability, and Bottazzi and Giachini (2019) show that with fractional-Kelly traders the price does not converge to the true probability even though, when more than one trader survives, the average price beats every individual belief. Mantovani and Filippin (2026) find in a lab that risk preferences did not move prices, double auctions sat closer to average beliefs than call auctions, and traders updated toward the price, not the true state.

For the two platforms in question, the closest evidence is recent and mostly unrefereed. Le (2026) finds, in 353 million trades across 429,000 contracts, persistent underconfidence in political markets on Kalshi and Polymarket, with prices compressed toward 50%, concludes that "a price's meaning depends on what, when and how much is traded," and estimates that about half of the raw variation in calibration slopes is noise. Bürgi, Deng and Whelan (2026) find a clear favorite-longshot bias on more than 300,000 Kalshi contracts, with the fee falling on takers, who hold the more extreme beliefs, and sports contracts present only since early 2025. Dubach (2026) documents a longshot spread premium and a wash-trade share with a 1% median and a 22% upper tail on Polymarket, and shows that trade direction read from the public feed is right only about 59% of the time. All three are working papers or preprints.

Manipulation evidence splits by setting and by stakes. Rhode and Strumpf (2006), with a field experiment worth 2% of IEM volume in 2000 and the 2004 TradeSports attack, and Hanson, Oprea and Porter (2006), in the lab, find that price distortions are short-lived. Deck, Lin and Porter (2013) find in the lab that well-funded manipulators can destroy price informativeness while bids and asks stay informative. Rasooly and Rozzi (2025) find, in 817 markets, that random trades remain visible 60 days later, smaller in markets with more traders, volume or an external probability source; their platform, Manifold Markets, runs on a play-money currency that cannot be cashed out, so the result bears on thin, low-stakes markets most directly. A2 turns on this point, because a decision-maker who acts on a price gives someone a reason to move it.

Answer to A1. The literature says markets exist to aggregate dispersed information into forecasts. The price equals the probability under named conditions: log-utility or well-mixed traders, no fees, enough depth to resist manipulation, and a short horizon. None of these is guaranteed on a commercial platform, and the platform-specific evidence found shows deviations that depend on contract type and horizon.

### A2. How decision-makers use prices

The evidence is thin, and almost none of it is about use. Cowgill and Zitzewitz (2015) show accurate internal markets at Google, Ford and Firm X and, in their first sentence, that businesses and policymakers "have been slow to adopt them in decision making." The headline 25% reduction in mean squared error is the Ford comparison against formal expert forecasts, at p = 0.104; Google and Firm X had no formal expert forecasts to compare against. Montgomery et al. (2013) describe the Ford market from the inside and list benefits that concern information flow, such as comments and bypassing bureaucratic limits, more than a decision rule. Cowgill, Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2009) show that Google's participants skewed toward programming roles, quantitative backgrounds and an interest in investing or poker. Chen and Plott (2002) report the HP sales markets beating official forecasts in six of eight events, with a selected group of employees. O'Leary (2015, abstract only) shows participation decaying over time. Velasco and Jukić (2012, abstract only) say directly that research has concentrated on accuracy and that actionability is the open question.

The experimental evidence on use points toward trust, not accuracy, as the controlling variable. Choo, Kaplan and Zultan (2022) find that prices reveal the true state in over 90% of markets without manipulators and in 75% or 68% with them, yet managers under suspicion of manipulation leave 37% of the available payoff on the table, and with manipulators present earn less than they would by ignoring the market. Dianat and Siemroth (2021) find that the theoretically superior market design did worse for manager decisions without top-down advice to participants. Seemann, Enders and Hungenberg (2009, abstract only) find that trust in results rose with participants' enjoyment of trading. Siemroth (2021) shows in theory that when a decision changes asset values, full revelation can fail because pricing becomes a self-defeating prophecy, and gives the corporate "deadline security" as the example.

The policy and governance proposals are designs, not observed practice. Hanson (2013) gives the futarchy design and works through twenty-five objections, Abramowicz (2004) the predictive cost-benefit design (and records the DARPA FutureMAP project, cancelled after controversy), Berg and Rietz (2003) the conditional-market illustration from 1996, Chen et al. (2011) show that honest elicitation in a decision market requires the decision-maker to risk taking every action, and Teschner et al. (2017) show that manipulation in conditional markets depends on thinness and on how tightly the decision tracks the price. The one live-governance check found is a retrospective, deterministic simulation on DeSci DAO proposals whose authors list hindsight bias among its limits (Weidener and Shilina 2025).

Answer to A2. There is evidence that corporate markets forecast well and some experimental evidence on how managers respond to them. I found no field study that traces a price to a decision and its consequence, and no study of how users of public platforms (as against employees of a firm) use a Polymarket or Kalshi price as a decision input. The Part 3 essay can say that the literature is silent on that use; it should not say that the use is harmful on this facet's evidence.

### A3. What the market asks of the participant, and what a decision-maker needs

The instrument the literature praises is specified as a forecasting device, and the designer's intent is probability reporting by an individual fused with group consensus (Hanson 2007; Hanson 2003 on market makers for thin markets). A participant on a commercial platform is asked for something else. The texts describe a participant who takes a position at a quoted price and pays the taker fee (Bürgi, Deng and Whelan 2026), faces a spread and depth profile (Dubach 2026), pays fees that alter the return profile (Whelan 2023), and sizes the position. The sizing literature says this is a separate skill with its own failure modes: Thorp (2008) states that finding positive-expectation bets is the first problem and "how much to bet" the second; Kelly (1956) makes the growth-optimal bet a function of the true probabilities; the rule needs an estimate of the probability (Baker and McHale 2013, abstract only, on shrinking when it is uncertain), delivers large drawdown risk (MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba 2010, abstract only), and depends on the gap between belief and price (Meister 2024). A decision-maker's question is different: what is the probability, and what should I do? The tournament instruments answer the first half directly by asking forecasters for a probability and scoring it (Mellers et al. 2015; Atanasov et al. 2017), and Chen et al. (2011) show the second half requires the decision-maker's own commitments.

Two further mismatches appear. First, the question a decision-maker has may not be a listed contract: Adegbenro (2026) finds that listing follows settlement legibility, so inventories reflect what platforms can settle, with football dominating the African inventory and the Sudan war appearing in four contracts. Second, the price acts back on the decision-maker's world: Nechepurenko (2026, a preprint) argues public probabilities can serve as coordination devices whose authority decouples from accuracy, and Johnson and Chan (2025, a letter, abstract only) describe features such as short event cycles, 24/7 access and push notifications that encourage re-entry.

Answer to A3. The market asks the participant to trade at a price, manage a position and absorb fees and spreads. The decision-maker needs a calibrated probability and a rule for acting on it. The literature supports describing this as a mismatch of task, but it supports it only indirectly, since no entry compares a user's market-based decision with an alternative instrument for the same user. This is an inference from several papers, and the essay should present it that way.

### Gaps

1. No study found of how retail users of Polymarket or Kalshi use prices to decide anything. Every platform paper is about prices, volume or microstructure.
2. No field study found that links a corporate or policy price to a decision and its outcome. The use evidence is lab experiments, case descriptions and proposals.
3. The platform-specific calibration and microstructure evidence (Le; Bürgi, Deng and Whelan; Dubach; Dudley and Magdaleno; Adegbenro; Nechepurenko) is preprints and working papers.
4. Thirteen entries remain unread in full: ten abstract only (Atanasov et al. 2017 and 2022; Gjerstad; Buckley; Seemann et al.; O'Leary; Velasco and Jukić; MacLean et al.; Baker and McHale; Johnson and Chan) and three not obtained (Arrow et al.; Tetlock and Gardner; Malone), plus three added verify-list items not obtained (Berg et al. Handbook chapter; Tetlock 2005; Atanasov et al. 2025).
5. Several full texts are working-paper or accepted-manuscript versions rather than the publisher's version (Wolfers and Zitzewitz 2004; Manski; Snowberg and Wolfers; Gandhi and Serrano-Padial; Bottazzi and Giachini; Deck et al.; Dianat and Siemroth; Ottaviani and Sørensen 2009; Cowgill and Zitzewitz 2015, author copy; Rhode and Strumpf 2004). Page numbers cited are those copies'.
