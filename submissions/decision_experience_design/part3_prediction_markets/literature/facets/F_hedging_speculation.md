# F: Who participates in currency and commodity derivative markets, and why (hedging against speculation)

*Facet F of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 (local session, seat F of a four-seat run). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's. Nothing here may be copied into a draft without the author's reading.*

What this facet covers. The premise under test is the author's P6: "In Forex the ordinary participant gets what they need; only speculators win or lose. Speculation is not a triadic activity." The facet establishes, from primary data and the theory read in full where a copy was reachable, who trades currency and commodity derivatives and why, and then states in section (c) what P6 can and cannot say. The questions are F1 to F5. F1: what do the BIS Triennial Survey and its Quarterly Review commentaries say about FX turnover by counterparty and instrument, and what is known about retail FX traders' outcomes? F2: what does the CFTC Commitments of Traders (COT) data say about commercial and non-commercial participation in a grain contract and a currency contract, and what does the CFTC itself say "commercial" means? F3: what does the theory of hedging and speculation (Keynes, Hicks, Working, the hedging-pressure literature, Hirshleifer, Stout, Posner and Weyl) say a hedger and a speculator are, and whether hedger-speculator trade is risk-sharing or zero-sum? F4: is there a documented class of participants in sports event contracts or on betting exchanges who hold positions to offset a pre-existing exposure, and is any hedging use of election or macro event contracts documented? F5: the five propositions (a) to (e) the brief asks for.

### B1. Foreign exchange: who trades, and how retail traders fare (RQ-F1)

`bis2025triennial` | Bank for International Settlements (2025). *Triennial Central Bank Survey: OTC foreign exchange turnover in April 2025*. Monetary and Economic Department, 30 September 2025. 25 pp.
- *Record:* bis.org publication page "OTC foreign exchange turnover in April 2025" (citation_publication_date 2025-09-30). Not a Crossref item.
- *Read status:* full text, https://www.bis.org/publications/202509-commentary-otc-derivatives.pdf. The detailed-tables annex (`statistics-rpfx25-fx-annex.pdf`) was downloaded and used only to confirm Table 2's totals.
- *What it reports:* 52 jurisdictions, "more than 1,100 banks and other dealers"; "Turnover data are reported by the sales desks of reporting dealers" (introduction). Daily turnover "reached $9.6 trillion per day in April 2025 ('net-net' basis, all FX instruments), up 28% from $7.5 trillion three years earlier" (Highlights). By counterparty (Table 2, p. 11, net-net daily averages, April 2025): total 9,595 bn; with reporting dealers 4,372 bn (45.6%); with other financial institutions 4,780 bn (49.8%); with non-financial customers 443 bn (4.6%). In 2022 the same rows read 7,468; 3,495 (46.8%); 3,546 (47.5%); 427 (5.7%). By instrument in 2025, non-financial customers took 144 of 2,957 bn in spot (4.9%), 84 of 1,847 bn in outright forwards (4.5%), 149 of 3,986 bn in FX swaps (3.7%), 16 of 172 bn in currency swaps (9.0%) and 50 of 634 bn in options and other products (7.9%) (Table 2). The text: "the share of trading with non-financial customers extended its downward trend: it accounted for 5% of global turnover in 2025, down from 6% in 2022 and 7% in 2019" (p. 9), with the caveat in footnote 6 that "non-financial corporates could use their financial affiliates ('financing arms') for FX trading, which would have contributed to the turnover share with 'other financial institutions'" (p. 9). Inside the other-financial group: non-reporting banks $2.4 trillion (24% of global turnover), institutional investors $1.3 trillion (13%), hedge funds and proprietary trading firms 8% (p. 9; Table 3, p. 13). Table 3's "of which" items give "Retail-driven" turnover of 242 bn per day in 2025 (109 bn spot, 21 bn forwards, 80 bn FX swaps, 31 bn options), that is 2.5% of 9,595 bn (my division). The explanatory notes define retail-driven transactions as dealers' "(i) transactions with 'wholesale' financial counterparties that cater to retail investors (ie electronic retail trading platforms and retail margin brokerage firms), and (ii) direct transactions with 'non-wholesale' investors (ie private individuals)" (p. 20), and non-financial customers as the residual group outside reporting dealers and other financial institutions, the latter defined as "typically regarded as foreign exchange and interest rate derivatives market end users ... smaller commercial banks, investment banks and securities houses, and mutual funds, pension funds, hedge funds, currency funds, money market funds, building societies, leasing companies, insurance companies, other financial subsidiaries of corporate firms and central banks" (p. 19). On April 2025 specifically: institutional investors "with dollar asset exposures" sold dollars forward "to limit further FX losses on their portfolios"; "This ex post hedging activity contributed to the high turnover in outright forwards" (p. 9).
- *Bearing:* **refutes** the volume reading of P6. The counterparty most closely tied to trade in goods and services holds 4.6% of turnover; 95.4% is between financial institutions. The survey does not classify trades as hedges or speculation, so no hedging share can be read from it; it classifies counterparties.

### B2. Commodity and financial futures: the CFTC's classification and two contracts (RQ-F2)

- *What it says:* "All of a trader's reported futures positions in a commodity are classified as commercial if the trader uses futures contracts in that particular commodity for hedging as defined in CFTC Regulation 1.3, 17 CFR 1.3(z). `keynes1930treatise2` | Keynes, J. M. (1930). *A Treatise on Money, Volume II: The Applied Theory of Money*. London: Macmillan. Chapter 29, section (v), "The Theory of the 'Forward Market'", pp. 142–144.

`hicks1939value` | Hicks, J. R. (1939). *Value and Capital: An Inquiry into Some Fundamental Principles of Economic Theory*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Chapter X, "Equilibrium and Disequilibrium", section 4, pp. 135–139.
- *Record:* first edition, Clarendon Press, "First published February 1939", per the scan's title page. Crossref holds only reviews.

- *What it argues:* forward trading is "the device" by which "expectations and plans can be (at least partially) co-ordinated" under private enterprise (p. 135). ## (c) Synthesis: the five propositions

Each proposition names its sources; figures carry their denominators and dates. Where a number is my computation from a source's table, the entry says so.

**(a) What share of FX, and of a representative futures market, is plausibly hedging or commercial need against speculation.**

1. The BIS does not measure hedging. It classifies counterparties. In April 2025 the counterparty closest to trade in goods and services, "non-financial customers", took 443 of 9,595 billion dollars a day, 4.6% (Table 2, `bis2025triennial`); the BIS text rounds it to 5% and gives the trend 7% (2019), 6% (2022), 5% (2025), with the caveat that corporates' financing arms are counted as financial (p. 9). Reporting dealers took 45.6% and other financial institutions 49.8%. Retail-driven trades were 242 billion, 2.5%. FX turnover was 72 times world trade in 2025 (Annex Table A.1, `huang2025fxhedging`). The BIS economists' own words: "trading for financial motives has come to dominate trading for the purpose of goods and services exchange" (p. 24).
2. Hedging inside the financial 95% exists but cannot be separated from speculation in the survey: "These instruments can be used to adjust exposures to currency risk on existing positions or to speculate on future currency moves" (`huang2025fxhedging`, p. 24). The hedgers the BIS can see in April 2025 were asset managers hedging dollar portfolios (p. 9 of the release; pp. 24, 29 of the article), that is, hedgers of financial positions, not of trade.
3. In a grain contract the picture is the textbook one. CBOT corn, 29 September 2026: producers, merchants, processors and users held 38.5% of all positions and swap dealers 12.2%, so "commercial" 50.7%; managed money 29.1%; other reportables 11.4%; unclassifiable small traders 8.8% (my computation from `cftc_cot_corn_20260929`). Commercials were net short 39.5% of open interest and managed money net long 20.5%: hedgers on one side, speculators on the other, as in Keynes and Hicks.
4. In a currency contract the "commercial" label stops meaning what the author means. CME Euro FX, same date: legacy "commercial" 56.6% of positions, but by the TFF breakdown dealers held 18.8% and asset managers 50.8% (together 69.6%), leveraged funds 19.2%, and the category the CFTC describes as "mostly ... using markets to hedge business risk" (corporate treasuries, central banks, smaller banks) 3.2% (`cftc_cot_eurofx_20260929`, `cftc_tff_notes`). The CFTC says of all these labels that staff "classifies traders not their trading activity" and "cannot know with certainty that all of that trader's activity is hedging" (`cftc_cot_disagg_notes`).
5. So: in FX the trade-related share is about 5% by turnover and about 3% by futures positions; in a major grain contract commercial positions are about half. "Hedging" proper is bounded above by these shares and is not measured by anyone.

**(b) Whether "the ordinary participant gets what they need" is defensible, and for which definition of ordinary.**

1. By count of participants: not defensible. The participants who are many are retail account-holders. Every national regulator's study ESMA collected found 74% to 89% of retail CFD and rolling-spot-forex accounts losing over the period measured (CySEC, about 290,000 accounts, 76%; KNF, 177,883 accounts, 79.69%; AMF, more than 89% over four years; CONSOB, 75% in rolling spot forex), and the EU risk warning reads "Between 74-89 % of retail investor accounts lose money when trading CFDs" (`esma2018cfd`, recitals 35–36, Annex II). In the US data, "on average, traders lose money when they trade"; the average US retail FX portfolio lost 17% a month before the 2010 leverage cap and 10% after (`heimer2019leverage`, pp. 16, 47). These people are not getting what they need, unless what they need is the bet.
2. By volume: not defensible as stated. The "ordinary" dollar of FX turnover is traded between two financial institutions (95.4%), and the BIS cannot say whether it hedges or speculates. The sentence "only speculators win or lose" has no referent in the data, because the BIS has no speculator category.
3. By market purpose (the exporter, importer, producer or processor with a pre-existing exposure): defensible, and this is the only reading the evidence supports. Hicks defines the class: a hedger is one who "is somehow otherwise committed to making a sale or purchase at the date in question" so that "the forward transaction lessens the riskiness of his position" (`hicks1939value`, p. 137). The class is identifiable in the data (non-financial customers in the BIS; producer/merchant/processor/user in the COT), it gets a fixed price, and it is small: about 5% of FX turnover and about half of corn positions.
4. One more qualification from Working: even the commercial class is not purely a hedging class. Commercial firms practise "discretionary hedging, involving a firm in the practice of both hedging and speculation", hedging when the basis favours it and carrying stocks unhedged when they expect a rise (`working1953futures`, p. 320). The "ordinary participant" who always hedges and never bets is rarer than the category.

**(c) Whether a hedger's gain or loss on the derivative is offset by the underlying exposure.**

1. Yes, by construction, for the pure hedge: the producer who sells forward "can go full steam ahead, selling his product forward and running no risk" (`keynes1930treatise2`, p. 142); the hedge "lessens the riskiness of his position" (`hicks1939value`, p. 137). That is the sense in which the hedger "gets what they need whichever way the price moves": the derivative's loss is the cash position's gain and the reverse.
2. Three corrections, all from sources read in full. First, the offset is paid for. Normal backwardation "measures the amount which hedgers have to hand over to speculators in order to persuade the speculators to take over the risks" (`hicks1939value`, pp. 138–139); Keynes put the cost at 10% a year or more for seasonal crops (p. 143); de Roon, Nijman and Veld find hedging pressure priced in 20 futures markets 1986–1994 (`deroon2000hedging`). The hedger does not "win or lose" on price, but he pays a premium for not doing so. Second, the offset is imperfect: "hedging does not eliminate risks arising from price variability. Risk is less than on stocks held unhedged, but it still exists" (`working1953futures`, p. 325), because the hedged quality or location differs from the contract's (basis risk). Third, Working's hedger is doing arbitrage on the basis, not buying insurance: "hedging ... is not properly comparable with insurance. It is a sort of arbitrage" (p. 325), done "usually in the expectation of a favorable change in the relation between spot and futures prices" (p. 326). He wins or loses on the basis.
3. The test that separates a hedge from a bet is Stout's: a hedge reduces "a preexisting economic risk"; offsetting a bet one has already placed is "hedging a bet", which "simply passing off some of the risk they have already created for themselves" (`stout2011`, p. 25). Lockhart's "extant versus created risk" (facet L, `lockhart2025`) is the same line. This is the test to apply to any claim that a Kalshi position is a hedge.

**(d) The narrowest form of P6 the evidence supports, and the strongest form it refutes.**

1. Supported, narrow form: *Currency and commodity derivative markets contain an identifiable class of participants—exporters, importers, producers, merchants, processors—whose derivative position offsets an exposure they already carry, so that the market lets them fix a price whichever way it later moves; that class is small (about 5% of FX turnover in April 2025, about 3% of Euro FX futures positions, about half of corn futures positions in September 2026); it pays speculators a premium for the service; and no equivalent class is documented for sports event contracts beyond anecdote.* Every clause has a source above.
2. Refuted, strong form (the author's wording): *"In Forex the ordinary participant gets what they need; only speculators win or lose."* By count the ordinary participant is a retail speculator who loses (ESMA; Heimer and Simsek). By volume the ordinary participant is a financial institution whose motive the BIS cannot classify. The hedger who "gets what they need" is a few per cent of the market, and even he pays and carries basis risk. "Only speculators win or lose" also fails on Stout's reading of futures evidence: self-identified speculators lose on average (`stout1999`, pp. 746–747), so they do not "win or lose" symmetrically; the dealers and makers win.
3. The clause "Speculation is not a triadic activity" needs a split. Speculator-with-speculator trade on disagreement is a wager between two parties: "HE speculation is a form of wagering where the gamblers bet on market prices" (`stout1999`, p. 742), zero-sum before costs and negative after (p. 745). That is the author's dyad, and it is what Whelan models on Betfair and Bürgi et al. measure on Kalshi. But hedger-with-speculator trade is a three-role structure: the hedger transfers a risk he did not choose, the speculator takes it for a premium, and the exchange's price co-ordinates them, which Hicks calls "the cost of the co-ordination achieved by forward trading" (p. 139). In that structure the speculator is the third party the hedger needs. So the evidence supports "a bet between two bettors is not co-ordination through a third party" and refutes "speculation plays no part in a triadic activity": in FX and grain, speculation is the part of the triad that carries the risk.
4. The comparison the essay can therefore make is not FX against Kalshi but *hedger-with-speculator* against *speculator-with-speculator*. FX and grain contain both; sports event contracts and betting exchanges, on the evidence found, contain only the second, plus a documented but unmeasured sliver of bookmakers and promotion-liable businesses offsetting bets they chose to carry.

**(e) Mansour's reply that speculators supply the liquidity hedgers need: evidence for and against.**

1. For. Hicks: "No forward market can do without the speculative element" (`hicks1939value`, p. 137 n.). Working, from the data: hedgers "prefer to use the exchange which has the largest volume of speculative trading" (p. 319), because the scalper's margin is a cost of hedging that falls with volume, so "if a hedger has two futures markets to choose between he tends to do his hedging in the more active one" (`working1953futures`, p. 336). De Roon, Nijman and Veld: the premium hedgers pay speculators is measurable in returns (`deroon2000hedging`). The corn COT: managed money net long 20.5% of open interest against commercials net short 39.5% (`cftc_cot_corn_20260929`). The argument is sound and old, and Kalshi's own grain analogy (facet M, A3–A4) is Working's.
2. Against, first: the argument presupposes hedgers. Working's hedging is "the driver" and speculation "a companion going where hedging gives it opportunity to go" (p. 318); where no commercial exposure exists, there is nothing for the liquidity to serve. Kalshi conceded in 2024 that game contracts "are unlikely to serve any 'commercial or hedging interest'" (facet L, `kalshi2024appelleebrief`, p. 45); the CFTC in 2023 found hedging use not demonstrated for the congressional contracts (`cftc2023kalshiorder`, p. 19); Macey and Enriques call hedging use theoretical (`macey2026`). The only hedgers found for sports contracts are anecdotes (`kbtx2026kalshi`). A market of speculators supplying liquidity to speculators is Stout's HE market: "a trading market composed primarily, or even entirely, of speculators hoping to outpredict each other" (`stout1999`, p. 746), in which "when transaction costs are positive, trading makes HE speculators, on average, poorer" (p. 746).
3. Against, second: in FX itself the ratio has run past any hedging need. Turnover is 72 times trade (`huang2025fxhedging`); Stout's test for the OTC market of 2008 ("It is difficult to see how buying a $1 million dollar fire insurance policy on a $250,000 house is 'hedging'", `stout2011`, p. 24) applies with the same arithmetic. The liquidity argument justifies some speculation; it does not fix how much, and the BIS numbers say most FX volume is not serving the 5%.
4. Against, third: the liquidity is paid for by the losing side. On Betfair, takers lose and makers break even after commission (`whelan2026betfair`, p. 32); on Kalshi, takers averaged −31.46% per contract (facet A, `burgi2026makers`); in retail FX, 74% to 89% of accounts lose (`esma2018cfd`). The "liquidity hedgers need" is, in a market without hedgers, a transfer from takers to makers. Hanson's version of the same reply (noise traders attract informed traders; facet H, H9) has the same dependence: it needs someone who wants the price, and Hanson supplies a sponsor or a decision-maker, not a hedger.
5. What the essay owes Mansour: the concession that in grain and currency futures his premise is true and documented, and the demand that he name the hedger in a sports contract. The record shows Kalshi itself could not in 2024.

### Gaps

2. No CFTC or NFA aggregate of the §5.5(e) profitability disclosures was found. US retail FX outcomes rest on Heimer and Simsek's data and on firm-level disclosures.
3. No source measures bookmakers' lay-off volume on exchanges; the HBLB paper records the practice without a number. No source documents a sportsbook hedging on Kalshi or Polymarket.
