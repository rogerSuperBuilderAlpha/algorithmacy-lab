# L: The law and regulation of event contracts versus gambling

*Facet L of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature base. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03 by seat L of the eight-seat research run. The author has not reviewed it; none of the text below is the author's. This is a research note by a non-lawyer agent, not legal advice. Facts here moved quickly through September 2026 and will keep moving; every date is as of 2026-10-03.*

What this facet covers. (1) The legal texts that decide when a CFTC-registered exchange may list an event contract: the Commodity Exchange Act's special rule, CFTC Rule 40.11, the 2008 concept release, the 2011 rule, the 2012 Nadex and 2023 Kalshi orders, the 2024 proposed rule and its 2026 withdrawal, the 2026 advance notice and proposed rule, the staff advisories of 2025 and 2026, and the court opinions on political and sports contracts. (2) The scholarship on where derivatives end and gambling begins. (3) Regulator statements, dissents, CRS analysis, and gaming-regulator and problem-gambling positions on sports contracts. (4) What the record says about consumer-protection differences between a CFTC exchange and a state-licensed sportsbook. Seat M holds Kalshi's and its CEO's own filings and statements; this facet cites rulings, rules and scholars, and quotes a Kalshi position only where a court or the CFTC records it.

## (b) Entries

### B1. The legal texts

`usc7a2c5C` | Commodity Exchange Act § 5c(c)(5)(C), 7 U.S.C. § 7a-2(c)(5)(C) (added by Dodd-Frank Act § 745, Pub. L. 111-203, 2010).
- *Record:* govinfo USCODE-2023-title7-chap1-sec7a-2.
- *Read status:* read (full, the subsection).
- *Operative language:* "(i) Event contracts. In connection with the listing of agreements, contracts, transactions, or swaps in excluded commodities that are based upon the occurrence, extent of an occurrence, or contingency (other than a change in the price, rate, value, or levels of a commodity described in section 1a(2)(i) of this title), by a designated contract market or swap execution facility, the Commission may determine that such agreements, contracts, or transactions are contrary to the public interest if the agreements, contracts, or transactions involve— (I) activity that is unlawful under any Federal or State law; (II) terrorism; (III) assassination; (IV) war; (V) gaming; or (VI) other similar activity determined by the Commission, by rule or regulation, to be contrary to the public interest. (ii) Prohibition. No agreement, contract, or transaction determined by the Commission to be contrary to the public interest under clause (i) may be listed or made available for clearing or trading on or through a registered entity." Clause (iv) sets a 90-day deadline for final action.
- *Bearing:* the test is two-step and permissive: the Commission *may* find a contract contrary to the public interest, and only *if* it involves an enumerated activity. "Gaming" is undefined in the Act. Every dispute below runs through the words "may," "involve," and "gaming."

`cfr40_11` | 17 C.F.R. § 40.11, Review of event contracts based upon certain excluded commodities (adopted 76 FR 44776, 27 July 2011).
- *Record:* eCFR, point-in-time 2026-10-01.
- *Read status:* read (full).
- *Operative language:* "(a) Prohibition. A registered entity shall not list for trading or accept for clearing on or through the registered entity any of the following: (1) An agreement, contract, transaction, or swap based upon an excluded commodity, as defined in Section 1a(19)(iv) of the Act, that involves, relates to, or references terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or an activity that is unlawful under any State or Federal law; or (2) [similar activity the Commission determines by rule to be contrary to the public interest]. (b) [Reserved] (c) 90-day review and approval of certain event contracts. The Commission may determine ... that an agreement, contract, transaction, or swap ... which may involve, relate to, or reference an activity enumerated in § 40.11(a)(1) or § 40.11(a)(2), be subject to a 90-day review."
- *Bearing:* the rule turns the statute's "may determine" into a categorical "shall not list," and widens "involve" to "involves, relates to, or references." Whether the agency had the authority to do so is the question the Ninth Circuit's Judge Lee flagged (B4) and the 2026 NPRM concedes (B2).

`cftc2008concept` | CFTC, Concept Release on the Appropriate Regulatory Treatment of Event Contracts, 73 FR 25669 (7 May 2008).
- *Record:* FR Doc. E8-9981; comments closed 7 July 2008.
- *Read status:* read (targeted).
- *What it says:* the Commission recounts that under former CEA § 5(g) a board of trade had to show that futures trading "will not be contrary to the public interest," and that "The public interest test of Section 5(g) included an 'economic purpose' test, subject to a final test of the public interest" (p. 25672). It defines the two functions: "Hedging occurs when positions acquired are economically appropriate to the reduction of risks in the conduct and management of a commercial enterprise ... Price basing, a function of price discovery and dissemination, can occur when commercial entities enter into transactions in a particular commodity based upon commodity futures prices" (p. 25672, n. 16). Its questions include "3. How should the Commission address the potential gaming aspects of some event contracts and the possible pre-emption of state gaming laws?" (p. 25670) and "12. What objective and readily identifiable factors ... could be used to distinguish event contracts that could appropriately be traded under Commission oversight from transactions that may be viewed as the functional equivalent of gambling?" (p. 25673).
- *Bearing:* supports. The agency itself posed the essay's question in 2008 and never answered it by rule. It also records the catastrophe-insurance-option precedent for event contracts (CBOT single-event insurance options, p. 25670 area), which is the hedging genealogy the sports contracts lack.

`cftc2011part40` | CFTC, Provisions Common to Registered Entities, Final Rule, 76 FR 44776 (27 July 2011).
- *Record:* FR Doc. 2011-18661; RIN 3038-AD07.
- *Read status:* read (targeted; the § 40.11 discussion).
- *What it says:* the CME asked that "gaming" be defined; "The Commission agrees that the term 'gaming' requires further clarification and that the term is not susceptible to easy definition" (p. 44785). The Commission "determined to prohibit contracts based upon the activities enumerated in Section 745 of the Dodd-Frank Act and to consider individual product submissions on a case-by-case basis" (p. 44785), and said its prohibition "is consistent with Congress's intent to 'prevent gambling through the futures markets' and to 'protect the public interest from gaming and other events contracts'" (p. 44786, quoting the Lincoln–Feinstein colloquy, 156 Cong. Rec. S5906, 15 July 2010).
- *Bearing:* complicates. The agency's own adopting release ties the gaming prohibition to preventing gambling, which is the reading Nevada and the Ninth Circuit later used against the agency's permissive 2025–2026 posture.

`cftc2012nadex` | CFTC, Order Prohibiting the Listing or Trading of Political Event Contracts (In re North American Derivatives Exchange), 2 April 2012; press release 6224-12.
- *Read status:* read (full; 4 pp.).
- *What it says:* the Commission found the 2012 election binaries "involve gaming as contemplated by CEA Section 5c(c)(5)(C)(i)(V) and Commission Regulation 40.11(a)(1)" and "are contrary to the public interest" (p. 4). It reasoned that "the legislative history of CEA Section 5c(c)(5)(C) indicates Congress's intent to restore, for the purposes of that provision, the economic purpose test that was used by the Commission ... pursuant to CEA Section 5(g) prior to its deletion by the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000"; "the restored economic purpose test calls for an evaluation of an event contract's utility for hedging and price basing purposes"; and "the unpredictability of the specific economic consequences of an election means that the Political Event Contracts cannot reasonably be expected to be used for hedging purposes" (p. 3). It also used the federal UIGEA definition of "bet or wager" ("the staking or risking by any person of something of value upon the outcome of a contest of others") and said "taking a position in a Political Event Contract fits the plain meaning" (p. 3).
- *Bearing:* supports. This is the first and clearest statement that the public-interest test *is* the economic-purpose (hedging and price-basing) test. Nadex did not sue.

`cftc2023kalshiorder` | CFTC, Order (In re Certification by KalshiEX LLC of Derivatives Contracts with Respect to Political Control of the U.S. Senate and House), 22 September 2023; press release 8780-23.
- *Read status:* read (targeted; pp. 1–3, 8–10, 15–20 of 24).
- *What it says:* review opened 23 June 2023 under § 40.11(c); 1,378 comments (p. 2, n. 1). The contracts "involve 'gaming,' ... because taking a position in the Congressional Control Contracts would be staking something of value upon the outcome of a contest of others" (p. 10). On hedging: "control of a chamber of Congress does not, in and of itself, have sufficiently direct, predictable, or quantifiable economic consequences for the Congressional Control Contracts to serve an effective hedging function" (p. 15); "it has not been demonstrated that the Congressional Control Contracts could reasonably be expected to be used for hedging and/or price basing on more than an occasional basis or ... predominantly by market participants having a commercial or hedging interest" (p. 19). The order adds an election-integrity ground: the contracts could create "monetary incentives to vote for particular candidates" (p. 20). The contract terms themselves gave higher position limits to participants with "demonstrated established economic hedging need" (p. 2).
- *Bearing:* supports the thesis's "dress" reading: the agency applied the hedging test and found no hedge. The order was vacated a year later (B4), on the "involve" question, not on hedging.

`behnam2023` / `mersinger2023` / `pham2023stmt` | Statements of Chairman Behnam, Commissioner Mersinger (dissenting) and Commissioner Pham on the Kalshi order, 22 September 2023.
- *Read status:* read (targeted).
- *What they say:* Behnam: "Betting or wagering on elections, as proposed by Kalshi, meets the definition of gaming. And in many states, betting or wagering on elections is prohibited by statute or common law." Mersinger dissents because the order "Finds that the Congressional Control Contracts are contrary to the public interest on the basis of an 'economic purpose test' that— Is not mentioned in the statute; Has been eliminated for other purposes as a result of amendments to the CEA; and Was not designed for the type of contract at issue," and because "The Commission's practice of adding words to the CEA's event contract provisions began early, when it implemented CEA Section 5c(c)(5)(C) by promulgating CFTC Rule 40.11." Pham's 26 August 2022 dissent on the review had already argued that "the underlying event of political control of Congress is neither gaming nor illegal under Federal or State law" and that "the contracts can be used to hedge predictable financial, economic, and commercial consequences."
- *Bearing:* the two sides of the 2023 Commission map onto the two circuits of 2026 (B4): does "involve" look at the underlying event or at what the trader does?

`cftc2024nprm` | CFTC, Event Contracts, Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, 89 FR 48968 (10 June 2024); extension 89 FR 55528 (5 July 2024).
- *Record:* FR Doc. 2024-12125; RIN 3038-AF14; vote 3–2 (Behnam, Johnson, Goldsmith Romero for; Mersinger and Pham against, p. 48992).
- *Read status:* read (targeted).
- *What it proposed:* a definition: "'gaming' means the staking or risking by any person of something of value upon: (i) The outcome of a contest of others; (ii) The outcome of a game involving skill or chance; (iii) The performance of one or more competitors in one or more contests or games; or (iv) Any other occurrence or non-occurrence in connection with one or more contests or games" (proposed § 40.11(b)(1), p. 48992), with elections, awards and sports games as listed examples. It restates the history: "Pre-CFMA guidelines articulated the economic purpose test as an evaluation of whether a contract reasonably can be expected to be, or has been, used for hedging and/or pricing basing on more than an occasional basis" (p. 48978). On gambling: "The Commission is not a gaming regulator. The CEA and Commission regulations are focused on regulating financial instruments and markets, and do not include provisions aimed at protecting against gambling-specific risks and concerns, including customer protection concerns inherent to gambling. Permitting event contracts involving gaming, as proposed to be defined, to trade on CFTC-regulated markets would in effect permit instruments commonly understood as bets or wagers on contests or games to avoid these legal regimes and protections" (pp. 48982–83; its footnote 122 lists state statutes on self-exclusion, problem-gambling notices, employee training and advertising disclosures). The benefit claimed is to keep contracts that "exist predominantly to enable gambling" from trading "including by retail market participants, as financial instruments on CFTC-regulated markets" (p. 48990).
- *Dissents (appendices 3–4, pp. 48992–48998):* Mersinger calls the proposal "this wolf in sheep's clothing" and objects that the Senators' colloquy "talked about 'gaming' only in the limited context of sporting events." Pham: "I respectfully dissent from the Event Contracts Proposal because it takes the CFTC's regulation of event contract markets backwards with its fundamental misunderstanding of how we regulate derivatives and the States regulate gaming"; "When taking a position in a derivatives contract is gaming, the Commission starts to look like a gaming regulator."
- *Bearing:* supports, strongly. The 2024 Commission wrote the essay's claim in regulatory prose: sports contracts on a CFTC exchange let wagers "avoid" state gambling protections. The proposal was never finalized (next entry).

`cftc2026letter2608` | CFTC Staff Letter 26-08, Division of Market Oversight, "Prediction Markets Advisory," 12 March 2026; press release 9193-26.
- *Read status:* read (targeted; pp. 1–4 of 6).
- *What it says:* event contracts are "rapidly increasing in popularity with the American public both as a financial asset class and as a proven source of reliable information"; staff "believes it is important to encourage innovation and growth in these markets." It reads the swap definition to cover them: "an event contract that is settled based on the outcome of a sporting event is an agreement providing for a payment dependent on a future occurrence" (p. 2). `cftc2026nprm` | CFTC, Prediction Markets; Public Interest Determinations, Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, 91 FR 35806 (12 June 2026).
- *Record:* FR Doc. 2026-11854; RIN 3038-AF65; comments to 27 July 2026; Chairman Selig voting alone (p. 35871). Companion: Data Reporting Requirements for Certain Event Contracts, 91 FR 40102 (1 July 2026) (record only), and the joint CFTC–SEC Request for Comment on Further Definition of "Swap," 91 FR 37873 (24 June 2026) (obtained, not read).
- *Read status:* read (targeted).
- *What it proposes:* (1) "'gaming' means any activity that: (i) One or more participants typically engage in for purposes of recreation or to entertain others; (ii) Is governed by rules; and (iii) Includes measurable occurrences or outcomes that depend on the participants' luck, skill, or athletic ability during the activity" (proposed § 40.11(b)(1), p. 35861): sports are gaming; elections and awards are not. (2) An "event-focused" standard: a contract "involves" an activity "if their settlement is determined by an occurrence, the extent of an occurrence, or a contingency in that activity," focusing "on what happens in the world ... rather than on how trading happens" (p. 35846), which adopts the D.D.C. reading and abandons the 2012–2023 orders. (3) The Commission "preliminarily interprets the Special Rule to require that the Commission determine that event contracts" are contrary to the public interest contract by contract, dropping § 40.11(a)'s categorical "shall not" (p. 35818). (4) The economic-purpose test is demoted: "event contracts' reasonable potential for a hedging or pricing function would be a significant factor against a finding that they are contrary to the public interest" (p. 35829), and "price discovery and information aggregation utility" becomes a public-interest factor in its own right. (5) For sports: "the extent to which event contracts settle based on the overall outcome of a sporting event—including final scores, point differentials, win-loss results, tournament advancement, individual or team statistical performance or season long performance metrics—would be factors against a finding that the event contracts are contrary to the public interest" (p. 35835); contracts on player injury, officiating decisions, "discrete actions, altercations" and "pre-collegiate" sports are "likely to be found to be contrary to the public interest" (pp. 35836, 35871); "event contracts involving games whose outcome depends on random chance—e.g., pure luck—are likely to be contrary to the public interest" (p. 35835). The preamble reports that "in 2025, the total trading volume across CFTC-registered prediction markets exceeded $25 billion" (p. 35807) and cites the agency's own amicus brief for Crypto.com against Nevada (p. 35808). It notes comment letters from eight Senators asking that sports contracts be categorically prohibited and from the players' associations asking that injury contracts be prohibited (pp. 35818, 35836).
- *Bearing:* complicates and supports at once. Complicates: the agency now defines the sports contract as gaming *and* proposes to let aggregate-outcome sports contracts trade, on the ground that they "may serve price discovery functions and provide meaningful information" (p. 35835). Supports: the agency has quietly replaced hedging with "information" as the public purpose, which is the move the essay's thesis names: the forecasting frame licenses the betting product. The proposal is not final as of 2026-10-03.

### B2. Court opinions

`ddc2024kalshi` | *KalshiEX LLC v. CFTC*, No. 1:23-cv-03257-JMC, Memorandum Opinion (D.D.C. 12 Sept. 2024) (Cobb, J.), 2024 WL 4164694; stay denied, 119 F.4th 58 (D.C. Cir. 2024); appeal dismissed on the CFTC's motion, May 2025.
- *Read status:* read (targeted; pp. 1–2, 13–15, 26–27 of 27). The D.C. Circuit stay order and dismissal are recorded from the D. Md. opinion and the 2026 NPRM (p. 35808 area, nn. 149–151), not opened.
- *What it holds:* "The CEA does not define 'gaming.' ... Dictionaries define 'gaming' as 'the practice or activity of playing games for stakes' and 'the practice or activity of playing games.' ... The Court finds no reason to stray from the ordinary definitions" (p. 15). The contracts' question "involves ... elections, politics, Congress, and party control; but nothing that any Party to this litigation has identified as illegal or unlawful activity. Nor does that question bear any relation to any game—played for stakes or otherwise" (p. 26). "The Court's only task is to determine what Congress did, not what it could do or should do. And Congress did not authorize the CFTC to conduct the public interest review it conducted here" (p. 2). The court reviews without *Chevron* deference after *Loper Bright* (p. 13). It records the pre-2000 "economic purpose test" history (p. 13 area) and the CFTC's hedging finding (p. 2 area) but does not reach the public-interest step.
- *Bearing:* complicates. "Gaming" means games. By that reading sports contracts are gaming on their face, which the Ninth and Sixth Circuits later say, and which the 2026 NPRM concedes by definition.

`stout1999` | Stout, L. A. (1999). Why the Law Hates Speculators: Regulation and Private Ordering in the Market for OTC Derivatives. *Duke Law Journal*, 48(4), 701–786.
- *Record:* Crossref DOI 10.2307/1373070; OSF preprint 10.31228/osf.io/fcd9z (the text read; its running heads carry the Duke pagination).
- *Read status:* read (targeted; pp. 701–720, 735–760).
- *What it argues:* "One of the most fundamental of these doctrines is the common law requirement that speculators who want to wager on prices through futures and options agreements have to make and accept delivery of the goods and services they trade" (p. 713). ## (c) Synthesis

### (i) The legal test, in the texts' own words

Congress wrote a permissive, two-step test. Under 7 U.S.C. § 7a-2(c)(5)(C)(i) the Commission "may determine that such agreements, contracts, or transactions are contrary to the public interest if [they] involve— (I) activity that is unlawful under any Federal or State law; (II) terrorism; (III) assassination; (IV) war; (V) gaming; or (VI) other similar activity." A contract so determined "may [not] be listed or made available for clearing or trading" (clause (ii)). The statute defines neither "gaming" nor "public interest." The Commission's 2011 rule, 17 C.F.R. § 40.11(a)(1), converts the permission into a prohibition: a registered entity "shall not list ... any ... swap based upon an excluded commodity ... that involves, relates to, or references terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or an activity that is unlawful under any State or Federal law." The adopting release says the prohibition carries out Congress's intent to "prevent gambling through the futures markets" (76 FR 44786).

What "public interest" means has been answered twice, in opposite ways. From 2012 to 2024 the Commission said the Special Rule restored the pre-2000 "economic purpose test," which "calls for an evaluation of an event contract's utility for hedging and price basing purposes" (Nadex order, p. 3; Kalshi order, pp. 15–19; 89 FR 48978). In June 2026 the Commission proposed to demote that test to one "significant factor" and to add "price discovery and information aggregation utility" as a distinct public good (91 FR 35829). What "gaming" means has also been answered twice. The 2024 proposal defined it by the trader's act, "staking or risking ... something of value upon ... the outcome of a contest of others" (89 FR 48992), which would have swept in elections and sports. The 2026 proposal defines it by the underlying activity, one that participants "engage in for purposes of recreation or to entertain others," "governed by rules," with outcomes that "depend on the participants' luck, skill, or athletic ability" (91 FR 35861), which makes sports gaming and elections not. The D.D.C. had already held in 2024 that "gaming" takes its ordinary meaning, "the practice or activity of playing games for stakes," and that election contracts "bear [no] relation to any game" (pp. 15, 26).

Two things follow for sports contracts. First, under every reading now on the table, judicial and administrative, a contract on a football game "involves gaming." Second, the only question left is whether the Commission chooses to find such contracts "contrary to the public interest," and the current Commission has proposed factors under which contracts settled on "final scores, point differentials, win-loss results" are not (91 FR 35835), while injury, officiating, altercation and pre-collegiate contracts are. As of 30 September 2025 no sports contract had been approved or reviewed; all were self-certified (CFTC Letter 25-36, n. 4). The proposed rule is not final.

A separate test sits upstream of the Special Rule: whether a sports contract is a "swap" at all. The statutory definition reaches a payment "dependent on the occurrence ... of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence" (7 U.S.C. § 1a(47)(A)(ii)). The Third Circuit reads "associated with" literally and finds sponsors, advertisers and networks enough (172 F.4th 220, slip op. 8). The Sixth Circuit requires that the event be one "commonly understood to require financial hedging or price discovery" (slip op. 24), and the Ninth refuses a reading under which "Congress 'hid an elephant in a mousehole'" (slip op. 41). That is the economic-purpose test reappearing inside the definition of the instrument.

### (ii) Scholarly positions on the gambling/derivative boundary, by who argues what

*The classical line: a hedge transfers an existing risk; a wager creates one.* Stout (1999) documents the common-law rule against difference contracts, under which a contract settled by paying price differences without delivery "constitutes nothing more than a wager" (p. 715, quoting *Irwin v. Williar*), its indemnity exception for parties with an insurable interest (p. 719), and the economic reason for the hostility: disagreement-based speculation "is a zero-sum game" that turns negative-sum once transaction costs enter (p. 745). Posner and Weyl (2012, record only) propose to revive the insurable-interest doctrine as an approval test for new financial products. Lynch (2011, record only) names the category "purely speculative derivatives" and calls them gambling by another name. Lockhart (2025) tests five candidate distinctions and keeps one, "extant versus created risk" (p. 2221), while showing that the CFTC's own 2024 rationales (no hedging utility, opaque information, manipulation, participant harm) "fail to distinguish many recently popularized investment products from some gambling products, in particular sports gambling" (p. 2176).

*The regulatory-arbitrage line: the label picks the regime.* Hazen (2005, abstract) asks whether any "rational basis exist[s] for widely differing regulatory schemes" for securities, derivatives, insurance and gambling when the activities are "so similar in nature." Hurt (2006, abstract) finds the same "speculation paradox" and would liberalize gambling rather than restrict finance. Weidner (2022) argues from the sociology side that both markets "possess addictive properties."

*The information line: prediction markets are not gambling because their product is a forecast.* Cherry and Rogers (2008) rest the case on "the participant's expression of their knowledge, skill, and opinion" and "a transcendent expressive purpose—the generating of a conclusion about a future event" (p. 855), and build a First Amendment defense on it; Bell (2002, 2009) and Abramowicz (2004) argue for the markets' legality and administrative use on the same ground (records and abstracts only). Macey and Enriques (2026) take the line to its limit: a prediction-market contract "is a side bet" that "channels no capital to any productive enterprise," hedging use is "in theory" only, and "The informational function is the virtue of such markets" (p. 30). This is also the 2026 CFTC's position: aggregate sports contracts "may serve price discovery functions and provide meaningful information" (91 FR 35835).

*The functional-gambling line: whatever the law calls it, it is gambling for the person doing it.* NCPG (2026) states it as "functionally gambling" by the elements "consideration, chance, and prize" (p. 2); Johnson and Chan (2025) and Kavla and Çoşkunol (2026) state it in addiction journals (records and abstracts only); Judge Roth's dissent ("virtually indistinguishable from the betting products available on online sportsbooks," slip op. 21), the Ninth Circuit ("distinctions without differences," slip op. 29; "The only thing that changes is Kalshi's vocabulary," *Blue Lake* slip op. 24) and the 2024 Commission ("instruments commonly understood as bets or wagers on contests or games," 89 FR 48983) say it from the bench and the agency.

The map has a seam the essay can use. The information line and the functional-gambling line do not disagree about facts. Both accept that no one hedges a Raiders game. They disagree about which fact governs the legal category: what the market produces (a probability) or what the user does (stakes money on an uncertain event). The essay's thesis is that the interface resolves this: a venue whose product is a probability but whose screen asks for a bet has chosen the user's side of the seam.

### (iv) Bearing on the essay's thesis

*Supports.*
1. The regulator that licensed the product said, in 2024, what the essay says: permitting gaming contracts on CFTC markets "would in effect permit instruments commonly understood as bets or wagers on contests or games to avoid these legal regimes and protections," and "The Commission is not a gaming regulator" (89 FR 48982–83). That text was withdrawn, not refuted.
2. Two circuits and a dissenting judge in the third describe the sports contract as a sportsbook wager under another name: "performative sleight," "distinctions without differences," "Kalshi may reshuffle the cards, but it cannot change the hand." Those are judicial findings about the dress.
3. The hedging claim has been abandoned by its proponents. Kalshi conceded in 2024 that game contracts are "unlikely to serve any 'commercial or hedging interest'" and that its sports contracts have "no inherent economic significance" (quoted at *Martin* p. 16 and *Schuler* p. 27); Macey and Enriques call the hedging use theoretical; the 2026 CFTC demotes hedging to a factor and substitutes "information aggregation." The public purpose that remains is the forecast, which is the essay's point of departure: the venue's licensed purpose is the probability, and its interface sells the bet.
4. The volume mix the essay needs has a court and a CRS source: "Over 90% of Kalshi's trades in 2025, representing 95% of its revenue, were sports related" (*Assad* slip op. 14); "reportedly ... more than 85% of trading volume on Kalshi" (CRS LSB11441). Seat D's "about 80%" can be upgraded, with the caveat that the Ninth Circuit's figure comes from the litigation record and CRS says "reportedly."
5. Age and protections: the 2024 NPRM's footnote 122 and the NCPG comment list what state gambling law requires (self-exclusion, limit-setting, problem-gambling notices, advertising disclosures, age 21 in NCPG's recommendation) and the CEA does not. The Ninth Circuit records that the contracts "create risk, largely for ordinary consumers, where none previously existed."

*Complicates.*
1. The Third Circuit's holding is law in New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware: there the contracts are swaps and state gambling law is preempted. The essay cannot say the product "is" sports betting as a matter of settled law; it can say that two of three circuits, a state court, a tribal-law panel and the 2024 Commission so describe it.
2. The information defense is coherent and has serious proponents (Cherry and Rogers; Macey and Enriques; the 2026 CFTC). If aggregate sports prices do carry information that someone uses, the design critique must concede the product has a non-gambling output and argue about the interface, not the output. That is where the thesis already sits, but the essay should quote the defense fairly.
3. The CFTC's reversal cuts both ways: it shows the category is a policy choice, but it also means a federal agency has formally proposed that these contracts serve "price discovery" and are not contrary to the public interest. The essay is arguing against a live regulatory position, not a loophole.

*Refutes.* Nothing in the record refutes the design claim, because no legal source addresses the interface. The sources that come closest, the Roth dissent and *Blue Lake*, describe the screen (point spreads, props, parlays) and treat it as evidence of gambling; none asks whether a different interface would change the category. The gap is the essay's.

*Not legal advice; a note on altitude.* The author is a management scholar. The essay can safely state the statute and rule verbatim, the dated holdings, and the agency's two definitions. It should not characterize what the law "is" on preemption beyond "split, pending."
