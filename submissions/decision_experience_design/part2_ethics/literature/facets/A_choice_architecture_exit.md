# A — Choice architecture, disclosure, and the price of exit

*Facet A of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 2 literature review. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-01 (a parallel research agent working in the `wt-dxd` worktree). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The ethical defense of choice architecture (Thaler and Sunstein's libertarian paternalism) and the conditions it needs; the digital and adaptive versions of choice architecture; the notice-and-consent and transparency critiques; Hirschman's exit, voice and loyalty as applied to platform work and automated decisions; the contestability literature as a voice channel; and the documented price of non-participation in credit, hiring, clinical algorithms and platform dispatch. **The questions the synthesis answers** are RQ-A1 to RQ-A4 in OUTLINE.md section (d): when the libertarian-paternalist defense holds and which conditions an adaptive, multi-sided intermediary breaks (A1); what the disclosure critique establishes and whether it already reaches the no-exit case (A2); how exit, voice and loyalty map onto unavoidable systems (A3); and what non-participation costs in credit, triage, hiring and dispatch (A4).

### B1. The defense of choice architecture and the conditions it needs (RQ-A1)

**thaler2008nudge** — Thaler, R. H., & Sunstein, C. R. (2008). *Nudge: Improving decisions about health, wealth, and happiness*. Yale University Press. [DOI: none; ISBN not verified]
- *Read status:* card (reused: `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/thaler2008.md`). The card records a secondary read: Thaler's 2018 *Science* editorial (doi:10.1126/science.aau9241) and Sunstein's draft, not the book. I did not open the book.
- *What it argues:* choice architects can improve decisions "without restricting any options"; the test is whether choosers are better off "as judged by themselves" (both quotations from the 2018 editorial, per the card).
- *What Part 2 can rely on:* two conditions carry the defense. (i) Freedom to choose otherwise at low cost: Schmidt and Engelen (2020, p. 5) quote the book (p. 6) that a nudge must be "easy and cheap" to avoid; Hansen (2016, p. 156, n. 12) records the same test but cites a footnote on p. 8. The two secondary sources disagree on the page, so cite neither until the book is checked. (ii) An architect who serves the chooser's own ends. Section 3 of the outline can state both conditions as the book's, citing these two secondary sources for the page.
- *Bearing:* **supports** the outline's reading that the defense presupposes avoidability; **cannot support** any page-level quotation until the book is checked.

**hansen2016definition** — Hansen, P. G. (2016). The definition of nudge and libertarian paternalism: Does the hand fit the glove? *European Journal of Risk Regulation, 7*(1), 155–174. https://doi.org/10.1017/s1867299x00005468
- *Read status:* read in full (CC-BY PDF, 20 pp.).
- *What it argues:* Hansen separates nudge from libertarian paternalism and offers a revised definition: a nudge works through "cognitive boundaries, biases, routines and habits" and "independently of" forbidding or adding rationally relevant options, changing incentives, or providing factual information (p. 158, abstract and introduction; restated p. 168).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* (1) p. 156: the "easy or cheap to avoid" test is "a little too vague to be useful"; imposing a tax is not a nudge, charging 5 cents for plastic bags is. (2) p. 160: the Thaler-Sunstein definition rearranged as "any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behaviour in a predictable way without 1) forbidding any options or 2) significantly changing their economic incentives." (3) p. 158: because nudges work independently of information provision, disclosure belongs to libertarian paternalism but is not itself a nudge.
- *Bearing:* **complicates.** The pitch and outline treat "disclose, arrange options, let the chooser decide" as one thing. Hansen shows the nudge literature itself separates information from choice architecture; Section 3 should say which tradition each critique targets.

**schmidt2020ethics** — Schmidt, A. T., & Engelen, B. (2020). The ethics of nudging: An overview. *Philosophy Compass, 15*(4), e12658. https://doi.org/10.1111/phc3.12658 [Part 1 key]
- *Read status:* read in full (Tilburg repository PDF, 13 pp.). Pages below are pdf pages of that 13-page file; the page-number headers sit ambiguously, so treat each as plus or minus one.
- *What it argues:* the autonomy, manipulation, dignity and structural-reform objections do not defeat the nudge program as a whole but guide case-by-case judgment.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* (1) p. 5, Section 4.1(ii): nudges should not be so strong that they stop being "easily resistible" or "easy and cheap" to avoid (quoting Saghai 2013 and Thaler & Sunstein 2008, p. 6); "effectiveness" is "a double-edged sword" because a more effective nudge is a less resistible one, though "given the typical effect sizes of nudge policies, such worries about resistibility are unlikely to apply to most nudges." (2) p. 4: "intentional nudging can often be avoided, but choice architecture cannot", the inevitability argument. (3) Section 4.1(iv), pp. 6–7, on domination: transparency and democratic control are the proposed answers to the worry that nudging escapes control. (4) p. 10, the closing research agenda: "digital choice architecture" is "a particularly fruitful and underresearched area"; its transparency "is far from" adequate, machine learning can create "black boxes in choice architecture where we do not even understand afterwards why an algorithm presented choices", and digital nudges "are often not only inevitable but also effective"; current approaches "focus too much on individuals while neglecting collective action."
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 3 (the defense rests on resistibility, transparency and a controllable architect, and the overview itself marks the digital case as open). **Complicates** the outline's implication that nudge ethics has no reply to scale: the overview argues most nudges are weak enough that resistibility worries rarely bite (p. 5).

**rebonato2014critical** — Rebonato, R. (2014). A critical assessment of libertarian paternalism. *Journal of Consumer Policy, 37*(3), 357–396. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10603-014-9265-1
- *Read status:* abstract only (SSRN copy blocked).
- *What it argues:* the abstract says the "distinction between effective and nominal ability to reverse a nudge is more important than its theoretical ease of reversibility", especially if anchoring, framing and status-quo bias are as powerful as nudge advocates claim, and that non-transparent effective nudges raise legitimacy and autonomy questions.
- *Bearing:* **supports** the outline's claim that the defense depends on exit being effective, not nominal. This is the closest source to the outline's phrase "priced as marginalization" inside the nudge debate itself. Needs a full read before quotation.

**bargill2020rethinking** — Bar-Gill, O., & Ben-Shahar, O. (2020). *Rethinking nudge: An information-costs theory of default rules* [SSRN working paper]. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3582129
- *Read status:* abstract only. **Preprint, not peer reviewed** in the version I found.
- *What it argues:* opt-out cost is the design variable; it includes "mechanical" effort and, more importantly, the information needed to opt out intelligently. High information costs "need not make defaults stickier; they may instead make the defaults 'slippery'" through "uninformed opt-out."
- *Bearing:* **complicates** and **supports.** It supports the thesis that disclosure without capacity does not make exit meaningful (an opt-out without the knowledge to use it). It complicates Ruling 2 only slightly: information still matters, which is the predictability-and-learning point. Verify the published version before citing.

**lemken2024architects** — Lemken, D., Erhard, A., & Wahnschafft, S. (2024). A choice architect's guide to the (autonomous) galaxy: A systematic scoping review of nudge intrusiveness in food choices. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 11*, Article 1030. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-024-03555-8
- *Read status:* read in full (CC-BY PDF, 15 pp.).
- *What it argues:* a scoping review of 146 food-choice studies (251 interventions) derives three design mechanisms that can intrude on autonomy: the effort to opt out (economic and physical), affective influence, and non-transparency (p. 4).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* of 251 interventions, 74 (29.4%) altered the effort to opt out, and just under a third of those (29.7%) set obstacles that "run" against autonomy (p. 4). The discussion (p. 10) names "effort to opt out" as "the first criterion" for judging intrusiveness and introduces "nudge stacking": the collective effort to opt out of several nudges, which "can sum up to a 'shove'" and makes each nudge harder for a watchful chooser to identify (p. 10, citing Coons & Weber 2013 and Ivanković & Engelen 2019).
- *Bearing:* **supports.** Gives Section 3 an operational, citable version of "exit priced": opt-out effort as a measurable design property. **Limit:** food choices only; nothing here is about intermediaries.

**yeung2017hypernudge** — Yeung, K. (2017). "Hypernudge": Big Data as a mode of regulation by design. *Information, Communication & Society, 20*(1), 118–136. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1186713 [Part 1 key]
- *Read status:* read in part (the author-accepted manuscript in the lab library; the card records a full read). The AAM lacks the version-of-record pagination, so I locate by section.
- *What it argues:* Big Data nudging reconfigures the choice architecture continuously and in real time "in three directions": refinement from the target's behavior, data feedback to the architect, and refinement from population-wide trends. It is "nimble, unobtrusive and highly potent," hence "hypernudge" (Section 2, AAM pp. 5–6).
- *What Part 2 can rely on:* Section 3 asks whether "the rearrangement of the informational choice architecture intended to nudge the agent's choices in particular directions" counts as an "interference" with choice at all, noting that "Choices cannot be made abstracted from their context" (AAM Section 3, on autonomy).
- *Bearing:* **supports** the outline's claim that the identifiable-architect and fixed-menu assumptions fail for adaptive systems. **Refutes** the pitch's description of choice architecture as "passive user presented with static options" (PITCH.md line 121): the best-known critique of choice architecture in the data-driven setting is about its dynamism.

**susser2019technology** — Susser, D., Roessler, B., & Nissenbaum, H. (2019). Technology, autonomy, and manipulation. *Internet Policy Review, 8*(2). https://doi.org/10.14763/2019.2.1410 [Part 1 key]
- *Read status:* read in full (CC-BY PDF, 22 pp.).
- *What it argues:* online manipulation is "the use of information technology to covertly influence another person's decision-making, by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities" (abstract); the deeper harm is to autonomy.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* (1) p. 5 (nudging section): they define nudging as intentional alteration of "decision-making context" and note Thaler and Sunstein's argument that designers "can't help but influence" decisions. (2) p. 6: informational nudges such as nutrition labels are not manipulative, while the cafeteria placement is "plausibly manipulative" because it operates "outside the individual's conscious awareness"; manipulative does not mean unjustified. (3) p. 7: digital platforms are "dynamic, interactive, intrusive, and adaptive choice architectures." (4) p. 8: gig firms such as Uber use "soft control" through app prompts, for example reminding drivers who try to leave the app of "their progress toward some earnings goal."
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 3 and the Section 2 platform-work example. **Complicates** any blanket claim that nudges are manipulation: the paper sorts them by hiddenness and exploitation of vulnerability.

### B2. The disclosure critique and the no-exit case (RQ-A2)

**nissenbaum2011contextual** — Nissenbaum, H. (2011). A contextual approach to privacy online. *Daedalus, 140*(4), 32–48. https://doi.org/10.1162/daed_a_00113
- *Read status:* card (reused: `nissenbaum2011.md`; the card records the full essay read twice, locators from the typeset PDF). I did not re-read it.
- *What it argues:* notice-and-consent ("transparency-and-choice") fails for structural reasons, not for lack of better notices.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on (per the card):* p. 35, "there is considerable agreement that transparency-and-choice has failed"; p. 36, the "transparency paradox": "transparency of textual meaning and transparency of practice conflict in all but rare instances," because the practice is "a recursive capacity that is indefinitely extensible"; p. 36, in medicine consent works within "a framework of supporting assurances," whereas online "individual consent agreements must carry the entire weight of expectation"; p. 43, "burdening individuals with the full weight ... is unlikely to yield success."
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 2, with a qualification the pitch misses. Nissenbaum's argument does not need absent exit: consent fails even where refusal is possible, because notice cannot carry the weight. The "exit" argument is a second, separate argument.

**anannycrawford2018seeing** — Ananny, M., & Crawford, K. (2018). Seeing without knowing: Limitations of the transparency ideal and its application to algorithmic accountability. *New Media & Society, 20*(3), 973–989. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444816676645
- *Read status:* abstract only for the article itself (card, reused: `anannycrawford2018.md`, records abstract plus secondary readings). I also read Edwards and Veale's (2017, p. 67) summary of it (entry below), which attributes to Ananny and Crawford the point that transparency can support "neoliberal models of agency" and leave individuals "disconnected from power."
- *What it argues:* the abstract: "Being able to see a system is sometimes equated with being able to know how it works and govern it"; the authors "present 10 limitations" of the transparency ideal and sketch an alternative typology of accountability.
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 2 on the limits of transparency. The ten limitations are not enumerated in what I could read; do not list them. Not read in full.

**edwards2017slave** — Edwards, L., & Veale, M. (2017). Slave to the algorithm? Why a "right to an explanation" is probably not the remedy you are looking for. *Duke Law & Technology Review, 16*(1), 18–84. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2972855 (SSRN preprint DOI; the journal has none)
- *Read status:* read in part (lab library PDF; the card records a full read; I re-read the abstract and Part IV.A, pp. 66–67).
- *What it argues:* an individual "right to an explanation" risks a "transparency fallacy" like the "notice and choice fallacy."
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* p. 66: consent online has become "a mere husk of its former self," "meaningless" or "illusory," and it "legitimises the extraction of personal data from unwitting data subjects." p. 67: "Individuals are mostly too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise to meaningfully make use of these individual rights"; "in some ways, the transparency fallacy is even worse than its consent cousin, since the explanation itself may not be meaningful enough to confer much autonomy even on the most empowered data subject." Their remedies are systemic and ex ante (privacy by design, impact assessments, certification), not individual disclosure.
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 2 and the "distributes information without capacity" claim; the time, resource and expertise trio is the nearest legal statement of it. **Limit:** a GDPR-specific argument about explanation rights, not about mandatory use; the card flags that EU law has moved since 2017.

**obar2020biggest** — Obar, J. A., & Oeldorf-Hirsch, A. (2020). The biggest lie on the Internet: Ignoring the privacy policies and terms of service policies of social networking services. *Information, Communication & Society, 23*(1), 128–147. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2018.1486870
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues and finds:* in an experimental survey (N = 543) joining a fictitious social network, 74% skipped the privacy policy by choosing "quick join"; 97% agreed to the privacy policy and 93% to the terms of service. Adult reading speed implies 29–32 minutes for the privacy policy; those who read it averaged 73 seconds. "Information overload" predicted not reading the terms.
- *Bearing:* **supports** the outline's claim that disclosure ritualizes consent. **Limit:** a fictitious service where refusing cost nothing. It measures non-reading, not forced use.

**kizilcec2016much** — Kizilcec, R. F. (2016). How much information? Effects of transparency on trust in an algorithmic interface. In *Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 2390–2395). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858402
- *Read status:* abstract only. Refereed CHI paper.
- *What it finds:* in an online field experiment on algorithmic peer grading (three levels of transparency), people whose expectations were violated by a low grade "trusted the system less, unless the grading algorithm was made more transparent through explanation. However, providing too much information eroded this trust." For people whose expectations were met, transparency changed nothing.
- *Bearing:* **complicates** the pitch. The pitch says disclosure "does nothing to prepare someone." This study shows disclosure can calibrate trust at the right amount, and that the amount matters. Under Ruling 2 it is evidence about when predictability holds, not about skill: it measures trust, not performance. Handle as that.

**draper2019corporate** — Draper, N. A., & Turow, J. (2019). The corporate cultivation of digital resignation. *New Media & Society, 21*(8), 1824–1839. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444819833331
- *Read status:* abstract only (closed access).
- *What it argues:* "digital resignation" is "the condition produced when people desire to control the information digital entities have about them but feel unable to do so." The authors "conceptualize digital resignation as a rational response to consumer surveillance" and argue that routine corporate practices encourage the helplessness.
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 2. This is the nearest published term for what the pitch calls "coopted": a named, cited construct (digital resignation) for the state in which disclosure exists and no one acts on it. Prefer it to any coinage. **Limit:** about privacy, not decision quality.

**bagger2023digital** — Bagger, C., Einarsson, A. M., Andelsman Alvarez, V., Klausen, M., & Lomborg, S. (2023). Digital resignation and the datafied welfare state. *Big Data & Society, 10*(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517231206806
- *Read status:* read in full (CC BY-NC-ND PDF, a 6-page commentary).
- *What it argues:* digital resignation research should extend from commercial platforms to public institutions, where use is required.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* p. 3: schools demand responsiveness from parents on a platform; such practices of being "forced to comply with digital systems ... to avoid social and personal consequences" are what Barassi (2019) calls "coerced digital participation"; "the datafied welfare state is not just cultivating digital resignation but also mandating digital participation." p. 3: in Danish healthcare, "for patients as for parents, opting out of digital and datafying systems is generally not perceived as an option," and a "good patient" is equated with "a digital patient."
- *Bearing:* **supports** the no-exit case explicitly, for public services. **Limit:** a commentary with ongoing-research examples, not a measured study; Denmark only.

**bietti2020consent** — Bietti, E. (2020). Consent as a free pass: Platform power and the limits of the informational turn. *Pace Law Review, 40*(1), 310. https://doi.org/10.58948/2331-3528.2013
- *Read status:* abstract only (the OA PDF was bot-blocked).
- *What it argues:* notice and consent are "normatively futile" and "positively harmful" in the platform economy. Narrow views that focus on "voluntariness and disclosure ... fail to account for the systemically unjust background conditions within which voluntary individual acts of consent take place."
- *Bearing:* **supports** Section 2. This is a law-review argument that reaches the no-exit case from the background-conditions side. Needs a full read for page locators.

**diazandrade2021digital** — Díaz Andrade, A., & Techatassanasoontorn, A. A. (2021). Digital enforcement: Rethinking the pursuit of a digitally-enabled society. *Information Systems Journal, 31*(1), 184–197. https://doi.org/10.1111/isj.12306 (online 2020)

## (d) Synthesis

### RQ-A1. When the libertarian-paternalist defense holds, and what adaptive intermediaries break

The defense holds on three conditions, and the sources agree on them. First, avoidability: the nudge must be "easy and cheap" to avoid (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008, p. 6 per Schmidt & Engelen, 2020, p. 5; Hansen, 2016, p. 156, cites a footnote on p. 8 for the same test). Second, no forbidden options or changed incentives (Hansen, 2016, p. 160). Third, an architect who aims at the chooser's own ends and can be held to account, which Schmidt and Engelen (2020, Section 4.1(iv)) answer with transparency and democratic control.

Adaptive, multi-sided intermediaries strain all three. On avoidability, Rebonato's (2014) distinction between nominal and effective reversibility, Lemken et al.'s (2024, pp. 9–10) opt-out effort and "nudge stacking," and Bar-Gill and Ben-Shahar's (2020) "uninformed opt-out" give the nudge literature's own measures of when a formally free exit is not free. On the identifiable architect, Yeung (2017, Section 2) and Susser et al. (2019, pp. 5–8) describe choice architecture reconfigured continuously and per person, and Schmidt and Engelen (2020, p. 10) say that with machine learning "we do not even understand afterwards why an algorithm presented choices." The third condition, an architect serving the chooser's ends, has the thinnest evidence here. The Hirschman card (and Calo & Rosenblat, 2017, on drivers) point to an intermediary with its own objective; I found no source that tests the condition directly.

One point cuts the other way and the outline should keep it: Thaler and Sunstein's inevitability argument ("intentional nudging can often be avoided, but choice architecture cannot," Schmidt & Engelen, 2020, p. 4) is the strongest published support for the DXD claim that no interface is neutral. The pitch can use it.

### RQ-A2. What the disclosure critique establishes, and whether it reaches the no-exit case

It establishes two things that the pitch runs together. The first holds with or without exit: disclosure does not confer capacity. Nissenbaum (2011, pp. 35–36, per the card) shows that notice cannot describe a recursive practice; Edwards and Veale (2017, p. 67) show that individuals are "too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise" to use rights to explanations; Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2020) show 74% skipping and 97% agreeing in a setting where refusing cost nothing. The second does depend on exit: consent presupposes that refusal is possible. That second argument is made explicitly for the no-exit case by Bagger et al. (2023, p. 3: opting out "is generally not perceived as an option"), Díaz Andrade and Techatassanasoontorn (2021), Park and Humphry (2019), Draper and Turow (2019, as a named condition, digital resignation), and Bietti (2020, "systemically unjust background conditions"). So the answer to the second half of RQ-A2 is yes, in several peer-reviewed sources, though mostly about privacy and welfare, not decision quality, and mostly qualitative.

### RQ-A3. Exit, voice and loyalty for unavoidable systems

Hirschman supplies the frame and two usable conditions. Where exit is unavailable, "voice must carry the entire burden" (1970, p. 34), and voice presupposes members "endowed with some capacity to articulate" discontent (p. 34) and "influence and bargaining power" (p. 40). Both conditions are what a DXD design obligation can supply: the capacity to specify intent and a low-cost channel to do it. The platform-work literature shows voice channels that exist but are bounded (Gegenhuber et al., 2021: "microphones, not megaphones"), suppressed by the app (Kougiannou & Mendonça, 2021), or answered by templates (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016, p. 3771). The one experiment I read on appeals found none of the designs improved perceived fairness over no appeal (Vaccaro et al., 2020, Section 5.1). Two cautions. Hirschman (1993) shows exit and voice can reinforce each other, so the outline should not call them substitutes. And the Hirschman card's reminder stands: his firm wants to recover, which an intermediary pursuing a different objective may not.

### Pitch claims the evidence contradicts or qualifies

1. **"Choice architecture assumes a passive user presented with static options"** (PITCH.md line 121). The facet's best sources describe choice architecture as dynamic and personal (Yeung, 2017; Susser et al., 2019), and the nudge literature itself separates information provision from nudging (Hansen, 2016, p. 158). The contrast the outline wants (arrange options versus design conditions for learning) survives, but the "passive/static" description of choice architecture does not.
2. **"Disclosure assumes there is an exit"** (PITCH.md lines 23, 115). True of consent-based disclosure and false of the main transparency critiques, which fail even where refusal is free. State two arguments, not one: disclosure cannot confer capacity (with or without exit), and consent presupposes refusability (fails under mandatory participation).
3. **"There is no exit. You cannot opt out of modern credit, healthcare triage, corporate management, or information discovery"** (PITCH.md line 117). Overstated. About 45 million U.S. adults lived outside scored credit in 2010 (Brevoort et al., 2015); a vignette study finds half of respondents prefer a human clinic (Robertson et al., 2023); platform workers do exit, or build "pirate" alternatives (Maffie, 2023). The outline's own phrase, "unavailable or ruinously priced," is the supportable version, documented for credit and platform dispatch, partly for hiring, and unmeasured for triage and information discovery.
4. **"Triadic coordination is pervasive and inescapable; we are all coopted"** (PITCH.md line 23). Overgeneral. Workers report autonomy (Wood et al., 2019) and consent produced "through constant and confined choices" (Cameron, 2024). That strengthens the case for design but weakens "coopted": the participant may not feel coerced. Section 2 should address this explicitly.
5. **"Revealing code, weights, or plain-language data disclosures does nothing"** (PITCH.md line 23).
