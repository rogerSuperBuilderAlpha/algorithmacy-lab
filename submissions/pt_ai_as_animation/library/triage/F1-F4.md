## F1 Philosophy of life: definitions and their critics (c3)

No lab library holds a card on the definition of life or on artificial life as a field; the registry has no
Trifonov, Machery, Cleland, Bedau, Langton or Dorin. Most of the anchors are closed: Trifonov, Machery,
Cleland & Chyba and Bedau (2003) are paywalled articles and Cleland (2019) is a Cambridge book, so five of the
eight go to `BROWSER_LIST.md`. The open slots are Bich & Green (green), Dorin & Stepney (bronze; a copy is
already held in Berthon's arm, CITED_WORKS) and the one swap. I replaced Schrödinger's *What is life?* (a
metadata card the sketch never needs) with Bedau, McCaskill, Packard & Rasmussen (2010), "Living technology":
its abstract defines "living technology—that is, technology that is based on the powerful core features of
life", which is the *making* sense of life c4 and c12 need, and the boundary with Berthon's lineage question.
The counter is stocked twice: Machery argues the definitional project is pointless, and Bich & Green argue
operational definitions survive him. Knuuttila & Loettgers (R1) is the open third voice if Bich & Green
cannot be had.

## F2 Organizational and autopoietic accounts (c4)

Checkpoint A ruled for the organizational claim and raised F2 to 9. The ruling's list is Maturana & Varela,
Varela et al., Moreno & Mossio, Montévil & Mossio, Thompson, Di Paolo, Froese & Ziemke, Bongard & Levin and
Meincke. I kept eight and swapped one: Thompson's *Mind in Life* (a closed book whose ground Di Paolo and Froese
& Ziemke cover in open articles) goes to reserve, and Villalobos & Dewhurst (2018) takes the slot. Their abstract
says they "demonstrate that a computational system can meet the criteria for autonomy laid down by classical
enactivism", namely operational closure and structural determinism, and aims "to challenge the assumption that
computational systems are necessarily heteronomous systems". That is the head-on reply to Moreno & Mossio's
self-production requirement that c4 must answer (Checkpoint A, ruling 1). It also cuts against c4 in one
respect: if a bare Turing machine can be operationally closed, closure alone cannot say why the harness, and not
the model, makes the AI. The counter side is stocked by Moreno & Mossio and Montévil & Mossio (closure needs
self-produced constraints), Meincke (process, not organization) and, in reserve, Roli, Jaeger & Kauffman (only
organisms can leverage new affordances).

Two plan-relevant finds. The HAL record of *Biological Autonomy* (hal-01151449) is a deposit titled
"Introduction" whose file is named `Book_Autonomy_web.pdf`; S4 opens it to see whether it holds the introduction
or more. And Bich (2024), *Biological Organization* (R3), is an open-access Cambridge Element that "presents an
updated version" of the organizational approach (abstract): if Moreno & Mossio stays closed, Bich is the open
book-length statement.

## F3 Intelligence and the intentional stance (c3, c5)

Checkpoint A set F3 at 7 and required Floridi. The seven: Legg & Hutter twice (the 70-odd definitions and the
formal definition), Chollet (intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency, the benchmark frame), Dennett's book

FACETS calls Dennett (1971) "the article S5 reads in full", but OpenAlex marks it closed (*Journal of
Philosophy*, Philosophy Documentation Center), as it does "Real patterns" (1991). Second, "Real patterns" has an
open route anyway: the MIT Press published it in 2026 as a chapter of an open edited volume, *Dennett's Real
Patterns in Science and Nature*, pp. 33–58. So the stance argument has one open text, and §2's answer to the
instrumentalism charge can be read in full. Dennett (1971) moves to reserve as a closed duplicate of the book's
argument; the *BBS* Précis (R2) is the better closed substitute, since its abstract already states that any
predictable system is "an intentional system, whatever its innards", which bears on c2.

## F4 Animation production pipeline (c6, c1)

F4 has the two upgrades and seven dxda pointers FACETS names, and no card on the production craft itself. The
FACETS six were Williams, Catmull, Burtnyk & Wein, Reeves, Crafton and Hooks. The sketch, though, makes
stop-motion "the contrast case (a person authors every frame)" in §3 and §5, and no F4 anchor covers it; F5's
puppetry anchors cover puppets, not frame-by-frame production. So I added Purves (2014), *Stop-motion Animation:
Frame by Frame Film-making with Puppets and Models*, and moved Reeves to the top of the reserve: Catmull (1978)
already carries in-betweening as the problem, and Burtnyk & Wein (1976) the rig. The counter is Catmull: his
abstract says the systems aimed "to let the computer produce missing drawings based on extreme drawings
produced by animators", and that the difficulty is that drawings are "two dimensional projections of three
dimensional characters as visualized in the animators head, hence information is lost". Read one way, this
supports c7 (the missing frames cannot be recovered from the frames); read the other way, it shows missing
frames were an engineering problem the field went on to solve, which strains "there will always be missing
frames" unless §4.2 restates it as motion (FACETS counter). Almost everything here is closed: four books and
two ACM papers that OpenAlex marks closed.
