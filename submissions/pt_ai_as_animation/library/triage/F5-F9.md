## F5 Puppetry, performance capture, liveness (c10, c6)

F5 is the limit case of §5, and the lab holds only Silvio (2019, metadata) and Silvio (2010, abstract). Every
FACETS anchor confirmed, and the Kleist gap closed: Crossref holds the *TDR* translation, "On the Marionette
Theatre" (1972), as an item with pages, so the card no longer rests on a 2011 companion chapter; the German text
is public domain (Wikisource, below). Almost everything is closed: four of the five F5 works are books or
paywalled journal items, and the one open work is a G-tagged preprint. I took Auslander's third edition (2022)
over the second (2008): its abstract says the author "thoroughly updates his provocative argument to take into
account the impact of the internet", which is the edition an LLM argument should answer; the 2008 record (650
citations on OpenAlex, against 93) goes in the key notes as the fallback.

The counter is stocked twice. Tillis (the visible double of object and operator) and Kaplin (distance and
ratio between performer and object) give the axes on which §5 must place an LLM, and both cut against c6: if
the LLM sits nearer the puppet than the cartoon, the paper's choice of animation needs defending. Allison
supplies the middle case: performance capture already blends performer and animator.

Two works from the Consensus G file are puppetry works about AI, so I card them here with a **G tag**, as the
lead allowed. Hayes-Roth (2006) calls AI systems "puppets" so as "to respect the importance of the programmers and
builders who were actually responsible for their accomplishments", and names "artificial life" among the efforts
to cross from puppetry to "creationism" (abstract). That is the intelligence-versus-life contrast of §2 and the
§5 limit case in one three-page column. Sun & Gualeni (2025) place "AI agency as existing somewhere between that
of an inanimate puppet and a performing actor" (arXiv abstract), which is Kaplin's axis applied to LLMs. Both
bear on c12: neither frames *development* as animation craft, but the G triage must know they exist.

## F7 Epistemic opacity, in principle vs practical (c7)

The registry holds Burrell (2016) in seven libraries and nothing from the philosophy-of-science opacity debate:
no Humphreys, Creel, Zerilli, Durán, Boge or Sullivan (the `humphreys2010` and `humphreys2005` keys are other
Humphreys). Roger's "in principle black box" is Humphreys's essential epistemic opacity, and the whole debate
since 2018 is about how far "in principle" reaches. The facet is unusually open: five of the seven read in full
from hybrid or PhilSci-Archive copies.

The counter is the larger half of the shortlist, by design. Creel's abstract proposes "transparency as having
three forms: transparency of the algorithm, the realization of the algorithm in code, and the way that code is
run on particular hardware and data"; each is practical, not in-principle. Zerilli (2022) argues XAI is right to
prioritise "interpretability at the expense of completeness". Zerilli et al. (2019), in *P&T* itself, ask whether
we hold machines to a double standard. Sullivan's abstract argues "it is not the complexity or black b[ox nature]"
that limits understanding (truncated in the record). On the other side, Durán & Formanek and Boge argue what
essential opacity does and does not license. I swapped Humphreys's *Extending Ourselves* (2004, a closed book
that would be a metadata card) out to reserve and put Sullivan (2022) in: the 2009 article carries the term,
and the facet needed the understanding counter more than a second Humphreys.

## F9 Co-optation, lock-in, suspension of disbelief (c8)

F9 joins two literatures, and the lab already holds one of them in full: lock-in economics and organisational
co-optation sit in `research` as full-text cards (Arthur, Shapiro & Varian, Farrell & Klemperer, Selznick, D.

all on the animation side, where the registry holds nothing (no Coleridge, Walton, Gerrig, Tomko, Murray). The
`monograph/walton2007question` key is a different Walton (*JPSP* 2007).

I swapped two FACETS anchors. Ferri and Murray go to reserve; Gerrig (1993) and Green & Brock (2000) come in,
as the lead asked. Green & Brock make "transportation" a measured construct with a *JPSP* record, which gives c8
an empirical leg the other four books lack. Gerrig is the lead's pick; the record's subtitle, "on the psychological
activities of reading", marks it as the psychology of entering a narrative world. Whether it argues that disbelief
must be actively constructed rather than willingly suspended (the reading that would serve the counter) is for the
read, not for this note.

The counter is the consent asymmetry: Coleridge's phrase is the *willing* suspension of disbelief, and the
platform user may not consent. Coleridge (1817) supplies the word "willing" with a page from a named edition;
Tomko traces what the phrase has been made to do since. Walton drops belief altogether, which removes the thing
to be suspended. If c8 treats the viewer and the user as one mechanism, the consent gap is the finding.
