# von Kleist (1972 [1810]), On the marionette theatre

## What it argues

English phrases in quotation marks below are my translations of the German; the Loci give the originals.

Heinrich von Kleist's essay is a dialogue, and its claims belong to Herr C., the leading dancer of the opera,
not to the narrator, who doubts him throughout. C. tells the narrator that a dancer can learn from the
marionettes in the market square. A marionette's every movement has a centre of gravity. The machinist governs
only that point, and the limbs, "nothing but pendulums", follow mechanically. The line the centre of gravity
traces is simple, yet it is "the path of the dancer's soul". So the machinist can find it only by placing
himself in the puppet's centre of gravity: he must, in effect, dance. His fingers relate to the puppet's
motion "as numbers to their logarithms". C. then goes further. Even this last fraction of spirit could be
removed, and the dance produced by a crank.

The marionette's advantage over a living dancer is first negative: it is never affected. Affectation appears
when the soul (*vis motrix*) sits anywhere but at the centre of gravity, as in the dancer who bends at the
small of the back or the one whose soul sits "in the elbow". The puppet is also "antigrav": it touches the
floor only to skim it. Such faults, C. says, are unavoidable since we ate of the tree of knowledge. Two stories
press the point. The narrator tells of a youth who lost his grace after catching himself in a mirror in the
pose of the Spinario and could never repeat the gesture. C. tells of a chained bear that parried every thrust
of a fencer and ignored every feint. C.'s conclusion: as reflection grows darker and weaker in the organic
world, grace grows more radiant. Grace returns after knowledge "has passed through an infinity", and it
appears purest in a body with no consciousness or an infinite one, "the jointed doll, or the god". To return
to innocence we would have to eat of the tree of knowledge again: "the last chapter of the history of the
world."

## What the paper uses it for

- **c6** (animation composes a living character from inert parts): the strongest fit. Kleist describes the
  exact mechanism. One governed point, the centre of gravity, and limbs that are "dead, pure pendulums",
  produce motion graceful enough to shame living dancers. C. also insists the work needs feeling. The
  machinist must "dance", and mechanical ease does not mean the task can be done "without feeling". That
  supports the life frame's tending over the intelligence frame's specifying. Kleist's subject is grace, not
  life; the paper must make that step itself.
- **c10** (where the analogy breaks; puppetry; the LLM makes its own in-betweens): two passages. The
  logarithm line makes the operator's input and the puppet's output relate artfully but not one-to-one, which
  is the puppeteer's case. The crank passage imagines the last fraction of spirit removed and the dance handed
  wholly to mechanical forces. That is the limit the paper reaches when the model produces its own
  in-betweens. The use stretches Kleist in one place: a crank-driven marionette repeats, and it does not
  answer back. So the essay says nothing about liveness in the sense §5 needs.
- **c5** (intelligence frame versus life frame is a stance choice): at a stretch. C. separates grace from
  consciousness. Grace peaks where reflection is absent (the doll, the bear) or infinite (the god), and
  self-consciousness destroys it (the youth). The paper can use this to show that a living quality and
  intelligence can come apart. Kleist does not frame it as a choice of stance, and his scale runs over
  consciousness, not intelligence.
