# C. Arora (2024), Proxy assertions and agency

## What it argues

Chirag Arora (Twente) asks whether the speech of a voice assistant or chatbot counts as an assertion. Some
philosophers say yes, because the output looks like human speech and does the same job for the hearer.
Arora calls this a "functionalist" view, and rejects it with two cases. In SOMNILOQUIST a sleeper happens
to mutter the right answer; in RANDOM a device prints a random verdict. The functionalist counts both as
assertions when they come out true, which is odd, because the traditional accounts of assertion tie it to
what the speaker knows, intends or answers for.

His alternative builds on Nickel's idea of proxy speech. A machine utterance can be a proxy assertion, made
on behalf of the designers or deployers, but only if their epistemic responsibility "can reasonably be tied
back" to the utterance. Nickel's version leaves open when that holds, so Arora adds conditions. Naming the
designer is not enough. The human parties must claim responsibility, or their states must connect to the
utterance. His LEARNING MACHINE and LEARNED MACHINE cases show how it fails: a deep-learning device may
outperform what its designer believes of it, or many people may have trained it and none can vouch for its
output. Three consequences follow. Alexa's COVID-19 answer is a proxy assertion, since the designers and the
CDC stand behind it. ChatGPT's output is not, because OpenAI warns that it "may occasionally generate
incorrect information", so users should not expect assertion-grade reliability. Designers of such systems
should either temper expectations or discourage use, and they should avoid human-like speech where it will
lead people to take outputs as assertions.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c9** (triadicity, narrowed: the studio determines what the viewer gets): Arora supports the first half.
  On his account the designers and deployers speak through the machine, and he says the proxy view "makes
  the role played by human agents, such as designers of the machines, explicitly visible". That matches the
  paper's picture of the studio standing behind the character. The use stretches him in four ways. (1) His
  word is responsibility for an assertion, not determination of what the audience receives. The paper's
  claim is about control of content; his is about who answers for it. (2) He says that, for ML systems,
  responsibility does not automatically fall on the studio: with many trainers, "none may be in a position
  to claim responsibility" (p. 12), so a proxy assertion can fail to exist. That weakens "the studio
  determines" as a general statement. (3) His responsible party can be "a collective", which in footnote 11
  may include users. That matches Stark's many animators and the user-on-the-animator's-side problem, not
  a single studio. (4) He has no third party between speaker and hearer in the lab's sense (binding
  determination, own interest, keeping the parties apart). The designer is a hidden speaker, not an
  intermediary. Nor does he mention animation.

## Caution

- **Whose idea.** The proxy account is Nickel's (2013). Arora's contribution is the refinement (the tie
  between the designers' responsibility and the utterance). The paper should credit both, and should cite
  Nickel's own text, which this card did not read, for the original claim.
- **Scope.** Arora's cases are speech acts of voice assistants and chatbots, and his sense of "assertion" is
  the philosophical one (knowledge norms, epistemic and ethical responsibility). He does not discuss
  animation, characters or the viewer's perception.
- **Assumption.** He assumes machines cannot have mental states (footnote 4, p. 6) and declines to assess
  Green and Michel's argument against that. The paper's life-frame claims (c4, c5) sit at odds with that
  premise, and the paper should not cite Arora as endorsing or refuting them.
- **Dates and version.** Received 31 August 2023, accepted 5 January 2024, online 30 January 2024 (PDF p. 1).
  Volume 37(1), March 2024 (Crossref). Publisher version of record; no `registered_year` needed.
- **Read depth.** `full_text` covers the article (pp. 1-16). The reference list was not read, so every
  attribution to Nickel, Freiman and Miller, Green and others is Arora's, seen only in his text.
- **What the paper may not claim from it:** that the studio determines what the audience receives, that a
  designer is a third party in the lab's sense, or that responsibility always lands on the studio.
