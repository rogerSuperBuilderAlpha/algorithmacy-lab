# Hoerl (2015), Seeing motion and apparent motion

## What it argues

Christoph Hoerl argues that philosophers have misdescribed what apparent motion looks like, and that the
mistake has hidden a third position on temporal experience. In the classic experiment, two brief stationary
flashes appear in succession at two places, and viewers report movement between them. Rick Grush
(intentionalist, "Orwellian") and Barry Dainton (extensionalist, "Stalinesque") disagree about how to explain
this. Hoerl shows that both assume the visual system constructs something that is not there, so both rule out
direct realism (§§1–2).

His §3 goes back to Wertheimer. Wertheimer's "pure motion" cases show an impression of movement while it is
"introspectively obvious" that only two stationary stimuli are seen. So Wertheimer himself rejected the idea
that perception fills in the intermediate positions ("subjective supplementation"). Hoerl then borrows Michael
Martin's comparative reading of "looks" statements: the display *looks like* motion because its spacing and
timing resemble paradigm cases of visible movement. Nothing extra is experienced. Apparent motion shows a
"psychological relativity", not a "psychological construction", and a direct realist can accept it.

§4 separates cinema from apparent motion. Hoerl denies that Wertheimer's phenomenon explains motion at the
cinema. With Gregory Currie, and against Andrew Kania, he treats cinematic motion as real motion of "cinematic
images". His key tool is Goodman's notion of a *dissective* property, one that every part of a thing shares.
Colour and pitch are non-dissective; so is cinematic motion. Slowing the projector reveals static frames, but
that shows only that the motion is not present in the temporal parts, not that it is illusory. The billiard
ball's motion is continuous because the ball exists continuously, not because motion must be continuous. The
real question is ontological: whether to admit "re-identifiable spatial particulars other than physical
objects".

§5 draws the line that keeps this realism from swallowing apparent motion too. Hoerl reviews the evidence for
the "indistinguishability claim" (Wertheimer, Boring, Dimmick and Scahill, De Silva) and finds it weak. Kaplan
and Sperling's ratings show that viewers who have seen multi-stimulus displays rate two-flash displays near
zero. What subjects report, he suggests, is that they "can't shake off the impression of movement", not that it
looks like real motion. His conclusion (§6): in apparent motion only stationary spots are seen; in cinematic
motion, moving images are seen, and they really move. The paper is philosophy of perception. It never mentions
animation, persistence of vision, or anything beyond film projection and laboratory displays.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c7** (necessary opacity: no frame holds the motion): this is the contrast. SKETCH §4.2 proposes that
  "No frame contains the motion; the viewer's perception supplies it (apparent motion, Wertheimer 1912)."
  Hoerl objects to two steps in that sentence. First, cinema's motion is not Wertheimer's apparent motion
  (EV p. 13 [688]). Second, even in apparent motion, perception supplies nothing: Wertheimer's pure-motion
  cases rule out filling-in (EV p. 9 [684]). On Hoerl's view the motion on screen is real motion of cinematic
  images, so the viewer does not supply it. But he hands the paper a better version of its own point. Motion
  is a temporally *non-dissective* property: no frame has it, and the sequence does (EV pp. 14–15 [689–690]).
  "No frame holds the motion" survives, and the perceptual-supply clause should go. Carrying
  non-dissectivity over to "no component holds the intelligence" is the paper's move, not Hoerl's: he writes
  about motion, colour and pitch, never about systems or intelligence.
- **c6** (animation composes a living character from inert parts): Hoerl's realism supports a strong reading.
  The moving image is a real, re-identifiable particular composed from static frames, and it "actually
  move[s]". Two limits apply. He writes about photographic cinema, not animation or character. And his realism
  covers motion, not life or agency.
- **c8** (co-optation; the viewer is co-opted): one useful finding, at a stretch. Viewers "can't shake off the
  impression of movement" even when they know only two flashes were shown (EV p. 19 [694]). That supports
  co-optation that persists despite knowledge, which bears on SKETCH §4.3's willing/unwilling question. Hoerl
  makes no claim about narrative, belief or dependency.

## Caution

- **Version and pagination.** The held PDF is Wiley's 2012 Early View layout. Its footer reads "European
  Journal of Philosophy ••:•• ISSN 0966-8373 pp. ••–••", so it prints no issue pages. WRAP's cover sheet calls
  it the version of record; OpenAlex labels the WRAP file "acceptedVersion". The issue pages 676–702 come
  from crossref.json. The mapping EV p. n = p. 675 + n is inferred from the matching length (27 pages) and is
  unchecked. S8 should confirm it against the 2015 issue PDF, and any wording change between Early View and
  the issue is unchecked too.
- **Year (DECISIONS correction applied).** Crossref gives online 2012-08-29 and print 2015-09, volume 23(3);
  OpenAlex gives publication_year 2012. The card takes the volume year 2015 with `registered_year: 2012`
  (README rule 4).
- **Prior card.** `dxda/hoerl2015seeing` (abstract depth, status candidate) asked what Hoerl thinks underlies
  motion at the cinema. The full text answers it: cinematic motion is real motion of cinematic images, which
  Hoerl defends with Currie against Kania (§4). That card is left alone.
- **Wertheimer through Hoerl.** Hoerl quotes Wertheimer from the 1961 partial English translation (Shipley,
  ed.), not from the 1912 German. He notes that some relevant passages "are not even reproduced in the
  abridged English version". The paper should not quote Wertheimer 1912 through Hoerl. The text layer also
  renders the Greek phi as "f" in §3 (PDF pp. 8–9), so any quotation from those pages needs the PDF image.
- **Scope.** Hoerl writes about film projection and laboratory displays. He says nothing about animation,
  drawn frames, persistence of vision or Anderson & Anderson. Any use for animation is the paper's extension.
- **The contrast cuts at the sketch, not at the thesis.** Hoerl undermines SKETCH §4.2's "the viewer's
  perception supplies it" and its tie to Wertheimer. He does not touch c1–c2 (what an AI system is). His
  non-dissectivity point may serve c7 better than the sentence it corrects.
