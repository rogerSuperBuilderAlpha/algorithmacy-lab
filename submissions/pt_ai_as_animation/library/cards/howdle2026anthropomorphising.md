# Howdle (2026), Anthropomorphising AI: Two modes, two errors

## What it argues

Giles Howdle (Utrecht University) attacks a single assumption in the debate on anthropomorphising
social AI systems (his "SAIs", such as chatbots): that users err only if they "really mean
it", that is, takes on an ontological commitment to machine mental states. Howdle separates two
modes of attribution. In the metaphysical mode, "the model believes p" purports to describe a fact
and commits the speaker to machine mental states; the error, if any, is ontological. In the
pragmatic mode, the same words apply Dennett's intentional stance as an interpretive tool, with no
commitment. He reconstructs the metaphysical reading from Stuart & Kneer, Marchegiani and Coghlan,
rejects both "Exclusively Literal" and "Exclusively Non-Literal", and defends Mixed
Anthropomorphism: both modes occur.

His main move is a second error. A pragmatic anthropomorphiser commits a "Pragmatic Error" by
"Adopting the intentional stance toward SAIs when another interpretive strategy would better serve
one's aims." The error is comparative, like using a screwdriver when a hammer is at hand. Howdle
grants that SAIs usually qualify as intentional systems on Dennett's predictive test: the physical
stance is intractable, and the design stance is too coarse for systems with so many functions
("What single 'designed function' could capture this diversity?"). Strasser and Wilby's "AI stance"
he reads as careful advice on using the intentional stance, not an alternative to it. Prediction,
however, is not the only aim. In three cases (a loan denial where Sarah needs recourse, a companion
app where Marcus wants to know what he is talking to, a customer chat where Lin needs to know
whether politeness norms apply) the intentional stance predicts well but fails the operative aim.
Choosing a stance therefore takes practical wisdom: context-sensitive, aim-relative, and attentive
to effects on others. He closes with guidance for designers (build systems that scaffold several
interpretive strategies) and a flag for policymakers, since the stance that suits AI companies need
not suit users.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c5** (intelligence frame versus life frame is a stance choice): contrast, and the most useful
  foil the venue offers for c5. Howdle supplies the machinery the paper needs: stances are judged
  by fit to an aim, not by truth, and choosing one can be a mistake without any false belief. What
  he would object to in the paper's thesis: (1) the paper's "life frame" is not among his stances.
  It is a further, biomorphic stance, and on his account it is wrong wherever another stance better
  serves the operative aim. "Better conceived as creating artificial life" cannot be a
  context-free verdict; it has to name the aim it serves. (2) If the paper means "AI development is
  making life" literally, it moves into his metaphysical mode and takes on the ontological risk he
  describes. The paper must say which mode its life frame is in. (3) His stances are a *user's*
  interpretive tools for an SAI's behaviour; the paper's frames are a *developer's* conception of
  the job. Carrying his analysis over to developers is the paper's extension, not his claim.
- **c11** (implementation and evaluation under the life frame): contrast. Howdle's Sarah case and his
  footnote on Günther and Kasirzadeh say that for recourse, accountability and transparency a
  design-level interpretation beats the intentional stance. A reviewer can turn this on c11:
  evaluating a deployed system by "believability and consistency over time" (SKETCH §6) may be the
  wrong tool exactly where business processes need recourse and audit. His designer guidance
  (scaffold several stances) suggests the life frame should sit beside, not replace, design-level
  evaluation. His last point, that the stance best for AI companies may not be best for users,
  bears on c8 and c9 as well.
- **Stretch to avoid:** Howdle says nothing about life, animation, puppetry or development pipelines.
  He also does not argue that anthropomorphism should be reduced; he argues it should be chosen well.
