# Gao, Newman & Scholl (2009), The psychophysics of chasing

## What it argues

Tao Gao, George Newman and Brian Scholl take one form of perceived animacy, chasing, and measure it. In their
displays a "wolf" disc pursues a "sheep" disc among identical distractors. The authors vary "chasing
subtlety", the angle by which the wolf may stray from a perfect heat-seeking path, and "directionality",
whether the wolf faces the sheep. They argue that earlier work used hand-scripted animations and
free-report or rating measures, which are hard to quantify and open to task demands. They replace those with
performance tasks: a search for the chase (Experiments 1 to 3) and an interactive task where the viewer
steers the sheep and must avoid the wolf (Experiments 4 and 5).

Four results follow. Detection of chasing drops sharply once subtlety passes 30 degrees (above 60% correct
only at 0° and 30°; at chance by 120° and 150°) [Corrected by Fable, S7: the card read "falls to chance once
subtlety passes about 30 degrees"; the text, §2.3 Results, puts chance at 120° and 150°], though the wolf still closes in. A wolf that heads for a mirrored phantom sheep with equal statistical correlation is
much harder to spot, so the percept is not general correlation detection. Played in reverse, the same chase
is harder to detect. And in the avoidance task the wolf catches the viewer most often at moderate subtlety,
where the chase is real but unperceived, which the authors call "stalking". Facing the direction of
travel helps viewers escape even though it does not change how the wolf moves. The authors read the limits as
evidence that perceived animacy is a constrained, perhaps modular, process. They also note that, in lifeless
displays, there is no objective fact of animacy except for chasing, which they define by an algorithm.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c7** (necessary opacity: no frame holds the motion, no component the intelligence). Support, with
  stretches. Gao and colleagues show that what viewers see as animate depends on a relation between moving
  parts over time, not on any one part: the chase exists only in the interaction of wolf and sheep, and
  viewers see it in discs that are lifeless. Their stalking result also gives an example of a real pattern
  that the viewer's perception cannot see. The stretches: their displays are algorithmic 2-D trajectories, not
  drawn frames, so the card does not support a claim about frames or in-betweens. They study chasing in adult
  viewers and say that other animacy percepts may follow other rules. And nothing here bears on intelligence
  or on AI. The jump from "animacy lives in the relation, not the part" to "intelligence sits in no
  component" is the paper's. Scholl and Tremoulet (2000), a card in this run, carry the wider review.

## Caution

- **Narrow scope.** Gao and colleagues study chasing only. They say the cues "might not transfer to other
  types of perceived animacy" (p. 175). The paper should not generalise to all animacy, still less to film.
- **Who is "correct".** The authors say viewers are wrong in seeing life in these displays (p. 176). That
  cuts both ways for c7. It supports the point that the animacy is supplied by the viewer. It also gives a
  reader grounds to say the percept is an error, which the life frame would have to answer.
- **Depth.** I read the whole article. I scanned the statistics tables (Tables 1 to 3) and figures without
  checking the numbers, and the paper makes no claim from them. The reference list was scanned only.
- **Version.** The held PDF is the publisher's typeset article as hosted on a lab page. It is not an accepted
  manuscript.
- **Authors.** Brian Scholl also co-wrote Scholl and Tremoulet (2000); the two cards share a research line, so
  they are one school and not independent confirmation.
- **What the paper may not claim from it:** that frames or in-betweens produce the percept; any statement
  about intelligence, liveness in AI, or viewer dependency.
