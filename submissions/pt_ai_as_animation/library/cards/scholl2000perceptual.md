# Scholl & Tremoulet (2000), Perceptual causality and animacy

## What it argues

Brian Scholl and Patrice Tremoulet review work on a narrow class of displays: a few moving 2-D shapes that
viewers nonetheless see as causing each other's motion or as alive. They trace the line from Michotte's
launching displays and Heider and Simmel's film of triangles and a circle to the research of the 1990s.
Their review has three parts: perceived causality, perceived animacy, and an evaluation of whether these
impressions are truly perceptual.

On animacy, they report that viewers attribute personality traits and emotions to the shapes whatever the
instructions, that this holds across cultures and in young children, and that infants and chimpanzees seem
to read goals into simple motion. They then collect the motion cues that raise or lower the impression:
timing contingencies between shapes, starts from rest, turning to avoid an obstacle, direct movement towards a
goal, and sudden changes in speed and direction in a single moving particle. Their key control is a pair of
altered versions of the Heider and Simmel film. Removing the shapes' features left the animate descriptions
largely intact; removing motion information cut them sharply. Hence motion carries the effect.

Their own position is that these impressions are perceptual and perhaps modular. They call them fast,
mandatory and encapsulated from what the viewer knows. They set this against high-level inference about
causes, and they cite Schlottmann and Shanks for the dissociation. They also say the claim is not settled:
reports differ across viewers on first showing, and Michotte's observers were poorly described. The authors
contributed equally and are listed alphabetically. The article is a review, not a new study.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c7** (necessary opacity: no frame holds the motion, no component the intelligence). Anchor, with a
  stretch. The article shows that animacy is a percept built from motion. Viewers see it in displays where,
  in the authors' words, nothing of the kind exists "objectively" in the events or the retinal image. Taking
  motion information out of the Heider and Simmel film took the animacy out with it, while taking out
  featural detail did not. That is the nearest empirical support for the SKETCH's sharpening, "what animation
  lacks is not frames but motion". The stretch is twofold. First, the paper's step from "no single frame
  contains the perceived motion" to "no component contains the intelligence" is the paper's own inference.
  Scholl and Tremoulet say nothing about intelligence, about film, or about anything but 2-D shapes. Second,
  their temporal quantization removed motion information; it is not a test of whether any one frame holds the
  motion. The apparent-motion source for that move is Wertheimer (1912), which another library holds.
- **c8** (co-optation: dependency grows with use; the viewer is co-opted). Partial support only, and the use
  needs care. The article gives the perceptual layer of "coopted into the suspended reality": the percepts
  are fast, nearly irresistible, and survive the viewer's knowledge that the display is neither causal nor
  animate. It also records Michotte's remark that viewers who miss the causal percept at first see it after
  repeated showings, which resembles "the more you use". But the effect is a property of a visual module for
  causality, not a dependency that accumulates, and nothing here concerns consent. The SKETCH's Coleridge
  contrast (a willing suspension of disbelief) would run against this source: Scholl and Tremoulet describe
  an unwilling effect. Use the article for the perceptual floor of co-optation, not for dependency.

## Caution

- **Review, not a primary study.** The motion-versus-features result at p. 303 is the authors' report of a
  study they cite (their ref. 53), not their own data. I did not read that study. Cite it through them or
  find the original.
- **Contested claim.** The authors say the perceptual-and-modular reading is "largely consistent" with the
  evidence and not proven (p. 307), and they name White (1995) as a dissent. The paper should not state that
  animacy is a proven module.
- **What counts as perception.** The article's value for c7 and c8 depends on its distinction between
  perceiving and inferring animacy. Gao, Newman and Scholl (2009), another card in this run, supply the
  method that tests it.
- **Early methods.** The authors say Michotte's observers "were not well described" and that his methods drew
  "much criticism" (p. 300). Treat the 1946 effects through this review's hedges.
- **Version.** The held PDF is the publisher's typeset review as hosted on a lab page, which the authors'
  site makes public. It is not a preprint. The reference list (pp. 307–309) was scanned for the cited
  studies, not read. The body text, pp. 299–307, was read in full.
- **Registry.** The Crossref record gives no abstract field; the abstract above was read from the PDF.
- **What the paper may not claim from it:** anything about film, frames, LLMs or intelligence; that the
  percepts depend on use; or that viewers consent to them.
