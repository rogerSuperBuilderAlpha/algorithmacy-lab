# Coleridge (1817), Biographia literaria, ch. XIV

## What it argues

This card covers ch. XIV, read whole, and two later passages on "negative faith". It does not cover the
rest of the two volumes.

In ch. XIV Samuel Taylor Coleridge tells how he and William Wordsworth planned the *Lyrical Ballads*. Their
talk turned on two "cardinal points of poetry": sympathy won by truth to nature, and novelty won by the
colours of imagination. So they split the work. Coleridge took supernatural or romantic persons and
characters. His task was to give these "shadows of imagination" enough human interest and "semblance of
truth" to win "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith".
Wordsworth took everyday things and tried to wake the mind "from the lethargy of custom". Coleridge then
turns to the controversy over Wordsworth's preface. He says he never agreed with much of it, and he
defines his terms before saying where he differs.

A poem, on his definition, proposes pleasure, not truth, as its immediate object. It seeks "delight from the
whole" that is compatible with a distinct pleasure from each part. The parts of a legitimate poem "mutually
support and explain each other". The reader should be carried forward "by the pleasureable activity of mind
excited by the attractions of the journey itself", not by curiosity about the end. Poetry is the wider term.
It flows from the poet's imagination, a power that the will and understanding first set in action and then
hold under a "gentle and unnoticed" control. The chapter ends on an image of the poem as a body: good sense
its body, fancy its drapery, "motion its life", imagination its soul.

Two later chapters sharpen what poetic faith is. In ch. XXII Coleridge sets "illusion" against "delusion".
The faith a fiction asks for is "negative": it lets images work "by their own force" while judgment neither
affirms nor denies that they exist. Placing a fiction beside known fact destroys it. In ch. XXIII he says
the poet may ask the reader only for "that sort of negative faith" which "we willingly give to productions
professedly ideal".

## What the paper uses it for

- **c8** (co-optation: dependency grows with use; the viewer is co-opted): the anchor for the animation side
  of §4.3, and also the source of its strain. The scan settles the wording and the place: the phrase is
  "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith", Vol. II p. 2 of the
  1817 edition. Three points in Coleridge cut against reading the viewer as simply "coopted into the
  suspended reality". (1) The faith is *willing*. The reader gives it, as ch. XXIII says, and in ch. XXII
  the reader's judgment stays awake and merely abstains. (2) It holds "for the moment", whereas c8's
  platform dependency grows with use. (3) Coleridge suspends *disbelief*, not reality, and he contrasts
  this illusion with "delusion", the state of someone who has "believed himself under supernatural agency".
  The paper's phrase "suspended reality" is Roger's, not Coleridge's. The source helps one part of the
  co-optation claim: in Coleridge the *maker* does the work. The poet must "procure" the reader's faith by
  transferring "a human interest and a semblance of truth" to his characters, and the faith fails when the
  author's machinery shows. That puts the maker between reader and work, which the paper can use, with care,
  for the animator's role. Reading the consent gap (the viewer is willing, the platform user may not be) as
  the finding, as FACETS proposes, fits the text. Treating Coleridge's reader and the platform user as one
  mechanism would stretch him.

## Caution

- **Edition.** Every quotation comes from the 1817 Rest Fenner first edition in this scan, as DECISIONS
  requires. It is never mixed with the Engell & Bate edition. Ch. XIV opens Vol. II, so the key line is
  Vol. II p. 2, not Vol. I.
- **Records not in the folder.** The folder holds the PDF and its text layer only: no Open Library record,
  no `provenance.txt`, no `sha256.txt`, and `FETCH_LOG.tsv` has no row for this key. The Open Library ids
  in DECISIONS (OL26034W, edition OL23281404M) were not seen this run. Author, title, place, publisher,
  year and volumes come from the scan's two title pages (PDF pp. 9 and 309) and from the PDF's own
  metadata. The OCR garbles the publisher ("REax PENNER", "REST PENNER") and the item id
  ("biographialiteraOOcolerich"). The page images read "Rest Fenner", and the PDF metadata reads
  "biographialitera00colerich". S8 should add the Open Library record and a provenance line.
- **SKETCH §4.3** marks Coleridge's phrase "(unverified)". The scan confirms "willing suspension of
  disbelief" for 1817, Vol. II p. 2. FACETS (F9 anchors) quotes the phrase starting with a capital
  "That"; in the scan "that" falls mid-sentence. Both files are left alone.
- **Text layer.** The .txt holds 626 form feeds, one per PDF page [Corrected by Fable, S7: the card said 618; `tr -cd '\f' | wc -c` gives 626]. For the pages quoted, the form-feed
  count and `pdftotext -f N -l N` agree, but I took every locator from the per-page extraction and the
  page image, not from the form-feed count.
- **Read depth.** I read ch. XIV in full and searched the whole text layer for "illusion", "delusion",
  "disbelief", "make-believe" and "negative faith", reading each relevant hit in context. The rest of the
  two volumes is unread. A search of an OCR layer can miss words the OCR garbled.
- **"Motion its life"** (Vol. II p. 12) is a tempting epigraph for an animation paper. Coleridge means the
  movement of verse and thought in a poem, not moving images; any use of it there would be ornament, not
  support.
- **What the paper may not claim from it:** that Coleridge describes dependency, platforms or any growing
  commitment; that he speaks of a "suspended reality"; or that the reader's faith is involuntary.
