# Tomko (2015), Beyond the willing suspension of disbelief

## What it argues

This is an abstract card; I read no text of the book. The "abstract" is the publisher's description that the
Open Library work record quotes and credits to "Bloomsbury Publishing". Crossref gives the title, subtitle,
publisher (Bloomsbury Academic), year 2015 and four ISBNs, but no author. The author's name comes from Open
Library.

According to the description, Michael Tomko treats Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief" as a pivotal
moment in literary theory. He goes back to Coleridge's thought and Shakespeare criticism and rebuilds the idea
as a form of "poetic faith". On that basis he lays the foundations of a theologically oriented literary
criticism. He brings Coleridge into dialogue with Augustine, Josef Pieper, Stephen Greenblatt, Terry Eagleton,
J. R. R. Tolkien and Wendell Berry. The book offers a method of reading for post-secular criticism that is
historically and politically aware and engaged with aesthetic form. The Crossref chapter record gives the first
chapter's title as "The willing suspension of disbelief".

## What the paper uses it for

- **c8** (co-optation: dependency grows with use; the viewer is co-opted): Tomko is a contrast source. The
  paper says the viewer of an animation "ultimately become[s] coopted into the suspended reality of the
  narrative and screen", and it treats AI dependency as the same mechanism. On the description, Tomko
  reconstructs Coleridge's suspension as a form of "poetic faith". If, as "willing" and "faith" suggest, he
  reads it as something the reader does rather than something done to the reader, his account cuts against
  calling the viewer's absorption co-optation. The description does not say this; it is the card's inference
  from two words and must be checked against ch. 1. If it holds, the objection sharpens the consent asymmetry
  the sketch already flags in §4.3: animation's suspension is willed, and platform dependency need not be.
  Until ch. 1 is read, the paper can cite Tomko only for the claim that the willing suspension has been
  reconstructed as a form of poetic faith; reading that faith as active, and arguing that the AI case lacks
  the will, are the paper's moves. The use also stretches him in subject matter. His subject is literature,
  Shakespeare criticism and theology. Nothing in the records concerns animation, screens or technology.
