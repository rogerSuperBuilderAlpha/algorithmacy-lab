### Research question

Aytac never prints a single interrogative as “the research question.” The question is asked in these places, as a claim about what must be shown:

1. **Abstract, first sentence.** “This article argues that social media companies’ power to regulate communication in the public sphere illustrates a novel type of domination.” The question behind it: what kind of domination is corporate regulation of the digital public sphere?
2. **Introduction, thesis paragraph.** “I argue that social media companies’ power to regulate communication in the public sphere illustrates a novel type of domination that is particularly problematic from a democratic theory perspective.” Same question, now with the democratic-theory stake attached.
3. **Introduction, the category question.** “Why does it matter if social media companies’ powers amount to quasi-public domination instead of private domination?” Located after the coinage. This is the payoff question, not a second thesis.
4. **Section hinge into the application.** “Can Private Actors Publicly Dominate?” The subsection title is the question the coinage answers.
5. **Algorithmic section.** “Why is such algorithmic control dominating?” The hinge before the second mechanism.
6. **Resource test.** “Then the question is whether citizens have the necessary objective and cognitive resources to reflect on and choose their mode of discursive engagement… when algorithmic control is not around.” Pettit’s resource clause, asked of the second mechanism.

### Thesis, and its shape

**The claim.** Social media companies dominate citizens in two ways — direct control of political speech through content moderation, and indirect algorithmic control of modes of discursive engagement — and that domination is quasi-public: a private actor dominating others *qua* citizens, typed by target domain rather than by agential origin.

**The shape.** A dated scene, then the full thesis by the second paragraph, then a coinage in one stipulation (“an instance of domination is quasi-public if it amounts to a private actor dominating others qua citizens”), then a paradigm case (the patriarchal household that begins to curb political rights), then two mechanisms announced and delivered, then three objections in the objector’s indicative, each answered by diagnosis or by concede-then-contain. The paper is diagnostic and conceptual. Prescription is deferred.

### Contribution moves (in order)

1. Scene: Senate hearing, Culbertson, public discontent.
2. Full thesis: novel domination, private in origin, not mere private domination.
3. Two theoretical innovations named: a novel argument under the domination lens; a recategorization of forms of domination by target domain.
4. Coinage of quasi-public domination, with the choice-menu test.
5. Payoff of the coinage: urgency (public domination multiplies private risk) and anti-state-centrism (solutions need not run only through the state).
6. Two mechanisms announced: direct speech control; algorithmic control of visibility and mode.
7. Roadmap (his; house bar will strip ours).
8. Pettit groundwork: domination, interference, arbitrariness, social justice vs political legitimacy, contestatory democracy, the public sphere.
9. Category argument: type by target domain; household paradigm; systemic effects; radical-republican contrast (lobbying vs direct interference); slave analogy; practical implication (non-state agency).
10. Application: content moderation as arbitrary will; Lawyers for Civil Rights case; exit costs and consent; conventional-media alternatives rejected; first objection (expansion of the menu) answered by the debtor–creditor analogy; transparency and the Oversight Board fail unconditionality.
11. Second mechanism: algorithms arrange who hears whom; modes of engagement (deliberative, antagonistic, mixed); engagement-maximizing algorithms; even a civility algorithm would dominate; empirical hedge on echo chambers; dynamic human–algorithm interaction; resource test answered.
12. Institutional hints, then the diagnostic limit.
13. Three objections: state regulation (discretion + temporal gap); conventional media (network effects + intimacy of control); inconsequential harm (micro-domination).
14. Conclusion restates the two mechanisms.

### Move vocabulary (read off this paper)

- **SCENE** — dated, named actor opens the paper.
- **THESIS** — “I argue that…” full claim early.
- **COIN** — genus-differentia stipulation, then immediately tested.
- **ANNOUNCE** — a count named, then delivered in order (“two different ways,” “two theoretical innovations,” “two reasons”).
- **GROUND** — borrowed definition with a page pin (Pettit 2002: 52).
- **CLAIM** — paragraph-opening declarative, no throat-clearing.
- **SUPPORT** — instance or citation after the claim (“For instance…”; “A vivid illustration…”).
- **MECHANISM** — causal chain, often one long sentence.
- **HINGE** — a question the next sentence answers.
- **ANSWER** — the sentence that spends the hinge.
- **OBJECT** — “One might object…” then the objector in the indicative.
- **DIAGNOSE** — “The first problem with this objection is…”
- **CONTAIN** — “Even if we acknowledge that…, this does not mean…”
- **BRIDGE** — “Considering the central role…, I will now explain…”
- **PAYOFF** — “Hence…” or “As a result…” closing a run.
- **LIMIT** — diagnostic not prescriptive; future research named as a task.
- **RECAP** — conclusion restates mechanisms as claims.

### Register

**Thirty verbs, written from the page:** argues, contend, dominate, dictate, interfere, exercise, regulate, illustrate, demonstrate, propose, identify, focus, amount, narrow, jeopardize, reply, explain, envisage, require, challenge, compensate, prevail, crystallize, guarantee, categorize, curb, resemble, overestimate, suspend, deprive.

They are almost all literal. The figurative ones that do appear are dead or conventional: *crystallize* (norms), *fly under the radar* (inside an objection), *cartloads* (inside a Langvardt quotation, not his voice). His own voice does not reach.

**Metaphors.** One constructed analogy (the slave and the second master) and one borrowed economic analogy (debtor–creditor). No epigrams. “Quasi-public domination is more politically invasive” is a verdict, not a figure.

**Key term.** He repeats *domination*, *quasi-public domination*, *choice menu*, *contestatory*, *arbitrary*. He does not cycle synonyms. *Digital domination* appears in the title and the section head and is otherwise the same object as quasi-public domination of citizens by social media companies.

**Abstraction.** Agents do the work: social media companies, citizens, the state, moderation teams, algorithms. Nominalisations (*capacity*, *interference*, *contestation*) sit where Pettit’s terms require them. He does not write “the presence of a capacity for interference” where “they can interfere” would do, except when he is quoting Pettit.

**What it never does.** Aphorism. The knowing aside. *Actually*. *In fact* as a nudge. Elegant variation on the construct. A sentence that comments on its own care. Emphasis markers (*crucially*, *importantly*).

### Applied-section register (Cutolo and Kenney, 2021)

Read from the AAM. Verbs: *organize, mediate, attract, overlook, show, unravel, term, explore, navigate, coalesce, illustrate, demonstrate, extend, enrich, mitigate.* Subjects are named actors: the entrepreneur, the platform, Amazon, a restaurant, a hotel, policy makers. Terms are glossed at first use (PDE, complementor, platform per Gawer). Citations sit mid-sentence or terminal, rarely as the grammatical subject. Sentences alternate a short claim (“Power is an attribute of the relationship…”) with a *Because of network effects…* build. They repeat *platform-dependent entrepreneur* rather than cycling synonyms. What they never do in voice: epigram, “I argue” as a drumbeat (they use *we* for labour). AMP siblings (Cennamo 2021; later AMP platform papers) run the same named-actor, mid-length declarative register. A trait only Cutolo has (the Google-Search existence riddle) is his, not the venue’s, and is not a target.
