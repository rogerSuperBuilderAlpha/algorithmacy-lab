# Worked cases — H15 and H17

The two remaining open claims are empirical: they assert that two things vary independently, which a worked
case can show by populating the cells that would be empty if the things moved together. Each case uses real,
verifiable examples; unverifiable candidates were dropped, and the honest caveats are kept.

## H15 — digital sovereignty and coordinative sovereignty vary independently

Digital sovereignty is a firm's capacity to read and control its data, infrastructure, and code. Coordinative
sovereignty is a firm's standing within the algorithmic coordination it depends on. If the two were one
construct, only the diagonal cells would fill. They do not; the off-diagonal cells are populated with real
cases, which is what independent variation looks like.

| | high digital sovereignty | low digital sovereignty |
|---|---|---|
| **high coordinative sovereignty** | firm on its own stack, no binding external mediator (enterprise B2B software) | platform workers under EU Directive 2024/2831; CoopCycle courier-members |
| **low coordinative sovereignty** | **App Store developer; Amazon Marketplace seller** | solo gig courier |

**The key cell (high digital, low coordinative).** An EU app developer can own its code and customer data, hold
data-portability rights, and comply fully with the GDPR and the DMA, and still hold no leverage over the App
Store ranking and review decisions that set its visibility. The Digital Markets Act enforcement of 2025 reached
Apple's anti-steering conduct — a communication right — and the ranking-transparency requirement that follows is
transparency, not contestability: a developer will see how it ranks and still cannot contest the algorithm that
ranks it. An Amazon Marketplace seller is in the same position from another platform: GDPR-compliant, in control
of its own inventory and supply chain, and unable to contest the Buy Box algorithm that decides the large
majority of its sales. High digital sovereignty, low coordinative sovereignty, in the same firm at the same time
(Cutolo & Kenney, 2021).

**The other off-diagonal (low digital, high coordinative).** A platform worker owns none of the cloud,
algorithms, or data systems that coordinate the work, and under Directive (EU) 2024/2831 now holds standing over
that coordination: information on the automated systems, human oversight of consequential automated decisions, a
right to have such decisions reviewed by a human, restrictions on the data the platform may process, and a
mandate on member states to promote collective bargaining (European Parliament & Council, 2024). Standing rose
while infrastructure control stayed at zero. A CoopCycle courier is the cooperative version: the federation runs
on shared and outsourced infrastructure the couriers do not own, yet each cooperative holds an equal vote over
the pay scheme and the allocation rules — voice without ownership.

**Caveat.** The DMA's steering remedy and the Directive's transposition (due December 2026) are recent and
contested, and the App Store developer coalition reported in late 2025 that little had changed in practice. The
cell assignments describe the standing each instrument confers, not a settled outcome; the point is that the two
sovereignties move independently, which the cases show regardless of how the enforcement fights end.

## H17 — infrastructure openness/control and coordinative sovereignty are orthogonal

Openness versus control is a property of the infrastructure. Coordinative sovereignty is a property of the
coordinated actor's standing. The claim is that they do not track each other: a closed, state-sovereign platform
can leave its users without voice, and an open platform can give users voice without any state control.

**Closed and sovereign, yet co-opting.** WeChat is integrated into Chinese public administration and, in places,
into local policing, and its users are subject to content control they neither see nor contest. The state holds
a strong form of digital sovereignty over the platform; the users hold no coordinative sovereignty within it.
Russia's sovereign-internet regime is the same shape at the network layer: the state builds control over a
domestic RuNet and directs users toward state-aligned platforms, and the coordinated users gain no standing from
the arrangement. National digital sovereignty is secured; the people coordinated through the platform are
co-opted under a domestic flag.

**Open, and empowering.** Som Mobilitat, a Catalan mobility cooperative, runs an open, member-governed platform
where members decide services and priorities by assembly on one-member-one-vote terms — standing that owes
nothing to state control. The fediverse is the architectural version: federated instances with local governance
and the ability to migrate to another instance while keeping one's followers, so users hold both exit and voice
without any central or state authority. Open infrastructure, high coordinative sovereignty.

The pairing shows the orthogonality directly. The distinction has scholarly backing: the sovereignty invoked by
a state over its digital infrastructure is a different thing from, and can stand in opposition to, the autonomy
of the people and communities coordinated within it (Couture & Toupin, 2019).

**Caveat.** Cooperative or federated structure does not guarantee coordinative sovereignty in practice — the New
York Drivers Cooperative shows formal member governance coexisting with disputes over whether that governance
delivers the promised terms, and fediverse instances range from democratic to autocratic. Structure enables
voice; it does not ensure it. That qualification strengthens rather than weakens H17: coordinative sovereignty
is a property of standing actually held, not of the infrastructure's form, which is exactly the orthogonality
claimed.

## Effect on the chapter

Compact worked versions of both cases are folded into §8 — H15 into the scholars' disaggregation of the two
constructs, H17 into the openness/control reframe — turning two bare assertions into grounded examples. The one
new citation added is the primary instrument, Directive (EU) 2024/2831; the rest rests on already-verified
sources (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Couture & Toupin, 2019) and on well-known platform examples. Database: H15 and
H17 move from open to supported.
