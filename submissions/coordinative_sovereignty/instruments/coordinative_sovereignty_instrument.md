# Coordinative sovereignty: a measurement instrument

Construct-development specification for coordinative sovereignty, an actor's standing within an algorithmic
coordination it cannot exit. Candidate items are proposals for the content-validity stage, written fresh and
styled after the closest validated scales. No validated individual-level measure of this construct exists, so
the instrument is built from the construct's four theoretical foundations and scoped, throughout, to a *named
mediator the respondent depends on and cannot readily leave*. That scoping is what separates the construct from
generic workplace empowerment, which assumes an exit the platform-dependent actor lacks.

## Construct and content domain

Coordinative sovereignty is the standing to shape the terms on which one is mediated, to contest the mediator's
arbitrary power, and to hold a share in the governance of an arrangement one cannot leave; its currency is
effective voice. The instrument measures that standing along three dimensions drawn from the construct's
foundations, under a scoping condition that establishes the coordination is not readily exitable.

- **Scoping condition — dependence without exit.** The respondent coordinates through the mediator and cannot
  practically leave it without losing access to the market. This is not a dimension of standing but the
  condition under which standing is the relevant question; it is measured to confirm the construct applies and
  to separate the sample from cases where ordinary exit is available.
- **Non-domination** (Pettit; Muldoon & Raekstad, 2022) — the mediator's power over the actor is constrained by
  rules the actor can know and invoke, rather than exercised at will.
- **Effective voice** (Hirschman, 1970; Van Dyne & LePine, 1998) — the actor can shape the terms of its
  mediation, and its expressed terms hold because it has the standing to make them hold, beyond the mere act of
  speaking.
- **Governance share** (Markell, 2008; Ostrom, 1990) — the actor holds a place in how the coordination is
  governed, through a collective, a body, or a channel, rather than only a guarantee against the mediator's
  worst conduct.

## Position against existing scales

Psychological empowerment (Spreitzer, 1995) is the nearest validated construct: meaning, competence,
self-determination, impact. Coordinative sovereignty differs on three counts the empowerment scale does not
capture. It is scoped to an *unexitable external mediator* rather than one's own role in an organization one
could leave; its non-domination dimension concerns *another party's* arbitrary power rather than one's own felt
autonomy; and its governance-share dimension concerns a *share in a coordination one does not own* rather than
influence within one's employer. Employee-voice scales (Van Dyne & LePine, 1998; Maynes et al., 2013) measure
voice as a behavior; coordinative sovereignty measures voice as *effective*, an achieved standing in which terms
hold, not a disposition to speak. Digital sovereignty, where it is operationalized at all, measures control over
data and infrastructure (Fratini et al., 2024; Hummel et al., 2021); coordinative sovereignty measures standing
within a mediation whose infrastructure the actor does not control. The instrument must discriminate from
empowerment, from voice behavior, and from digital sovereignty, and the empowerment discrimination is the
load-bearing one.

## Candidate items

Response format: 7-point Likert, strongly disagree to strongly agree, referring to a named mediator P the
respondent depends on. Items marked (R) reverse-scored. Candidates for expert rating, not a validated set.

### Scoping — dependence without exit

1. If I left P, I would lose access to a large share of my customers or work.
2. There is no alternative to P at the scale my business or work needs.
3. I could move off P without much loss. (R)
4. My position depends on decisions P makes that I cannot avoid by dealing directly with the other side.

### Non-domination (the mediator's power is rule-bound, not arbitrary)

5. P can change the terms that determine my outcomes without my having any way to challenge it. (R)
6. There are rules I can invoke when P treats me in a way I consider unfair.
7. P's decisions about my account follow stated criteria I can hold it to.
8. Whether P favors or penalizes me depends on its discretion more than on rules I can point to. (R)
9. I can obtain a human review of an automated decision P makes about me.

### Effective voice (the actor can shape the terms, and they hold)

10. When enough of us raise the same concern, P changes how it treats us.
11. My input, or my group's, has actually changed a rule or practice on P.
12. Raising a concern with P changes nothing that matters. (R)
13. I can negotiate the terms on which P mediates my work, not only accept them.
14. There is a channel through which the terms I press for on P can be made to hold.

### Governance share (a place in how the coordination is governed)

15. I have a say, through some collective or body, in how P's rules are set.
16. People in my position are represented where P's policies are decided.
17. Decisions about how P governs the people who depend on it are made without any of us in the room. (R)
18. I share in the governance of the arrangement I depend on, not only in its use.

## Dimensionality and validation

- **Content validity** — an expert panel rates item–dimension fit; the scoping block is validated as a separate
  screen rather than folded into the standing dimensions.
- **Dimensionality** — exploratory then confirmatory factor analysis, predicting three standing factors
  (non-domination, effective voice, governance share) with the scoping block loading separately; the agent-noted
  possibility of a hierarchical structure — local standing over a global non-optionality condition — is tested
  by modeling the scoping block as a second-order or moderating factor rather than a fourth standing dimension.
- **Reliability** — internal consistency and composite reliability per dimension.
- **Convergent and discriminant validity** — moderate correlation with psychological empowerment (Spreitzer,
  1995) and with voice behavior (Van Dyne & LePine, 1998), and a clear separation from both, established by a
  shared-variance comparison; the primary discriminant test is against empowerment, and against a digital-
  sovereignty measure the correlation is predicted to be weak, which is the chapter's H15.
- **Nomological validity** — coordinative sovereignty should be predicted by algorithmacy
  (`algorithmacy_instrument.md`) and should predict bargaining outcomes with P, resilience under an algorithm
  change, and — moderated by mediator type — survival when P is disintermediated; the necessary/contingent
  classification of P moderates which strategy pays (`README.md`, the two-step design).
- **Formal correspondence** — the reported standing measured here is predicted to correlate with the *afforded*
  standing computed as a coordinated actor's Shapley share and core membership in a model of its coordination
  (`formal_standing.md`); this is the AGENDA's first item as a testable correspondence.

## Cautions

The construct is scoped to an unexitable mediator; applying the standing items where exit is easy measures
ordinary empowerment and not coordinative sovereignty, which is why the scoping screen is not optional.
Self-report invites a courtesy or resignation bias — a resigned actor may under-report standing it in fact
holds, and a hopeful one over-report — so the nomological and formal-correspondence checks carry weight the
self-report alone cannot. Items name a concrete mediator, because an actor's standing differs across the
platforms it depends on.
