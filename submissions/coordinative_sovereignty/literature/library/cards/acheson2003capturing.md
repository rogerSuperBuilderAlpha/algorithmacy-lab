## Summary
Acheson's monograph is the book-length treatment of the case that Schlager and Ostrom used as an illustration. It asks why the Maine lobster fishery, unlike most open-access fisheries, has not been degraded, and answers that resource degradation is not inevitable where users manage to build institutions. According to the publisher description, the book shows how the Maine lobster industry, its fishermen, and local groups solved a series of collective action problems by creating three distinct sets of rules: informal territorial arrangements held by harbor gangs, trap limits and related effort controls, and formal conservation legislation at the state level. The analysis is framed in rational choice terms and is offered both as a practical model for fisheries management and as a theoretical contribution to how institutions for conserving natural resources come into being. It is the fullest account of fishers holding de facto management and exclusion rights over grounds the state owns.

## Use in the chapter
Section 6 writes that Schlager and Ostrom's "Maine lobster fishers hold de facto proprietorship over grounds the state owns, governing access and effort without holding title." The audit (S24) confirms the example is in Schlager and Ostrom (1992) but flags it as single-sourced and proposes Acheson 2003 as the second source. Role: second source for the claim that the claimant and proprietor positions (management and exclusion without alienation) can be held collectively over a resource someone else owns, which is the chapter's model for standing over a platform's rules without ownership of the platform.
