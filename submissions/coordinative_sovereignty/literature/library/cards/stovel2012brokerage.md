## Summary
A review of brokerage as a general social mechanism, one of a small number of ways that disconnected or isolated individuals or groups can interact economically, politically, and socially. The review stresses brokerage's double face: it has the capacity to ease social interaction, enhance economic activity, and facilitate political development, yet it often breeds exploitation, the pursuit of personal profit, corruption, and the accumulation of power. Its macro-level significance runs through its effect on the permeability of group boundaries, while understanding it requires close attention to micro-level relations and social psychological processes, because brokerage is built from informal, personal relationships. The review synthesizes work on broker roles and positions, the tension between brokers and the parties they connect, and the conditions under which brokered relations persist or dissolve, noting that brokerage is rarely treated as a central concept in the discipline's theoretical arsenal despite its ubiquity.
