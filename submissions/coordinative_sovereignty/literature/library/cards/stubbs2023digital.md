## Summary
Stubbs argues that users' digital engagement is a form of labour that digital firms co-opt for private profit, and that this collective production of "knowledge goods" (data and user-generated content) grounds a claim to democratic governance over the platforms that capture it. The argument is normative, extending the tradition of participatory democracy, worker self-management and economic democracy (Pateman, Gould, Schweickart, Malleson) from the workplace to the platform. Its central principle is participatory autonomy, which entails equal formal decision-making power in institutions that users collectively constitute and for which they produce value. Stubbs answers the objection that users receive something in return (communication, information) by analogy with the wage: receiving a wage does not dissolve the worker's claim to control, and access does not dissolve the user's. The remedy is experimentation with democratic governance of the "digital commons," with the platform cooperative, in user-owned, city-owned and union-sponsored forms, as the paradigm case, embedded in a broader cooperative ecosystem.

## Use in the chapter
Not yet cited. Strand 4 proposes it as SHOULD for §6 under the heading "commons under foreclosed exit." The chapter's definition of coordinative sovereignty rests on standing held by actors who cannot leave; Stubbs supplies a value-based ground for that standing (they produce the value the mediator captures) that is distinct from the republican ground (non-domination) the chapter takes from Pettit, Hasan and Aytac. Role: support for §6, and a position to distinguish in §7: Stubbs's remedy is ownership through the commons, whereas the chapter holds that "ownership does not make a necessary mediator leaveable." The article grounds its democratic claim in users' labour rather than in platform power as such, which the author flags as the difference from Engelmann et al. (2020).
