## Summary
This ARIST review surveys the data literacy literature from 2000 to 2025 across disciplines to understand how the concept is defined, framed and assessed. Using a critical review approach, the authors trace the concept's evolution and sort its theoretical foundations into competency-based models, critical theory approaches and learning-centred perspectives. The thematic section discusses data citizenship and everyday-life data literacy, including Hovious and Sutton's Dimensions of Data Wellness framework, and reports survey evidence that data and privacy literacy track educational attainment and internet use, with a community survey near Denton, Texas, showing that respondents with graduate degrees scored far higher and that those with no more than high school were less likely to engage with emerging technologies. The authors note that by PIAAC Level 3 proficiency more than half of US adults would not count as data literate, and that a pipeline gap runs from school to workplace. The review closes with gaps and an agenda.

## Use in the chapter
Strand 5 lists it as a SHOULD for §4. The chapter argues there is no single algorithmacy any more than a single literacy, and that algorithmic competence is distributed with socioeconomic advantage. Lund and colleagues give the parallel finding for data literacy: a fragmented construct, multiple competing frameworks, and a gradient tied to education and access. Role: support for the unevenness claim and for the chapter's positioning of algorithmacy beside data and information literacy rather than inside them; Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) is the algorithmic-literacy counterpart.
