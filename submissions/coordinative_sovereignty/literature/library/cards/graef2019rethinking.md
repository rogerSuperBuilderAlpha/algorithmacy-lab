## Summary
Graef examines whether the EU essential facilities doctrine, developed for physical infrastructure and dormant in recent enforcement, can be rethought for digital markets in which the assets that matter are data and rankings rather than bridges or ports. The article surveys the doctrine's conditions (indispensability, elimination of competition, absence of objective justification), the market characteristics of platform markets, and the Google competition cases, which she reads as seemingly bypassing the doctrine while reaching comparable results. It also reviews legislative and policy developments regulating access outside competition law. Her central argument is that the doctrine can be revived for the digital economy if its application is aligned more closely with the underlying economic interests at stake, so that access claims to data or to ranking are assessed on their economic substance rather than forced into a physical-infrastructure template. The method is doctrinal legal analysis.
