## Summary
The article reviews the emerging policy vocabulary of AI sovereignty and argues that conventional geopolitical framings are analytically insufficient and socially consequential. Its vehicle is a critical reading of the Tony Blair Institute's "strategic agency" framework, against which the authors press three limitations: the framework underspecifies how infrastructural power constrains a state's choices; it does not account for the mechanisms by which dependence becomes coercion; and, applied in post-colonial settings, it risks reinforcing existing global hierarchies under the banner of digital imperialism. The constructive move is to treat AI governance as an irreducibly "intermestic" problem, extending Manning's 1977 coinage to show that international infrastructural dependencies and domestic accountability deficits are linked rather than separable. The authors propose "power-aware governance", operationalised through stress-testing and scenario analysis, and conclude that established international relations concepts need theoretical extension before they can carry the weight now placed on them in AI policy. It is a conceptual review rather than an empirical study.
