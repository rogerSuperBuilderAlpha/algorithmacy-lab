## Summary
Armstrong builds three theoretical models of markets in which two groups of agents interact through platforms and each group's benefit from a platform rises with the number of agents from the other group on it. The first is a monopoly platform; the second has competing platforms where every agent joins exactly one; the third, which he calls "competing bottlenecks," has one group joining all platforms while the other single-homes. In the bottleneck case each platform holds monopoly access to its own single-homing members, so competition is fought over the single-homing side, which is priced low or subsidized, while the multi-homing side pays every platform to reach the users behind it. The paper's general result is that equilibrium prices depend on the relative size of the cross-group externalities, on whether fees are lump-sum or per-transaction, and on whether a group joins one platform or all of them. Later work (Armstrong and Wright 2007; Jeitschko and Tremblay 2020) extends and partly qualifies the pricing result.

## Use in the chapter
Section 3.3 cites it directly: "Armstrong (2006) named the configuration a competitive bottleneck: where one side single-homes ... and the other multi-homes." The application store example (consumers hold one operating system and pay nothing, developers publish on both and pay the commission) follows the model. Role: support for the mechanism by which mediation converts into rent. The audit (S8) notes the chapter elsewhere attributes "bottleneck power" to Rochet and Tirole, who do not use the term, and recommends citing Rochet and Tirole for two-sidedness and Armstrong for the bottleneck, adding Armstrong and Wright 2007 and noting Jeitschko and Tremblay 2020 contest the multi-homing-side-pays result. The audit (S18) also proposes Armstrong as second source for §5.3's claim that multihoming makes the bottleneck contestable.
