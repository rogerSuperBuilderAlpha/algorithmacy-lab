## Summary
Fox challenges the assumption, common in governance and development practice, that transparency produces accountability on its own. Transparency works through the power of shame, and shame fails against actors who are immune to exposure; disclosure of facts does not by itself bring sanctions or redress. He reframes the question as which kinds of transparency lead to which kinds of accountability under what conditions, and answers with a two-by-two distinction. Transparency can be clear, revealing who does what and who is responsible, or opaque, releasing information that does not reveal how institutions actually behave. Accountability can be soft, limited to answerability, the obligation to explain and justify, or hard, adding enforceability, the capacity to impose consequences. Only clear transparency coupled to institutions that can enforce produces hard accountability; much that passes for transparency reform produces answerability at best. The argument is conceptual, drawn from the author's work on civil-society monitoring in Latin America.
