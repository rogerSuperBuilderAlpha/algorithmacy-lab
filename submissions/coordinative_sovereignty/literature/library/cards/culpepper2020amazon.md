## Summary
Culpepper and Thelen identify a distinctive source of political influence held by some technology firms, which they call platform power. It inheres in companies of economic scale that set the terms of access through which large numbers of consumers reach goods, services, and information. Firms with platform power enjoy deference from policymakers, but the article argues that this deference does not come primarily from lobbying, campaign contributions, or the threat of disinvestment, the channels comparative political economy usually studies as instrumental and structural business power. It comes instead from the tacit allegiance of consumers, who can become a formidable source of opposition to regulation that threatens the platforms they use. The consumer-platform alliance also explains the firms' distinctive vulnerabilities: events that split consumers from the platform, or that cue citizen rather than consumer identities, are where platform power breaks. The piece is conceptual and comparative, drawing on cases from the rich democracies.

It pairs with Valdez (2023) and Rahman and Thelen (2019) as the political-economy account of platform power that coordinative sovereignty is positioned against, since all three locate power with the firm rather than asking what standing the coordinated actor holds.
