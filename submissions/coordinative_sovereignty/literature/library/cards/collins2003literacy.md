## Summary
Collins and Blot synthesize literacy studies into an account of literacy's relation to power, working across colonialism, state formation, schooling, and the distinction between official and popular literacies, and asking how literacies shape class, gender, and ethnic identities. Against determinist great divide theories, which credit writing with producing rationality, development, or a distinct literate mentality, they argue that literacy functions contextually within specific power relations: there is no single literacy with uniform effects, only situated literacies embedded in particular social, political, and historical settings. They also resist the opposite temptation of purely localized accounts, holding that neither sweeping generalization nor pure particularism explains literacy's social workings. Case material runs from colonial encounters and indigenous adaptations to the contemporary American educational landscape. The book's two organizing threads are literacies as they intersect with power structures and literacies as they form subjectivity and identity.
