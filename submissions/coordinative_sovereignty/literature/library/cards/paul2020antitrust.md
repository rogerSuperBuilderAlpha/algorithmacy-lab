## Summary
Paul argues that the reigning antitrust paradigm has turned competition into a talisman while in practice functioning as a sorting mechanism that elevates one species of economic coordination and undermines others. The ideal of competition and its companion, allocative efficiency, have been deployed against disfavoured coordination, namely horizontal coordination beyond firm boundaries, democratic market coordination, and labour unions, while a specific exception has been written into the law for one form only, the traditionally organized top-down business firm. She traces the appearance of this firm exemption, shows that it is a policy choice that cannot be derived from corporate law, contract, or property, and argues that the contractarian theory of the firm cannot explain or justify it. The conclusion is that because antitrust has established a state monopoly on the allocation of coordination rights, those rights should be seen as a public resource to be allocated in the public interest, with policy implications that include expanding the right to coordinate horizontally across firm boundaries.
