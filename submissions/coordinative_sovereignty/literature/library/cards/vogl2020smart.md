## Summary
A study of how UK local authorities adopt "smart" technologies, defined as computational algorithmic tools capable of some independent action, specifically autonomous agents and predictive analytics. Combining desk research, a survey questionnaire, and interviews, the authors find adoption at an early but foundational stage and argue that these tools are changing how public administrators and algorithms become imbricated in service delivery. They name this imbrication algorithmic bureaucracy and offer it as a sociotechnical framework for studying public administration in the era of smart technologies. Against the earlier thesis that digitalization shifts discretion from street level to system level (Bovens and Zouridis), they find something more distributed: where a shift to the system level occurs, multiple stakeholder groups shape design and implementation, and where tools remain at street level, attention falls on how the technology mediates informational feedback loops and collective intelligence. Practitioners deal with complexity not by fixing procedures but through adaptive predictive models that learn from new inputs.
