## Summary
Wang and Wright model consumers who search for firms either directly or through a platform that lowers search costs but charges firms for the transactions it facilitates. Platform fees create the possibility of showrooming, in which consumers search on the platform and then buy directly at a lower price. Search platforms such as Booking.com have defended price parity clauses (PPCs) as necessary to prevent showrooming. The model allows for showrooming yet finds that PPCs often harm consumers. The mechanism is that a platform's fee is constrained by four forces: consumers' willingness to search directly, showrooming, competition from other platforms, and firms' willingness to delist. Narrow PPCs remove the first two constraints and leave platform competition to discipline fees; wide PPCs remove platform competition as well, leaving only delisting, which yields the monopoly-platform outcome of higher fees and prices. The authors support banning wide PPCs and make the case on narrow PPCs conditional on whether platforms would remain viable without them. The conclusion also notes that steering through rankings is a less restrictive alternative to parity clauses.
