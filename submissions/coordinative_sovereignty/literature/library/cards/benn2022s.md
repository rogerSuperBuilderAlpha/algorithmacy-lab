## Summary
Benn and Lazar define Automated Influence as the use of AI to collect, integrate and analyse people's data in order to deliver targeted interventions that shape their behaviour. They take the three standard objections to it, privacy, exploitation and manipulation, and show in each case that a structural version of the objection has more purchase than its interactional counterpart: the wrong is not best located in any single transaction between a system and a consenting user but in the population-level arrangement that the system creates and maintains. The argument is philosophical rather than empirical. By rejecting the interactional focus of "AI ethics" in favour of a structural, political philosophy of AI, the authors conclude that the real problem with Automated Influence is the crisis of legitimacy it precipitates: the power to shape behaviour at scale is exercised without the kind of standing and accountability that would make it legitimate.
