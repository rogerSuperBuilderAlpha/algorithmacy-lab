## Summary
Shen and colleagues observe that formal algorithm audits, whether by developers or external researchers, have limits: many harms surface only after deployment, in contexts the auditors did not anticipate. Meanwhile everyday users frequently detect harmful behaviours through routine use, as when Twitter users noticed that image cropping favoured white faces, yet this bottom-up activity has had little academic attention. They introduce the concept of everyday algorithm auditing, defined as a process in which users detect, understand and interrogate problematic machine behaviours through their day-to-day interactions with algorithmic systems. Drawing on documented cases, they characterize the process, distinguish it from formal auditing, and ask what makes some everyday audits effective at producing change while others dissipate. They close by considering how platforms and researchers could support and collaborate with such user-driven auditing. The paper is conceptual, building on case analysis rather than new fieldwork.

## Use in the chapter
Strand 7 lists it as a MUST for §4 and §7. In §4 it supports the inferential side of algorithmacy, the capacity to form and revise a working theory of a system from its outputs, and shows that capacity operating collectively. In §7 it matters for term ownership and for remedies: everyday auditing is competence that produces accountability only when someone with standing takes it up, which is the chapter's distinction between competence and standing. Calacci and Pentland (2022) on Shipt workers pooling pay data is the worker-side instance.
