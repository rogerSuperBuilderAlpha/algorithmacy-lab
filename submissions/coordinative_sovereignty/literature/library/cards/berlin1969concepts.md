## Summary
Berlin's 1958 inaugural lecture, reprinted in Four Essays on Liberty, separates two questions that the word liberty has been used to answer. Negative liberty asks over what area a person is left alone; it is the absence of interference by others. Positive liberty asks who governs a person; it is self-mastery, being one's own master. Berlin argues that the two, though they seem close, diverged historically into opposed ideologies, because the positive conception's appeal to a "real" or higher self can licence coercion in the name of a freedom the coerced person is said not to understand. In the course of the argument he examines the ascetic or stoic retreat, in which a person secures freedom by contracting desires until nothing can be frustrated, and argues that this is not liberation but a surrender of the field on which liberty would be exercised. That passage, rather than the typology, is what the chapter draws on.

## Use in the chapter
Section 1 cites it: "Berlin (1969) named the general shape of that error: a person does not become free to walk by ceasing to want to walk." The point supports the chapter's claim that an actor that withdraws its demands from a mediation it cannot leave has abandoned autonomy rather than secured it. Role: support. The audit (O7) notes the walking example is the chapter's own illustration of Berlin's retreat-to-the-inner-citadel argument, not Berlin's example, and the sentence should say so; the audit also asks for the page range to be added to the reference entry.
