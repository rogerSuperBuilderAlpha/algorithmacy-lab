## Summary
Graff's study, first published in 1979 and reissued by Transaction in 1991 with a new introduction, examines the place of literacy in nineteenth-century society through an intensive comparative analysis of three Ontario cities, using census manuscripts and other quantitative and qualitative sources. The target is what he names the literacy myth: the belief, embedded in historical scholarship, development policy and social doctrine, that literacy by itself produces economic advancement, social mobility, civic participation and moral order. Graff finds that literacy's effects were mediated by class, ethnicity, gender and local social structure, that illiterates were not uniformly disadvantaged, and that mass schooling served cultural integration and social control at least as much as it served individual advancement. The book reevaluates the role assigned to literacy and argues for a redefined, contextual understanding of it. It became a founding text of the social-historical and "ideological" approach to literacy later associated with Street.
