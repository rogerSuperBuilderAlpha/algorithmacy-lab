## Summary
Written from the H-Net review opened this session rather than the book itself. Eisenstein argues that the shift from script to print was a communications revolution whose consequences historians had underrated. Print made texts fixed, standardized and cumulative: for the first time knowledge could be preserved and accumulated rather than degraded through copying, and readers could lay editions side by side and compare them. She traces the effects through three movements, the Renaissance, the Protestant Reformation and the Scientific Revolution, showing that each responded differently to what print made possible, and that the Reformation's spread through mass-produced vernacular religious texts was an unexpected consequence of the new medium. The argument is historical and synthetic, drawing on the secondary literature of book history rather than new archival work, and it has been both influential and contested; critics charge that it makes print too autonomous an agent. For the chapter it is the standard source on how a medium reshaped the authority of church and state.

## Use in the chapter
§2.2 cites it in the media-history pairing: "from Innis (1951) on how a civilization's dominant medium biases its form of power to Eisenstein (1979) on the printing press and the authority of church and state; a sovereignty and its competence form together." Role: support for the claim that forms of sovereignty and the competences their media require arrive together. The reviewer's account of print enabling comparison and accumulation matches the chapter's use, but the chapter should be aware that Eisenstein has been read as technologically determinist (see Collin and Street 2014 and Finnegan 1988 in this library) and that the claim should be attributed to her rather than asserted.

## Key quotations
"The capacity of printing to preserve knowledge and to allow the accumulation of information fundamentally changed the mentality of early modern readers, with repercussions that transformed Western society." (H-Net review by Duffy, describing the argument)
