## Summary
The paper studies how online travel agencies shape hotel pricing, consumer channel choice and competition in the hospitality sector. Using proprietary booking data from major hotel chains in eight European countries, the authors estimate a structural model of demand and supply across distribution channels and then simulate policy counterfactuals. The central empirical finding is that OTAs expand the market overall without exerting much downward pressure on prices, because substitutability between channels, in particular between an OTA and a hotel's own website, is low. On policy, a cap on OTA commissions would benefit hotels and consumers but harm rival platforms, while measures that promote direct contact between hotels and customers, of the kind contained in the Digital Markets Act's disintermediation and anti-parity provisions, would shift bookings toward hotel websites and compress OTA margins. The work is one of the first to model the DMA's effect on the hotel channel mix rather than on prices alone.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 6 lists it as a SHOULD for §5.5 and the gaps report names it, with Pantazi 2026, as the frontier on post-2024 Booking compliance. Its low channel substitutability is the chapter's necessary function stated econometrically: the traveler who found the hotel through the OTA does not treat the hotel's own site as a substitute, so the aggregation survives the bypass. Its DMA counterfactual also qualifies the chapter's pessimism about a decade of parity enforcement: direct-contact measures, as opposed to price-parity removal, are predicted to move bookings. Role: support, with the caveat that it is a working paper and its results are simulated.
