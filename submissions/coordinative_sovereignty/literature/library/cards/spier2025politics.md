## Summary
Spier offers a political analysis of the platform and sharing economy, contrasting its two central models, mainstream platforms and cooperative platforms, which offer similar services (home-sharing, food delivery) but differ fundamentally in ownership and governance, economic model and technical design. Building on the politics-of-technology literature, the paper combines Winner's inherently political technologies, Feenberg's critical theory of technology and Pfaffenberger's technological dramas into an approach for the political analysis of platform technologies, then applies it. Three findings follow. When incorporated into particular social arrangements, platforms become means of shaping social reality rather than tools for specific uses. Mainstream platforms perpetuate capitalist conditions in the digital sphere and require platform capitalism to function, whereas cooperative platforms resist and undermine it. The dynamics between the two models embody a struggle over the question of the good life in the digital economy. The paper also identifies a weakness in Winner's definition of inherently political technologies that it argues the field should address.
