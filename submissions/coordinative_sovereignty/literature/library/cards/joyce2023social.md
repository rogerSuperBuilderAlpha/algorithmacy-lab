## Summary
This special-issue introduction argues that a residual technological determinism still shapes research on technology, the labour process, and industrial relations despite its formal rejection. The authors identify three symptoms: overly rigid periodisations such as platform capitalism, a narrow conceptual repertoire that reifies notions like algorithmic control, and a constricted empirical focus. On algorithmic control specifically, they argue that the concept is rarely unpacked, that it pairs an impoverished notion of control with an algorithmic catch-all, and that practices Kellogg et al. (2020) list as algorithmic (rating by customers, harsh firing, opaque pay) have been commonplace since early capitalist work organisation. They also note that researchers reach sharply divergent conclusions about how much control algorithms add, from Kellogg's algorithmic cage to Wood et al.'s emphasis on autonomy, without debating the difference. They propose a social shaping of technology approach that restores attention to social relations, contestation, and continuity with earlier waves of technology.

## Use in the chapter
The audit (S2) proposes it for §3, where the chapter writes that "Kellogg et al. (2020) likewise find algorithmic control distinct from the technical and bureaucratic control employers have used for a century" (§3). Joyce et al. dispute that novelty claim directly; the audit's instruction is to cite them as the counter-position or to soften "finds" to "argues". Role: contests the novelty of algorithmic control. The chapter's own position is compatible, since it says the coordination logic is old and locates the novelty in opacity, adaptivity, and interest.
