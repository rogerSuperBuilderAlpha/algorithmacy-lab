## Summary
Cobbe examines the structural effects of automated content moderation, which she calls algorithmic censorship, on social platforms. Starting from the difficulty of moderating at scale given the volume, cultural sensitivity and nuance of human communication, she argues that automated suppression is distinctive for two reasons: it can potentially bring every communication carried on a platform within reach, including private messages, and it allows platforms to take a more active and interventionist stance toward what is said. Together these features would let a handful of platforms exercise an unprecedented degree of control over public and private communication, inserting commercial priorities further into the everyday speech of billions of people. Because a few platforms dominate the web, escape is difficult or impractical for many users. The paper is conceptual, drawing on surveillance studies and Foucauldian accounts of power, and closes by noting that opportunities for resistance nonetheless exist.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the current draft. Strand 7 lists it under Contests for §6: Cobbe reads platform power through governmentality and disciplinary effects that operate across a population, whereas the chapter's republican move (via Cicerchia) needs an agent, the firm, whose arbitrary power can be contested. The role is contests: it is the standing objection that algorithmic control is diffuse and productive rather than agentic, and the chapter's §6 paragraph on why the firm is the right agent should answer it in a sentence. Its second use is on the data side of the triad, since the users whose speech is moderated are also coordinated actors without standing. Hasan (2021) makes the parallel objection from within republicanism.
