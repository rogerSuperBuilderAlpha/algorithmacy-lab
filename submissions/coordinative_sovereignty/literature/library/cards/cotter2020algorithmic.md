## Summary
Cotter and Reisdorf treat platform algorithms as gatekeepers and arbiters of truth online and ask whether knowledge of how they work is evenly distributed. Drawing on the long history of knowledge-gap research, they hypothesize that algorithmic knowledge varies with socioeconomic advantage. They add a mechanism: algorithms are "experience technologies" that are understood mainly through use, and because socioeconomic background still shapes how people use information and communication technologies, it shapes algorithmic knowledge indirectly as well. The empirical test uses a survey of a random sample of internet users in the United States and concentrates on online search, where the authors find support for the association between algorithmic knowledge and socioeconomic background. They present this as preliminary evidence that existing structural inequalities underlie algorithmic knowledge gaps, and they frame such gaps as a new front in digital inequality alongside access and skills.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §4: "algorithmic knowledge varies with socioeconomic advantage instead of spreading evenly across those subject to it (Cotter & Reisdorf, 2020)". The role is support for the claim that algorithmacy is unevenly held with a pattern. The audit (S12) flags a mismatch the card should carry forward: the evidence is citizen-level and about search engines, not about firms, so it cannot by itself carry the chapter's inference that competence concentrates in large platform-dependent firms (audit G1 says the §4 concentration claim "is an inference and should say so"). Second sources proposed: Gran, Booth and Bucher (2021) and Chung and Wihbey (2024), the latter finding the gradient uneven across countries. Klawitter and Hargittai (2018) is the only seller-level study and belongs beside this one.
