## Summary
Krasner argues against the view that sovereign states are losing relevance, on the ground that they were never as sovereign as the conventional picture supposed. He separates several meanings of sovereignty (international legal sovereignty, Westphalian sovereignty as exclusion of external authority, domestic sovereignty, and interdependence sovereignty) and shows that political leaders have honoured the first while routinely compromising the second whenever power and interest required. The pattern he names organized hypocrisy: longstanding norms that are widely endorsed and frequently violated, so that the norm and the violation coexist as a stable feature of the system. Case studies cover minority rights, human rights, sovereign lending and the creation of new states, and the book's central explanatory claim is that differences in national power and interest, rather than shared international norms, account for how states actually behave. The work is a landmark of international relations theory, written from a realist position against constructivist accounts of norms.

## Use in the chapter
Listed in the chapter's Additional Reading only; not cited in the body. Its relevance is the disaggregation of sovereignty into distinct bundles that can be held and violated separately, which parallels the chapter's move of treating sovereignty as a standing that can be partial and shared, and its demonstration that a norm can be universally affirmed and routinely breached, a pattern the chapter's "compliant but captured" diagnosis of EU digital regulation resembles. Role: optional support, background to §1 and §2.3.
