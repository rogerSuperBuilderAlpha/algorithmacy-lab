## Summary
Nussbaum argues that public education is crucial to the health of democracy and that recent educational initiatives in many countries focus narrowly on science and technology while neglecting the arts and humanities. This narrowing, she contends, threatens democratic prospects, because economic growth oriented schooling does not by itself produce citizens capable of democratic self-government. Drawing on the capability approach and on Rabindranath Tagore's educational thought and practice, she identifies three capacities that quality education should cultivate in citizens: critical thinking, the ability to examine oneself and one's traditions in Socratic fashion; world citizenship, the ability to see oneself as bound to all other human beings across divisions of nation, class, and religion; and the narrative imagination, the ability to imagine sympathetically the situation of another. The article is a normative and programmatic statement rather than an empirical study, and it ties educational quality to democratic citizenship through the capabilities framework she shares with Sen.

## Use in the chapter
Section 2.2 cites it, paired with Sen (1999), for the constitutive link between literacy and democratic sovereignty: "citizens govern themselves to the degree they can read what institutions write" and hold institutions to account. Role: support for the claim that democratic self-government rests on citizens' critical and interpretive capabilities. The audit (O4) flags overreach: Nussbaum does not assert the proportionality the "to the degree" phrasing implies, nor does she locate accountability specifically in textual means; her argument concerns capabilities for critical reasoning, imagination, and world citizenship. The chapter's sentence should be softened to "depends on".
