## Summary
D'Cunha argues that the rule of law, understood as the equal subjection of everyone to law irrespective of wealth or status, is a check on power wherever power sits, and that today's tech giants throw it out of kilter by assuming the trappings of the state while shunning its accountability. The title borrows Edmund Burke's charge against the East India Company, "a State in disguise of a Merchant", and the article traces a line from Burke through Sherman's antitrust rhetoric and Alcoa to the present. Its diagnosis is that the platforms dissuade, capture and evade regulatory attempts, that delegating state functions to them entrenches their power, and that accountability, unlike delegation, cannot be handed over. The essay is doctrinal and polemical rather than empirical. It closes by arguing that legislative ingenuity will not repair extreme concentration of corporate power so long as underlying social injustice and over-deference in democratic institutions go unchallenged: Leviathan, whether government or corporation, cannot coexist with the rule of law.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 7 lists it as optional for §6, where the chapter defines coordinative sovereignty as standing within a mediation one cannot leave. The term-ownership summary notes that "platform sovereignty" is not an established title term and that the idea lives under Cohen, D'Cunha, Lehdonvirta and Törnberg. The role is support: a public-law voice for the claim that platforms exercise state-like power without state-like accountability, which underwrites the chapter's move from sovereignty as control over infrastructure to standing within it. Use sparingly; Gowder (2023) and Lehdonvirta (2022) carry the same idea with more institutional detail, and Dawson (2023) in the same journal supplies the accountability framework.
