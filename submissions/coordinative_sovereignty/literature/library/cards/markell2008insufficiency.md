## Summary
Markell argues that Pettit's neo-Roman ideal of freedom as non-domination cannot serve as the single organizing value of a political theory. Domination, on Pettit's account, concerns one dimension of agency, control: whether another can interfere arbitrarily with one's choices. Markell identifies a second dimension, involvement, and names its characteristic injury usurpation: being displaced from participation in the activities and decisions that shape one's situation, even where no one interferes arbitrarily. He develops the point through Pettit's account of freedom as discursive control and through the Roman-law distinction between dominium, ownership, and imperium, authority, showing that attention to both domination and usurpation is needed to make sense of imperialism, slavery and democracy. The essay's conclusion is that no single overarching ideal captures the range of political injustices that demand theoretical attention, so non-domination must be supplemented rather than replaced.
