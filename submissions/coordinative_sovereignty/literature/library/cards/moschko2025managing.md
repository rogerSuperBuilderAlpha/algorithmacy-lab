## Summary
Moschko, Blazevic and Piller move the data-sovereignty debate from the societal and policy level to the firm. Their setting is open value creation, the collaboration of independent stakeholders who share data and knowledge to create value none could create alone, and their puzzle is why industrial incumbents that call for open data sharing behave so restrictively in practice. Using an abductive qualitative design with 27 expert interviews and three industry roundtables with 19 further experts, and the strategy-as-practice framework as lens, they define organizational data sovereignty as the self-determined and deliberate exercise of control over an organization's data assets, including recognizing their value, managing collection, storage, sharing and analysis, and applying the data in inter-organizational collaboration. They identify six practices (regulatory oversight, constituting the value of data, consistent valuation, standards and technology, anonymization, trust-building) and argue that, within an organizational frame that allows openness, agility and interdisciplinarity, these let firms realize the potential of open value creation.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. The audit (E6, §8 opening) finds that Fratini et al. (2024) and Hummel et al. (2021), which the chapter uses to frame organizational digital sovereignty, are respectively a national typology and a data-sovereignty review, neither of which operationalizes the concept at the organizational level, and recommends citing Hartmann, Shajek and Schmuntzsch (2025) and this article instead. The role is support for §8's claim that digital sovereignty has an organizational register. The fit is partial and should be stated: the article's sovereignty is control over one's own data assets for sharing with partners, closer to the chapter's "sovereignty of independence" than to coordinative sovereignty as standing within a necessary mediation. It is the management-literature baseline the chapter's construct departs from.
