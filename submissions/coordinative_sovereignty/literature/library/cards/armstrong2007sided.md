## Summary
Armstrong and Wright build a model of two-sided markets that allows different degrees of product differentiation on each side. The central case is one in which sellers regard platforms as homogeneous while buyers regard them as heterogeneous. Under those conditions the "competitive bottleneck" configuration that Armstrong (2006) had imposed by assumption arises endogenously: platforms do not compete directly for sellers but compete indirectly by subsidizing buyers to join, and sellers are left with none of the gains from trade. The paper's striking result is that the side that is squeezed, the sellers, is nevertheless the side that chooses to multihome. The final part of the analysis asks what exclusive contracts do in such a setting, treating them as instruments platforms can use to prevent multihoming. The paper is theoretical, with no empirical component, and is a standard reference for why multihoming on one side does not by itself deliver bargaining power to that side.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the current chapter. The evidence audit (S8, §3.3) found that the chapter attached "bottleneck power" to Rochet and Tirole (2003), who do not use the term, and recommends citing Rochet and Tirole for two-sidedness, Armstrong (2006) for "competitive bottleneck", and adding this paper beside Armstrong 2006 as the second source, with Jeitschko and Tremblay (2020) noted as contesting the multihoming-side-pays result. The relevant chapter sentence is "Armstrong (2006) named the configuration a competitive bottleneck" in §3.3. The role is second source for the competitive-bottleneck mechanism. The wording the chapter must avoid is the one that got the July entry cut: this paper does not name the configuration, it derives it endogenously, and it shows that the extracted side multihomes rather than the reverse.
