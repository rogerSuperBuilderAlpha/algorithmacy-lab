## Summary
DeVito examines how LGBTQ+ users of social platforms build folk theories of algorithmic systems to guide their self-presentation, and what happens to those theories when the platforms keep changing. The study is qualitative and interview-based, set on platforms whose feeds and recommendation logic shift repeatedly. Its argument is that algorithmic literacy on such platforms cannot be a fixed body of knowledge: users must repeatedly reassess what the system is doing and revise their theories, a process the paper calls adaptive folk theorization. Two analytic constructs organize the findings, theorization complexity level, the sophistication of a user's model of the system, and perceived platform spirit, the user's sense of what the platform is for and whom it serves, which shapes how changes are interpreted. DeVito proposes that cultivating users' capacity for ongoing theorization, rather than transmitting facts about particular algorithms, is the realistic path to algorithmic literacy under constant change. The paper also offers the definition of algorithmic literacy that the chapter quotes.
