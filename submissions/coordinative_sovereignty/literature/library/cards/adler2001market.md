## Summary
Adler distinguishes three ideal-typical forms of organization and the coordination mechanism proper to each: market and price, hierarchy and authority, community and trust. Real institutions mix the three in varying proportions. He argues from economic and organizational theory that price and authority handle knowledge-based assets poorly compared with trust, so a knowledge-intensive economy should see high-trust forms proliferate. The paper is conceptual, supported by a review of trends in employment relations, interdivisional relations, and interfirm relations, which he reads as evidence of a halting but self-reinforcing movement toward trust. The trust in question is a modern "reflective trust" rather than traditional or blind trust. Adler closes with a speculative claim: because reflective trust threatens currently dominant actors, the trend advances unevenly, yet its momentum may eventually challenge the foundations of the capitalist form of society while laying the foundations of a postcapitalist one.

## Use in the chapter
The chapter cites Adler in §3 as one of four anchors for the sentence "markets coordinate through price, hierarchies through authority, and networks through trust and reciprocity (Adler, 2001; Powell, 1990; ...)". The role is support for the standard trichotomy against which platforms are then posed as a fourth term (Stark and Pais; Vallas and Schor). Note that Adler's third form is named community rather than network, so the chapter's "networks through trust" quietly merges Adler with Powell; the attribution of trust as the third mechanism is accurate to Adler. Strand 5 and gap C5 (Ahrne and Brunsson; Kirchner and Schüßler) propose partial organization as the rival to this trichotomy, which is the more pressing issue for §3 than any wording problem here.
