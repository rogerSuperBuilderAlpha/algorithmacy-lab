## Summary
Duncan examines how calls for collective approaches to governing data about people have been envisioned and enacted by their proponents, focusing on four types of data intermediary: data trusts, decentralised autonomous organisations, data cooperatives and data unions. The method is a documentary study of how each mechanism has been imagined and implemented across jurisdictions. These mechanisms build on individualist data rights by treating data as a form of collective value and redistributing its benefits to members; where most privacy law balances commercial, public and private interests, intermediaries work to align the social and economic value of aggregated data with the normative interests of the individuals described in it. The study finds demand for collective data governance across many jurisdictions and across otherwise divergent ideological positions. That partial consensus, Duncan argues, is an opening for lawmakers inside and beyond the European Union to strengthen individual data rights by giving legal recognition to collective mechanisms that can intervene in data collection, management and circulation.
