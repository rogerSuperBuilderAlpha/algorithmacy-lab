## Summary
Bellesia, Mattarelli and Bertolotti ask how freelancers on a major online labour platform interpret the algorithmic scores that index their quality and future performance, and with what behavioural and emotional consequences. Drawing on technology affordance theory and on interviews and public profile data from knowledge-intensive gig workers, they build a grounded model in which four perceived affordances of algorithms explain different responses: scores read as a barrier, as individual visibility, as self-extension, or as the rule of the game. Newcomers without reviews experience scores as barriers and comply; with time, workers who see scores as visibility act directly on the algorithm, while those who see scores as extensions of themselves act indirectly by working on client relations. Negative feelings such as anxiety arise at identifiable points rather than uniformly. The paper reframes the algorithmic management debate away from what workers cannot see toward what they can see and use, and it supplies an agentic, affordance-based account of score management.

## Key quotations
"we argue, to fully understand how freelancers deal with algorithmic scores, we first need to investigate how they interpret scores and, more specifically, what scores can do for them, i.e., perceived algorithmic affordances and constraints." (Abstract)
"when workers see algorithmic scores as tools enhancing their individual visibility, they engage in behavioural responses aimed at directly influencing algorithms and algorithmic computations. On the other hand, when informants perceive algorithmic scores as extensions of their self, they indirectly influence algorithms by working on their relations with clients." (Discussion)
