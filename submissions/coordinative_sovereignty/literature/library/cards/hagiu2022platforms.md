## Summary
The paper builds a tractable model of a platform that can operate in dual mode, selling its own products alongside third-party sellers of competing products, to assess the welfare effects of the regulatory bans proposed in India (2019) and in the US Ending Platform Monopolies Act (2021). The model endogenises the platform's choice of business model (seller, marketplace or dual) after a ban, allows direct and intermediated channels, and lets the platform imitate successful third-party products and self-preference through recommendation made after prices are set. The headline result is that banning dual mode at the product level can reduce consumer surplus and total welfare even when the platform would otherwise imitate and self-preference, because the platform's own product disciplines third-party prices and because a banned platform may switch to a worse mode. In the empirically most relevant scenarios, targeted rules against imitation and self-preferencing do better than an outright ban. The authors position the work against de Cornière and Taylor (2019), whose divestiture result differs, and against Etro's concurrent model of platform entry.

## Use in the chapter
Not yet cited. Strand 2 and strand 6 both flag it, and the gaps report lists it among works that contest specific claims. It bears on §3.3 (interested mediation), §5.4 (the "reducible conduit" treatment of Amazon's reseller function in the portfolio argument) and §8 (implications for remedies). Role: contests. The chapter treats the platform's own-product function as a contingent mediator that the bypass test can strip away; this paper argues that stripping it can make the coordinated sellers' customers worse off and that the interested mediator's own products discipline third-party prices. The chapter should state that its diagnostic concerns the seller's standing over the platform's choices, not the welfare verdict on the dual mode, and cite this paper as the efficiency defence.
