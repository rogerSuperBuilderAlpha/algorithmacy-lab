## Summary
The authors argue that, for most kinds of explanation and most kinds of algorithmic decision, there is no moral right to an explanation. After distinguishing what the phrase might mean, they take the most plausible versions in turn: a right to an explanation of a specific bureaucratic decision, a right grounded in public reason, and a right to a higher-level explanation of an entire automated system. Each, they argue, is either superfluous to the normative good it is meant to protect, impossible to deliver, or not the best means of securing that good. Informed self-advocacy, the interest Vredenburgh's account rests on, can usually be served by second-order knowledge and outcome-based audits without opening the model. They also object that proponents single out algorithmic decisions for a justificatory burden not imposed on other uses of science in policy. The argument is conceptual, framed in Scanlonian terms, and concludes that there are other important rights in the area, in particular a right to contest bureaucratic decisions.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the chapter. Strand 3 lists it as optional for §7, where the chapter separates explainability (reasons) from contestability (a channel to challenge) and treats the GDPR's explanation provisions as part of the compliant apparatus. Karlan and Kugelberg contest the premise that explanation is owed at all, and their conclusion that the right that survives is a right to contest is, read one way, support for the chapter's emphasis on channels over reasons. Their remark that algorithmic literacy of recipients often does more than technical explanation also bears on §9. Role: contests the explanation premise; converges on contestation.
