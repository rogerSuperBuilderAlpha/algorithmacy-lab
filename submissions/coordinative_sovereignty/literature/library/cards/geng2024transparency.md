## Summary
Geng argues that platform transparency policy has been pursued as a goal in itself and should instead be designed backwards from specified behavioural outcomes. What transparency means, how to create meaningful transparency and which behaviours it can change are all open legal and policy questions, so the paper proposes starting with the "why" before the "how": define the behaviour one wants from a named audience (users, regulators or platform providers) on a named kind of platform, then test and select the transparency tactics that produce it. Three illustrative outcomes are developed: getting users to engage with social media less seamlessly and more thoughtfully, getting regulators to set limits on price variation between groups on shopping platforms, and getting platforms to track amplification of false messages in coordinated campaigns. The paper is conceptual and US-policy oriented, drawing on Kaminski's observation that algorithmic governance is hard because we cannot agree on why to regulate, and it concludes that no single set of tactics addresses all platform harms, so an outcomes-focused selection method is the durable approach.
