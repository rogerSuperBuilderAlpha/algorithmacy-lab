## Summary
Savolainen and Ruckenstein reorient the literature on agentic engagements with algorithms, which has run under labels such as algorithmic literacy, folk theories and resistance, by asking what autonomy means in human-algorithm relations. They distinguish two horizons. In the instrumental horizon, algorithmic systems are technical procedures that order social life at a distance by rules that can only partly be known; autonomy here is activated through reflective, informed choice and the ability to enact one's goals and values within technological constraints, which is where algorithmic competence and negotiated autonomy sit. In the intimate horizon, algorithms approach minds and bodies through feeds and recommendations, and autonomy acquires affective dimensions: quests for it arise from disturbance and from comfort in positions of vulnerability. The authors argue that distinguishing these dimensions directs attention to issues of ethical and political importance, since autonomy is both a public value and a lived concern beyond theory. The paper is conceptual and draws on the authors' earlier empirical work.
