## Summary
The article analyses the EU's recent use of the terms digital sovereignty and strategic autonomy and reads that vocabulary as an indicator of tension between the Union's established role as an economic and normative power and its newer geopolitical ambitions. The authors root the tension in a structural disparity: the EU has considerable regulatory and market power in digital matters but a limited mandate and thin capabilities in foreign and security policy, which member states guard as national prerogatives. Tracing the terminology across cybersecurity and digital-technology policy since roughly 2016, they identify three trends in the geopoliticisation of the digital agenda: the instrumental use of classic internal-market policies to exert geopolitical influence, the imposition of foreign-policy imperatives on national markets, and new hybrid digital policies that combine internal-market, fundamental-rights and geopolitical concerns. They conclude that digital sovereignty sits uneasily with the EU's normative power, risks resonating with authoritarian cyber-sovereignty narratives, and may produce a strategic cacophony rather than autonomy.
