## Summary
Goodrich takes up a worry voiced by the Neo-Brandeisian antitrust scholars about the business model in which Alphabet, Meta and others collect data about users in order to maximise the effectiveness of advertising on their platforms. He observes that philosophers have paid little attention to this body of antitrust argument and aims to give it the attention it deserves. The paper, in Philosophy & Public Affairs, reconstructs the Neo-Brandeisian concern in the vocabulary of domination: control over the means of prediction, the data and models that let a platform anticipate and steer behaviour, is a form of power over users and dependent firms that is objectionable independently of consumer-price effects. It then asks what antitrust, as opposed to privacy law, can do about such power. The title's Marxian echo signals the remedy orientation: structural measures aimed at who controls the predictive apparatus rather than at disclosure to those it predicts. Only the short abstract was opened this session.
