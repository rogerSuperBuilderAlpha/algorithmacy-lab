## Summary
Hildebrandt examines how smart technologies reshape society through their capacity to anticipate and pre-empt human behaviour, preferences, health, financial risk and criminal propensity, and argues that society is moving from an information economy to a data-driven one with deep consequences for governance. The machine-learning systems that power what she calls data-driven agency threaten privacy, identity formation, autonomy, equality, procedural fairness and the presumption of innocence, and in doing so they challenge the purposes of law in a democracy: justice, legal certainty and the common good. Her central analytic move is to treat law as itself dependent on a medium. Modern law was articulated in the technology of the printed text, and its mode of operating through written rules does not carry over straightforwardly to a world governed through code and data. Rather than refusing the technologies, she calls on lawyers, technologists and civil society to engage with them so that the rule of law can be rearticulated in the new medium.
