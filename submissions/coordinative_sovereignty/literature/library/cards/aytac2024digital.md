## Summary
Aytac argues that the power of social media companies to regulate communication in the public sphere constitutes a novel type of domination in the republican sense. Because these companies can partially dictate the terms on which citizens participate politically, they hold the capacity to interfere arbitrarily with choices individuals make as citizens. The argument distinguishes two channels. First, where companies directly control political speech, they exercise quasi-public power, since their moderation of platforms entails the capacity to interfere arbitrarily with democratic contestation. Second, algorithmic governance shapes what mode of discursive engagement citizens can choose in their relations with one another; by raising the cost of deliberative engagement it narrows the citizens' menu of choice. The piece is conceptual, working within Pettit's framework of freedom as non-domination, and it ties the diagnosis to the absence of meaningful exit and to contestatory remedies rather than to consent or interference alone.
