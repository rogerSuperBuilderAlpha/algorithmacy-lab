## Summary
Sivetc examines how Russia governs online expression through the material and legal architecture of the network rather than through content rules alone. The state has been coaxing foreign internet companies into building the Yarovaya-Law infrastructure by listing them as "information disseminators", a designation that obliges them to store content data. That infrastructure, the article argues, might develop into a state-controlled content layer for the sovereign Russian internet, a new digital lock to curb free expression: control exercised "by infrastructure" precedes and outlasts any individual takedown decision. The article also documents the strategy's limits. By the summer of 2020, the building of the Yarovaya-Law infrastructure had faltered on cost and implementation obstacles, which may have hindered the continuation of the RuNet sovereignization strategy. The case shows state digital sovereignty pursued through compelled private infrastructure, with the users of that infrastructure holding no standing in the system built around them.

## Use in the chapter
Audit S32 proposes it for §8, where the chapter's claim that "Russia's sovereign internet regime has the same shape at the network layer" rests on Stadnik (2021) alone. Role: second source beside Stadnik for the RuNet case in the openness-versus-control discussion. It reinforces the chapter's point that a closed national infrastructure reproduces co-optation under a domestic flag: sovereignization here gives users neither exit nor voice, and control migrates into the infrastructure layer where contestation by the coordinated is hardest.
