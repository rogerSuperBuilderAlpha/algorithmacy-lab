## Summary
The Digital Services Act sets horizontal obligations for intermediary services in the EU, graduated by the size and function of the provider, and amends the e-Commerce Directive. For the chapter's purposes the relevant provisions are the due-process articles and the recommender articles. Article 17 requires a statement of reasons for restrictions; Article 20 obliges online platforms to run an internal complaint-handling system; Article 21 lets recipients take unresolved disputes to certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies, whose decisions are not binding on the platform. Article 27 requires every online platform to set out in its terms the main parameters of its recommender systems and any options to modify them, a duty that runs to recipients of the service, and Article 38 adds a non-profiling option for very large platforms. Article 40 gives Digital Services Coordinators and vetted researchers access to platform data. These details are stated from the Act's structure as the chapter and audit cite it, since the article text was not read verbatim this session.

## Use in the chapter
Named but not listed. §1 places the Act among the instruments that "give European actors a degree of control over data, infrastructure, and code"; §3.2 says it "adds recommender transparency and researcher access"; §7 and its table treat Articles 20 and 21 as the thin case-level channel; §8 says its "recommender transparency runs to a platform's users and does not replace" the P2B duty to business users. The audit (U5) asks for a proper entry with Articles 19, 20 to 21, 27 and 40 pinned, and (O2) notes that Article 27(1) is a parallel recommender duty that the §8 claim about the "only duty in European law to disclose the main parameters of ranking" must be squared with; Nolteernsting (2025) makes the same point from the legal side. The role is legal instrument. Leerssen (2023) contests the reach of its transparency rights into curation and demotion.
