## Summary
Heidebrecht asks whether EU governance of the digital single market has become less market-liberal and more public-interventionist, against a background in which the single market has often been portrayed as a Trojan horse of neoliberalism while recent rhetoric on digital sovereignty signals a wish for more control over the digital sphere. Using a historical-institutionalist model, the article presents a case study of two key elements of the DSM, digital services regulation and data protection, from the 1990s to the 2020s. It finds that policy goals have broadened beyond competitiveness, that stakeholders and public authorities rather than business actors have become more important in governance processes, and that more market-correcting instruments have been introduced. The change is gradual and layered: in the 2010s, efforts to build trust added public authority at Member State level, and in the 2020s the EU added further public elements and redirected the Commission's supranational powers toward oversight of very large online platforms.
