## Summary
Frankenfeld introduces technological citizenship as a membership status, with rights and obligations, held by persons living within societies governed by complex technologies. The framework is designed to reconcile the freedom to innovate with protection of individual autonomy and dignity, and it specifies the rights such a citizen should hold: to information, to participation in decisions about technologies that impose risk, to informed consent, and to limits on total harm. Obligations run the other way, including a duty to learn enough to participate. The article frames this as a new social contract of complexity, a compact both among people with unequal knowledge and between humans and the technologies they depend on, and it proposes that lay participation in managing hazards and verifying safety is the practical expression of the status. It is a normative and conceptual contribution intended to give risk studies a shared vocabulary in place of the fragmented terms the field had used.
