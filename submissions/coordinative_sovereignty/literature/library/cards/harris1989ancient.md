## Summary
Harris offers the first thorough survey of the levels, types and functions of literacy in the classical world, from the invention of the Greek alphabet around 800 BC to the fifth century AD. His central estimate is that the social and technological conditions of antiquity made mass literacy unthinkable: neither Greeks nor Romans came close to a modern written culture, and both relied on oral communication far more than has usually been assumed. Literacy was restricted, but its effects were various. It stimulated critical thought in some quarters, and as written communication it became an instrument of power indispensable to running empires. Harris also notes a paradox of late Roman religious culture, in which writing was more and more revered while less and less practised. The book is the standard reference for the point that ancient literacy was a minority competence distributed by class, occupation and gender rather than a clerical preserve, and it grounds any careful statement about who held literacy before the modern state.
