## Summary
Bakos and Halaburda revisit a central result of two-sided platform theory, the strategic interdependence between the two sides of a platform that leads platforms to subsidize one side to maximize total profit. They show that this result depends largely on the assumption that at least one side single-homes. Using a Hotelling-style model of differentiated platform competition, they analyse the case, increasingly common as joining several platforms becomes cheap, where agents on both sides multihome. In that setting the interdependence between the two sides diminishes or disappears, and the standard strategic advice to subsidize one side may be limited or wrong. The paper is theoretical and short, and its contribution is a caveat: the competitive-bottleneck logic that makes a platform's hold over a single-homing side valuable, and that motivates subsidy, weakens when both sides can be on several platforms at once, which in turn changes who bears the platform's price.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 2 lists it as optional for §5.3, where the chapter reads exit through multihoming: "where an actor can hold more than one platform" the constraint on it loosens. Bakos and Halaburda supply the formal counterpart: when both sides multihome, the platform's leverage over either side falls, which is the economic content of the chapter's claim that a multihomed gate is contingent rather than necessary.
