## Summary
Khurana returns to Simmel's triad and argues that the literature on third parties in markets has developed only two of Simmel's three categories, the tertius gaudens who profits from the competition of two unconnected parties and divide et impera, both of which feed Burt's structural-holes theory of brokerage. The third category, the mediator or intermediary, is underdeveloped: here the two parties know of each other and could deal directly but choose to work through a third whose benefit comes from producing the "concord of two". Drawing on a two-year field study of two of the four largest executive search firms, together with interviews with candidates and board members, he identifies three market conditions that give rise to the intermediary role (few buyers and sellers, high risk to both, and institutionalized gaps between them) and three elements of the role itself: coordinating, mediating and legitimating. The general claim is that a third party's role is contingent on the structure of the exchange rather than fixed by position.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited; strand 5 lists it as optional for §5.2. The chapter builds its triad on Simmel's tertius gaudens (§3, via Vallas and Schor) and on Burt and Obstfeld (§5.2). Khurana supplies the missing Simmelian category and, more usefully, a worked case of a mediator whose parties could deal directly yet choose not to, which is the chapter's "partial mediator" with real integrating work alongside a bypassable route. The role is optional support for the bypass test: his three conditions for the intermediary role are a checklist for when a mediator will survive the open tie. Gaps C6 (Schüßler et al.) already presses the chapter to move beyond a single tertius gaudens reading; Khurana is the sociological precedent for doing so.
