## Summary
Mueller argues that applying sovereignty to cyberspace governance is inappropriate to the domain. Historically sovereignty forged together supreme authority and territory; in cyberspace the two are sundered, so the concept cannot be transferred without distortion. He develops a technically grounded definition of cyberspace as a distinct domain of action, conflict and governance, clarifies its relation to territory, and reviews the literature since the early 1990s to show how explicitly pro-sovereigntist ideas and practices emerged in the 2010s. Linking the debate to IR scholarship on the historical emergence of sovereignty, he argues that technologies routinely change the basis of international order and that territorial sovereignty is not a stable, uniform principle that can be presumptively extended to the internet. He then shows how different conceptions of cyber-sovereignty serve the interests of the United States, Russia and China, and proposes instead a governance model based on the global commons, answering the standard objections and explaining what would change if cyberspace were conceived that way.
