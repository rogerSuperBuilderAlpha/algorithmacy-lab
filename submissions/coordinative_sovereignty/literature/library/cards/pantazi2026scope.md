## Summary
Pantazi examines Article 5(3) of the Digital Markets Act, which forbids gatekeepers from preventing business users from offering better prices or conditions through other platforms or their own sales channels. The provision grew out of the Amazon and Booking parity cases, both firms now designated gatekeepers, but its wording reaches further than a ban on contractual parity clauses. Recent antitrust investigations show gatekeepers pursuing the same end by other means: downgrading in search results the business users who price lower elsewhere, and undercutting their prices directly. The article asks whether such practices fall within Article 5(3), and argues for an integrated reading that considers the combined effect of the practices in light of the DMA's objectives and its enforcement machinery rather than treating each practice in isolation. The piece is doctrinal, working from the text, the legislative history and the enforcement record.

## Use in the chapter
Strand 6 lists it as a MUST for §5.5 and §8. The chapter's Booking case argues that opening the parity gate by contract and competition law leaves demand aggregation, the necessary mediation, untouched; Pantazi shows the parity constraint migrating from contract into ranking and pricing, which is exactly the chapter's point that the gate has moved into the algorithm (Hunold et al. 2020 is the empirical companion). Role: support. In §8 it qualifies any claim that the DMA closes the parity question.
