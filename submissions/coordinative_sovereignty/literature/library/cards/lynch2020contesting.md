## Summary
Lynch examines a grassroots movement in Barcelona organized around the claim to "technological sovereignty" (TS): a broad, decentralised network of cooperatives, associations and community initiatives experimenting with locally rooted, open-source digital development. The study is ethnographic, drawing on fieldwork with the movement's dossiers, conferences and projects. Against critiques of the smart city that stop at diagnosis, Lynch asks what alternative modes of digital development in urban life might look like beyond securitisation and capital accumulation, and reads TS through the literatures on prefigurative urban politics and postcapitalist economies. The movement's concept of sovereignty is traced to food sovereignty (Via Campesina) rather than to state power, and TS sits alongside energy, food, housing, cultural and health "sovereignties" as a demand to put the vital systems of everyday life under direct democratic control. The movement's practices centre on democratic and cooperative forms of work, property, production and consumption, an ethics of care, and work through local communities.

## Use in the chapter
Not yet cited. The audit (S26) names it as the second source for the §6 sentence "Couture and Toupin (2019) document grassroots and emancipatory variants" of digital sovereignty. Lynch is a detailed case of such a variant, and it sharpens the chapter's contrast: the Barcelona movement answers the ownership question by building alternatives, whereas coordinative sovereignty asks what standing an actor holds "without owning the infrastructure or leaving it." Role: second source for the grassroots variant; it can also be cited in §7 beside platform cooperativism as evidence that the exit route requires a surrounding cooperative ecosystem.
