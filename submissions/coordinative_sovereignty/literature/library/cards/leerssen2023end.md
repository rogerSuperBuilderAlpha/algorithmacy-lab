## Summary
Leerssen offers a legal analysis of shadow banning, understood as content-moderation sanctions that are undetectable to those they affect. Visibility-management practices such as delisting and demotion differ from removal or suspension in that the affected user may never learn of them, which frustrates both accountability and contestation. The paper reads the EU Digital Services Act as the first legislation to address transparency for visibility-based remedies and finds that its statement-of-reasons and notice duties substantially restrict shadow banning, with narrow exceptions. It then identifies a tension between two models of moderation, the administrative-law model of reasoned decisions and the security model that treats opacity as a defence against manipulation, and a definitional problem: what counts as an actionable "reduction of visibility" when ranking is continuous. Leerssen proposes that demotion be defined as an ex post adjustment to engagement-based relevance scores, while conceding that the DSA leaves the platform's broader ranking power, curation rather than moderation, largely unregulated.
