## Summary
This editorial introduces a Policy & Internet special issue whose six articles seek to "unthink" digital sovereignty: to deconstruct the concept, problematize its roots in state sovereignty, and reconceive it from different viewpoints, including postcolonial, Indigenous, and Global South perspectives. The authors trace the term's distinct genealogies: the EU's autonomy discourse, driven by the lack of domestic tech giants; China's internet and information sovereignty, deployed since the 1990s out of fear of foreign interference and cultural imperialism; Russia's securitized RuNet; and BRICS and African uses that mix resistance to foreign digital dominance with justification of authoritarian practice. Rather than a static condition, digital sovereignty appears as a set of practices and discourses that lend legitimacy to demands for autonomy in digital governance, whatever the claimant. The piece updates and extends Pohle and Thiel's 2020 conceptual survey, and the issue's contributions range from discourse analysis of multistakeholderism's decline to comparative regulatory studies of the EU and China.

It strengthens the chapter's move in §2.3 and §7, since the editorial itself detaches digital sovereignty from state ownership and treats it as legitimacy-conferring practice open to multiple claimants, which is friendly ground for coordinative sovereignty's non-state, non-ownership answer. Cite it alongside Pohle and Thiel 2020 wherever the field's current state is characterized.
