## Summary
Kaminski and Urban ask what appeals rights, if any, people should have when they are subjected to decisions made by artificial intelligence. They argue that the right to challenge a decision with significant effects is a core principle of the rule of law, and that contestation is the neglected half of algorithmic due process: while the EU recognises an individual right to contest automated decisions through the GDPR and related instruments, US proposals have gravitated toward systemic governance (audits, impact assessments) and left the individual's ability to push back undeveloped. The article builds theoretical scaffolding for contestation from due process theory, distinguishing four archetypes that vary between rules and standards and between procedural and substantive approaches. It then analyses existing contestation systems, GDPR implementation, copyright notice-and-takedown, credit-card dispute resolution and the right to be forgotten, to show what works and what fails in practice. It closes with normative guidance for designing contestation rights for AI decisions, and presents itself as the first in-depth analysis of the GDPR contestation right for a US audience.

In §3.2 the chapter argues that disclosure cannot reach the mediator and that complaint channels are thin; Kaminski and Urban give the legal-theoretical account of why transparency without a workable right to contest is incomplete, and their analysis of existing schemes supports the chapter's point that contestation mechanisms are often formal rather than consequential.
