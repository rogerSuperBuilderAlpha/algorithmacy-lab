## Summary
The authors ask when and why ecosystems emerge and what makes them a distinct governance form, complementing a literature that had mostly described what ecosystems are. Their answer is that modularity is the enabling condition: when a system's components interface at thin, standardized points, distinct but interdependent organizations can coordinate without hierarchical fiat. Ecosystems then address multilateral dependence through complementarities of several kinds, supermodular or unique, unidirectional or bidirectional, and the paper argues that non-generic complementarities lie at their core, together with the creation of sets of roles that face similar rules rather than bespoke contracts with each partner. The managerial summary adds that ecosystem members are bound together by the non-redeployability of their collective investment elsewhere. The definition later quoted across the field is of a set of actors with varying degrees of multilateral, non-generic complementarities that are not fully hierarchically controlled. The paper closes with factors affecting ecosystem emergence, evolution and failure.

## Use in the chapter
Strands 2 and 5 list it as a MUST for §5.4, beside Baldwin (2008) on modularity and transaction boundaries. The chapter's §5.4 argues that firms cut at thin crossing points and that the platform's bundling of functions is a strategy rather than a hedge; Jacobides et al. supply the ecosystem-level statement that modularity permits coordination without hierarchy while non-generic complementarity and non-redeployable investment hold members in place. Role: support. It is also the anchor for the term "ecosystem" that Kretschmer et al. (2022) contest with "meta-organization".
