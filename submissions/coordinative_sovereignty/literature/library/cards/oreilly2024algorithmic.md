## Summary
The authors outline a theory of algorithmic attention rents in digital aggregator platforms. As platforms grow they become increasingly able to extract rents from users, suppliers and advertisers through their algorithmic control over the allocation of user attention. The analysis centres on advertising business models, in which attention harvested from users is resold to suppliers and advertisers, but the authors hold that the theory applies to other online models. A rent is identified by a deviation from the best possible attention allocation of which the platform is capable and on whose promise it drew its users; the platform uses that discretion to extract pecuniary rents from suppliers and advertisers or to allocate more value to its own information. Because the allocation is opaque, the deviation is hard to observe from outside. The policy conclusion is that regulation should mandate disclosure of the operating metrics platforms use to allocate attention and shape the free side of their marketplaces, together with details of how attention is monetised.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §3.3: "O'Reilly et al. (2024) argue that platforms extract rents through algorithmic control over the allocation of attention", that the market is opaque, and that "the rents follow from discretion over allocation rather than from any service rendered". Audit S9 finds the last clause stronger than the source, whose baseline is the best allocation the platform could make for users, and recommends "rather than from value created for users". The source's own formulation, deviation from the allocation on whose promise the platform drew its users, supports the audit's wording. Role: support for opacity-as-rent, replacing Cutolo and Kenney and Zuboff in the July bib.
