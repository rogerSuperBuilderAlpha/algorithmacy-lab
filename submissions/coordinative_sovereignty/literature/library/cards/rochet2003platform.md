## Summary
The founding formal treatment of two-sided markets. The abstract opens: many if not most markets with network externalities are two-sided, and to succeed, platforms in industries such as software, portals and media, payment systems and the internet must "get both sides of the market on board". The paper builds a model of platform competition in which the structure of prices between the two sides, and not just the total price level, determines volume, because each side's participation confers an externality on the other. It analyzes how price allocation and equilibrium market shares are determined under different governance forms, comparing profit-maximizing platforms and nonprofit joint undertakings against integrated monopoly and Ramsey pricing benchmarks. The general lesson carried into later platform economics is that a mediator serving two interdependent constituencies chooses a fee structure that can subsidize one side and tax the other, so the division of surplus reflects the platform's position between the sides rather than either side's costs.

## Use in the chapter
Section 3.3 cites it in the sentence "That structural position gives the mediator bottleneck power (Rochet & Tirole, 2003)", contrasting the mediated triad with parties who transact directly and divide the surplus between them. Role: support for the economics of two-sided mediation. The audit (S8) flags a misattribution: "bottleneck" is Armstrong's term, not Rochet and Tirole's, so the sentence should either cite Armstrong (2006) for the term or cite this paper only for two-sidedness and the mediator's grip on the price structure.
