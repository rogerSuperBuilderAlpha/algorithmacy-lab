## Summary
Wilkinson's monograph, with contributions by Alan Davies and Dorothy Atkinson, introduced the term "oracy" for competence in speaking and listening, coined on the model of literacy and numeracy, and argued that spoken language deserved the same educational attention as the written. The ERIC abstract describes it as proposing modifications of current assumptions both about the nature of spoken language and about its functions in personality development. It surveys the contribution of linguistics to English teaching in Britain, examines the environmental factors that shape oral ability (early upbringing, schooling, social context, mass media and cultural identity), and sets out approaches to teaching spoken English together with methods of assessing oral expression and listening comprehension, with sample test materials. It closes with a discography and a selected bibliography on language. It is a work of English education rather than of media theory, and its interest for the chapter is lexical and historical: it is where the first term in the oracy, literacy, algorithmacy sequence was made.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. The audit (O6, §2.1) flags that the chapter writes "Oracy, as Goody (1977) and Ong (1982) describe it", but neither Goody nor Ong uses the term, which Wilkinson coined in 1965. The audit's fix is to write "the oral competence Goody and Ong describe"; an alternative is to keep "oracy" and credit Wilkinson in a parenthesis or in Key Terms, which already names the three historical forms as "oracy, literacy, and algorithmacy". The role is support for the term's provenance. Wilkinson's use (an educational competence in speaking and listening) is narrower than the chapter's (a competence for working the medium through which authority is exercised), and the chapter should mark that it is extending the word.
