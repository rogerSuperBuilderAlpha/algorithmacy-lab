## Summary
Fratini, Hine, Novelli, Roberts and Floridi treat digital sovereignty as a popular but still emerging concept claimed by competing global actors whose narratives are often inconsistent. They systematically analyse a corpus of 271 peer-reviewed articles to extract descriptive features, how digital sovereignty is pursued, and value features, why it is pursued, and from these derive four models of national digital sovereignty: rights-based, market-oriented, centralisation, and state-based. They then evaluate the models within a framework of robust governance, asking whether each can absorb disruptions from technological advance, geopolitical change, and shifting social norms. The central finding is negative: none of the four models fully combines comprehensive regulation of digital technologies with sufficient responsiveness to fast-moving technological, social, and economic change. The paper offers the typology as a map for policymakers seeking a robust form of digital sovereignty. Its unit of analysis is the state or jurisdiction, and it does not measure the sovereignty of organizations.

## Use in the chapter
Cited four times. Section 3.2, "Fratini et al. (2024) sort existing models of digital sovereignty as rights-based, market-oriented, centralizing, or state-based," is the accurate use and should stay. Section 1 attaches it to the claim that EU instruments give "a degree of control ... that no other jurisdiction matches"; audit O1 notes the paper does not rank the EU and should be dropped from that sentence or the superlative given to Bradford alone. Section 8 opens by saying Fratini et al. "operationalize organizational digital sovereignty"; audit E6 finds that overreach and proposes Hartmann, Shajek and Schmuntzsch (2025, ch. 1) and Moschko et al. (2025) instead. Role: support for the typology and for the finding that no model combines regulation with responsiveness, which the chapter can read as the literate model's limit.
