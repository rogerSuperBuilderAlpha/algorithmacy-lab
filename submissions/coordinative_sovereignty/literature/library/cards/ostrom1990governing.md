## Summary
Ostrom examines how resources used in common by many individuals can be governed without either of the two solutions conventionally recommended, state control or privatisation, neither of which has been uniformly successful with common-pool resource problems. She first sets out the three models most often used to justify those recommendations, the tragedy of the commons, the prisoner's dilemma and the logic of collective action, and then presents theoretical and empirical alternatives. The core of the book is institutional analysis of long-enduring and of failed cases of self-governance: communal tenure in Swiss meadows and Japanese forests, Spanish and Philippine irrigation communities, groundwater basins in California, and fisheries. From these she derives design principles, including clearly defined boundaries, congruence between rules and local conditions, collective-choice arrangements in which those affected can modify the rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions and conflict-resolution mechanisms. The central result is that common-pool problems are sometimes solved by voluntary organisation rather than by a coercive state or a market.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §6: "Ostrom (1990) supplies the institutional form, and applying her to a platform takes one adjustment." The chapter notes that a platform is not an unowned pasture but enclosed and owned, and uses Schlager and Ostrom (1992) to locate the available claim in collective-choice arrangements over the coordination's rules rather than ownership of the platform. Role: support for the institutional form of coordinative sovereignty, in particular the collective-choice design principle, by which those affected by operational rules can participate in changing them.
