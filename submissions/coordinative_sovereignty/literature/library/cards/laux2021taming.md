## Summary
Laux, Wachter and Mittelstadt examine the Commission's DMA and DSA proposals and their shared reliance on platform size as the trigger for obligations: gatekeepers under the DMA, very large online platforms under the DSA. They argue that this focus has two costs for the DSA. Risks disseminated by platforms below the threshold fall into a regulatory blind spot, and the largest platforms can turn their market power against the auditors and risk assessors the DSA makes mandatory, a danger the authors name "audit capture". If auditors depend on a handful of clients, societal risks may be left undiscovered or downplayed. The article traces the legislative history of the size criteria, argues for safeguards against capture and against the incentive structures the DSA creates, and draws on the post-2008 debate on financial audit reform for blueprints. It is a doctrinal and institutional-design analysis rather than an empirical study, written before the final texts were adopted.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited; strand 3 lists it as SHOULD for §7 and §3.2. In §3.2 the chapter says the DSA "adds recommender transparency and researcher access" and that the literate wager "mostly fails"; Laux et al. give a further reason, that the audit layer meant to make disclosure reliable can be captured by the audited. In §7 the chapter proposes that pooled data "can feed the audits that give contestation its evidence"; the role is support with a caution, since audits are only as independent as their funding structure. The article also connects to the chapter's recurring theme that instruments keyed to size miss the platform-dependent firm below the threshold.
