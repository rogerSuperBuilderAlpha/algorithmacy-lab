## Summary
Stadnik argues that discussion of Russian internet sovereignty has focused on control of harmful content and its supposed effects on the regime and society, but that content control is not the only way to exercise sovereignty over digital media. The Russian government, she writes, came to realise that without control of infrastructure most strategies for filtering and blocking sites and services are wasted, and it therefore invested heavily in laws and regulations for RuNet's infrastructure, where centralised control of traffic was a real novelty. The article works through four linked cases: the Revizor system that monitors ISP compliance with blocking orders; the failed attempt to block Telegram; Federal Law 90-FZ, the "Sovereign RuNet" law; and the experiment with free access to "socially significant" websites, which could in future function as a white list. Its contribution is to show the gap and the convergence between political ambition and technical implementation: the infrastructure-layer project gives the state capacities that content regulation alone could not.
