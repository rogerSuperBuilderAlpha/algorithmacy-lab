## Summary
Bai and Velamuri treat the sharing economy as a governance structure in its own right rather than as a business model, and ask under what contextual conditions it outperforms the alternatives. The abstract opens from the observation that the sharing economy has shown "unprecedented capability in large-scale mobilization of dispersed and underutilized private assets for collective usage". The authors characterize it as a combination of two elements, a two-sided market platform and a modular architecture, and examine how the several actors interact within such ecosystems. Drawing on transaction cost economics and the resource-based view, they develop comparison frameworks that identify the conditions under which the sharing economy functions better than markets and firms, and also better than adjacent newer forms such as platform economies and collaborative ecosystems. The editorial that introduces the special issue reports the resulting two-dimensional framework (reliance on centralized information processing, strong or weak; boundary of information exchange, closed or open) and reads the paper as showing that markets and firms blend rather than oppose each other once technology changes how activity is organized.
