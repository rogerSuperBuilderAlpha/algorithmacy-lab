## Summary
Binns and Veale examine how Article 22 GDPR, which gives data subjects a right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, applies to real decision systems that are multi-stage rather than single-shot. Real pipelines chain profiling, scoring, triage, and human review, and automation may apply only to subsets of data subjects. The authors identify five complications: automation may be selective across subsets of subjects; it is unclear where in the chain the "decision" occurs; it is unclear whether significance attaches to potential or realized effects; upstream automation can foreclose downstream outcomes even where a human is formally involved; and the law's emphasis on the final step misleads. The article is doctrinal and analytical rather than empirical. Its central result is that the threshold question of what counts as a solely automated decision is difficult to apply, which matters because Article 22 is often read as prohibiting in-scope decisions lacking a legal basis.

## Use in the chapter
Proposed by the evidence audit (U10) for §3.2, where the chapter says of Article 22 that "Its threshold is high, its practice thin" without a source. The audit recommends Binns and Veale for the threshold difficulty and the two CJEU judgments (SCHUFA, Dun & Bradstreet) for the point that "high" is no longer safe, with a reword to "its threshold is contested and its enforcement record thin". Role: support for the threshold clause in §3.2; pairs with cjeu2025c20322 and cjeu2023c63421.

## Key quotations
- "The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her." (Article 22(1) as quoted in the article)
- "These ambiguities are particularly important as Article 22 is often interpreted ... as prohibiting decisions within its scope lacking a legal basis." (body text)
