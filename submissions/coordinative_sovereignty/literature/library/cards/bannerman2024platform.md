## Summary
Bannerman argues that communications law, spanning speech regulation, intellectual property, defamation and privacy, has historically supplied the legal infrastructure through which imperial power operates, and that digital platforms now extend that structure as a form of platform imperialism. Rather than asking whether law can keep pace with technological change, she asks whether the ideas of autonomy and sovereignty at the centre of platform and technology regulation are themselves adequate. Drawing on feminist, decolonial and critical race theory, she proposes a relational conception of sovereignty, in which sovereign standing is constituted through relations with others rather than through independence from them. The article is conceptual and historical rather than empirical. Its central claim is that rebalancing the structural asymmetry between platforms and those they govern requires reconceiving sovereignty as relational, prioritising voices the current order renders invisible, and repairing inequitable power relations embedded in existing legal systems rather than adjusting those systems at the margin.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §6: "Bannerman (2024) reads platform power through a relational and historical lens." The chapter places her beside Pasquale's functional sovereignty and Cohen's platform sovereign as an account of the authority platforms exercise, then distinguishes coordinative sovereignty as the standing of the coordinated within that authority. Strand 7 records that "relational sovereignty" is Bannerman's term, so the chapter should keep its own term distinct from hers. Role: support for the relational framing of sovereignty in §6.
