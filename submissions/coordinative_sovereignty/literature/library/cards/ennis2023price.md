## Summary
Ennis, Ivaldi and Lagos measure the effect of most-favoured-nation or price-parity clauses on hotel prices by exploiting two natural experiments in the vertical contracts between hotels and the major online travel agencies. The first is the EU-wide narrowing of wide parity to narrow parity in 2015; the second is the complete elimination of parity clauses for the top OTAs by France and Germany. Using transaction data from hotel chains, they find that after the interventions direct sales by hotels became relatively cheaper than OTA sales for mid-level and luxury hotels, with an opposite pattern for budget hotels; comparisons with hotels outside the EU confirm the relative reduction. Regulating the clauses thus produced significantly cheaper direct-channel sales in two of three hotel types. Their most pointed result is that the primary effects come from the narrow-parity intervention rather than from the later complete elimination, which suggests that once wide parity fell most of the price gain had already been taken.

## Use in the chapter
Section 5.5 narrates a decade of European action against parity clauses (2015 abandonment of wide parity, national bans, the 2021 narrowing, the 2024 C-264/23 judgment) and reads the rise in OTA share of European overnight stays (Schegg 2024) as evidence that opening the contingent gate did not reduce dependence. Strand 6 and the gaps report list this paper as a MUST because it supplies the outcome evidence the chapter currently lacks: prices on the direct channel did fall when the gate opened, so the contingent reading is confirmed on price, while the necessary function (aggregation) held on volume. Role: support, with the caution that its budget-hotel result runs the other way and that it measures price, not channel share.
