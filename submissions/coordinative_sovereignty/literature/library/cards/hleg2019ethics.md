## Summary
The European Commission's expert group defines trustworthy AI as lawful, ethical and robust and sets out seven requirements, among them human agency and oversight, transparency, and accountability with redress. It names the power asymmetry "between employers and employees, businesses and consumers or governments and citizens" (p. 12) and the people "for whom decisions are made by an AI system" without their being users (p. 23). Procedural fairness, in its words, needs "the ability to contest and seek effective redress", which requires that "the entity accountable for the decision must be identifiable" (p. 13). The document is soft law and states that it creates no legal rights or obligations (p. 6).

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the chapter. It is the source the transparency-first literature maps onto, and it helps §3 from both sides. It grants the affected person a right to contest, to "demand a suitable explanation" (p. 18) and to redress, yet it places the override with the humans who operate the system (p. 16), so the person holds no standing inside the mediation. That fits the chapter's claim that "absent procedural standing, information disclosure yields no actionable accountability" (§3). Its own line that "transparency cannot prevent non-discrimination or ensure fairness" (p. 34) supports the claim. Role: a construct to position against, credited for naming contest and redress.

## Key quotations
"The procedural dimension of fairness entails the ability to contest and seek effective redress against decisions made by AI systems and by the humans operating them." (p. 13)

"Certification can however never replace responsibility." (p. 23)

"Note that transparency cannot prevent non-discrimination or ensure fairness, and is not the panacea against the problem of scoring." (p. 34)
