## Summary
Street's monograph challenges the assumption, shared across psychology, linguistics and anthropology, that literacy is a neutral technical skill with uniform cognitive and social consequences. He labels this the autonomous model and traces it through Goody, Olson, Hildyard and others. Against it he proposes an ideological model: literacy consists of socially situated practices whose meaning and effects depend on the institutions and power relations in which they are embedded, so there are literacies rather than literacy. The empirical core is his fieldwork in an Iranian village in the 1970s, where maktab (Quranic school) literacy, commercial literacy developed by fruit traders during an economic boom, and state-school literacy coexisted with different uses and statuses. Later chapters examine adult literacy campaigns by UNESCO and Western agencies and argue that they import the autonomous model. The book founded the New Literacy Studies and remains the standard citation for the socially embedded view.

## Use in the chapter
Section 4 cites it with Scribner and Cole (1981) for the claim that the strong literacy thesis "did not survive the ethnographers," and then turns Street's caution to the chapter's advantage: "there is no single algorithmacy any more than there is a single literacy." Role: support. Audit S14 and G8 recommend softening "did not survive" to "was cut back by," adding Halverson (1992) on the implosion of the literacy thesis, and inserting one sentence in §2 acknowledging that the Goody and Ong great divide is contested (Street, Finnegan 1988, Halverson). Collin (2013) and Collin and Street (2014) record the later debate over whether Street's critique discarded what was sound in Goody.
