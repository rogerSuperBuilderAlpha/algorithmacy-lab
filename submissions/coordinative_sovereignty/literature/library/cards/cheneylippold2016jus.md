## Summary
The article begins from the NSA documents released in 2013 and asks how citizenship can be evaluated in a networked world in which the state cannot see persons directly. Its answer is that the NSA built an algorithmic, data-based version of citizenship and foreignness: a user is treated as legally foreign when his or her selectors, such as telephone numbers, IP addresses or language, are judged foreign at 51 percent confidence or more. Those selectors become the effective arbiters of citizenship online. Cheney-Lippold names this jus algoritmi, by analogy with jus soli and jus sanguinis, and argues that it replaces a formal political status with a statistical inference from communications data, revisable with each new datum. The article explains what algorithmic citizenship means, describes what the NSA's categories look like in practice, and draws out the implications of grounding a formal rubric of U.S. citizenship exclusively in algorithmic readings of data.
