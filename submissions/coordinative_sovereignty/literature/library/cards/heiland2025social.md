## Summary
Heiland examines two order-allocation algorithms at Foodora and Deliveroo through interviews, ethnography, and analysis of courier chats, asking what role workers' mental models play in how algorithmic management works. Against the view of algorithms as fixed objects, the paper argues they are ontogenetic and socially constructed: they become effective only through use, so the theories workers hold about them shape their effects. Couriers develop theories of the algorithms' mode of operation from assumption and experience, framed by a hermeneutics of suspicion, and typically conclude that order assignment tracks performance and compliance. These theories are objectively wrong, yet they discipline conduct: riders respond with anticipatory obedience to a control that is presumed rather than real, and the platforms leave the misunderstanding uncorrected. Opacity thus functions as a control instrument in its own right, which Heiland calls black box power, while the algorithms' ontogenetic character still leaves workers limited but real agency.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the chapter's current text. The sweep proposes it for §4 and §9 as a contesting source. §4's worked example has the seller inferring "what the system rewards from the movement of her own listings", and §9 proposes anchoring an algorithmacy instrument to "the accuracy of an actor's predictions" of system behavior. Heiland contests the accuracy link: couriers' folk theories are often false yet still discipline conduct, so inference need not be accurate to produce compliance, and a behavioral-accuracy criterion would miss the competence-like practices that false theories sustain. The chapter should either narrow the criterion or treat accuracy as one dimension among several.
