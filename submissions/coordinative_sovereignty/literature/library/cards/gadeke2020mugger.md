## Summary
Gädeke starts from a puzzle for republican theory: a mugger has an uncontrolled capacity to interfere arbitrarily with his victim, yet calling the mugging domination seems wrong, and excluding it by requiring a standing relationship seems ad hoc. Her solution is that domination is a structurally constituted form of power. Paradigm cases such as traditional marriage and slavery show that the dominator's capacity to interfere is created by legal institutions and informal norms that systematically empower some and disempower others; physical superiority is only a resource that structures turn into dominating power. Episodic exercises of power therefore count as domination only when the power exercised is itself structurally enabled; a mugger who relies on no such norms is a physical threat but does not dominate. She distinguishes the capacity question from the arbitrariness question (a judge's power is structurally constituted but may be controlled) and extends the account to systemic domination in which no single agent holds the power.

## Use in the chapter
The audit proposes it twice for §6. U20: the chapter raises "a ranking system is not an agent, so its power may not be arbitrary" as a standing objection attributed to no one; Gädeke (with Hayward 2011) can be cited as the literature that takes the agency objection seriously and answers it. S22: the chapter relies on Cicerchia (2022) alone for structural domination not requiring an agent; Gädeke is the second source, since her account makes the structure rather than the agent the bearer of the dominating capacity. Role: second source and, through the mugger case, a test for whether platform ranking power is structurally constituted (it is: terms of service, contract law and network effects) rather than episodic.

## Key quotations
"Domination, I propose, is best conceived of as generally characterized by the structurally constituted capacity to interfere (arbitrarily)." (section II.A)
"episodic forms of power are only instances of domination when the relevant power is itself structurally enabled. If they are based on a mere opportunistic capacity to interfere, they are not cases of domination" (section II, conclusion)
"Not every structurally constituted capacity to interfere is dominating. The power of a judge is structurally constituted, yet whether it is subject to effective controls, and thus whether it is dominating, is a separate matter." (section II.A, note)
