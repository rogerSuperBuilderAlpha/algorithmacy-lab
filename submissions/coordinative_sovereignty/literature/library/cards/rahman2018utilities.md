## Summary
Rahman argues that debates over too-big-to-fail banks, net neutrality, and online platforms are one debate about how to conceptualise and regulate new forms of concentrated private power, particularly where firms control the terms of access to services on which communities and economic actors depend. Drawing on Progressive Era ideas of private power and public utility, he argues that what makes such firms distinctive is private control over infrastructural goods that form a backbone for social and economic activity. He then sets out three elements of a twenty-first-century public utility framework: firewalling (structural separation), imposing public obligations (non-discrimination, access, fair terms), and creating public options. Applied to platforms such as Google, Amazon, and Uber, these concepts offer a portable, trans-substantive framework for contesting private power. The piece is a work of legal and political theory, and it reorients business law toward questions of power and inequality.
