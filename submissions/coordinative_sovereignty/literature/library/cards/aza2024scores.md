## Summary
The article examines the consequences of the Court of Justice's judgment in SCHUFA Holding (Scoring) for labour law. In SCHUFA the Court held that a probability value produced by automated profiling can itself be a "decision" within Article 22 GDPR when it produces legal or similarly significant effects for the person, because a third party draws strongly on the score. Aza carries that reasoning into employment, where algorithmic management increasingly generates scores and rankings that shape recruitment, allocation of work, evaluation and termination. The argument is that if scores count as decisions, then Article 22's prohibition in principle, and its rights to human intervention and contestation, reach much further into algorithmic management than has been assumed, including into systems where a human formally signs off. The piece is doctrinal, reading the judgment against the structure of Article 22 and the labour-law literature on algorithmic management, and it concludes that the decision materially expands protection for workers subject to scoring.

## Use in the chapter
