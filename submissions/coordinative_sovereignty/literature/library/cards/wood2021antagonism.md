## Summary
The article asks why gig workers who see themselves as self-employed freelancers nonetheless engage in collective action of the kind associated with regular employment. Using ethnographic evidence from the remote gig economy in North America, the United Kingdom and the Philippines, the authors argue that labour platforms increase workers' agency to contract with clients, letting them decline jobs and set terms, and so reduce the risk of false self-employment in the worker-client relation. In doing so, however, platforms create a new source of subordination to the platform itself. The authors name this subordinated agency and show that it entails a structured antagonism with the platform that surfaces in three areas: fees, competition and worker voice mechanisms. Subordinated agency generates a desire for representation, greater voice and even unionisation directed at the platform, while leaving entrepreneurial attitudes toward clients intact. The paper thereby relocates the site of labour conflict from the employment relation to the platform relation.
