## Summary
Rahman and Thelen explain the changing nature of twenty-first-century capitalism by attending to the political coalitions and institutional conditions that support it, rather than to markets and technology alone. They trace the transformation of the twentieth-century consolidated firm, through the firm conceived as a network of contracts, to the platform firm, and argue that political forces drove each step. Placing the United States in comparative perspective, they show how distinctive US political-economic institutions, including weak labour protections, permissive antitrust and the alignment of consumers and investors against workers, facilitated the platform model and exacerbated the inequalities that came with it. The platform firm is presented as a political as much as an economic achievement, sustained by a coalition of consumers drawn by convenience and investors drawn by patient capital, with workers and dependent suppliers bearing the costs of the arrangement.
