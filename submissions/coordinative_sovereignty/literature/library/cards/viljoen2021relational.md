## Summary
Viljoen argues that the data practices of powerful technology companies aim primarily at population-level, relational insights rather than insights about any one data subject, and that those insights are then applied to everyone who shares the relevant population features. This economic motivation matters conceptually for law. Regimes built on individual rights, consent, access, deletion, cannot address harms that arise from what is inferred about groups and applied to third parties who never transacted with the collector. Data governance law, she contends, misdescribes its object when it treats data as an individual possession, because the value and the harm of data are relational: what is learned from one person's data is learned about others. The article's normative proposal is to move from individual data-subject rights toward more democratic institutions of data governance, collective institutional forms of ordering in which the people affected by population-level inferences have a say in the terms on which data are collected and used. The argument is theoretical and legal rather than empirical.
