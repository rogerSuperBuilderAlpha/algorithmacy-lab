## Summary
Teh and Wright model an informed, profit-maximizing intermediary that steers consumers by recommending firms, taking into account both the commissions firms offer it and the prices they set. Steering of this kind raises commissions and consumer prices, so consumers gain from intermediation only when their own search costs are high enough that the intermediary's recommendation is still worth its markup. The model produces a striking reversal of the usual relation between competition and price: with a steering intermediary, prices rise with the number of competing firms, because more firms compete harder for the recommendation by offering higher commissions, which are passed through. The authors then use the framework to evaluate policies aimed at such markets, including absolute and relative commission caps, commission disclosure, measures that promote information provision, and penalties for inappropriate advice. The paper is theoretical and is one of the core models of "interested mediation" in the economics of intermediaries.
