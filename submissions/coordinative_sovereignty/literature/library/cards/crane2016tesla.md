## Summary
Crane analyses the state-by-state fight between Tesla Motors and the car dealers' lobby, supported by the incumbent Detroit manufacturers, over Tesla's right to sell its cars directly to consumers. He traces the history of automobile dealer franchise laws, which began as protection for dealers against opportunism by the manufacturers who had franchised them and are now on the books in all fifty states, and shows how laws written to govern a manufacturer's relations with its own dealers have been extended to bar direct distribution by a manufacturer that never had dealers. The article's central claim is that incumbent technologies can block new ones by erecting legal barriers to the innovative distribution methods a new technology needs for market acceptance, that judicial review of such special-interest capture is weak in the post-Lochner era, and that the Tesla wars are creating new political alliances against crony capitalism. Crane concedes that dealers can lower a manufacturer's distribution costs and hold local market knowledge, and that service margins exceed sales margins.
