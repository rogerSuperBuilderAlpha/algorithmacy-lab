## Summary
Rousseau's treatise asks how political authority can be legitimate given that people are born free, and answers with the social contract: each associate alienates all rights to the whole community and receives in return membership in a collective body, the sovereign, whose will is the general will. Book I moves from the rejection of force and of natural subjection (chapters 1 to 4) through the act of association (chapter 6), which creates a "moral and collective body" with its own unity, identity, life and will, to the account of the sovereign (chapter 7) as the body in which political power ought always to reside. Later books develop the inalienability and indivisibility of sovereignty, the distinction between the general will and the will of all, the theory of law as the sovereign's declaration of the general will, and the separation of sovereign from government. For the chapter's purposes the relevant inheritance is the idea that sovereignty is the standing of members of a collective body over the terms that govern them, not a property of the ruler.
