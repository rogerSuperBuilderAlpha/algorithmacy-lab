## Summary
Skinner's extended essay, developed from his inaugural lecture as Regius Professor of Modern History at Cambridge, recovers what he calls the neo-Roman conception of liberty in early-modern Britain. On that view, drawn from Roman law and the republican writers of the seventeenth century, a person or a state is unfree not only when interfered with but when living in dependence on the will of another, since the mere possibility of arbitrary interference is itself a condition of servitude. He traces how this understanding was eclipsed by the liberal conception of liberty as non-interference and argues that its defeat was a choice rather than a discovery. The book closes with a reflection on the value of intellectual history as a discipline: by showing that our present values reflect choices made at different times between possible worlds, the historian can loosen the hold of the vocabulary we now take for granted. It is the historical companion to Pettit's systematic account of freedom as non-domination.

## Use in the chapter
Section 6 cites it with Pettit: "republican theory, takes freedom as non-domination, the absence of another's arbitrary power, a stronger condition than the absence of interference (Pettit, 1997; Skinner, 1998)." Role: support for the first theoretical strand of coordinative sovereignty, the claim that a platform-dependent actor left alone but living at the mercy of a power that could interfere at will is unfree. Skinner supplies the historical pedigree and the dependence formulation; the agency objection (a ranking system is not a will) is answered in the chapter through Cicerchia 2022 and, per strand 4, Hasan 2021.
