## Summary
Evrard traces the essential facilities doctrine in EU competition law through the Court of Justice's 1998 Oscar Bronner judgment and its aftermath. The doctrine imposes on the owner of an essential facility a duty to deal with competitors, an exception to the general freedom to choose one's trading partners. Evrard argues that Bronner narrowed the doctrine substantially: access must be indispensable, meaning there is no actual or potential substitute and duplication is not economically viable for a competitor of comparable scale, not merely convenient. He reads Bronner as restricting the doctrine to situations where the facility owner holds more than a simple dominant position, and as importing a forward-looking concern with preserving incentives to invest, closer to American monopolization analysis than to a broad abuse-of-dominance duty. The article surveys the case law before and after Bronner and assesses when refusal to grant access remains abusive under Article 102.
