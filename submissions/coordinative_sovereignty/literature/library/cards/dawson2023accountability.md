## Summary
Dawson asks what accountability requires of powerful private actors in digital governance and which normative goods it serves. He builds a general framework around four goods: openness, non-arbitrariness, effectiveness and publicness, and shows that demands for digital accountability usually point to a deficit in one or more of them. The argument is that while lawyers have rightly concentrated on tying digital giants to rule-of-law guarantees, accountability is a distinct and demanding yardstick that lets citizens contest decisions beyond strict legality, since an actor is accountable not by discharging an institutional duty but by showing before some forum that its decisions were open, limited, effective and public. The framework is meant to be both analytical, disaggregating what different claimants are asking for, and normative, indicating how deficits might be remedied. Applications include platforms over-removing content when made to police it, discrimination in the sharing economy, and the privatization of dispute resolution by oversight boards.
