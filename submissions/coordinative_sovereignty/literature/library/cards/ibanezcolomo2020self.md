## Summary
Ibáñez Colomo argues that self-preferencing, now central to competition-law debate about digital markets, is not a coherent category of abuse. Favouring one's own affiliate is ordinary competitive conduct that follows from the benefits of integration, and the label lacks a clear legal or economic foundation: it can cover conduct as different as classic tying, refusals to deal, and interventions in product design and business models. He presses two concerns. First, if indispensability (the refusal-to-deal standard) is abandoned as the limiting principle, the scope of intervention becomes open-ended and reaches the design choices of any vertically integrated firm. Second, the effects analysis that EU abuse doctrine requires must be preserved rather than replaced by a presumption that self-favouring harms competition. The article is doctrinal, written against the background of Google Shopping and the then-forthcoming Digital Markets Act.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the current draft. Strand 2 and the gaps file list it under Contests for §5.2 and §8, where the chapter treats ranking and self-preferencing as a lever of interested mediation and reads the DMA's self-preferencing ban as a response. The role is contests: Ibáñez Colomo denies that self-preferencing is a stable object for law, which bears on the chapter's claim that the gate has migrated from contract into the algorithm and on its §8 reliance on the Apple and Amazon proceedings. The chapter can absorb the objection by noting that coordinative sovereignty asks about standing to contest a ranking rather than whether a given ranking is an abuse, which sidesteps the limiting-principles problem.
