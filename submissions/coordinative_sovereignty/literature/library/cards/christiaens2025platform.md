## Summary
Christiaens argues that political philosophy has paid less attention to emancipatory solutions for gig workers than to diagnosing their situation. The two common proposals, stronger bargaining power through unionisation and better exit options in the labour market, each carry side effects and neither exhausts the potential for worker self-government that digital platforms make possible. Working within the republican conception of freedom as non-domination, he revives G. D. H. Cole's guild socialism as the relevant tradition: platform companies rely on relational and structural domination to undermine worker autonomy, and guild socialism was designed to secure autonomy in the workplace itself. Applied today, Cole's programme amounts to a defence of worker-owned cooperative platforms, which put workers in control of platform design and governance. The article closes by taking seriously the objections that platform cooperativism must answer if it is to become a realistic alternative, including the Michels and Luxemburg lines of criticism about oligarchy and about reform within a capitalist market.

## Use in the chapter
Section 6 currently rests the claim that algorithmic management is domination, and that "domination can survive a change of owner," on Muldoon and Raekstad (2023) alone; the audit (S21) proposes Christiaens as the second source. Role: second source for the republican reading of platform power and for the chapter's point that cooperative ownership "can change who sets a necessary mediator's rules, but it cannot make the mediator leaveable." His treatment of the objections to cooperatives gives the chapter an independent statement of the managerial-decay worry. Note that the abstract frames the argument around Cole and guild socialism, so an in-text cite should attach to the non-domination and cooperative-ownership claims rather than to algorithmic management as such.
