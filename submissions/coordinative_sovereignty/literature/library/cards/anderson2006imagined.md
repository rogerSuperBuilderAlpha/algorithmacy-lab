## Summary
Anderson's argument, as far as the publisher blurb opened this session allows, is that the nation is an imagined political community, a form of belonging that compels people to kill and die for an idea, and that this form originated not in Europe but in the creole societies of the Americas before being adopted and transformed by nineteenth-century European populist movements. The mechanism that the chapter relies on, the rise of print capitalism, which let readers of a vernacular newspaper or novel imagine thousands of unseen others moving through the same calendrical time, belongs to the book's central chapters and was not opened this session, so it is reported here from the chapter's own use rather than from text verified today. The revised edition adds a chapter on the census, map and museum as instruments through which colonial states shaped national imagination, and a 2006 afterword on the book's reception.

## Use in the chapter
Cited once in §2.2: "the print-borne imagining of a shared community underwrote the democratic nation-state (B. Anderson, 1983/2006)". The role is support for the literacy-to-democratic-sovereignty link, placed beside Habermas on the reading public. The audit (G9, O3) flags that this literacy-democracy link is contested by Graff's literacy-myth work and sits in tension with Paglayan as cited in §9, so Anderson carries the positive half of a claim the chapter should hedge. Strand 5 proposes Goody and Watt (1963) as the origin of the literate-democracy claim and Vesting (2018) for the media stages of legal authority; Anderson is the print-capitalism step between them.

## Key quotations
"What are the imagined communities that compel men to kill or to die for an idea of a nation? This notion of nationhood had its origins in the founding of the Americas, but was then adopted and transformed by populist movements in nineteenth-century Europe." (Verso publisher blurb)
