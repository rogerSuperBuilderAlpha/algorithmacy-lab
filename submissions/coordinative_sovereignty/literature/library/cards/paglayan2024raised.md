## Summary
The book asks why Western governments decided to provide primary education to all children, and answers that the expansion was not mainly a response to industrialisation, not driven by democratic ideals, and not aimed primarily at literacy or skills. It was motivated by elites' fear of the masses and by the wish to turn the children of the lower classes into future citizens who would obey the state and its laws. Drawing on two centuries of evidence on education provision across Europe and the Americas, with data on the timing and character of primary-school expansion, Paglayan shows that governments invested in primary schooling when internal threats, episodes of violence and disorder, raised elites' anxiety about the breakdown of social order. Schooling therefore preceded and was not caused by democratisation. The book closes by arguing that the original disciplinary purpose remains at the core of how most public schools operate today. It belongs to the Princeton Economic History of the Western World series and builds on the author's earlier quantitative work.

## Use in the chapter
