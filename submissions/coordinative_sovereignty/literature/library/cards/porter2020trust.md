## Summary
Porter's book is a foundational work in the historical and social study of quantification. It asks why quantitative methods came to carry such prestige, first in the sciences and then across business, government and social research, and argues that the answer lies in politics rather than in epistemology. Quantitative rigor, on his account, is not inherent in science; it is adopted under social and political pressure, above all where experts lack the standing or trust to have their judgment accepted and so substitute rules and numbers for discretion. Objectivity in the sense of impersonal, mechanical procedure is a response to distrust and to the need to communicate across distance and between strangers, which is why it flourishes in bureaucracies and in public accounting more than among secure elites. The 2020 edition reissues the 1995 text with a new preface reflecting on the intervening rise of metrics.
