## Summary
Buhmann and Fieseler split epistemic concerns about AI into strategic, expert and true opacity. Strategic opacity is deliberate, with motives that include "avoiding accountability and evading regulations" (p. 151). Expert opacity can come from disclosure itself when it overwhelms citizens with information (p. 151). Drawing on political CSR, they argue that AI firms are obliged to take part in deliberation and propose distributed deliberation across expert venues, the public sphere and mini-publics. They also warn that corporate engagement can be "merely a means of deflection" (p. 167).

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the chapter. It supports §3's claim that "absent procedural standing, information disclosure yields no actionable accountability" (§3). Disclosure can itself produce opacity (p. 151), and "the impossibility of attaining complete 'AI transparency' can be used as an excuse" (p. 157). Their remedy sits at the level of publics and venues rather than with the coordinated person, so it supports the diagnosis but not the chapter's remedy. Role: support for the diagnosis.

## Key quotations
"Expert opacity can also arise inadvertently through attempts at disclosure and transparency that overwhelm citizens" (p. 151).

"the impossibility of attaining complete 'AI transparency' can be used as an excuse for organizations not to fulfill ethical duties" (p. 157).

"Normative stakeholder engagement in this case would thus constitute merely a means of deflection." (p. 167)
