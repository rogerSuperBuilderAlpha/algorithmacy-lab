## Summary
The article asks whether individual digital sovereignty, the EU's framing of digital sovereignty as something users obtain by being informed about how applications work and choosing what personal data to share, is attainable. The method is a qualitative thematic comparison of selected EU and Chinese legislation on AI and data, chosen because platforms and data are the two realms in which the individual dimension of digital sovereignty is at stake. The comparison finds that the EU's individual-level measures closely resemble measures adopted in China under a very different political system, which undercuts the claim that they express a distinctive European conception of autonomy, and that the EU's limited capacity to make its interests prevail outside its territory, given the scarcity of EU platform giants, limits its ability to deliver on the promise. The conclusion is that the promise cannot be kept by regulation alone without a strong domestic industry; the authors caution against generalising beyond AI and data governance and note that the Data Act and Data Governance Act were too new to assess.
