## Summary
Boik and Corts model sellers who reach consumers through intermediary platforms and who sign platform most-favored-nation clauses, contractual promises not to sell at a lower price through any rival platform. They motivate the model with the e-book and travel cases and ask what such clauses do to platform fees, retail prices, and entry. The central result is that platform MFNs typically raise platform fees and retail prices, because a platform that raises its fee no longer loses sales to a cheaper rival channel once the seller is bound to price uniformly, and that the clauses curtail entry or skew the positioning of would-be entrants pursuing low-fee, low-price business models. The paper is theoretical and belongs to the wave of work that gave competition authorities a mechanism for treating parity clauses as harmful to inter-platform price competition. It provides the baseline economic account against which the narrow-versus-wide distinction and the later natural experiments are read.

Role: support for the claim that the parity clause is a gate rather than integrating work, by supplying the economic reason parity clauses raise fees and block low-fee entrants.
