## Summary
This edited collection set the agenda for the relational autonomy literature. Its premise, as the publisher describes it, is that individual autonomy has social and relational dimensions that mainstream accounts of autonomy neglect. The contributors reject the feminist charge that autonomy is an inherently masculinist ideal and instead use feminist perspectives to reconstruct the concept, examining how oppressive social relationships can impair the capacity for autonomy and how supportive ones can constitute it. The essays also explore connections between autonomy and self-conception, self-worth, memory and imagination, and bear on wider philosophical debates about agency, identity and moral responsibility. The shared claim is that autonomy is not a property an individual has prior to relationships but something developed and exercised within them, so that the shape of a relationship, not its mere presence, is what enlarges or diminishes autonomy.

## Use in the chapter
Section 6 cites it: "relational autonomy, holds that autonomy is constituted through the relationships that structure a person (Mackenzie & Stoljar, 2000; Nedelsky, 2011)." The chapter uses it to dissolve the apparent choice between being mediated and being autonomous: a well-shaped mediation enlarges the coordinated actor's autonomy and a badly shaped one diminishes it, so the object of sovereignty is the shape. Role: support for the second theoretical strand behind coordinative sovereignty. The chapter applies a concept developed for persons to firms and other organizational actors, which is an extension the card reader should note; Viljoen 2021 (relational theory of data governance) is the nearest bridge.
