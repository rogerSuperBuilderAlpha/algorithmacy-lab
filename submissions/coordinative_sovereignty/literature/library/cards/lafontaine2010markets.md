## Summary
Lafontaine and Scott Morton use the 2008 to 2009 auto crisis, when General Motors and Chrysler announced plans to terminate about 2,200 dealerships, to examine the US system of state franchise laws governing the relation between car manufacturers and their dealers. The laws, enacted state by state from the mid-twentieth century, restrict manufacturers' ability to terminate dealers, to add new dealers in an existing dealer's territory, and to sell directly to consumers. The authors argue that these protections raise distribution costs and retail prices and lock in a dealer network sized for an earlier market, burdens that fell especially on the Detroit manufacturers with their older and denser networks. They trace how the laws shaped the manufacturers' options during the crisis and the bankruptcy process, and they consider alternative distribution arrangements, including direct sales, that might serve consumers better. The piece is an economics survey, written for a general audience, rather than an econometric study.
