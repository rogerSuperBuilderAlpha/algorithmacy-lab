## Summary
Hagiu and Jullien model an information intermediary that lets consumers search among affiliated stores, knows more than consumers about which store matches them, and earns a fee from each store visit. They ask when such an intermediary profits from not maximizing the quality of its search service, that is, from diverting search so that consumers visit more stores than they would choose. Two motives emerge. First, consumers do not internalize the surplus their visits create for stores, so they search too little from the intermediary's point of view; adding noise raises visits per consumer at the cost of total traffic. Second, diversion is an instrument for disciplining stores' strategic choices, especially prices, because altering the composition of each store's demand can force prices down and raise consumer traffic overall. Diversion disappears only if the intermediary can charge consumers access fees, subsidize their second search, and control and commit to store prices at once. Since most intermediaries lack that set of instruments, diversion is a pervasive and resilient strategic tool, not eliminated by competition among intermediaries.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 2 and the gaps report list it as a MUST for §3.3, where the chapter argues that a platform mediator "reads both sides and pursues objectives set by a firm that neither of them works for." Hagiu and Jullien supply the formal economics of interested mediation: the matching function is degraded on purpose because the intermediary's revenue and its wish to govern the stores' conduct diverge from the consumer's interest. Role: support. Together with de Cornière and Taylor 2019 (biased intermediation) and Teh 2022 (governance distorted by fee structure), it lets §3.3 say that interested mediation is an equilibrium property of the form rather than a malfunction.

## Key quotations
"We identify two original motives for diverting search (i.e., inducing consumers to search more than they would like): (i) trading off higher total consumer traffic for higher revenues per consumer visit; and (ii) influencing stores' choices of strategic variables (e.g., pricing)." (Abstract)
"our analysis suggests that search diversion is an essential strategic instrument for intermediaries and its use should be widespread." (Conclusion, section 6)
"Furthermore, our analysis implies that some search diversion may be required for welfare maximization, and that it is not necessarily eliminated by competition among intermediaries." (Introduction, section 1)
