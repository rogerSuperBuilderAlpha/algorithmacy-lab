## Summary
Osiander observes that the 350th anniversary of the Peace of Westphalia passed almost unnoticed in international relations, even though the discipline treats 1648 as the birth of the state system it studies, while at the same time debating whether the "Westphalian system" is ending. Such debates imply historical comparison, and Osiander's contention is that IR judges current trends against a past that is largely imaginary, a product of the nineteenth- and twentieth-century fixation on sovereignty. Examining the Thirty Years' War, the 1648 treaties, the post-1648 Holy Roman Empire and the European system in which it was embedded, he shows that the treaties did not establish sovereign territorial statehood in the sense later attributed to them, and that the Empire remained a layered, non-sovereign order for a further century and a half. He calls the resulting distortion the ideology of sovereignty and argues it has hampered IR theory; a more accurate reading of the early modern order, he suggests, may help in understanding contemporary international politics.
