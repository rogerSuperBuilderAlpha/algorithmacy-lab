## Summary
Fourcade's 2020 BJS lecture argues that the twentieth-century expansion of social citizenship, which blunted the effects of economic inequality, is being succeeded by a form of inclusion organised around measurement. New rights created new divisions by sorting citizens according to their ability to do well through them; in the twenty-first century, digital technologies have multiplied the possibilities for classifying, sorting, slotting and scaling people in every domain of life, from credit and employment to health and social media. Echoing the earlier rise of meritocracy, this regime produces its own winners and losers, partly recycling old inequalities and partly creating new ones, while demands for self-care and individual fitness erode the universal and solidaristic basis on which social citizenship rested. Fourcade names the result ordinal citizenship: social inclusion that thrives on measurement, differentiation and hierarchy, and in which a person's standing is inferred continuously by scoring systems rather than conferred by status. The essay is theoretical and synthetic.
