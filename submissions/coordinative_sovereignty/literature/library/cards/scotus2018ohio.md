## Summary
The Court held, five to four, that American Express's antisteering provisions in its merchant contracts do not violate Section 1 of the Sherman Act. The provisions prohibit merchants from implying a preference for non-Amex cards, dissuading customers from using Amex, persuading them to use other cards, imposing special conditions or fees on Amex cards, or promoting other cards more than Amex. The majority characterised credit-card networks as two-sided transaction platforms that serve cardholders and merchants simultaneously, so that the relevant market must include both sides and the plaintiffs had to show net anticompetitive effects across the whole platform. Evidence that merchant fees rose was held insufficient without showing that the price of transactions as a whole exceeded a competitive level or that output fell. The opinion notes that the provisions do not prevent merchants from steering customers toward debit cards, checks or cash. The dissent (Breyer, J.) rejected the two-sided market framing.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Audit U6 asks for it as the authority behind §5.4's example of a payment network that "enforces a rule against steering the customer toward a cheaper method at the till", which the chapter classifies as a contingent gate. The audit notes that the provisions did not bar steering to cash or debit, so "cheaper method" is accurate only if a cheaper card is meant. Role: legal instrument; it also supplies the two-sided-platform framing that the chapter's function-split diagnostic is implicitly arguing against.
