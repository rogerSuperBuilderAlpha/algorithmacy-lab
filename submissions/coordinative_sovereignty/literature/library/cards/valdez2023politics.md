## Summary
Against depictions of platform firms as holders of structural and instrumental power who prevail in regulatory battles, Valdez documents how they have often adapted to regulation and provide different services across locales. Platform firms hold a specific type of power, infrastructural power, that stems from their position as mediators across a variety of actors: consumers, workers, allied organizations, and governments that come to depend on the service. This power is shaped by pre-existing regulation and by the firm's strategy of "contentious compliance", a double movement of adapting to existing rules while continuing to challenge them. Comparative case studies of Uber in New York City, Madrid, and Berlin show the mechanism: weak prior regulation let Uber grow an ecosystem and accumulate infrastructural power in NYC, while stricter regimes in Madrid and Berlin capped its growth and left it unable to block re-regulation. Notably, NYC regulators eventually turned Uber's infrastructural power against it, justifying caps and a driver pay floor by the harm to those who depend on the platform.

## Use in the chapter
Strand 7 proposes it for §3.3 and §8 as a construct to position coordinative sovereignty against; not yet cited in the body. Role: position_against. "Infrastructural power" is Mann's term, applied to platforms by Valdez and by Rahman and Thelen, and it names the mediator's power over states and publics that accrues from becoming the infrastructure others depend on. The chapter looks at the same mediation from the coordinated actor's side: Valdez explains what the platform's position does to regulators, coordinative sovereignty asks what standing those coordinated through the position retain. The NYC episode, where dependence became the regulator's lever, is a usable bridge for §8.
