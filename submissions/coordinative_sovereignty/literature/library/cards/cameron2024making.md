## Summary
Cameron asks how the shift from human managers to algorithmic managers on digital platforms manufactures workplace consent. Most prior research expected the task standardisation and surveillance of algorithmic management to produce the quintessential bad job, yet many workers report liking the work and finding choice in it. Drawing on a seven-year qualitative study of ride-hailing, the largest sector of the gig economy, she describes how drivers navigate being managed by an algorithm and identifies two sets of tactics. Engagement tactics follow the algorithmic nudges without trying to get around the system; deviance tactics manipulate the worker's input into the system. Both are exercised through a constant stream of small, confined choices, and it is the experience of choosing, rather than the scope of what can be chosen, that generates consent. This choice-based consent masks the structurally problematic features of the work and helps explain the popularity of what Cameron calls the "good bad" job.
