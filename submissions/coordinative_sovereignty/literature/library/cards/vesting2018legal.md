## Summary
Vesting's book, a one-volume English condensation of four German monographs on the media of law (speech, writing, print, computer networks), argues that a media and cultural theory perspective is productive for legal theory. Law is treated as an artificial and constructive element within culture, and the book's four parts trace the possibilities that each medium opened to law, from oral custom through scripture and print to digital networks. Drawing on McLuhan, Kittler and Luhmann, Vesting holds that media are the techno-infrastructural conditions of possibility for meaning in law and culture alike, so that transitions between media reconfigure the imaginary and symbolic orders within which legal authority is produced. The chapters on print, for example, reread Bodin's sovereignty as bound to the social rules, institutions and language conventions that preceded the monarch. The closing parts on computer networks argue that the network epistemology of the digital present demands a rethinking of legal theory's own medial assumptions.
