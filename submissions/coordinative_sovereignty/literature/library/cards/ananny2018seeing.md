## Summary
Ananny and Crawford examine the assumption that making a system visible is the same as knowing how it works and being able to govern it. They trace the transparency ideal through its intellectual history, from Enlightenment faith in visibility to contemporary calls for open algorithms, and then set out ten limitations that transparency meets when applied to algorithmic systems: among them that transparency can be disconnected from power, can harm, can create false binaries of secrecy and openness, can invoke neoliberal models of agency, can privilege seeing over understanding, and can be resisted by systems that do not sit still long enough to be seen. The piece is conceptual rather than empirical, working through examples from algorithmic accountability debates. Its central result is that transparency is inadequate as a governing ideal for algorithmic systems on its own; the authors propose instead that accountability be located in the relations among systems and the actors who can act on what is seen.
