## Summary
Clanchy's classic traces how England moved, between the Norman Conquest and the death of Edward I, from a society that relied on memory and oral testimony to one that trusted written records. The third edition, according to the publisher description opened this session, adds a rewritten opening chapter on memories and myths of the Norman Conquest and a postscript in which the author reflects on the book's reception and on later scholarship on medieval literacy. The book covers the proliferation of documents, the technology of writing, the preservation of records, what Clanchy calls the literate mentality, practices of reading, the shifting line between literate and illiterate, and the cultural move toward trusting writing over memory. Its importance for the chapter is the demonstration that lay literacy and the practical use of documents spread well beyond the clergy from the twelfth century, so that literacy in this period was a graded practical competence rather than a clerical monopoly.
