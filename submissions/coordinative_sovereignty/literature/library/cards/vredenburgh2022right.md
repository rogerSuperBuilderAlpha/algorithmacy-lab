## Summary
Vredenburgh argues that individuals have a moral right to explanations of decisions made about them by institutions and algorithms, grounding the right in an interest-based, Scanlonian account on which a right is a constraint on decision-makers' discretion justified because it protects a weighty and widespread interest, protects it better than alternatives, and is tolerably costly. The protected interest is informed self-advocacy: the ability to understand and contest decisions that affect one's life. She distinguishes intentional, causal and normative explanations and argues that the right is best satisfied not by disclosure of the actual decision process, which may be impossible or intolerably costly, but by high-level, rule-based normative explanations and by causal explanations of how people with given properties fare, which decision-makers or free experts can provide. Functional transparency of an algorithm, knowledge of the high-level rules relating inputs to outputs, suffices; structural or run transparency is not required. Existing legal duties to give reasons show that such explanations are tolerably costly.

## Key quotations
Rights are "constraints on the discretion of individuals or institutions to act, which are justified on the grounds that they are necessary and feasible means to prevent unacceptable results that would flow from unlimited discretion." (Section "Rights and explanation", quoting Scanlon 2003, p. 3)
"I will argue that individuals are owed explanations in terms of high-level descriptions of the relevant rules, provided by decision-makers or by free experts." (Section "The justificatory burden")
"The complex algorithm or institutional rules can be used for decision-making, reaping the predictive and other benefits of complexity, while the simpler rules can be used to explain decisions, enabling informed self-advocacy." (Section "The justificatory burden")
