## Summary
Hagiu and Wright model the choice an intermediary faces between operating as a marketplace, where suppliers sell directly to buyers, and operating as a reseller, which buys from suppliers and sells on its own account. They frame the choice as an allocation of control rights over marketing activities. Which mode is optimal turns on who holds the more valuable information for tailoring marketing to a given product: if suppliers know more, marketplace; if the intermediary knows more, reseller. Reseller mode is also favoured when marketing creates spillovers across products or when network effects would otherwise discourage supplier participation. Cost differences cut by product type: a reseller's cost advantage favours marketplace mode for long-tail products and reseller mode for short-tail ones. The paper closes with predictions about which products an intermediary should carry in each mode and some empirical support. It is the standard reference for the hybrid firm that is part marketplace and part reseller.

## Use in the chapter
Not yet cited; strand 2 lists it as SHOULD for §5.4, "reseller function". The chapter's portfolio argument decomposes one marketplace into four mediator kinds and calls the reseller function "a plain conduit that a brand able to reach customers directly can route around, which is reducible". Hagiu and Wright supply the economics of why a single firm runs both modes and when, which is the support the chapter needs for treating "one firm, four kinds of mediator" as a deliberate design rather than an accident. The role is support. It also sets up Farronato et al. (2023) and Hagiu, Teh and Wright (2022), which ask what happens when the reseller competes inside its own marketplace.
