## Summary
Jeitschko and Tremblay build a model of two-sided platform competition in which consumers and firms observe platform prices and then decide whether to single-home, multi-home, or stay out, rather than having their homing fixed by assumption. The competitive-bottleneck allocation, consumers single-homing and firms multi-homing, is always an equilibrium, but so are mixed allocations with both single- and multi-homers on each side, which the authors match to smartphones, game consoles, video streaming and sharing-economy markets. The result that cuts against the received view is on prices: in standard models the multi-homing side faces higher prices because platforms need not compete for it, whereas here lower prices coincide with multi-homing, since agents multi-home only when joining a second platform is cheap. Endogenous homing can also sustain "straddle pricing", one side above and one below marginal cost, which deters undercutting and dampens competition, and it mutes the divide-and-conquer strategy by which a non-focal platform might tip a market.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. The audit (S8, §3.3) recommends noting that Jeitschko and Tremblay "contest the multi-homing-side-pays result" next to the chapter's Armstrong (2006) sentence that each platform "holds a monopoly over access to its own single-homing users and extracts from the multi-homing side, which must pay every platform". The role is contests: once homing is a choice made after prices are set, the extraction story is one equilibrium among several and high prices on the multi-homing side are not guaranteed. The chapter need not abandon the competitive-bottleneck framing, which the paper confirms is always an equilibrium, but the sentence should be hedged from "must pay" to a conditional. Note that the paper itself credits Armstrong and Wright (2007) as the prior endogenous-homing model, so the three sources form one lineage.
