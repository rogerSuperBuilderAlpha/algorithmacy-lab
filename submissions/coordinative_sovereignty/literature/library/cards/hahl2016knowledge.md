## Summary
Hahl, Kacperczyk, and Davis ask when brokers who span structural holes keep the returns to that position, given emerging evidence that the returns vary with whether the brokered parties can credibly threaten to disintermediate the broker and close the hole. They propose that knowledge asymmetry, the condition in which the broker perceives the structural hole but the alters do not, lowers the disintermediation threat and raises the broker's benefits. Using advice-network data from a technology firm, including measures of how accurately individuals perceive the network around them, they find support for the knowledge-asymmetry effect, moderated by the broker's reputation and by whether the alters act as resource providers or resource acquirers. The paper contributes to the network literature by linking perception of network structure to the persistence of positional advantage rather than treating structure alone as the source of brokerage returns.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §5.2: "Hahl et al. (2016) find that brokered parties who do not perceive the gap between them do not attempt to close it, so a structurally contingent mediator can look unassailable in any behavioral record." The audit (S15) flags this as overreach: the source shows that structural holes persist and yield higher returns under knowledge asymmetry; it does not show that unaware parties "do not attempt" closure.
