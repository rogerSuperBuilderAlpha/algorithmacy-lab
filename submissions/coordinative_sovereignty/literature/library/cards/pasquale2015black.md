## Summary
Pasquale's book argues that the most important decisions affecting reputations, credit, livelihoods, and information flows are increasingly made by hidden algorithms operated by finance and internet firms, systems "shrouded in secrecy and complexity" that were long treated as neutral technical arrangements. Corporations silently scrutinize the clues left by our work habits and internet use, and the resulting scoring and ranking systems can make or ruin reputations, decide the destiny of entrepreneurs, or devastate an economy. Drawing on evidence from whistleblowers and litigation, he contends that self-serving and reckless behavior is common and easy to hide in code protected by legal and real secrecy, spanning trade secret law, contractual opacity, and sheer technical complexity. The remedy he proposes is an "intelligible society": meaningful accountability and transparency obligations on Silicon Valley and Wall Street comparable to the scrutiny those industries impose on consumers, so that key decisions are fair, nondiscriminatory, and open to criticism.
