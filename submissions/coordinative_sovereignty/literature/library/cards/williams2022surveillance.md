## Summary
Williams and Raekstad give surveillance capitalism a republican diagnosis. Critics use the language of domination, but, the authors argue, no one had worked out what forms of domination the data economy introduces or what remedies would remove them. Drawing on recent republican theory, they identify two. First, structural domination: inhabitants of technologically advanced societies have no reasonable alternative to giving up their data if they want to use the internet and modern devices, so they are compelled to subject themselves to the uncontrolled power of private corporations. Second, domination over the terms of surveillance: because of the first, users have no control over what is extracted or how it is used, and corporations face incentives of extraction that intensify it. Traditional republican remedies, designed for state and workplace domination, do not fit. The authors therefore outline principles for an "Information Republic": users must be able to opt out of surveillance unilaterally, and users must be able to exert control over the terms of their surveillance, whether through democratic collectivisation of data extraction or individual empowerment to curate it.

## Use in the chapter
Not yet cited. Strand 4 and the gaps report rank it MUST for §6 as the republican treatment of the data side, complementing Muldoon and Raekstad (2023) on algorithmic domination in work. Role: support. The two forms of domination map onto the chapter's distinction: structural domination is the necessary-mediator condition (no reasonable alternative), and domination over the terms is the absence of standing; the authors' second principle, control over the terms of surveillance, is coordinative sovereignty applied to data. The chapter can cite it to show the republican literature already reaches the "terms" formulation, and then say what the coordinated-actor frame adds (the triadic structure and the bypass test).
