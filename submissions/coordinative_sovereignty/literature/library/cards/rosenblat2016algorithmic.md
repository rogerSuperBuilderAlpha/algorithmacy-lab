## Summary
Rosenblat and Stark report a nine-month empirical study of Uber drivers' experiences, drawing on driver forums and interviews, to examine how Uber manages a large, disaggregated workforce through its ride-hail platform while presenting drivers as entrepreneurs who enjoy freedom and flexibility. They find that Uber exercises substantial indirect control over how drivers work. Two conclusions follow. First, the information and power asymmetries the application produces, in surge pricing, blind ride acceptance, rating thresholds and deactivation, are fundamental to the company's ability to structure control over workers without formal command. Second, rhetorical appeals to technology and algorithms are used to structure an asymmetric corporate relation to labor in the company's favor, so that decisions are presented as neutral outputs of a system rather than as management. The authors argue that platform disintermediation, the replacement of a human manager by a software interface, deserves closer attention as a force shaping power relations and communication between employers and workers.

Strand 1 lists it as a MUST and the gaps report calls it "the foundational interested-opaque-mediator study," belonging in §3's canon alongside Lee et al. 2015, which named algorithmic management. The chapter's §3.1 account of the driver coordinating with a rider through a system that commits determinations about routing, pricing, matching and deactivation, and §3.2's claim that the system's opacity is a source of the firm's power, are both anticipated here.
