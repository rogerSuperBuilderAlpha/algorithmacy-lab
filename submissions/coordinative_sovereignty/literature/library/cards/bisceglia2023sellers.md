## Summary
The paper models a hybrid marketplace, such as Amazon, Zalando or the Apple and Google app stores, that distributes its own private-label products in competition with the third-party sellers it hosts and charges those sellers per-unit or ad valorem fees under the agency model. Two competing sellers distribute exclusively through the platform, which holds all bargaining power. The authors compare equilibrium prices and fees when sellers price non-cooperatively and when they maximise joint profit through merger or collusion. The central result is that the platform responds to seller cooperation by cutting both its fees and its private-label price, to mitigate double marginalisation and to steer consumers toward its own more profitable line. When products are close substitutes, cooperating sellers can end up setting lower retail prices, so consumers are unambiguously better off. Cooperation is therefore not necessarily anticompetitive when the platform is a vertically integrated gatekeeper on the agency model, is a close competitor to its sellers, and observes or anticipates their agreement.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 2 lists it among optional sources for §5.4, where the chapter describes the large marketplace as "one firm, four kinds of mediator at once", including the resale of goods it buys and the gating of which seller a buyer sees. Bisceglia and Padilla supply a formal account of the hybrid gatekeeper whose fee and private-label decisions discipline the sellers it hosts, which is the economics behind the chapter's claim that the marketplace both integrates and gates. Role: optional support; it also documents that third-party sellers may merge in response to gatekeeper bargaining power, a collective-action response the chapter does not discuss.
