## Summary
Khan's Note argues that antitrust's consumer welfare standard, understood as short-run price and output effects, cannot capture the architecture of market power in platform markets, with Amazon as the case. Amazon is at once retailer, marketplace, logistics and delivery network, payment service, lender, publisher, hardware maker and cloud host; it has grown enormously while earning thin profits by pricing below cost and expanding across lines, so that it now serves as infrastructure for businesses that also compete with it. Two features of platform markets make this rational and dangerous. The economics of platforms reward growth over profit, so predatory pricing is sensible even though doctrine treats it as implausible; and because platforms are critical intermediaries, integration across lines lets them control the infrastructure rivals depend on and use the information gathered from those rivals against them. The Note's remedies section was not opened this session.

## Use in the chapter
Section 5.4 decomposes a large marketplace into four mediators: fulfilment (necessary), matching (partial), the Buy Box gate (contingent), and first-party resale (reducible). The audit (U7) notes the decomposition is uncited and proposes Khan 2017 together with the Commission's IP/22/7777. Khan supplies the canonical statement that Amazon's lines of business are separable for analysis and that the dual role of marketplace and competitor is where the harm lives. Role: support. Her framing of integration across lines as the source of harm is the antitrust analogue of the chapter's contest-the-gate, accept-the-rail strategy.
