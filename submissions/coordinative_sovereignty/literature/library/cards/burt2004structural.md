## Summary
Burt sets out the mechanism by which brokerage yields social capital. Because opinion and behaviour are more homogeneous within groups than between them, people whose networks span the structural holes between groups are more familiar with alternative ways of thinking and acting; brokerage therefore provides "a vision of options otherwise unseen." After reviewing evidence consistent with the hypothesis, Burt studies the networks of managers in the supply chain of a large American electronics company, where the organization is rife with structural holes. Compensation, positive evaluations, promotions and good ideas are disproportionately in the hands of managers whose networks bridge groups; those brokers are more likely to express ideas, less likely to have them dismissed and more likely to have them judged valuable. The article closes with implications for creativity and structural change. The result is the canonical statement that the returns to a mediating position come from the disconnection of the parties on either side.

## Use in the chapter
§3 cites it in the lineage of the triad: "from the broker who bridges disconnected others (Burt, 2004) to the broker who joins the parties it stands between (Obstfeld, 2005)." §5.2 uses it to distinguish the bypass test from brokerage research, which "asks what a broker gains from standing between disconnected parties and whether another actor could occupy the position (Burt, 2004; Obstfeld, 2005)," and then observes that "Burt asks what a position yields given the ties that exist," whereas the bypass test asks whether the tie could be restored. Role: support for the claim that the mediator's profit derives from the absence of the direct tie; the chapter's reading is faithful to the abstract.
