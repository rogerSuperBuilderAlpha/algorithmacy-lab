## Summary
Christin argues that ethnographers should not treat algorithmic opacity as a wall but as something their method can work with and around. She proposes three analytical strategies. Algorithmic refraction examines the transformations that occur when computational systems, human actors and institutions meet, reading the algorithm through its effects on practice. Algorithmic comparison uses similarity and difference across sites or systems to isolate what is distinctive about a given system. Algorithmic triangulation enlists algorithms themselves as aids to qualitative data collection. The article draws on her earlier fieldwork in newsrooms and criminal courts and is methodological rather than empirical in its own right. Its underlying claim is that much of what passes for the black box is a product of how researchers approach the system: opacity is partly methodological, and appropriate ethnographic design can recover a good deal of what the system does and means to those who live with it.

## Use in the chapter
Strand 1 lists it as adjacent for §3.2, where the chapter sets out opacity as the first feature of algorithmic mediation following Burrell (2016). Christin qualifies the chapter's claim: some opacity is intrinsic, but some is a methodological artefact, and the inferential side of algorithmacy that the chapter describes in §4 is in effect a lay version of the refraction strategy. Role: support, optional, and a useful caveat against overstating intrinsic opacity.
