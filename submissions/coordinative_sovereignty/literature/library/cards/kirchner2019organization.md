## Summary
Kirchner and Schüßler apply Ahrne and Brunsson's theory of partial organization to sharing-economy platforms and argue that what looks like a market is in fact a digital marketplace created and run by a market organizer. Using Lyft and Airbnb as cases, they show how the organizer establishes a new market order by mobilising participants and resources and then maintains it through the five elements of organization that Ahrne and Brunsson identify: membership, hierarchy, rules, monitoring and sanctions. Registration and account terms decide membership; terms of service and algorithmic defaults set rules; ratings and data collection monitor; deactivation and ranking sanction; and the organizer sits above all of it as a decided hierarchy without becoming an employer. The chapter's thesis is that the rhetoric of sharing masks this organizing role: the platforms generate large profits precisely by imposing order on a marketplace they claim merely to host. The contribution is to dissolve the market-versus-organization binary for platforms and to supply a vocabulary for the partial organization of markets.

The gaps report lists it (C5, with Ahrne and Brunsson 2011) as a direct collision with §3, where the chapter introduces the platform as a "fourth mechanism" of coordination beside market, hierarchy and network. The partial-organization reading says platforms are markets organized through membership, rules, monitoring and sanctions, which dissolves the trichotomy the fourth-mechanism claim presupposes. Role: construct to position against. The chapter should state in §3 why "opaque, interested mediation" is not reducible to partial organization: the elements Kirchner and Schüßler list are decided and in principle legible, whereas the chapter's mechanism is adaptive and hidden from the parties it orders.
