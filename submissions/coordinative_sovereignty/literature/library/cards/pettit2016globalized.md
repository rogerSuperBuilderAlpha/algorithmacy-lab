## Summary
Pettit extends freedom as non-domination from the domestic to the global arena while keeping the assumption that there will continue to be many distinct states. The globalized ideal has two parts. Internally, a state should not dominate the members of its own people: it should be a republic in which citizens control the state under which they live. Externally, each people, considered as a corporate body and hence as an agent, should not be dominated by other agencies: not by other states, and not by international organizations or multinational corporations. The move that the chapter relies on is the treatment of a people as a corporate agent whose freedom matters for the sake of its members, so that a people can be undominated from within yet dominated from without. Pettit argues that the ideal is attractive in the abstract and also supports a concrete range of sensible, often radical, international policies.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §6: "Republican theory has already licensed the move, extending non-domination to corporate bodies while treating a corporate body's freedom as valuable for its members' sake (Pettit, 2016)." The role is support for extending coordinative sovereignty from persons to firms. The audit (S25) flags overreach: Pettit's corporate body is a people, not a firm, and the chapter's extension to the platform-dependent firm goes beyond what the article licenses; the recommendation is to soften the sentence to "has made an analogous move for peoples" and let the chapter own the extension to firms. The chapter's next sentence, that the firm's sovereignty matters for the people who make it up, is its own argument and should be presented as such.

## Key quotations
"It argues for a world in which states do not dominate members of their own people and, considered as a corporate body, no people is dominated by other agencies: not by other states and not, for example, by any international agency or multi-national corporation." (Abstract)

"Once we recognize that a free people is an agent, however, we can see that even if it is publicly un-dominated from within — even if its members control the state under which they live — it may still be dominated from without." (EUI preprint, p. 11)
