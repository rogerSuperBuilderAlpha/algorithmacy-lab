## Summary
Pettit restates republican theory and builds from it a model of democracy. On the republican view we are free persons to the extent that we are protected and secured in the same fundamental choices, on the same public basis, as one another; but such protection requires a coercive state, which raises the question whether the freedom it secures rests on a deeper political subjection. Pettit answers that state coercion does not amount to domination insofar as citizens enjoy an equally shared form of control over those in power, and he develops a realistic model of the institutions, contestatory as well as electoral, that could establish such control. The first part articulates freedom as non-domination, including the heuristic of the eyeball test, under which people are free when they can look others in the eye without reason for fear or deference; the second develops the account of democratic legitimacy as popular control; the third treats the model's institutional requirements. The book is the mature statement of Pettit's republicanism.
