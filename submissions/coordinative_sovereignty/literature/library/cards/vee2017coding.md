## Summary
Summary written from a published review (Frabetti, Computational Culture), not from the book itself. Vee argues that computer programming should be understood as a literacy, and that doing so shows how coding is changing what it means to be literate. She traces the history of textual literacy through its shifting justifications, religious in the Reformation, then civic, then tied to efficiency and productivity under industrialization, then to self-development, and uses that history to show that whether a literacy is liberatory or hegemonic cannot be settled once and for all. Coding and writing share affordances (discreteness, scalability, creative form), but code is also executable, descriptive for humans and performative for machines. Contemporary "coding for everyone" campaigns, she shows, recycle the four arguments of mass-literacy campaigns: individual empowerment, new ways of thinking, citizenship, and employability, along with their ideas of morality, productivity and standardization. Her conclusion is that programming must be thought of more broadly, as coding literacy, if the ability to program is to be distributed more widely.
