## Summary
Bovens and Zouridis argue that information and communication technology has changed the structure of large executive public agencies. Agencies that were machine bureaucracies, in which street-level officials exercised ample discretion over individual clients, have in some domains become system-level bureaucracies: window clerks are replaced by websites, case managers by expert systems, and the key actors are now system analysts and software designers who write the decision rules in advance. The article examines this shift from the standpoint of the constitutional state. Implementation of the law is, they say, virtually perfected, but discretion has not disappeared; it has moved to the designers, and new problems appear about the discretionary power of system-level bureaucrats and about due process and fairness in hard cases that do not fit the programmed parameters. The article closes with institutional innovations meant to embed system-level bureaucracies within constitutional control.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited in the chapter. Strand 5 lists it as a MUST, paired with Vogl et al. (2020) on algorithmic bureaucracy, as a construct to position against in §3. The chapter's §3 presents co-optation through an opaque, interested mediator as a new form of coordination; Bovens and Zouridis already describe decisions migrating from the encounter to the system and discretion migrating to the designer, which is the same displacement in the public sector without the mediator's private interest. Role: position against. The contrast the chapter needs is that the system-level bureaucracy is still answerable to constitutional control, while the platform mediator's rules are set by a firm whose interest is its own.
