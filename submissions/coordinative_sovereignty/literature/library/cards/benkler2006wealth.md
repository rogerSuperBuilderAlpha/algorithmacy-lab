## Summary
Benkler argues that the networked information economy, built on cheap processors and ubiquitous connectivity, makes possible a mode of production he calls commons-based peer production, in which large numbers of loosely coordinated individuals produce information, knowledge and culture outside both firms and price-driven markets. Free software, Wikipedia and distributed computing are the leading cases. He claims this shift improves individual autonomy, democratic discourse and a critical culture by widening the range of ways people can act and cooperate without passing through a firm or a market, and he examines the policy battles over intellectual property, spectrum and network architecture through which incumbents try to contain it. The book is normative political economy rather than empirical study; its method is institutional analysis combined with case description. Read two decades on, it is the optimistic baseline against which the platform-power literature measures how far coordination has since been re-enclosed.

## Use in the chapter
Listed in Additional Reading only; not cited in the body. Its role is background for the political-theory strand: the chapter's concern with standing inside a necessary mediation is partly a reply to Benkler's expectation that networks would enlarge autonomy by removing intermediaries. A reader of §6 or §7 who wants the pre-platform optimism about decentralized coordination should start here. No audit flag attaches to it.

## Key quotations
"Individuals are using their newly expanded practical freedom to act and cooperate with others in ways that improve the practiced experience of democracy, justice and development, a critical culture, and community." (p. 8)
"The networked information economy improves the practical capacities of individuals along three dimensions: (1) it improves their capacity to do more for and by themselves; (2) it enhances their capacity to do more in loose commonality with others, without being constrained to organize their relationship through a price system" (p. 9)
