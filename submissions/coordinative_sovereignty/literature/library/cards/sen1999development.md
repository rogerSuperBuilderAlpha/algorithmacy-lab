## Summary
Sen argues that freedom is both the primary end and the principal means of development. Against accounts that measure development by income or industrialization, he holds that despite unprecedented opulence, millions of people remain unfree, imprisoned by poverty, social deprivation, tyranny, or authoritarianism, and that development consists in removing those unfreedoms. The book develops the capability approach: what matters is the substantive freedoms people have to lead lives they have reason to value, and different kinds of freedom, political liberties, economic facilities, social opportunities such as education and health, transparency guarantees, and protective security, reinforce one another. Sen shows how social institutions enhance individual freedom while being sustained in turn by social values and public discussion, and he defends democracy and a free press instrumentally, for instance in the argument that famines do not occur in functioning democracies. The work is the canonical statement of development as the expansion of capabilities rather than output.

## Use in the chapter
Section 2.2 cites it in "citizens govern themselves to the degree they can read what institutions write and hold those institutions to account through textual means (Nussbaum, 2006; Sen, 1999)." Role: support for the claim that literacy and education are constitutive of democratic self-government, which Sen's treatment of social opportunities and public reasoning sustains. Audit O4 finds that neither Sen nor Nussbaum states the proportionality or the phrase "textual means," so the sentence should say that self-government depends on the capacity to read and hold institutions to account, with the cite attached to that weaker claim. The book's transparency guarantees, one of Sen's five instrumental freedoms, are also a natural bridge to §2.3's account of disclosure.
