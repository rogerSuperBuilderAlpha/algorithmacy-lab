## Summary
Ong's book is the classic synthesis of the orality-literacy thesis. It sets out the psychodynamics of primary oral cultures, those with no knowledge of writing, in which thought and expression are additive, aggregative, redundant, conservative, close to the human lifeworld, agonistic, participatory, and situational, because knowledge that cannot be stored must be held in memorable form and performed. Writing, Ong argues, restructures consciousness: it makes language an object, separates the knower from the known, enables abstract and analytic thought, and creates the author and the reader as solitary figures. Print intensifies these effects through closure and fixity, and electronic media produce a secondary orality that depends on writing and print. The book draws on Parry and Lord's Homeric studies, on Havelock, and on Goody, and it ranges across oral genres worldwide before considering the implications for literary theory and for the study of consciousness. The strong cognitive version of the thesis is contested.
