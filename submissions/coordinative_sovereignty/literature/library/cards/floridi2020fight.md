## Summary
Floridi's editorial defines digital sovereignty as control over data, software, standards, protocols, hardware, services and digital infrastructure, and argues that the fight for it touches everyone, including people who have never used an online service. The central contest is between multinational technology companies and states: companies can determine the nature and speed of technological change, while states retain the capacity to control its direction through regulation. Floridi presents the question as one of legitimacy and democratic governance rather than of technology alone, and asks at what level sovereignty should be exercised. His answer for Europe is that digital sovereignty should be articulated at both national and supranational levels, with the EU as the natural site for the supranational layer because no single member state can match the scale of the corporate actors involved. The piece is programmatic rather than empirical and has become a standard reference point for the control-oriented reading of the term.
