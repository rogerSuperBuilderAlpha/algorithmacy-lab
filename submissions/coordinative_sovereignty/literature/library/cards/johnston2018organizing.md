## Summary
The ILO working paper examines the obstacles to freedom of association and effective recognition of the right to collective bargaining for workers in the gig economy, and maps the strategies those workers are using to build collective agency and to promote effective regulation of gig work. It surveys cases across ride-hailing, delivery and crowdwork in several countries, covering worker-led associations, union-backed organising, cooperative alternatives, online forums used as proto-unions, litigation over employment status and engagement with local regulators. It treats the legal obstacle squarely: where workers are classified as independent contractors, collective agreements on pay may be read as cartels, and many platforms refuse to bargain. The authors nonetheless argue that collective bargaining holds promise as a form of responsive regulation that can balance the needs of platforms, requestors and those performing work through them, and they set out the policy conditions, including clarified employment status and competition-law exemptions, under which bargaining could be extended to platform work.

## Use in the chapter
§7 says that where platform workers or platform-dependent firms can organise, "they can negotiate over how assignments are made, how deactivation is decided, and how ranking is governed". The audit (U15) notes that no concluded agreement is cited and asks for "could negotiate", citing Directive (EU) 2024/2831 Article 25 and this report. Role: support for the claim that collective bargaining is an available voice instrument for the coordinated actor, while supplying the honest qualification that platforms have largely refused to bargain and that the legal route remains contested.
