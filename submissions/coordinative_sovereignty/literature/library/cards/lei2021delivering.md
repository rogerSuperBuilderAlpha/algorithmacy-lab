## Summary
Lei asks how and when labour control and management on platforms lead to collective resistance, using China's food-delivery economy as the case. She develops the concept of platform architecture to capture the technological, legal and organizational dimensions of control in the labour process and the variable relations among them. The evidence comprises 68 in-depth interviews, ethnographic observation, and 87 cases of strikes and protests, and the design compares two architectures, the service platform, where couriers are employed through subcontractors on fixed shifts, and the gig platform, where they work as crowdsourced independent contractors. The central finding is that differences in architecture diffuse or heighten contention. On the service platform, technological control generates dissatisfaction, but the legal and organizational dimensions contain grievances and reduce both the appeal of and the space for collective action. On the gig platform, all three dimensions reinforce one another, escalating grievances, making contention attractive and creating spaces for mobilising solidarity. Gig couriers are therefore more likely to see their work relations as exploitative and to act collectively, despite the atomisation of their work.
