## Summary
Braun and Hummel ask whether digital sovereignty, a term now pervasive in European digital policy, is normatively desirable at all. They argue that the policy discussion works with an oversimplified conception of sovereignty and set out three dimensions of the concept: sovereignty as absolute power, as embodied power, and as institutional power. Their central claim is that two of these dimensions, which concern the intricate relation between the sovereign and the addressees of sovereignty claims, are consistently overlooked in European debates, which tend to treat sovereignty as control over infrastructure and data. A fuller understanding, they suggest, could ground normative frameworks centred on vulnerability and freedom while remaining attentive to the tensions inherent in digital governance and regulation. The article is conceptual and philosophical in method, working from political theory and the ethics of technology rather than from empirical cases.
