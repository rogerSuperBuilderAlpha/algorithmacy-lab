## Summary
Gehl and Zulli study platform governance where most scholarship does not look: on Mastodon, a non-centralized, federated social network. Using survey data, developer discussions on GitHub and Discourse, instance Codes of Conduct, and participant observation, they argue that Mastodon's governance is an exemplar of the covenant, a concept from federalist political theory in which members bind themselves to one another by mutual promise rather than by contract with a central authority. They contrast this covenantal federalism with the contractual form of governance used by corporate social media, and they use the same theory to explain how Mastodon's users, administrators and developers justify revoking or denying membership in the federation, that is, defederating an instance. The study's contribution is to show a third form of governance beyond the dichotomy of corporate platforms and alt-right alternatives. Governance decisions in the study are taken largely by instance administrators and developers; ordinary users appear as the constituency those decisions are justified to.

## Use in the chapter
