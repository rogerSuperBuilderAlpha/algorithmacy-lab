## Summary
The essays collected in the book set out Innis's thesis that every medium of communication carries a bias toward either time or space, and that the dominant medium of a civilisation shapes the form of power it can sustain. Durable, heavy media such as stone and clay favour continuity, hierarchy and religious authority across generations; light, portable media such as papyrus and paper favour administration over distance, territorial expansion and secular, centralised rule. Institutions that command the points where knowledge is stored and transmitted hold what Innis calls monopolies of knowledge, which define what counts as legitimate knowledge and divide societies into those who hold the competence and those who do not. Such monopolies are unstable: groups at the margins take up newer, cheaper media and displace the old centres. Stable societies balance the two biases; empires fail when one bias overreaches. The argument rests on Innis's economic history and on wide reading in the history of writing and empire.

## Use in the chapter
Cited in §2.2: "from Innis (1951) on how a civilization's dominant medium biases its form of power." The chapter uses Innis, with Eisenstein, to establish that forms of authority have always been paired with the media that carry them, and the audit (O5) proposes attaching Innis and Goody to the chapter's stronger claim that "sovereignty has always required a competence equal to the medium," which is currently stated as fact rather than owned as thesis. Innis's monopolies of knowledge are the closest support for the competence half of that claim. Role: support.
