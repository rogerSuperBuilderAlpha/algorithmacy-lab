## Summary
The article analyses how the notion of sovereignty has been and continues to be mobilised in relation to the digital, where it increasingly describes forms of independence, control and autonomy over infrastructures, technologies and data. The authors' starting point is their own research with activist tech collectives, where they observed the term being used to emphasise alternative technological practices in ways that differ sharply from governmental policy usage. Reviewing a body of publications across state, Indigenous, social-movement and personal registers, they show that while the notion is generally used to assert some form of collective control over digital content or infrastructure, the precise subjects, meanings and definitions diverge, sometimes to the point of contradiction. The contribution is a map of these usages rather than a single definition, and it establishes that digital sovereignty is a contested concept whose grassroots and emancipatory variants sit alongside, and often against, the state-centred variant.
