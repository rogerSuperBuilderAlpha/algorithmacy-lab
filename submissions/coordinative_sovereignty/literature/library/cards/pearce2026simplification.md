## Summary
Pearce examines the EU Digital Omnibus, presented by the Commission as targeted regulatory streamlining and criticised by data protection observers as regression, erosion of fundamental rights and a loss of coherence in the digital acquis. He takes those concerns seriously but argues that the debate risks treating legislative reform as such as normatively suspect, which is untenable given persistent structural and operational weaknesses in the current framework. He identifies three: conceptual indeterminacy in the definition of personal data; uniform obligations that apply regardless of the scale or risk of processing; and inconsistent enforcement of individual rights. His central claim is that rejecting amendment out of hand does not preserve the framework but displaces change into less accountable channels, informal reinterpretation by regulators and judicial drift, rather than transparent deliberation. The question is therefore not whether EU data protection law should evolve but how reform can address the deficiencies without undermining legitimacy, and the article proposes evaluating the Omnibus against the framework's actual performance rather than an idealised version of it. The focus is data protection; the P2B repeal and the DGA fold-in are not its subject.
