## Summary
Heresi builds a theoretical model in which competing platforms design the search environment through which sellers and buyers meet, choosing the unit search cost consumers face, and asks how price parity clauses interact with that design. Without parity clauses, platforms obfuscate consumers in equilibrium through high search costs whenever platform differentiation is not too weak, a result that needs no informational advantage or behavioral assumption. When parity clauses are imposed exogenously on both platforms, they can raise or lower obfuscation, prices and consumer surplus. When platforms choose whether to adopt them, the unique equilibrium has parity clauses adopted if and only if they raise obfuscation and prices, so clauses that appear in practice are those that harm consumers, while clauses that would lower prices are not imposed. The model assumes sellers have no direct channel; the conclusion notes that adding one weakly lowers obfuscation without altering the main results, and flags ordered search and paid prominence as open questions.

## Use in the chapter
Not cited. Strand 6 lists it under contests for §5.5. The chapter treats the parity clause as a contingent gate held in place by contract and separable from the aggregation function; Heresi's model ties the clause to the platform's design of the search environment itself, so the gate and the matching function are jointly chosen and the welfare effect of removing the clause depends on how obfuscation adjusts. Role: contests the clean split between contingent gate and necessary function, in the same direction as Wang and Wright 2023 (parity funds search investment) and Hunold et al. 2020 (the gate migrated into the ranking). The chapter can concede the point and keep the diagnostic by noting that what survives removal of the clause is exactly the platform's design power.
