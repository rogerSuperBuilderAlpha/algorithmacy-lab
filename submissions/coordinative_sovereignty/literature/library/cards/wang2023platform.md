## Summary
A theoretical model of search platforms facing showrooming: consumers search on the platform, then switch to a cheaper channel to buy. Platforms defend price parity clauses as necessary to protect their incentive to invest in search and matching quality, the investment rivals and sellers can free-ride on. Incorporating both effects, the authors find that wide price parity clauses lead to excessive platform investment while narrow or no parity clauses lead to insufficient investment; taking the investment effects into account, wide parity lowers consumer surplus but has ambiguous effects on total welfare. Narrow parity does not help investment incentives, because a rival platform can still free-ride by undercutting fees. Without any parity clauses, platform search and matching becomes like a public good, which could justify subsidizing investment rather than permitting the restraints. The paper extends Wang and Wright 2020 and engages the empirical parity-ban literature (Hunold et al., Mantovani et al., Ennis et al.).

## Use in the chapter
Sweep MUST for §5.2, §5.4, and §5.5, with role contests. The chapter's §5.5 hotel case splits the OTA's functions cleanly: demand aggregation is necessary (voice), the parity clause is contingent (exit, "held in place by a contract"). Wang and Wright show the split is not clean, because the contingent gate funds the necessary function: parity protects the investment in search and matching that makes the aggregation worth having, so removing the gate can starve the function the chapter deems necessary. The chapter should concede the coupling and argue the standing question survives it, since even an investment-justified gate is a term the coordinated hotel needs standing to contest.
