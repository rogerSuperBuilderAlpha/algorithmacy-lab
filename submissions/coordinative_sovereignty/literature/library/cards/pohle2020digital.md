## Summary
Pohle and Thiel's concept essay traces how digital sovereignty became a central element of policy discourse over the past decade, popular in both authoritarian and democratic states yet highly contested. They first examine the challenges the digital transformation appears to pose to sovereignty, then retrace how sovereignty re-emerged as a key category for the digital, from concerns about infrastructure and security to economic and individual framings. Systematising the normative claims made under the label, they distinguish claims at the level of the state, the economy and the individual, and show that the term is deployed to reinstate the nation state, its economy and its citizens as relevant categories in the global governance of digital infrastructures. Their conclusion is that digital sovereignty is today understood more as a discursive practice in politics and policy than as a legal or organisational concept, which explains both its flexibility and its contestation.

## Use in the chapter
§2.3 cites it, with Floridi and Hummel et al., for the working definition of digital sovereignty as "the ability of states, organizations, and communities to exercise control over their digital infrastructures, data, and technologies." §6 cites it for the claim that "Most digital-sovereignty work asks who controls the infrastructure and answers with reclaimed control, whether by a state, a community, or a firm."
