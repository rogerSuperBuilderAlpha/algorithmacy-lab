## Summary
Spulber sets out an economic theory of the firm as intermediary. Firms, on this account, stand between customers and suppliers, set prices, and make markets; they emerge in equilibrium because centralised intermediation economises on search, matching, and transaction costs relative to direct exchange. The book integrates microeconomic and financial models of intermediation (dealers, market makers, brokers) to explain market functioning and the activities of firms, and it compares this intermediation view with the neoclassical, industrial-organisation, transaction-cost, and principal-agent theories of the firm. Its central result is that price-setting intermediaries, not the auctioneer of general equilibrium theory, clear markets, and that the firm's existence and boundaries can be derived from the returns to intermediation. The work is theoretical and formal.
