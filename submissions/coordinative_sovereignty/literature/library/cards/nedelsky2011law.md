## Summary
Nedelsky's book argues that autonomy, one of the core concepts of legal and political thought, is also one of the least understood, because it is conventionally modelled as independence from others. She proposes instead that selves are constituted by relationships, and that autonomy is developed and exercised through the relationships and social structures in which a person stands, including those that law itself creates. On this view the political project is not only to protect the individual from the state and keep the state out, but to use law to construct relations, with the state and among persons, that enhance autonomy. The book develops the relational approach for the self, for autonomy, and for rights, and applies it to contemporary legal problems such as violence against women, property, and the welfare state. Its contribution for the chapter is the shift of the object of concern from whether one is in a relationship to what shape the relationship has.
