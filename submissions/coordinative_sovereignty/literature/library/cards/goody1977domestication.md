## Summary
Goody's book, in the Cambridge Themes in the Social Sciences series, attacks the habit of explaining differences in the "mind" of human societies through a dichotomy between advanced and primitive, open and closed, or domesticated and savage, a family of we-they distinctions he argues blocks any serious account of the mechanisms behind long-term change in cognitive processes or of the changes under way in "traditional" societies. His alternative locates those mechanisms in the technology of the intellect, above all writing. Building on the 1963 essay with Watt, he argues that writing, and particular written forms such as the list, the table, and the formula, make possible operations on language that speech does not: decontextualization, comparison across occasions, scrutiny of contradiction, and cumulative critique. Oral cultures are not pre-logical; they lack the material means for certain kinds of logical work. The book is the canonical statement of the strong orality-literacy thesis that later ethnographers of literacy qualified.

## Use in the chapter
Section 2.1 cites it in "Oracy, as Goody (1977) and Ong (1982) describe it, was the competence" that let a member of an oral order participate in its authority, and §4 cites it for the claim that a change of communicative medium is a change in kind, noting that the strong version "did not survive the ethnographers." Role: support for the chapter's media-history premise that each medium demands a competence. Two audit flags: Goody never uses the word "oracy," so §2.1 should reword (O6); and §2, unlike §4, does not concede that the great divide is contested (G8), which one sentence citing Street, Finnegan, or Halverson would close. Goody 1986 is the better source for the oral-order legal sentences (U13).
