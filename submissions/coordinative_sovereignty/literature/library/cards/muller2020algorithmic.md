## Summary
This student Note argues that labour law has not caught up with the platform economy, in which firms such as Uber use machine-learning algorithms processing torrents of data to run apps that promise efficiency, flexibility and autonomy to the users who both deliver and consume services. Those tools give firms unprecedented information about and power over their services, yet they have been little examined in legal scholarship and barely addressed by courts. Muller's analysis starts from the incentives and constraints on platform firms, identifying situations in which their interests diverge from and may directly oppose those of their users, and from the vast asymmetries of information and market power that firms enjoy. The Note then catalogues economic harms that are specific to algorithmic work platforms, including pricing and dispatch decisions whose individual cost to a driver is small but which accumulate over continued transactions, and explores tort remedies as the most promising route for workers harmed by algorithm design choices, since the remedies available to them are as yet undeveloped.
