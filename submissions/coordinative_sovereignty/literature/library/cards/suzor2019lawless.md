## Summary
Suzor argues that the companies running search engines, social media platforms and internet infrastructure govern online life through rules that are largely undisclosed and unaccountable. Drawing on more than a decade of research into internet governance, the book documents how these private rules decide what can be said and seen, and how they fail in the face of abuse, misinformation and censorship, including censorship at the request of states. His remedy is a form of digital constitutionalism: the principles that constrain public power, among them transparency, due process, equality and the rule of law, should be brought to bear on platform governance, not by turning platforms into states but by making their rule-making and enforcement legitimate. The publisher frames the result as a vision of a diverse and flourishing internet that protects fundamental rights against "the lawless rule of tech." The reviewer in the European Journal of Risk Regulation reads the book alongside societal constitutionalism and legal pluralism, treating platform governance as a significant but unaccountable regulatory order.
