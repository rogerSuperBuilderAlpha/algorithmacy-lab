## Summary
The paper models a market with two differentiated sellers, a mass of consumers some of whom cannot observe sellers' offers, and an intermediary that can identify the best match but is vertically integrated with one seller and so recommends it more often than is objectively warranted. The authors' central distinction is between congruence and conflict between a seller's and its customers' payoffs: conflict arises when the cheapest way to offer consumers more utility is to cut the markup (standard price competition), congruence when higher utility goes with higher markups (quality-led competition). Bias acts as a demand shifter toward the integrated seller. Under conflict, bias always harms consumers. Under congruence it can benefit them, because the favoured seller gains a stronger incentive to offer higher utility, which may outweigh mismatch costs. The policy section shows that a fiduciary duty helps under conflict but may hurt under congruence, that a neutrality rule (equal prominence) is always ineffective, and that transparency and divestiture work only under conflict. Endogenising bias, the intermediary chooses more of it under congruence.
