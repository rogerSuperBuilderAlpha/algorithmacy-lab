# PART 1 — Theory & Structure (§1–§10)

## §1 Introduction

The opening move — a firm can comply with every EU instrument and still be strategically captured — is the right hook and it is rhetorically excellent. It is also the chapter's most legally exposed sentence, and a platform-regulation referee will stop on it first.

**The claim overreaches on ranking.** The abstract and §1 assert that market position "turns on ranking decisions it cannot see, predict, or contest" and that a merchant "can neither see nor predict nor contest" an app store's ranking and review decisions. EU law has moved on exactly this surface:

- The **Platform-to-Business Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 (P2B)** — conspicuously absent from the entire chapter and the reference list — requires online intermediation services and search engines to disclose the **main parameters determining ranking** (Art. 5), to give a **statement of reasons** for restrictions, suspensions, and terminations (Art. 4), and to provide an **internal complaint-handling system** and access to mediation (Arts. 11–12). This instrument is aimed with almost eerie precision at the chapter's own opening cast: the app developer, the marketplace seller, the ranked business user.
- **DSA Art. 27** requires platforms to set out the **main parameters of recommender systems** in plain language and (for VLOPs, Art. 38) to offer at least one non-profiling option.
- **DMA Art. 6(5)** prohibits gatekeepers from **self-preferencing in ranking**.

So the "cannot see, predict, or contest" trio is now, as a matter of positive law, partly false on *see* (parameter disclosure exists) and *contest* (P2B complaint-handling, DSA Arts. 20–21 exist). The chapter's argument does not need the strong version. Its actual thesis — legibility without standing — is *strengthened* by conceding these instruments and then showing they deliver disclosed parameters and process rights without a case-level channel to contest the coordinative determination. Right now the chapter argues against a straw "law = nothing on ranking," when the live instruments already exemplify its "legibility without standing" diagnosis. **Fix: name P2B, DSA 27, DMA 6(5); reframe the gap as disclosure-without-standing rather than absence.** This is the single most important substantive revision.

The EU inventory in §1 is otherwise accurate and current: GDPR (2016/679), AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689), DMA (Reg. 2022/1925), DSA (Reg. 2022/2065), NIS2 (Dir. 2022/2555). Descriptions are high-level but correct. Good use of Bradford's Brussels effect.

## §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities

The oracy → literacy → algorithmacy scaffolding is elegant and the media-theory grounding (Innis, Eisenstein, Habermas, Anderson, Scott) is deployed with restraint and the right hedge on media determinism (§2.2, line 106–108). §2.3's framing of digital sovereignty as "extended literacy," with the AI Act / GDPR / NIS2 as "technologies of reading and writing," is a defensible and useful characterization for a governance audience. No legal error here.

One agentless-passive violation to flag for Part 2 (line 58: "Sovereignty is usually sorted…"). The house style wants a named sorter.

## §3 The platform turn — and §3.2, the crux

§3's separation of three things that are individually old (coordination logic, triadic topology, the broker's position) from the one thing that is new (an *opaque, interested, adaptive* mediator) is the chapter's cleanest analytic move and it pre-empts the obvious "algorithmic management is just hierarchy" objection (Kellogg et al.; Vallas & Schor) well. Simmel/Burt/Obstfeld on the triad is the correct lineage.

**§3.2 — "Why literacy cannot reach it."** The four-part structure is sound and the first three parts are deployed correctly:

1. Burrell's third opacity (survives source release) — used exactly right.
2. Ananny & Crawford's transparency-without-institution — used right, and it is the load-bearing citation for the whole "legibility without standing" thesis.
3. Cutolo & Kenney / Rahman on absent leverage and the invisible cage — apt.

**Part 4 (Hildebrandt) under-reads its own source.** The chapter uses Hildebrandt (2015) as a flat limit: text-driven law "cannot straightforwardly govern" code- and data-driven modes, and the EU instruments therefore "stop short of the layer" (line 224–233). Two problems:

- **Hildebrandt is more constructive than the chapter admits.** Her project is not to declare text-law defeated but to *re-articulate legal protection into the code/data environment* — "legal protection by design," "agonistic machine learning," contestability built into the architecture. Hildebrandt is, in other words, the theorist of precisely the solution the chapter itself endorses in §7 (contestability by design, Alfrink). Citing her only as a wall misses that her arrow points at the chapter's own remedy. A Hildebrandt-literate referee will read the current use as a misappropriation-by-truncation. **Fix: add one sentence — text-driven law cannot reach the layer *in its disclosure modality*, but can be re-engineered into it as embedded procedural protection, which is what §7's contestability mandates are.** This also reconciles §3.2 with §7.

- **"Stop short of the layer" is an overclaim** for the reasons given under §1: P2B, DSA 27, DMA 6(5), GDPR Art. 22, and platform-work Art. 10 all reach into the ranking/automated-decision layer, imperfectly. The accurate and stronger claim is narrower: the instruments reach the layer as *disclosed parameters and prohibited conduct*, not as *case-level contestable determination*.

**Cohen and Pasquale** (line 229) are correctly enlisted for platform-as-sovereign: Cohen's "Law for the platform economy" (legal institutions reconstituting around platform logics) and Pasquale's functional sovereignty both support "the platform decides… what to surface and what to bury." Minor sharpening: Pasquale's functional sovereignty is centrally about platforms displacing *state* functions (adjudication, currency, identity); the chapter narrows it to ranking authority. Fine, but a nod to the state-displacement core would land the citation more squarely.

**§3.3 political economy.** Two-sided-markets economics is handled competently: Rochet & Tirole (2003) for two-sided markets, Armstrong & Wright (2007) for the **competitive bottleneck**, both attributed correctly. The "rent from topology, independent of service rendered" argument is economically literate and does real work for the co-optation claim.

## §4 Algorithmacy

Not primarily a legal section, but two governance-relevant strengths: the distribution argument (competence concentrates like capital; opacity is a rent-preserving strategy that sets platforms against the very institutions — contestability, researcher access — that would diffuse the competence, line 318–328) is a genuinely good political-economy hook that ties the construct to the digital-divide and data-access-asymmetry literatures. Consider one explicit tie to platform-power scholarship here. Agentless-passive to fix at line 275 ("Algorithmic literacy is theorized as…" → "Dogruel et al. (2022) theorize…").

## §5 The diagnostic (necessary/contingent) and §5.3 exit/voice

The bypass test (restore the direct tie, recompute whether the mediator still binds) is the formal spine and it is a real contribution: it turns Hirschman's exit/voice from a metaphor into a sorting rule keyed to a computable property of the coordination. The four-way taxonomy (necessary / contingent / partial / reducible) is clean.

**Is the exit/voice sorting of instruments legally sound?** Broadly yes, as a heuristic — with two gaps a competition/regulation referee will press:

- **The essential-facilities / mandated-access tradition is missing, and it breaks the clean binary.** The chapter maps exit → contingent, voice → necessary, and files interoperability under exit. But compelled access to a *necessary* bottleneck (the essential-facilities doctrine; FRAND access remedies; DMA Art. 6(7) real-time interoperability for number-independent messaging) is precisely a mandate that operates on a mediator you *cannot* leave, reshaping the terms of a mediation that survives the remedy. That is voice-via-access, a third path the two-column scheme does not have a cell for. **Interoperability is therefore not always exit.** DMA 6(7) interop, imposed on a designated (necessary-in-function) gatekeeper, is a terms-of-mediation remedy, not a departure. Recommend a paragraph distinguishing *exit-restoring* interoperability (contingent gate, e.g., DMA 6(4) sideloading, 6(9)–(10) portability) from *access-restoring* interoperability (necessary mediator, e.g., 6(7)).

- **GDPR Art. 22 is the missing legal embodiment of contestability** and its absence is conspicuous once §5.3/§7 turn to voice. Art. 22 (right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal/significant effect; right to human intervention; right to contest), as broadened by the CJEU in **SCHUFA (C-634/21, Dec. 2023)**, is EU law's existing — weak, much-litigated — contestation right. The chapter theorizes contestability as something to be *built* while a statutory version already exists and is being tested. Engage it: its limits (the "solely automated" threshold, the thin "human intervention") are exactly the chapter's "case-level, stops short of governance" point.

The Hirschman framing itself is correct, including the monopoly-restricts-exit-elevates-voice observation. Agentless-passive at line 398 ("The framework has been applied to platforms before…" → name Dasgupta et al. and Smith & Burrows as the appliers).

**§5.4 portfolio of mediators — the strongest passage in the chapter.** "The diagnostic classifies functions, not firms" is the sentence the whole institutional survey should hang on, and the worked cases (payment network; marketplace as necessary-fulfilment / partial-matching / contingent-gate / reducible-resale; app store distribution vs. payment gate; OTA billboard vs. rate-parity) are exemplary and legally accurate. The **OTA rate-parity** example is current and correct: wide-parity bans in France, Germany, Austria, Italy, Belgium, and the CJEU's **Booking.com (C-264/23, Sept. 2024)** holding that parity clauses are not ancillary restraints. Booking was also designated a DMA gatekeeper (May 2024). A one-clause citation to the CJEU ruling would harden an already excellent passage. The app-store payment-gate example (contingent, exit via sideloading) is now doubly apt given the **April 2025 Apple Art. 5(4) anti-steering decision** — see §8.

## §6 Definition

The four-strand construction (republican non-domination / Pettit–Skinner; relational autonomy / Mackenzie–Stoljar–Nedelsky; Markell's involvement-correction to non-domination; Ostrom's commons) is well-built and each strand earns its place. The Muldoon & Raekstad citation (algorithmic management as domination) is deployed correctly and is the right republican bridge. The distinction from neighbours — Bannerman's relational sovereignty, Pasquale's functional sovereignty, Cohen's platform sovereign, "looked at from the other end" — is handled with genuine care, and pre-empting the Repetto concept-stretching charge is smart defensive positioning.

**Data trusts (Delacroix & Lawrence, 2019)** are introduced here and in §7 and are deployed correctly: a bottom-up trust pools data rights under a fiduciary, giving a collective the bargaining power no individual data subject holds. Two refinements the referee would want (carried to §7 below): the legal vehicle question, and the dependence of trusts on portability rights.

Style note for Part 2: §6 is where the negation-defined argument peaks (lines 473–475, 507–537 stack "form no part of it," "owning none of it and escaping none of it," "insulation alone will not supply it"). The definitions are strong enough to survive being stated positively.

## §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty (heavily weighted)

The survey is the policy heart of the chapter and it is good, but it reaches for private/aspirational institutions while under-citing the in-force statutory ones that occupy the same cells.

- **Platform cooperativism (Scholz).** Correct, and the scale/capital limit is honestly stated.
- **Data trusts / cooperatives (Micheli et al.; Delacroix & Lawrence).** Correctly classed as voice. **Missing legal vehicle: the Data Governance Act (Reg. (EU) 2022/868, DGA)**, which creates a regulatory regime for **data intermediation services** and **data altruism organisations** — the EU's live legal embodiment of the data-trust idea, in force since Sept. 2023. A survey of "institutions through which data trusts could be built" that omits the DGA will read as out-of-date to a data-governance referee. Add it. Second refinement: bottom-up data trusts are **parasitic on GDPR Art. 20 portability** — they need exit instruments to have anything to pool. That means the exit/voice binary leaks in a productive way (an exit instrument powers a voice institution); the chapter half-says this in "the institutions compose" (line 614) but should say it sharply.
- **Collective bargaining / works councils.** Correct, and the Ananny & Crawford tie ("a bargaining unit is the institution that acts on disclosure") is the right analytic. Lei (2021) on platform architecture shaping where contention pools is a nice, accurate touch.
- **Contestability (Alfrink et al., 2023).** Deployed correctly — "open and responsive to dispute throughout its lifecycle, with mechanisms to review, correct, or override" is a faithful summary of "Contestable AI by design," and the honest limit ("case-level; stops short of a share in how the system is governed in general," line 588) is exactly right. But see GDPR Art. 22 above — the statutory floor for contestability belongs here.
- **Oversight boards (Klonick).** **Citation slip.** Klonick (2018), "The New Governors," is about platforms *as* the governors of online speech (rule-makers) — it is not the oversight-board paper. The oversight-board argument needs **Klonick (2020), "The Facebook Oversight Board," Yale Law Journal 129:2418 (VERIFY exact pagination)**. As written, the 2018 cite does not support the sentence it anchors. Fix the cite. And more importantly: the survey reaches for a *private, single-platform* body (the Meta board, whose limit the chapter itself notes) while omitting the **in-force statutory contestation architecture — DSA Art. 20 (internal complaint-handling) and Art. 21 (certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies)** — which are the strongest existing "standing channels" and are exactly oversight-at-scale. This is backwards for a governance audience: lead with the statutory ODS bodies, treat the oversight board as the weaker private analogue.
- **Interoperability / portability (exit).** Correct as far as it goes; see §5.4 on the access-restoring-interoperability exception (DMA 6(7)) the binary misses.
- **Regulation (DSA researcher access, systemic risk; platform-work directive).** DSA Art. 40 vetted-researcher access and Arts. 34–35 systemic-risk assessments are described accurately (though not by article number). The "partial steps, instructive in their partiality" framing is exactly the right register.

The closing "institutions compose / the diagnostic keeps the stack aimed" (line 612–621) is the chapter's best policy paragraph and should be foregrounded.

## §8 Implications (heavily weighted)

Strong on structure (scholars / managers / policymakers) and the independent-variation claim (high digital sovereignty + low coordinative sovereignty = "impeccably compliant and strategically captured") is the chapter's sharpest testable proposition. Two of its three legal anchors need correction.

**The 2025 DMA sentence conflates two instruments and mis-dates one (line 636–638).** "The market-conduct remedies of 2025 reached platforms' anti-steering rules and mandated ranking transparency, which lets a firm see how it ranks without letting it contest the ranking."

- **Verified:** the April 23, 2025 DMA decisions reached **anti-steering** — Apple fined €500M under **Art. 5(4)** for steering restrictions; Meta €200M (consent-or-pay). The anti-steering half is correct and current.
- **Wrong:** the 2025 decisions did **not** "mandate ranking transparency." Ranking transparency comes from **P2B Art. 5 (2019)** and **DSA Art. 27**, and the ranking *conduct* rule is **DMA Art. 6(5)** (no self-preferencing) — none of them a 2025 remedy. "Lets a firm see how it ranks" is the P2B main-parameters disclosure, not an anti-steering enforcement decision. The sentence fuses a 2025 conduct-enforcement action with a 2019 disclosure obligation under a single wrong date. **Fix: split them.** Anti-steering (2025 enforcement, exit-adjacent, restores the direct offer channel) is a *different* mediator function from ranking transparency (P2B/DSA disclosure). Getting this right actually helps the argument: anti-steering is an *exit* remedy on a *contingent* gate, ranking disclosure is *legibility without standing* on a function the firm still cannot contest — a clean §5.4 illustration.

**The platform-work directive is described accurately but incompletely (line 639–644).** Verified against Directive (EU) 2024/2831 (in force 1 Dec 2024; transposition deadline **2 Dec 2026** — so "now in transposition" is correct):

- Information rights over automated monitoring/decision systems (Art. 9) — correct.
- Right to human review of significant automated decisions (Art. 10) — correct.
- Limits/prohibitions on data the platform may process (Art. 7) — correct.
- **"A mandated channel for collective bargaining" — overstated.** Art. 25 requires Member States to **support and promote** collective bargaining in platform work; it does not mandate a bargaining channel. Soften to "measures to promote collective bargaining."
- **Omission that matters: the presumption of employment (Art. 5).** The directive's headline mechanism — a rebuttable legal presumption of employment status — goes unmentioned, and it sits awkwardly with the "voice within irreducible mediation" thesis in a way the chapter should confront, not dodge. Reclassification to employee status is arguably a move *out of* the platform triad and *into* hierarchy — an old coordination form restored, not voice built within the new one. A labour/platform referee will ask: is reclassification exit, voice, or neither in your scheme? Answering it (probably: reclassification changes the coordination logic rather than granting standing within the platform mediation, which is why the chapter rightly focuses on the algorithmic-management rights instead) would strengthen the diagnostic's coverage.

**Does the chapter overclaim what regulation cannot do? Yes, in one specific and fixable way.** §3.2 and §8 (line 682–684) say text-driven law "will continue to miss the layer at which co-optation operates." Given P2B, DSA Arts. 20–21 and 27, DMA Arts. 6(5)/(7), GDPR Art. 22, and platform-work Arts. 9–10, EU law is *demonstrably* reaching into the coordination layer — as embedded standing and contestation, not merely disclosure. The chapter's own prescription (build standing directly: contestation rights, collective-organization rights, governance channels) is *already the direction of travel* of the newest instruments. The honest and stronger claim is not "law cannot reach the layer" but "the *disclosure modality* of law cannot, and the *standing modality* — now emerging in DSA 20–21, GDPR 22, and platform-work Art. 10 — is the right one, and should be extended and aimed with the diagnostic." Reframing from "cannot" to "the emerging standing-instruments are the correct kind, under-built and mis-targeted" vindicates the thesis instead of inviting the referee to list counter-examples.

The openness/control reframe (line 686–702) — a closed sovereign national platform reproduces co-optation "under a domestic flag" — is legally resonant and lands the digital-sovereignty-vs-coordinative-sovereignty distinction well. Keep it.

## §9 Future research

Measurement program; not legal. The discriminant-validity worry (algorithmacy vs. Dogruel's algorithm-literacy scale; coordinative sovereignty vs. Spreitzer's psychological empowerment) is honest and well-posed. The two-step design (classify mediators, then measure whether response fits mediator type, with misallocation as a predictor) is the chapter's most promising empirical proposal and worth foregrounding. One governance addition: a **legal-instrument-to-mediator-type crosswalk** (which EU instrument bites on which of the four mediator kinds) would make the §8 policy claims testable and is the kind of table a law-review editor would ask for.

## §10 Conclusion

Recaps the argument cleanly. See Part 2 for the aphoristic-closer habit and the "neither reading alone nor control alone" echo.

# Verdict

**Major revision — strong accept on the idea, conditional on legal currency.** The conceptual apparatus (the necessary/contingent bypass test, the portfolio-of-mediators, the exit/voice sorting) is a real contribution to platform-governance scholarship and the writing is, by this repo's own standard, notably clean. Three fixes stand between it and a law-review-grade survey: (1) reckon with the in-force instruments the chapter's strong claim ignores — **P2B, DSA Arts. 20–21/27, DMA 6(5)/6(7), GDPR Art. 22, DGA** — and reframe "law cannot reach the layer" as "the disclosure modality cannot, and the emerging standing modality is the right one"; (2) correct the **§8 2025-ranking-transparency conflation** (verified: April 2025 was anti-steering, not ranking transparency) and the **Klonick 2018→2020** cite; (3) complete the **platform-work directive** (presumption of employment; soften the bargaining claim).

**Most important single fix:** the reframe in (1). It is not a retreat — it is the move that makes the thesis unfalsifiable-by-counterexample. Right now a referee refutes "law misses the layer" with a five-instrument list; after the reframe, that same list becomes the chapter's evidence that law is migrating from disclosure to standing and needs the diagnostic to aim it.

**Biggest strength:** the necessary/contingent diagnostic married to the portfolio-of-mediators. It gives the exit/voice tradition a computable sorting rule and gives a regulator a target-selection tool — "which instrument at which function" — that no current digital-sovereignty framework offers. The OTA and app-store worked cases (§5.4) are exemplary and legally accurate.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** the **instrument-to-mediator-type crosswalk** — a defended mapping of specific EU instruments onto the four mediator kinds (exit-restoring interoperability at contingent gates; access-restoring interoperability, GDPR-22 contestation, DSA 20–21, collective bargaining, and data trusts at necessary mediators), grounded in a worked classification of real platform functions. That table is where the chapter's policy claims become testable, and it depends on judgments — is fulfilment necessary, is matching partial — that only the author's formal companion work can adjudicate.
