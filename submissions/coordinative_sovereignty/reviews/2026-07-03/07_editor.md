The headline number the editor cares about: the sections that directly deliver "organizational implications of digital sovereignty" — §3, §5, §7, §8 — total roughly 5,060 words, about half the body. That is a healthy ratio. The political-theory and media-history material (§2 plus §6) totals roughly 2,300 words, under a quarter. The chapter does **not**, on aggregate, let the political theory crowd out the organizational payoff. The strain the abstract-review worried about is real but local, and it is about *placement and concentration*, not total budget. Details below.

### §1 Introduction — strong fit, keep

The opening does exactly what an edited-volume chapter should. It states the volume's own puzzle in the volume's own terms: a firm can comply with GDPR, the AI Act, the DMA, the DSA, and NIS2 and still lose its market position to ranking decisions it cannot see. The three concrete anchors — the independent software vendor, the GDPR-compliant merchant, the policy-abiding creator — land immediately for a management reader and are the right anchors for *this* book. The compliance-versus-capture gap is named cleanly and the two-sovereignty thesis (digital sovereignty asks who controls the infrastructure; coordinative sovereignty asks what autonomy remains) is previewed without jargon. This is a model first section for the audience. No change required.

One editorial note, not a defect: the neologism "algorithmacy" and the companion-paper citation arrive at line 41, before the reader has been given a reason to want a new word. That is a deliberate forward-reference and it is fine, but see the title discussion — the reader meets two coined terms (algorithmacy, coordinative sovereignty) in the first 800 words, and the chapter should make sure the ToC entry does not make that three.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities — the one place the balance strains

This is the section the editor flagged, and the flag is warranted, though not fatal. The oracy→literacy→algorithmacy spine is the chapter's organizing idea, and §2 is where it is laid down. The question is whether a 1,180-word runway of political and media history is the right thing to put between a management reader and the platform argument.

- **§2.1 Oracy and the oral order (324 words)** is the softest passage in the chapter for this volume's audience. It is pure pre-literate political anthropology — elders reciting law, witnessed oaths, custom held in living memory — with no organizational or digital content and no citation until the pattern is generalized. It earns a *conceptual* place: the oral case is where "a change of medium is a change in kind" can be shown "where the stakes are plain and the analytics simple," and that scaffolds the harder platform claim in §4. But a reader who picked this book up for digital sovereignty will feel the detour most here. Recommend compressing 2.1 by roughly a third and signposting its payoff earlier ("the oral case is the simple version of a pattern that returns, opaque, on platforms").
- **§2.2 Literacy and the democratic state (309 words)** is media theory — Scott, Habermas, Anderson, Innis, Eisenstein. Well-marshalled and genuinely load-bearing (persistence/portability/comparability is the property triple the whole "change in kind" argument reuses), but again no organizational content. It can lose ~50–80 words without losing the argument; the two-way "media and authority grow up together" qualifier (lines 104–108) is more caution than the chapter needs to spend here.
- **§2.3 Digital sovereignty as extended literacy (460 words)** is where §2 reconnects to the volume, and it is strong. Reading GDPR/AI Act/NIS2 as "technologies of reading and writing" is a genuinely useful reframe for the digital-sovereignty reader, and the "nesting" argument (each sovereignty layers on the old; the literate reflex is to demand more disclosure) is the intellectual payoff that justifies the whole section. Keep intact.

**Verdict on §2:** the spine earns its length by §2.3 and §4, but it is front-loaded. A management/IS reader hits ~1,640 words of history (Intro + §2) before §3 turns to platforms. Recommend trimming 2.1–2.2 by a combined ~150–200 words and adding one forward-pointing sentence at the top of §2 so the reader knows the payoff is coming. Do not cut the section; the "change in kind" argument does not survive without it.

### §3 The platform turn — strong fit, the argumentative hinge

Here the organizational argument arrives and the chapter is on home ground. The markets/hierarchies/networks setup (Powell, Williamson, Adler, Thompson) is exactly the frame this readership expects, and the handling of Stark and Vanden Broeck's co-optation is careful and honest: it concedes the obvious objection (platforms run the old logics in algorithmic form; "a fourth mechanism" overclaims) and relocates the novelty precisely — not a new coordination logic, not a new topology (Simmel, Burt, Obstfeld), but the conjunction of an *opaque* and *interested, adaptive* mediator. That is a disciplined move and it will satisfy the organization-theory readers on the panel.

- **§3.1 The mediated triad (195 words)** — the driver/rider/system vignette is concrete and correct. Keep.
- **§3.2 Why literacy cannot reach it (528 words)** is the hinge of the whole chapter, and it is the best-cited passage in the manuscript: Burrell's three opacities, Ananny and Crawford on transparency without an institution, Cutolo and Kenney on platform-dependence, Rahman's invisible cage, Hildebrandt on text- vs code-driven law. The four-part structure (object resists reading / reading without a channel / no leverage / regulation built for text) is the load-bearing argument for the volume's central worry. This is the section other contributors will cite. Keep intact.
- **§3.3 The political economy of interested mediation (316 words)** — the two-sided-markets / competitive-bottleneck framing (Rochet & Tirole, Armstrong & Wright) adds the economics the management reader will want and closes with the sharpest single line in the chapter: a transparent bottleneck is still a bottleneck. Keep.

### §4 Algorithmacy — strong fit, most accessible passage

The three-part competence (inferential, translational, temporal) is defined cleanly and positioned honestly against the adjacent literatures (Dogruel on algorithmic literacy; Pangrazio & Selwyn and Sander on critical data literacy; DeVito and Eslami on folk theories; Möhlmann on algorithm sensemaking; Bucher on anticipatory compliance). The distinction — spectatorship versus working the system from inside, "reading a system from the outside and working it from within" — is the right cut and it is defended, not merely asserted.

The **marketplace-seller vignette** (lines 296–316, "one morning her orders halve") is the most accessible and persuasive passage in the manuscript and the one a management reader will remember. It makes an abstract competence tangible. Keep it and consider foregrounding it earlier in the section; right now the reader gets two abstract passes at the three-part definition before the vignette makes it concrete. Minor length concern: the abstract definition (lines 262–284) and the vignette partly restate each other; ~100 words could come out of the pre-vignette exposition.

### §5 A diagnostic — the distinctive contribution, one dependency to flag

This is the chapter's original engine and its most decision-useful part for the volume. The necessary/contingent/partial/reducible typology, the bypass counterfactual (restore the forbidden direct tie and recompute whether the mediator still binds), and the mapping onto Hirschman's exit/voice are a genuine contribution, and the "a platform is a portfolio of mediators, classify functions not firms" move (§5.4) is precisely the kind of actionable framework an organizational reader can use. The payment-network, marketplace, app-store, and ride-hail worked cases are well-chosen.

Two editorial flags:

1. **The formal backing is off-page.** §5.1 rests the "does the coordination factor / is it genuinely triadic" question on "the integrated-information measures that make the check exact" developed in the companion paper (Author, 2026). Standing alone in this volume, the chapter asserts that irreducibility is "computable" and that a formal measure grades borderline cases, but supplies only the informal bypass counterfactual. For a reader who cannot see the companion work, the strongest claim (computability) is taken on trust. This is not a self-citation objection — it is a standalone-completeness concern. Recommend one or two sentences that make the bypass test self-sufficient *as presented*, so the chapter's diagnostic does not depend on an unseen paper for its central adjective ("computable"). The chapter already half-does this ("the runnable counterfactual of the next section is enough") — lean into that and claim slightly less for the off-page apparatus.

2. **The hotel/OTA case carries uncited empirical numbers** (see Part 2, VERIFY list). The case is excellent rhetorically; it needs sourcing.

### §6 Coordinative sovereignty: a definition — necessary, but the second-heaviest theory load

The definition itself is crisp and the four converging strands (republican non-domination via Pettit/Skinner and Muldoon & Raekstad; relational autonomy via Mackenzie/Stoljar and Nedelsky; Markell's insufficiency-of-non-domination correction; Ostrom's commons governance) are the right four and are handled with real command. The neighbor-distinguishing paragraph (Bannerman's relational sovereignty; Pasquale's functional sovereignty; Cohen's platform sovereign; Repetto's concept-stretching charge) is exactly the due diligence a crowded-construct paper owes, and it will reassure the IS/digital-sovereignty scholars this chapter must sit beside that the author knows the neighborhood.

The concern is load, not quality. After §2's history, §6 is the second sustained demand on a management reader's tolerance for political theory, and it comes late, when the reader wants the institutions. The three "clarifications" (more than worker rights; transparency does not produce it; not anti-platform) are valuable and should stay. But the republican/relational-autonomy quartet could be tightened by ~100–150 words — each strand currently gets a full mini-exposition where a claim-first sentence plus the citation would carry it for this audience. This is the best place, after §2.1, to recover length.

### §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty — strong fit, keep

The institutional survey (platform cooperativism/Scholz; data trusts/Delacroix & Lawrence, Micheli; collective bargaining and works councils; contestability/Alfrink; interoperability and portability as *exit* moves; oversight boards/Klonick; regulation via the DSA and the platform-work directive) is concrete, current, and correctly sorted by the §5 diagnostic (exit-restoring instruments at contingent functions, voice-building institutions at necessary ones). The "institutions compose / no single instrument delivers it / the diagnostic keeps the stack aimed" close is a strong, useful ending for the practitioner reader. This is the section that makes the chapter *do organizational work*. Keep intact.

### §8 Implications — the volume's payoff, strongest fit

If the editor had to point the volume's audience at one section, it is this one. The scholars/managers/policymakers structure is conventional and, here, fully earned. The central empirical claim — digital sovereignty is a literacy construct, coordinative sovereignty an algorithmacy construct, and a firm can be "impeccably compliant and strategically captured" — is stated with admirable clarity and illustrated by the independent-variation cases (high-DS/low-CS app developer; low-infrastructure/rising-standing platform worker under the 2024 directive). The SME framing ("falls hardest on the small and medium-sized enterprises") is exactly the organizational payoff the volume promises. The openness-versus-control reframe at the end (a walled national platform whose users have no voice "reproduces the co-optation it was meant to escape, now under a domestic flag") is a genuinely useful contribution to the digital-sovereignty debate and the kind of line the volume's introduction will want to quote. Keep. One number needs sourcing (the "market-conduct remedies of 2025," Part 2).

### §9 Future research directions — good fit, appropriately modest

Four measurement directions in dependence order, honest that neither construct yet has a validated instrument, and specific about the hard part (discriminant validity against algorithmic-literacy scales and against psychological empowerment/Spreitzer). The behavioral-anchoring suggestion is sensible. The fourth direction (correspondence between reported standing and a formal-model structural property) again leans on the companion work but is framed as a virtue of the construct, which is fair. Appropriate altitude — confident about the agenda, modest about what is proven. Keep.

### §10 Conclusion — competent, lightly over-argued

Does its job. The "algorithmate few / constitutive priesthood" image is vivid and ties back to the literacy-diffusion theme. The one editorial caution: §10 re-states the exit/voice and two-sovereignty conclusions in near-final-section-of-§8 language; trim any sentence that re-argues rather than recalls. The final line ("Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it") is a clean close.

### Title check

Current: *Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era.*

For an edited-volume table of contents, this title has a discoverability problem. It leads with a neologism ("algorithmacy") that no browsing reader will recognize, and the phrase carrying the volume's own topic — **digital sovereignty** — does not appear at all, nor does any organizational hook. A reader scanning the ToC of *Organizational Implications of Digital Sovereignty in the Age of AI* cannot tell that this is the chapter that reframes digital sovereignty for platform-dependent firms. Recommend a title that keeps the coinage but earns it against a recognizable term and an organizational anchor, e.g.:

- *Beyond Digital Sovereignty: Coordinative Sovereignty and the Algorithmacy Platform-Dependent Organizations Need*
- *When Compliance Is Not Enough: Coordinative Sovereignty for Platform-Dependent Organizations*

The author should decide, but the current title should not go to press unchanged.

## PART 2 — Register-aware slop audit

The editor's standing worry with LLM-assisted submissions is the antithesis metronome, agentless passive, and uniform closers. This manuscript is, by that measure, **notably clean** — cleaner than most submissions in this volume's queue. The deliberate impersonal register is not mistaken for slop below.

**Antithesis machine — largely absent.** A scan of the whole body returns only two `, not` constructions and one `rather than`, and zero `is not a/the/an`. That is well under the house threshold and far below the machine-cadence baseline. The ~68 negations in the text ("cannot," "does not," "could not") are overwhelmingly legitimate propositional negations, not the define-by-opposition tic. This is a genuine strength; the author has controlled the dominant slop signature.

**Agentless passive — four real tells, all fixable.** These are the machine fingerprints an editor should ask the author to convert to named-agent active voice. Each has an obvious agent already available in the sentence:

1. **Line 275** — "Algorithmic literacy **is theorized as** awareness, understanding, and skill in interacting with algorithmic systems (Dogruel et al., 2022)." Agent (Dogruel et al.) is right there.
2. **Line 398** — "The framework **has been applied** to platforms before, to the constrained voice of platform workers (Dasgupta et al., 2025) and to the politics of exit (Smith & Burrows, 2021)." Agents in the parentheticals.
3. **Line 508** — "The digital-sovereignty literature **has been charged** with concept stretching … (Repetto, 2025)." Agent (Repetto) is the citation.
4. **Line 647** — "Algorithmacy **has been named** as a competence but has no validated instrument." Off-limits if the naming is the companion work's; rewrite to name the agent truthfully (see Part 3).

Rewrites for all four in Part 3.

**Uniform closers — mild, deliberate, watch it.** Several sections end on a compressed aphoristic sentence: "…what the argument turns on" (§1), "…mistaken the necessary function for the contingent one" (§5.4), "…what standing will the people coordinated through it hold" (§8), "Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it" (§10). Individually each is good. As a repeated shape it becomes a faint metronome. This is a rhythm preference, not a defect; flagging so the author can vary one or two so the aphoristic close does not read as a template. No action required for acceptance.

**Self-narrating honesty / rigor phrases — none found.** The manuscript does not announce its own care ("stated plainly," "to be clear," "the honest version," etc.). Clean.

**Fragment openers / punchy-opener metronome — under control.** A few short blunt sentences appear ("One firm, four kinds of mediator at once."), used sparingly and effectively, not as the shape of every paragraph. Within tolerance.

**Em-dashes — 36 across ~10,300 words**, roughly one per 290 words, well within one-per-paragraph. Not a pile-up problem.

**VERIFY — four uncited empirical claims (editorial, must be resolved before press).** These are factual assertions presented without a source. The editor is not asserting they are wrong; they must be sourced or softened. Never invent citations to fill these.

1. **Line 451 — "an online travel agency that charges fifteen to thirty percent commission."** VERIFY and cite an OTA-commission source.
2. **Line 456 — "the rate-parity bans enacted in several European jurisdictions."** VERIFY; name jurisdictions or cite.
3. **Line 458 — "Direct-booking shares climb to roughly a third and then plateau."** VERIFY; this is a specific empirical trajectory doing rhetorical work in the flagship case and needs a source.
4. **Line 637 — "The market-conduct remedies of 2025 reached platforms' anti-steering rules and mandated ranking transparency."** VERIFY; identify the remedy/instrument or attribute it.

The platform-work directive references (lines 606, 642–643) *are* sourced to Directive (EU) 2024/2831 and are fine.

## VERDICT

**Accept with minor-to-moderate revisions.** This is a well-argued, well-cited, template-conformant chapter that fits the volume better than its title advertises. It is not a "major revisions" case: the argument is sound, the structure is complete, the register is clean, and there are no substantive analytical defects. The revisions are bounded and the author can complete them without new research.

**Conditions for acceptance:**

1. **Source or soften the four VERIFY claims** (Part 2), especially the hotel/OTA figures carrying the flagship case in §5.4 and the "market-conduct remedies of 2025" in §8. Non-negotiable before press.
2. **Convert the four agentless-passive tells** to named-agent active voice (Part 3). Trivial.
3. **Trim the front-loaded runway.** Compress §2.1–2.2 by ~150–200 words with an earlier signpost to their payoff, and tighten §6's political-theory quartet by ~100–150 words. Net target: recover ~300 words from the two theory-heavy stretches and let the organizational sections carry more of the reader's attention. The chapter stays comfortably over 10,000 words after this.
4. **Revise the title** so the ToC entry names digital sovereignty and an organizational anchor rather than leading with an unglossed neologism (Part 1, Title check).
5. **Make the §5 diagnostic self-sufficient as presented** — claim slightly less for the off-page "computable" apparatus and lean on the runnable bypass counterfactual the chapter already supplies.

Items 1–2 are required; 3–5 are strongly recommended and, in the editor's judgment, will materially improve how the chapter sits beside the IS and digital-sovereignty contributions.

**Will it sit beside the other chapters?** Yes. The jargon load is real (two coinages, a republican/relational-autonomy theory layer, an integrated-information gesture) but it is managed: every coined term is defined at first use and again in Key Terms, and the concrete anchors (app store, marketplace seller, hotel/OTA, driver/rider) keep the abstractions grounded. A digital-sovereignty or IS scholar will find the neighbor-distinguishing (§6) and the DS/CS disaggregation (§8) directly usable. Accessibility is adequate; the compression asks above will improve it.

**Biggest strength:** The two-sovereignty disaggregation delivered through the necessary/contingent diagnostic. The chapter takes a construct the field measures as one thing — organizational digital sovereignty — and splits it into a *literacy* capability (read and control your data/infrastructure/code) and an *algorithmacy* standing (voice within the coordination you cannot leave), then supplies a decision procedure (the bypass test, the portfolio-of-mediators view) that tells a manager which of exit or voice to pursue against which platform function. That is a real, teachable, citable contribution, and it is exactly the "organizational implication" the volume exists to publish. §8's "impeccably compliant and strategically captured" is the sentence the volume's introduction should quote.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** the empirical grounding for the flagship §5.4 hotel/OTA case and the §8 "2025 market-conduct remedies" — the specific commission range, the rate-parity-ban jurisdictions, the direct-booking-plateau figure, and the identity of the 2025 remedy. These numbers are doing persuasive work in the chapter's most concrete illustrations, and the editor cannot source them on the author's behalf without risking an invented citation. The author must either attach real sources or convert the claims to sourced ranges/qualified statements. Everything else in the required list is craft the author can finish in an afternoon; this one needs the author's own references.
