# Peer Review — Media / Literacy-Studies Lens

**Manuscript:** *Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era*
**Reviewer frame:** orality–literacy studies (Ong, Goody, Scribner & Cole, Street); media theory (Innis, Eisenstein, Benjamin); Habermas; Anderson; algorithmic/critical-data literacy (Dogruel, Pangrazio & Selwyn, Sander)
**Recommendation:** Major revision — accept in principle; the spine is sound and the one careful move (coordination-not-cognition) is exactly right, but the field it borrows from will demand it be pinned harder in two places.

---

## Step 0 — Register

Register is a correctly executed impersonal academic chapter: named agents in active voice ("the chapter argues," "Ong shows," "Scribner & Cole … showed"), claim-first, concrete anchors, APA 7, double-anonymized with the "Author, 2026" self-cites intact and off-limits. The house-style invariants are largely met; the antithesis machine is nearly absent (a genuine achievement — see Part 2), and the residual slop is a single pocket of agentless passive in §2.1 plus one four-times-verbatim definitional refrain. No register objection.

## Part 1 — Theory & structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction

The opening does real work: the compliance-yet-helpless paradox is the right hook, and the three anchors (app-store vendor, marketplace merchant, recommendation-orphaned creator) are concrete and well chosen. The load-bearing sentence for this reviewer is at lines 36–41: "Each historical form of sovereignty was paired with, and made possible by, a communicative sensibility." That is the media-theory thesis in one line, and it is stated as settled fact. It is not settled — it is a strong Innis-style claim about the co-constitution of media and power, and the chapter should signal here (not only in §4, 300 lines later) that it holds the *modest* version. As written, a media referee meets the boldest form of the claim first and the qualification last, which inverts the honest order.

Minor: "Their market positions are decided somewhere else" (line 32) is agentless passive in the one clause where naming the agent is the whole point — the platform decides. Fix in Part 2.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities — **weighted section**

This is where a literacy-studies referee spends the most time, and it is where the chapter is simultaneously most attractive and most exposed.

**§2.1 Oracy.** The portrait is vivid and, at the level of coordination, defensible. The problem is that it is written in **Great Divide prose** — precisely the register Scribner & Cole and Street spent careers dismantling. "Law was what the elders could recite… Beyond its range it simply stopped. An oral order could not lay its law down in a fixed text, check today's ruling against last year's, or transmit a rule past the reach of living memory" (lines 67–74). Every clause here is an *autonomous-model* claim: it attributes cognitive and institutional limits to the medium itself. The chapter will disavow exactly this move in §4 ("its strong version, that writing restructures the mind, did not survive the ethnographers"). So §2.1 and §4 are in tension: §2.1 *performs* the Great Divide, §4 *repudiates* it. A Street-aligned referee will catch the seam immediately. The fix is not to soften the vividness but to relocate the limits from the medium to the *practices the medium sustains at scale* — the same repair the chapter already knows how to make. (Goody 1977 and Ong 1982 are cited accurately; the issue is tonal register, not miscitation.)

**§2.2 Literacy.** The three properties — persistence, portability, comparability (lines 89–91) — are handled well and are the correct affordances to name. But note for the record: **this triad is itself the contested autonomous-model list.** It is essentially Goody's "technologies of the intellect." The chapter later claims the ideological/practice view (§4), yet its engine in §2.2 is the affordance-list view. The honest reconciliation is available and cheap: affordances are *potentials* realized only in situated institutions (the archive, the census, the press), which is in fact how the chapter uses them — "The modern state is a creature of the archive, the census, and the code of law" (line 92, Scott 1998, apt). One added clause tying the properties to their institutional carriers would inoculate the section against the New Literacy Studies objection.

*Innis (1951)* — cited accurately but generically ("a civilization's dominant medium biases its characteristic form of power," line 102–103). Innis's actual distinction — time-biased/durable media favor tradition and decentralized authority, space-biased/light media favor administration and empire — is *directly usable* by this chapter and is left on the table. The algorithmic medium is the space-biased case par excellence (real-time, control-favoring, administratively centralizing). Invoking Innis's specific axis rather than a generic paraphrase would sharpen the "what is the bias of the algorithmic medium" question the chapter never quite asks. Missed opportunity, not an error.

*Eisenstein (1979)* — accurate and standard (printing press reorganized the authority of church and state, line 104). Fine as used. One richer thread is unused: Eisenstein's core mechanism is the *breaking of the clerical monopoly* on scripture — the de-monopolization of a competence. The chapter's own §10 "constitutive priesthood" and §4 "clerical few" motifs are reaching for exactly this, and Eisenstein is the citation that would ground the democratization mechanism (see the diffusion critique below). Connect them.

*Habermas (1962/1989)* — cited correctly for the *rise* of the bourgeois public sphere (line 96), but used as a one-line prop. Two problems a Habermas referee will raise. (1) The chapter cites only the ascent and ignores the *refeudalization* half of Habermas's own argument — which is the more obviously platform-relevant half, since the platform diagnosis (opaque, commercially steered, re-privatized publicity) is close to a re-feudalization of the public sphere. (2) There is a live literature on the platform-era public sphere directly on this axis that the chapter does not touch. If the author wants to cite it, VERIFY the specific source before adding (e.g., Habermas's later reflections on the digital public sphere; Bennett & Pfetsch on disrupted public spheres — reviewer has not confirmed exact refs and will not invent them). At minimum, cite the refeudalization thesis the chapter is implicitly re-running.

*Anderson (1983/2006)* — accurate (print-borne imagining underwrote the nation-state, line 96–97). Used as decoration. The unforced extension — do platforms manufacture *algorithmic* imagined communities / publics? — is left unmade. Legitimate to omit, but a media referee will note that Anderson is invoked for prestige and then dropped.

*Benjamin* — **absent.** The brief lists him; the chapter does not use him, and the references do not include him. This reviewer's honest judgment: Benjamin is *not required*, and forcing "mechanical/algorithmic reproducibility" or an "aura" analog would be ornament. But a Benjamin-steeped referee will ask, so the author should be ready to say in the response letter why Benjamin is not load-bearing here (the argument is about coordination and authority, not about reproduction and authenticity). Do not bolt Benjamin on to satisfy a checklist.

**§2.3 Digital sovereignty as extended literacy.** The nesting argument (lines 126–144) is the structural highlight of the section and genuinely good: literacy nests on oracy, digital sovereignty nests on literacy, "the one completed change in kind is the first, from speech to writing: a scribe is a different creature from a better speaker" (line 129). This is the right way to make the "change in kind" claim survive media-determinism scrutiny — it locates the discontinuity in *one* place and treats the rest as extension. The diagnosis of "the characteristic error" — meeting a new medium with the old sensibility, demanding "more disclosure, more documentation, more rights to read" (lines 135–140) — is the chapter's sharpest single idea and lands cleanly.

**Overall §2 verdict:** the through-line is defensible but currently *too neat in tone and not quite honest about its own machinery.* The neatness is fixable with two admissions the chapter can well afford: (a) §2.1's limits belong to practices, not to the oral medium; (b) §2.2's three properties are the autonomous-model list, redeemed only by their institutional carriers. Make those two admissions and the New Literacy Studies referee has nothing left to swing at.

### §3 The platform turn

Strong. The three-way disaggregation — coordination logic (old), topology (old, Simmel 1908/1950; Burt 2004; Obstfeld 2005), and the *conjunction* of opacity + interested adaptivity (new) — is the correct way to defeat the "algorithmic management is just hierarchy in new clothes" objection (Kellogg et al. 2020; Vallas & Schor 2020), and the chapter states the objection fairly before answering it (lines 158–178). The Selznick (1949) co-optation contrast is handled with care ("Selznick's co-opted gained a limited seat, the platform-enrolled gain no seat and no share, only a determination," lines 154–156). §3.2's four structural reasons literacy cannot reach the layer (Burrell 2016 on opacity; Ananny & Crawford 2018 on transparency-without-standing; Rahman 2021 invisible cage; Hildebrandt 2015 text- vs code-driven law) are all correctly deployed — Burrell's third opacity (intrinsic to ML scale) and Ananny & Crawford's transparency critique are the two most important citations in the whole chapter for this reviewer's frame, and both are used exactly right.

One flag: §2.1 agentless-passive cluster aside, §3 leans hard on "produced by the system's ordinary operation" (line 178) and "its determinations are produced by high-dimensional systems" (line 172). Two "is produced" passives in one section; the second could name the model as agent.

### §4 Algorithmacy — **most weighted section**

This is the make-or-break section for a literacy-studies referee, because the entire question is whether **algorithmacy differs in kind from algorithmic/critical-data literacy or is a relabeling.** The chapter's answer has three moves; two work and one is currently under-defended.

**Move 1 — the coordination-not-cognition pin (lines 330–342): correct and the best thing in the chapter.** "The claim that a change of communicative medium is a change in kind descends from the orality-literacy scholarship (Goody, 1977; Ong, 1982), and its strong version, that writing restructures the mind, did not survive the ethnographers, who showed literacy's cognitive effects to be practice-specific and socially embedded (Scribner & Cole, 1981; Street, 1984). The claim advanced here concerns coordination, whatever the truth about individual cognition." This is exactly the right disavowal, exactly the right citations, and it pins the kind-claim to the *infrastructural/coordination* register where it is defensible. Scribner & Cole are cited for precisely what they showed (the Vai study: literacy effects are script- and practice-specific), and Street for the practice-embeddedness that undercuts the autonomous model. **Credit where due: many chapters in this space cite Ong and Goody and never notice they were refuted; this one notices and routes around the refutation correctly.** Keep this paragraph almost verbatim; it is the chapter's insurance policy.

**Move 2 — the empirical grounding (lines 286–306): good.** Anchoring the inferential part in Möhlmann et al. (2023) "algorithm sensemaking," the translational part in Bucher et al. (2021) "anticipatory compliance," and adding DeVito (2021) / Eslami et al. (2016) folk theories, shows command of the HCI/algorithmic-management field and keeps the construct from floating free. The marketplace-seller worked example (lines 296–316) is the best illustration in the chapter.

**Move 3 — the differentiation from algorithmic literacy (lines 274–284): the weak point, and it is the one a Dogruel/Pangrazio referee will attack.** Two problems:

1. **The "spectatorship" characterization is unfair to Pangrazio & Selwyn and Sander, and the chapter half-knows it.** Line 278: "These competences mostly describe spectatorship: recognizing that a system is at work, interpreting its outputs, reflecting on its effects." But the chapter's *own* preceding sentence quotes critical data literacy as "the capacity to identify how one's data are used *and to act on that knowledge* (Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2019; Sander, 2020)" (lines 277–278). Pangrazio & Selwyn's "personal data literacies" and Sander's "critical big data literacy" are explicitly *tactical and action-oriented* — that is their whole point against the skills-based model. Calling them "spectatorship" and then, one clause earlier, quoting their action dimension, is a contradiction the referee will circle. Fix: concede that critical-data-literacy already reaches for action, and locate the difference elsewhere (see below), rather than by demoting the neighbors to mere watching.

2. **The genuine differentiator is triadic + feedback, but §4 exemplifies it dyadically.** The construct is *defined* triadically — coordinating with a counterpart through a system that "interprets both sides" (lines 265–266). But every worked illustration in §4 is *dyadic*: the seller versus the ranking system. The buyer (the counterpart) is nearly invisible in the seller vignette (lines 296–316) and in the halved-orders vignette (lines 308–316). If the counterpart does no phenomenological work in the competence's own examples, a referee will conclude that algorithmacy-the-competence is just algorithm sensemaking (Möhlmann) + anticipatory compliance (Bucher) + adaptive folk theorization (DeVito) + a temporal dimension — i.e., a relabeling of documented practices with one addition. The triad is load-bearing for the *sovereignty* argument (§5–§6) but currently absent from the *competence* argument (§4). **This is the single most important theoretical fix.** Either (a) supply one worked example where the counterpart's presence changes what the actor must infer/translate (the driver who must model how the system reads *the rider* as well as herself), or (b) explicitly concede that the competence's novelty over algorithm sensemaking is the *temporal* dimension plus the *triadic embedding*, and carry that concession into the response letter.

**A deeper, friendlier warning.** The move the chapter makes to distinguish algorithmacy — "Reading a system from the outside and working it from within are different acts, and the difference is one of kind" (lines 283–284) — is *itself the New Literacy Studies thesis* about literacy. Street's entire argument is that literacy is never "reading from outside"; it is always working-from-within a social practice. So the inside/outside axis the chapter uses to separate algorithmacy from algorithmic literacy is an axis literacy studies already collapsed *within* literacy. A sharp referee (Street-aligned) can therefore say: "What you call algorithmacy is just algorithmic literacy understood *ideologically*, as embedded practice — which Pangrazio & Selwyn already do." The defense exists and the chapter should state it: the difference is not inside-vs-outside (that collapses) but the **optimization feedback loop** — your own moves become the system's inputs and are read back to you, and you are coordinating with a counterpart the system also reads. That structural feature has no analog in text literacy (a statute does not re-tune itself to your reading of it), and it, not "working from within," is what earns "change in kind."

**Terminology aid the chapter should adopt:** name Street's **autonomous vs. ideological model** explicitly. The chapter enacts the ideological model throughout §4 without naming it. Naming it (one sentence) both signals command of the field and pre-empts the relabeling charge, because it lets the author say "algorithmacy is the ideological-model reading of algorithm-facing competence, extended to the triad."

### §5 The diagnostic (necessary/contingent)

Outside this reviewer's core frame, but two media/literacy-adjacent notes. The exit/voice mapping onto contingent/necessary (Hirschman 1970) is elegant and the "portfolio of mediators" move (§5.4) is the practically strongest idea in the back half. The hotel/OTA billboard-vs-parity example (lines 450–461) is excellent and concrete. The one caution: §5.1's "does some cut of the modeled system lose causal information" (line 361) imports the companion paper's IIT machinery by gesture; for a media/literacy audience this is a black box, and the chapter is right to keep the formal apparatus offstage ("the runnable counterfactual of the next section is enough," line 365). Do not add more formalism here; the audience does not want it.

### §6 Coordinative sovereignty — definition

The four-strand convergence (non-domination, Pettit/Skinner; relational autonomy, Mackenzie & Stoljar/Nedelsky; Markell's insufficiency-of-non-domination; Ostrom's commons) is well marshalled and the neighbor-distinguishing (Bannerman 2024 relational sovereignty; Muldoon & Raekstad 2022; Pasquale 2023; Cohen 2017) is fair and precise. The concept-stretching charge (Repetto 2025) is met head-on, which a referee respects. For this reviewer's frame the relevant line is 539–545: the sensibility-as-precondition-of-sovereignty argument closes the oracy→literacy→algorithmacy loop cleanly ("Oracy made a member of an oral order capable of holding it to account. Literacy made a citizen capable of auditing a literate state. Algorithmacy makes a coordinated actor capable of the standing that coordinative sovereignty defines"). Good. One overreach to check: this triadic historical parallel treats oracy and literacy as *population-general* competences and algorithmacy as their heir, but algorithmacy as defined is a *strategic* competence of platform-dependent actors, not a general condition of citizenship. See the diffusion critique under §10.

### §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty

Institutionally thorough (platform cooperativism, Scholz 2016; data trusts, Delacroix & Lawrence 2019; collective bargaining; contestability, Alfrink et al. 2023; interoperability; oversight boards, Klonick 2018; DSA/platform-work directive). The Ananny & Crawford failure ("transparency without an institution to act on it yields nothing, and a bargaining unit is such an institution," lines 578–580) is the correct spine for the section. Not this reviewer's core frame; no objection.

### §8 Implications

The digital-sovereignty-is-literacy / coordinative-sovereignty-is-algorithmacy split (lines 628–631) is the payoff of the whole media analogy and it is stated well. The independent-variation cases (high digital / low coordinative = compliant-but-captured seller; low digital / high coordinative = directive-protected worker) are concrete and persuasive. Flag: "Organizational digital sovereignty is typically operationalized as a single capability" (line 626) is agentless passive where the field is the agent — fix in Part 2.

### §9 Future research

Honest about the measurement gap (no validated instrument for either construct). The discriminant-validity worry against Dogruel et al.'s algorithm-literacy scale (lines 709–718) is exactly the right worry and shows the author understands what a literacy referee would demand empirically: prove algorithmacy is not just algorithm-literacy-rescored. The behavioral-criterion anchor (prediction accuracy scored against system behavior) is a good concrete proposal. This section quietly answers the §4 relabeling charge on the empirical side; consider forward-referencing it from §4.

### §10 Conclusion

Rhetorically strong; the "constitutive priesthood" close (line 776) lands. But it exposes the **one theoretical debt the chapter never pays: the diffusion mechanism.** The conclusion rests the stakes on whether "algorithmacy can be democratized as literacy was" (line 775). But mass literacy did not diffuse on its own — it diffused through *mass schooling, print capitalism, and the nation-state* (the very Eisenstein/Anderson machinery §2.2 cites and then drops). The chapter borrows the *prestige* of the literacy-diffusion analogy without borrowing its *mechanism*. A media-history referee will ask directly: what is the equivalent carrier for algorithmacy — what plays the role print capitalism and compulsory schooling played for literacy? §7's institutions gesture at it, but the chapter never connects the institutional survey back to the historical diffusion analogy. Closing that loop (even in two sentences: "literacy diffused through school and press; algorithmacy will diffuse, if at all, through X") would convert the conclusion from evocative to argued.

## Part 2 — Style / slop audit (register-aware)

The chapter is unusually clean against the house tics. The antithesis machine is essentially defeated — grep for `, not` / `rather than` / `is not a/the/an` / `It is not` across 1,084 lines returns only four hits, all legitimate contrasts a reader would actually reach for (line 423 "functions, not firms"; 553 "not yet an institution"; 757 "does not present authority as a text"; 1059 glossary). No self-narrating-honesty phrases. No em-dash pile-ups. This section therefore reports the *residual* slop, which is small and localized.

**Offender 1 — agentless-passive cluster in §2.1 (the one real pocket).** Lines 67–69:

> "Before writing, authority was carried in speech, memory, and presence. … Oaths bound because they were witnessed, and … disputes were settled in the hearing of the community."

Failure: three agentless passives in three lines ("was carried," "were witnessed," "were settled"), in the one section whose whole point is that named human agents — elders, witnesses, the community — *are* the medium. The passive erases the agents the paragraph is about.

Rewrite (impersonal-agentive):
> "Before writing, speech, memory, and presence carried authority. … An oath bound because the community witnessed it, and the community settled disputes in the hearing of its members."

**Offender 2 — agentless passive erasing the deciding agent, §1.** Line 32:

> "Their market positions are decided somewhere else, inside the algorithmic systems through which the platform chooses what to surface and what to bury."

Failure: "are decided" hides the platform in the exact clause whose claim is that *the platform decides*. The second half names the agent, so the passive opener is pure drag.

Rewrite:
> "The platform's algorithmic systems decide their market positions somewhere else, choosing what to surface and what to bury."

**Offender 3 — agentless passive where the field is the agent, §8.** Line 626:

> "Organizational digital sovereignty is typically operationalized as a single capability built from regulatory and infrastructural components (Fratini et al., 2024; …)."

Rewrite:
> "Current research typically operationalizes organizational digital sovereignty as a single capability built from regulatory and infrastructural components (Fratini et al., 2024; …)."

**Offender 4 — "is theorized" / "is produced," minor.** Line 275 "Algorithmic literacy is theorized as…" → "Dogruel et al. (2022) theorize algorithmic literacy as…" (also strengthens attribution). Lines 172 and 178 both use "is/are produced by the system"; keep one, reword the other to name the model as agent.

**Offender 5 — verbatim repetition of the definitional triad (the one repetition tic).** The refrain "shape the terms … contest the mediator's arbitrary power … hold a share in the governance" appears near-verbatim four times: abstract (line 11), §1 (line 51), §6 definition (line 472), glossary (line 1046). The definition (§6) and glossary need it; the abstract and §1 do not both need the full three-part form. Vary one of the two early statements to a compressed form ("standing to shape, contest, and co-govern a mediation it cannot leave") so the full triad reads as a definition being introduced, not a slogan being repeated.

**Not-slop, keep:** "She has read nothing. There was nothing to read." (§4) — earned, effective. "A scribe is a different creature from a better speaker." (§2.3) — the best sentence in the chapter. "A transparent bottleneck is still a bottleneck." (§3.3) — legitimate single contrast.

   > Critical data literacy already reaches past recognition toward action, the capacity to identify how one's data are used and to act on it (Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2019; Sander, 2020). A statute does not re-tune itself to how one reads it. This system does, and that feedback, not a move from watching to doing, is what makes the competence differ in kind.

8. **§4, line 275** — attribute actively: "Dogruel et al. (2022) theorize algorithmic literacy as awareness, understanding, and skill in interacting with algorithmic systems."

9. **§8, line 626** — "Current research typically operationalizes organizational digital sovereignty as a single capability…"

10. **§10, after line 776** — pay the diffusion debt:

## Verdict

**Major revision, accept in principle.** The three-sensibility spine is a genuine contribution and the chapter's central act of scholarly care — pinning the "change in kind" claim to *coordination* rather than *cognition*, and citing Scribner & Cole and Street for exactly the refutation it routes around — is done correctly, which is rare in work that reaches for Ong and Goody. The chapter earns its media-history frame. It does not yet fully pay for it.

**Most important fix:** in §4, defeat the relabeling charge properly. Right now algorithmacy is *defined* triadically but *illustrated* dyadically (seller vs. ranking system, counterpart invisible), and it is differentiated from Pangrazio & Selwyn / Sander by calling them "spectatorship" one clause after quoting their action dimension. Relocate the kind-difference from "inside vs. outside" (an axis New Literacy Studies already collapsed within literacy) to the **optimization feedback loop plus triadic embedding** — your moves become the system's inputs, read back to you, while you coordinate with a counterpart the system also reads — and supply one worked example where the counterpart actually does work. That single repair converts algorithmacy from "documented practices, relabeled" into a defensible change in kind.

## Biggest strength

The nesting argument and its payoff: oracy → literacy → digital-sovereignty-as-extended-literacy, with the one true discontinuity located at speech-to-writing ("a scribe is a different creature from a better speaker"), and the resulting diagnosis of the *characteristic error* — meeting a new medium by demanding "more disclosure, more documentation, more rights to read, because reading is what the literate know how to do" (§2.3). That diagnosis is the chapter's most original media-theoretic move and it reframes the entire European-regulation debate in one paragraph.

## The one thing only the author can supply

The **diffusion mechanism** for algorithmacy. The whole argument rests its stakes on the analogy "algorithmacy is now what literacy was then" and asks whether it "can be democratized as literacy was" (§10). But mass literacy diffused through identifiable carriers — the printing press, print capitalism, compulsory schooling, the nation-state — which are the very Eisenstein/Anderson machinery §2.2 cites and abandons. Only the author can decide and state what plays that role for algorithmacy: is it the §7 institutions, is it schooling, is it a new professional class, or is there structurally *no* mass carrier because the competence is capital-intensive and rivalrous in a way literacy was not (a possibility §4's "distributed like capital" passage quietly raises and never resolves)? The historical analogy is doing the chapter's rhetorical heavy lifting; the author must either honor it with a mechanism or openly disown the mass-diffusion promise and rest on the narrower, still-strong claim that algorithmacy is an unevenly held strategic competence whose distribution is now a political question.
