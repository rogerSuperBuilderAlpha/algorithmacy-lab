## Part 1 — Findings, ranked by damage

The closed 2026-08-02 content spine holds. Regulatory tense, OTA chronology, App Store demo, Art. 22 personal scope, Apple / P2B / COM(2026) 178, and the companion/counterfactual split do not regress. What remains is one miscategorized illustration, one abstract that still states a stronger gap than the body argues, one load-bearing literature gloss that does not match its source, and one internal mismatch in the institutional repertoire.

### Finding 1 (damage: high) — §8’s “reverse pairing” bundles an employment presumption that may exit the triad with voice instruments that stay inside it

**Claim.** High digital sovereignty / low coordinative sovereignty is shown on the developer; the reverse pairing (low DS, high CS, “still arriving”) is shown on the platform-work directive as a single package, then called “coordinative sovereignty written into law.”

**Quote.**

> Under the European platform-work directive, member states must by 2 December 2026 provide a rebuttable presumption of employment where the platform controls the work, along with information rights over the automated systems, human review of consequential automated decisions, and limits on the data the platform may process (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024). (788–792)

> A legislature there ties collective voice directly to standing over algorithmic coordination, which is coordinative sovereignty written into law. Standing over the coordination is being built while control of the infrastructure stays at zero. (796–798)

**Why it damages this lens.** The Art. 25 sentence is accurate and is the right illustration. The presumption is not. On the chapter’s own terms (§3), hierarchies coordinate through authority. If the presumption bites, the worker is reclassified as an employee: the coordination logic changes, and the mediated triad the diagnostic is built to sort may dissolve rather than become governable. That is not “standing within an arrangement one cannot leave.” It is a possible exit from the arrangement. Chapter III (information, human review, data limits) and Art. 25 (collective voice over algorithmic management) are voice-within-mediation. Listing the presumption in the same breath, then letting “coordinative sovereignty written into law” cover the bundle, makes §8 deliver a cleaner reverse pairing than the instrument supports. Transposition tense itself is sound: “No member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written, so the standing it describes is a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact” (792–794). That clause should stay. The category error is the bundle, not the deadline.

**Fix.** Keep the presumption as background (and keep the 2 December 2026 / not-yet-transposed frame). Make the reverse-pairing case Chapter III plus Art. 25 only. One sentence: reclassification, where it occurs, changes the coordination form; it does not by itself confer coordinative sovereignty inside a necessary mediation.

### Finding 2 (damage: medium) — Abstract and §1 still state a “cannot see / contest” gap that §3.2 and §8 retract

**Claim.** The chapter’s actual thesis is standing, not total invisibility. The abstract and the opening vendor sentence still state the stronger claim.

**Quote.**

> A firm can comply with every instrument of Europe's digital regulation and still watch its market position turn on ranking decisions it cannot usefully see, predict, or contest. (5–6)

> an application store whose ranking and review decisions it can neither usefully see nor predict nor contest. (29–30)

The next two sentences walk it back: “Disclosure duties and complaint channels now reach parts of that layer, and section 3.2 takes them up. They still leave the merchant … without standing over the determination” (30–33). §3.2 then names P2B ranking-parameter disclosure, DSA recommender transparency, DMA gatekeeper conduct, and Art. 22 contestation (249–257). §8 adds P2B complaint-handling, mediation, and representative standing (779–781).

**Why it damages this lens.** A section delivers what it promises. The abstract promises a seeing-and-contesting vacuum; the body argues a standing gap after disclosure and thin channels. “Usefully” is doing too much work to reconcile the two. A referee who knows P2B Art. 5 and DSA Art. 27 will read the abstract as overclaim, then find the concession, and discount the hook. The body already has the right sentence. The abstract does not.

**Fix.** Put the concession in the abstract’s first sentence: comply, obtain disclosure and a complaint channel, still lack standing over the determination. Leave “usefully see” out of the sentence that has to survive a volume referee who stops at the abstract.

### Finding 3 (damage: medium) — Fratini et al. (2024) are made to say that no model addresses platform coordination

**Claim.**

> Surveys of the sovereignty concept confirm the residue: existing models sort sovereignty by the actor claiming it and the domain claimed, and none addresses the coordination through which platforms enroll and bind (Fratini et al., 2024). (259–261)

**Why it damages this lens.** Fratini, Hine, Novelli, Roberts, and Floridi (2024) map *national* digital-sovereignty models as rights-based, market-oriented, centralisation, and state-based. Their “none” is that none of those models fully combines comprehensive regulation of digital technologies with sufficient responsiveness. They do not sort by “actor claiming it and the domain claimed,” and they do not find that no model addresses platform enrollment. The chapter’s novelty does not need this sentence. Hanging it on Fratini invites a referee to check the abstract and treat the residue claim as manufactured. The neighboring point — existing digital-sovereignty work asks who controls infrastructure (647–650) — is already made, and is enough.

**Fix.** Drop the Fratini “none addresses the coordination” clause, or restate what that article actually finds (four national models; none robust on regulation-plus-responsiveness). Keep the chapter’s own claim in the chapter’s own voice.

### Finding 4 (damage: medium) — §7’s table and oversight paragraph under-deliver the channels §8 later treats as real

**Claim.** The institutional section sorts instruments by mediator type. Against a contingent mediator, “Contestability of individual decisions” is “marginal” (703). Oversight is introduced as platform-constituted boards (746–749).

**Quote.**

> | Contestability of individual decisions | marginal | voice at the level of the case | (703)

> Oversight boards and independent adjudication institutionalize voice at the level of the platform's rule-making, creating a body with some authority to review the platform's determinations (Klonick, 2020). (746–748)

Two pages later:

> That Regulation does more than disclose, since it also requires internal complaint-handling, offers mediation, and gives representative organizations standing to bring proceedings, which makes it one of the few instruments to attach a channel to a disclosure. (779–781)

**Why it damages this lens.** §8 cannot praise P2B as a rare channel-plus-disclosure instrument if §7 has already filed case-level contestability as marginal against the contingent mediators P2B is built for (delisting, suspension, ranking demotion of a business user). DSA Arts. 20–21 (internal complaint-handling; certified out-of-court dispute settlement) are the in-force Union-scale standing channels; they are unnamed. Klonick is the right private analogue and should stay. The missing statutory pair is a case the “building standing” section invites and does not use. This is inconsistency, not a demand for a new literature.

**Fix.** Relabel the contingent contestability cell as a thin existing channel (P2B Arts. 4, 11–12; DSA Arts. 20–21). Lead the oversight paragraph with those articles; keep Klonick as the weaker private form.

No other Part 1 findings. Clean stretches are in Part 2.

## Part 2 — Seat-specific audit (cases / facts / scope)

**Abstract / §1.** Thesis and keywords match the body except Finding 2. Sovereignty naming (Bodin; Habermas 1996; Repetto 2025 as specification, not stretching) is delivered early (69–80). NIS2 in the opening inventory is accurate and not overused later.

**§2.** Oracy / literacy / digital-sovereignty sequence delivers the two-part structure it promises. §2.3 now restricts the literacy frame to the regulatory strand and names the industrial strand (sovereign cloud, domestic capacity, data localization, procurement) in one clause (141–144). Key Terms match (1296–1299). No content defect.

**§3.** Triad, opacity–adaptivity–interest, and Selznick narrowing deliver. Art. 22 is out of the disclosure bundle, scoped to a natural person, high threshold, thin practice, firm outside it (253–257). P2B / DSA / DMA are named at the right grain. GDPR / AI Act / NIS2 as literate instruments in §2.3 is a fair characterization of documentation, notice, access, and written-governance duties.

**§4.** Delivers the competence, the DeVito concession, the triadic-index novelty, the eBay / ordinary-week illustration, and Cotter & Reisdorf on the gradient. Does not promise a formal measure. Clean.

**§5.** Delivers the diagnostic it announces. Factoring ≠ bypass (436–440). Counterfactual restores “the capability of direct dealing, not a prior relationship” (426–427); §5.5 stays in that vocabulary (556–560). Node vs edge is demonstrated on the App Store payment gate, not only the dealer: practicable duplication on the device vs restoring the developer-to-user payment tie (460–467). DMA anti-steering is correctly filed as the edge test run by a regulator (466–467). Companion owns the borderline measures; this chapter owns the plain counterfactual (402–405). “Computable” does not appear; the conclusion says “checkable” (905–906). No Φ, no promised apparatus undelivered.

**§5.5 OTA.** Sequence is internally consistent and correctly scoped: wide parity abandoned 2015; member-state legislation thereafter; national enforcement to the narrow clauses by 2021; Case C-264/23 (September 2024) that wide and narrow clauses are not ancillary restraints; Booking.com designated gatekeeper four months earlier (565–571). Schegg 2024 numbers are 2013–2023 (577–578). The 2024 judgment is marked too recent for those data; the decade tested is 2015–2021; mobile search and consolidation are named as co-causes of the *increase*; the function-split is said to predict non-erosion, not the rise (581–585). ECN 2017 commissions (549–550) and the Anderson / Beritelli & Schegg billboard caveat (551–553) are in place. Demand aggregation is called necessary *for the traveler who would not have found the hotel*, via “no direct channel at comparable cost” (557–560) — the right test, not “no other aggregator exists.” Clean.

**§6.** Definition, bearer (person and firm via relation; Pettit 2016), and Muldoon & Raekstad as ownership/voice rather than exit (657–661) all deliver. Digital sovereignty is not flattened to centralization; Couture & Toupin are named (646–649). Clean.

**§7.** Dual reading of interoperability plus COM(2026) 178 messaging / social-networking gloss (685–695) deliver. DGA is no longer frozen: the November 2025 Digital Omnibus would repeal it and move intermediation rules into the Data Act (724–728). Art. 22 again scoped to natural persons (709–710, 740–742); P2B channels to business users (710–712). Cooperativism filed as voice/ownership, with the scale-and-leaveability limit (714–718). Finding 4 is the defect. Otherwise the repertoire matches the diagnostic.

**§8.** Apple €500 million / April 2025 anti-steering is present, cited (European Commission, 2025b), and paired with the appeal (Case T-438/25) (775–776). P2B repeal is COM(2025) 837 via European Commission (2025a); “only duty” is scoped to main ranking parameters *for the business users being ranked*; DSA recommender transparency is named and left standing (781–785). Directive 2024/2831: deadline 2 December 2026, Art. 25 quote, not-yet-transposed (788–794). Finding 1 is the defect. WeChat (police modules; no user standing), RuNet (same shape at the network layer), CoopCycle (one-member-one-vote), federated social networks (exit plus local voice), and the New York Drivers Cooperative (qualification, not a counterexample) are named and used honestly (830–838). ## Part 3 — Paste-ready revisions

Highest-value only.

**Abstract, sentence 1 (lines 5–6).** Replace with:

> A firm can comply with every instrument of Europe's digital regulation, obtain the ranking disclosures and complaint channels those instruments now supply, and still lack standing over the determination that sets its market position.

**§1, lines 28–33.** Replace the vendor sentence and the two that follow with:

> An independent software vendor with impeccable data-protection practices still depends, for its entire access to customers, on an application store whose ranking and review decisions it can obtain parameter disclosures and a complaint file for, and cannot usefully predict or govern. Disclosure duties and complaint channels now reach parts of that layer, and section 3.2 takes them up. They still leave the merchant whose listings disappear, and the creator whose audience vanishes in a recommendation change announced nowhere, without standing over the determination that decided the outcome.

**§3.2, lines 259–261.** Replace with:

> Surveys of the sovereignty concept confirm the residue: existing models of digital sovereignty are sorted as rights-based, market-oriented, centralising, or state-based, and are judged for whether they combine comprehensive regulation with responsiveness (Fratini et al., 2024). None of that sorting addresses the coordination through which platforms enroll and bind.

**§7 table, contestability row (line 703).** Replace the contingent cell “marginal” with:

> thin case-level channel (P2B; DSA Arts. 20–21)

**§7, oversight paragraph (lines 746–749).** Replace with:

> The Digital Services Act's internal complaint-handling and certified out-of-court dispute-settlement bodies (Articles 20–21) institutionalize case-level contestation at statutory scale. Platform-run oversight boards offer a weaker private analogue: a body with some authority to review the platform's determinations, bounded by the platform that constitutes it (Klonick, 2020).

**§8, reverse pairing (lines 788–798).** Replace with:

> The reverse pairing is equally real, and it is still arriving. A platform worker owns none of the infrastructure that coordinates the work. Under the European platform-work directive, member states must by 2 December 2026 write in information rights over automated systems, human review of consequential automated decisions, and limits on the data the platform may process (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024). No member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written, so the standing it describes is a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact. The same directive's rebuttable presumption of employment, where the platform controls the work, is a different instrument: where it bites, it may move the worker from a mediated triad into a hierarchy, which changes the coordination form rather than conferring standing inside it. Article 25 is the voice clause. It obliges member states to promote collective bargaining in platform work, including measures "to facilitate the exercise of their rights related to algorithmic management set out in Chapter III." A legislature there ties collective voice directly to standing over algorithmic coordination, which is coordinative sovereignty written into law. Standing over the coordination is being built while control of the infrastructure stays at zero.

## Verdict

**Minor.**

**Single most important fix.** Split the platform-work package in §8: keep Chapter III and Art. 25 as the arriving reverse pairing; stop letting the employment presumption count as coordinative sovereignty inside a necessary mediation.

**Biggest genuine strength.** §5.5. The hotel/OTA case is the chapter’s content engine: two functions, two instruments, a decade of gate-opening, Schegg numbers that do not show the erosion a single-intermediary reading expects, and an explicit refusal to treat 2024 CJEU or the 2013–2023 rise as a natural experiment. A referee who remembers one passage will remember that one.

**Author-only.** Hunt et al. (2025) omission, and the companion-paper scope if a referee asks what the “measures that grade the borderline cases” (402–403) actually are. This seat does not invent a fix for either.
