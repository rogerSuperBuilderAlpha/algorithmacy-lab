## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

Findings ranked by damage to a political-theory referee. Closed-item status appears where relevant.

### §1 Introduction

The compliance-vs-capture opening lands. Berlin (1969) and Anderson (2017) arrive early and do real work:
independence is the wrong demand when the mediation cannot be left; the right to quit does not dissolve
employer-like authority. The sovereignty-naming defense (ll. 69–80) meets Bodin with Habermas's
desubstantialized popular sovereignty and Repetto's specification-not-stretching finding. **Closed finding
#1 holds.** The register is now owned: sovereignty here is standing to hold power to account through the
medium, shared and partial, not Bodinian supremacy.

Residual for this lens: the Berlin walking paraphrase (ll. 56–57) needs VERIFY (Part 3 / VERIFY). The
general shape — extinguishing a demand one cannot meet and calling the result freedom — matches Berlin's
retreat to the inner citadel, but the walking sentence is not a recognizable quotation and should not read
as one.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities

Clean for this lens. The pairing of medium and contestatory competence, and the claim that democratic
sovereignty tracked the spread of literacy, sit inside the Habermas–Anderson–Scott line the chapter cites.
No political-theory failure.

### §3 The platform turn

Not primarily political theory. The interested-mediator argument (firm as agent behind the algorithm)
pre-loads the Cicerchia move in §6 and is correctly placed. No accuracy problem.

### §4 Algorithmacy

Competence unevenness and Cotter & Reisdorf (2020) matter for who can exercise standing once institutions
grant it. The person/firm shift in who holds algorithmacy is now bridged in §6 (bearer). No open
political-theory defect here.

### §5.1–§5.2 Diagnostic

The bypass test is structural. The chapter is modest that formal Φ lives in the companion. One is/ought
caution carries into §5.3: "each is the right move where the mediator is contingent" (l. 492) reads a
normative prescription off a structural fact. Causal necessity does not by itself warrant voice-within
private ownership; utility regulation and ownership transformation are also political responses to necessary
monopoly. §7 already surveys those instruments. §5.3 should not sound as if the diagnostic *entails* the
prescription.

### §5.3 Exit and voice — heaviest weight for this lens

**Finding A (highest damage remaining).** The neat mapping still overstates, and the lazy-monopoly
correction is half-finished.

- "Exit maps onto bypassability, voice onto necessity" (ll. 484–485). Exit ↔ bypassability is earned.
  Voice ↔ necessity is not an entailment. Necessity forecloses exit and leaves voice as the residual lever;
  it does not produce voice. Voice remains available against contingent mediators too.
- The chapter now confronts Hirschman's complication (ll. 498–501): exit can substitute for voice; a
  monopolist can be comforted by the departure of the quality-sensitive. That is the right text to cite.
- The pivot then softens the point: "The necessary mediator is a different case and in one respect a clearer
  one" (ll. 501–504). Necessity is not clearer for voice. It is the condition under which voice is hardest
  to make effective: no exit to lend the critic leverage, and a captive population that may resign rather
  than complain. The chapter's own next clause ("whatever force voice has… has to be supplied by
  institutions") is the true claim. "Clearer" should become "harder, which is why institutions must carry
  the whole load." Presented as-is, a Hirschman referee still hears the rosy half after a one-sentence
  concession.

Loyalty remains dropped. Platform lock-in holds actors in the way loyalty does, but as involuntary
retention, not Hirschman's civic loyalty that activates voice. One clause would sharpen the mapping and
explain why voice does not automatically follow from lost exit. This is secondary to Finding A.

**Closed finding #7 holds** in the wider chapter: §6 and §7 file cooperativism as ownership/voice that does
not restore leaveability. §5.3's "Only voice remains" (l. 498) is consistent with that filing if voice is
the family name for contestation *and* share-in-governance. It is still thin as a sentence — ownership
transformation is doing heavier normative work in §7 than "voice" signals here — but that is compression,
not a regression of #7.

### §5.4–§5.5 Portfolio and OTA case

Portfolio logic is a political contribution: which response a dependence warrants becomes function-relative.
The OTA case is empirical scaffolding for the diagnostic, not a political-theory claim. No new defect for
this lens. The "right move" language of §5.3 recurs in managerial form at ll. 522–524; same is/ought caution.

### §6 Coordinative sovereignty — normative core

Definition (ll. 586–590) is clear; currency as effective voice is good. Strand by strand:

**Pettit / Skinner — accurate.** Non-domination as absence of arbitrary power, stronger than non-interference;
the kindly-master figure is faithfully compressed. Muldoon & Raekstad (2023) and Cicerchia (2022) are used
aptly; locating agency in the firm answers the "ranking system is not an agent" objection. Enhancement, not
defect: late Pettit's "controlled" interference (*On the People's Terms*) maps even more tightly onto
"terms that hold because the coordinated actors have the standing to make them hold," and is unused.

**Relational autonomy — accurate, one nit.** Nedelsky / Mackenzie & Stoljar dissolve the false choice between
mediation and autonomy; the object is the shape of the relation. "A well-shaped mediation enlarges… a badly
shaped one diminishes" (ll. 606–608) takes the substantive side of the procedural/substantive debate the
Mackenzie & Stoljar volume organizes. One owning clause would inoculate.

**Markell — accurate use, irony still open. Finding B.** Markell (2008) does argue that non-domination
protects without including; asking for a share in the determination is a legitimate appropriation. The
chapter now owns the popular register against Bodin in §1, which dissolves the general "is sovereignty an
oxymoron" objection. What remains unanswered *at the Markell citation* (ll. 610–613) is the sharper irony:
Markell's target is the sovereign picture of agency — freedom as independence, control, mastery — and the
chapter recruits him to license a construct named *coordinative sovereignty*. A referee who knows Markell
will ask for one sentence at the point of use: the debt is to his participation theme; the name uses
sovereignty only in the shared, popular sense already declared in §1, never as mastery held alone.

**Ostrom / Schlager — holds; prior under-defense is repaired.** The enclosure adjustment (ll. 615–624), the
claimant/proprietor distinction, and the Maine lobster analogy are the right shape for a platform the
mediator owns. Proprietorship without title — collective-choice arrangements the owner cannot unilaterally
rewrite — is what Ostrom licenses here. Scale hostility (vast, shifting user populations) remains a live
Ostrom caution; §7's cooperative-scale limit absorbs most of it. No regression finding.

**Bearer — Closed finding #6 holds.** Ll. 626–633 name person and firm via relation, cite Pettit (2016) for
corporate bodies valued for members' sake, and mark the conflict case as "a position taken rather than a
derivation." That answers the reviews_v2 demand to stop asserting the conflict rule as derived. Residual:
Pettit (2016) as the corporate-body license should be VERIFY'd (VERIFY section); if the passage is thin, a
different Pettit locus may be needed.

**Neighbor wedge — Closed finding #7 holds; wedge is now the right one.** Muldoon & Raekstad recommend
cooperative ownership; the chapter presses that ownership changes who sets rules and cannot make a necessary
mediator leaveable (ll. 648–655). Anderson's quit parallel reinforces. That is the defensible residue the
neighbors leave. Do not re-open as exit-filing; it is not.

Three clarifications (ll. 657–664) are sound. Algorithmacy as precondition of exercise rather than endowment
(ll. 666–671) is the right republican note: standing can be granted before competence is general; whether it
becomes real leverage is another question.

### §7 Building standing

The contingent/necessary sort of instruments is the applied payoff of §5.3. Cooperative ownership in the
table (l. 699): "changes the owner, not the leaveability" — consistent with #7. Contestability as voice at
the case level, oversight boards as bounded voice, data trusts as leverage toward voice: accurate placements
for this lens. Clean.

### §8 Implications

Independent variation of digital and coordinative sovereignty is the best demonstration that the construct
is not a relabeling of infrastructure control. Person-level (platform worker under the directive) and
firm-level (developer/seller) bearers both appear; §6 has already said why. WeChat / RuNet / CoopCycle /
Drivers Cooperative frame openness vs control without collapsing standing into either. No political-theory
failure.

### §9 Future research

The eye-to-eye / speak-plainly item (ll. 844–846) is recognizably Pettit's test of non-domination. Measuring
standing under the unexitable condition, with individual and organizational forms, follows from #6. Clean.

### §10 Conclusion

Restates the popular-sovereignty definition without reopening Bodin. Non-domination, relational autonomy,
involvement, and commons governance are named as the resting place of voice under necessity. Acceptable
close for this lens.

## Part 3 — Paste-ready revisions

**1. §5.3 — fix the Hirschman mapping and retire "clearer" (ll. 484–505).** Replace from "Exit maps onto
bypassability…" through "…leave untheorized." with:

> Bypassability is the availability of exit. Necessity does not produce voice; it forecloses exit and leaves
> voice as the residual lever, available in principle against contingent mediators too and, under a necessary
> mediator, the only lever left. Contingency is a policy and strategy variable: whether a coordinated actor
> can leave becomes a designable fact. Scholars have already carried Hirschman's pair to platforms, including
> the constrained voice of platform workers (Dasgupta et al., 2025).
>
> For a contingent mediator, exit is available in principle. The mediator is held by a liftable constraint, so
> lifting the constraint restores the direct tie. Deregulation, mandated interoperability, an antitrust remedy,
> the breaking of an exclusive — each opens exit where the mediator is contingent. Platform economics reads the
> same fact through multihoming: where an actor can hold more than one platform at once, the bottleneck is
> contestable (Eisenmann et al., 2006). What holds many actors inside a contingent mediation is not Hirschman's
> loyalty, the attachment that keeps the voice-prone from leaving, but involuntary retention — lock-in,
> multihoming costs, sunk investment — which suppresses exit without activating voice.
>
> For a necessary mediator, the bypass takes nothing, because the mediator's position never rested on the tie's
> absence. To demand independence from a necessary mediator is to demand the dissolution of the coordination
> itself. Only voice remains, and Hirschman's own analysis blocks any easy confidence about it. He observed that
> exit does not reliably strengthen voice and can substitute for it: the customers most sensitive to quality are
> the first to leave, and their departure removes the complaints that would have forced a remedy, so a monopolist
> can be comforted rather than disciplined by the availability of an alternative. Under a necessary mediator the
> difficulty is starker, not clearer. There is no exit to lend the critic leverage, and a captive population may
> resign rather than complain, so whatever force voice has must be supplied by institutions rather than by the
> market. Building those institutions is what the literate and digital forms of sovereignty leave untheorized.

**2. §6 — close the Markell irony at the point of use (after ll. 610–613).** After "standing to participate
in how the coordination is governed." insert:

> The debt to Markell carries a caution. His quarrel with non-domination is that it keeps picturing freedom as
> sovereign mastery, as control that makes one independent of others. Coordinative sovereignty answers that
> caution only by using *sovereignty* in the shared, popular sense already declared above, never as a mastery
> held alone.

**3. §1 — tighten Berlin (ll. 56–57).** Replace:

With:

**4. §5.3 — soften is/ought on contingent remedies (l. 492).** Replace "each is the right move where the

**5. §6 — own the substantive relational-autonomy commitment (ll. 606–608).** Replace the well-/badly-shaped

## Verdict

**Minor revisions.** The rebuild closed the sovereignty-naming, bearer, Ostrom/Schlager, and
Muldoon-filing problems that would have blocked a political-theory referee. What remains is not a redesign:
the Hirschman mapping still overstates ("voice onto necessity") and then softens the lazy-monopoly point with
"clearer"; the Markell citation still needs one sentence of irony-closure at the point of use; Berlin's
walking paraphrase should be tied to the inner citadel or dropped. Paste-ready revisions above discharge all
three.

**Single most important fix:** Revision 1 — retire "voice onto necessity" and "clearer one"; state that
necessity forecloses exit, makes voice harder, and therefore raises the institutional burden §7 carries.

**Biggest genuine strength:** The necessary/contingent diagnostic, paired with the portfolio view and with
cooperativism filed as ownership that does not restore leaveability, turns "can this actor leave?" into a
designable, institutionally constituted fact. That is the republican insight that liberty is an artifact of
institutions, and it is the wedge Muldoon & Raekstad, Bannerman, Cohen, and Pasquale do not offer.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** None that blocks submission. The bearer conflict rule is now
marked as a position taken. If the author wishes a stronger republican seal, adopting Pettit's later
"controlled" interference vocabulary in one clause would tighten §6 without changing the construct.
