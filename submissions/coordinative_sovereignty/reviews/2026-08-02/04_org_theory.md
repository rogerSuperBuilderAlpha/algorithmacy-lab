## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

Findings ranked by damage to an organization-theory referee.

### Finding A (highest damage) — The App Store node/edge demo is circular

**Closed finding #2 status: present, but constructed badly. Treat as residual, not closed clean.**

Lines 452–458 supply the platform demonstration the prior round demanded:

> Delete it as a node and ask whether another actor could process that payment on the device: under the platform's own rules none may, so every node test returns a mediator that cannot be replaced. Restore the developer-to-user tie for payment… and the gate's force disappears… Node says necessary and edge says contingent.

The divergence is asserted. The node test is not the one the neighbors actually run.

Essential-facilities doctrine asks whether an excluded rival could **viably duplicate** the facility (practicable duplication), not whether the facility owner's own rules presently forbid substitution. Burt asks whether another actor could occupy the position given the opportunity structure, not given the incumbent's house rules. Williamson asks make-or-buy given transaction attributes. The chapter runs a corrupted node test: it asks "under the platform's own rules none may," then lifts those same rules in the edge test, and reports disagreement. Of course they disagree. The constraint that manufactures non-substitutability is the constraint the edge test removes.

The franchised-dealer demonstration (lines 443–450) is cleaner because the constraint is **external law** (franchise statutes forbidding direct sale). Deleting the dealer under that law really does leave no lawful substitute in the territory; opening the manufacturer-to-buyer tie really does ask a different question. On the App Store, payment processing is widely duplicable (Stripe, PayPal, the developer's own checkout). What is non-substitutable is *access to the installed base under Apple's exclusion*. The chapter conflates the payment gate with the OS distribution bottleneck, then treats the house rule as a node fact.

A volume referee who knows platform antitrust will say: the only demonstrated clean divergence remains the car dealership; the platform case is tautological. Two sentences will not fix this. Either (a) run a real node test — can payment processing be duplicated *as a service*, holding access remedies aside — and admit that on that reading node and edge may agree that the gate is contingent, with the OS distribution layer as the separate necessary function; or (b) redefine the node being deleted as *access to the user population on this OS*, and show that deleting that node (not the payment processor) still yields non-substitutability while opening the payment edge dissolves only the gate. Option (b) is the honest portfolio move and fits §5.4. As written, Finding A undercuts the chapter's answer to the make-or-buy / substitutability charge on home ground.

### Finding B — Novelty overclaim: "none of them returns a strategy to the party inside"

Lines 468–471:

> Brokerage theory predicts the broker's returns. Transaction cost economics prescribes a governance form to a party that can move its boundary. Essential facilities tells a court when to compel access for an excluded rival. None of them returns a strategy to the party inside the coordination, and none of them is run by that party.

The node/edge operational contrast (lines 434–441) is real and should stay. The claim that neighbors return **no** strategy to the dependent party is false for this literature.

- Cutolo and Kenney (2021), cited elsewhere in the chapter, is explicitly a strategy paper for platform-dependent entrepreneurs.
- Multihoming (Eisenmann et al., 2006), cited at lines 493–494, *is* an exit strategy for the inside party.
- Burtian advice to the brokered parties (close the hole; cultivate alternative paths) and Obstfeld's *tertius iungens* are strategic for actors inside the triad.
- Make-or-buy is a strategy for the party that can move its boundary — which is often the dependent firm deciding whether to build a direct channel.

What the diagnostic actually adds, and can defend: it **sorts which strategy** (exit vs. voice) **by function**, from the dependent party's standpoint, by an edge counterfactual that holds the mediator in place. That is narrower and true. The sentence that none of the neighbors returns a strategy is the overclaim a hostile referee will quote. Drop it. Keep the operational contrast and the ancillary-restraints generalization (lines 460–467), which is the stronger, more honest novelty claim.

### Finding C — Is bypass "relabeled substitutability"? Verdict: earned distinction, soft edges

Pressing the charge directly: necessary/contingent looks like functional substitutability. Contingent ≈ the function can be substituted by direct dealing. Necessary ≈ it cannot.

The chapter's best defense is already in the text and mostly works:

1. **Operation:** neighbors delete/substitute a **node**; bypass adds an **edge** and leaves the mediator in place (lines 440–441). That is not the same question, when the constraint set is held honest (see Finding A).
2. **Unit:** function for a given dependent party, not firm or market once-and-for-all (lines 424–426, 509–519). More granular than classic essential-facilities runs.
3. **Standpoint and output:** run by the coordinated actor; returns exit/voice strategy rather than a legality finding (lines 464–467). The ancillary-restraints cousin is named rather than hidden — good altitude.
4. **Reconciliation with factoring:** lines 428–432 now state clearly that irreducibility-now ≠ necessity-under-bypass. Closed from the first panel. Holds.

What still softens the distinction into substitutability:

- **"Comparable cost" (line 419) is the entire operational content for discovery functions and is undefined.** Without a cost threshold, "could these two parties reach each other at comparable cost" collapses into the analyst's judgment that the mediator is or is not doing useful search — which is ordinary substitutability talk.
- **§5.5 reverts to the old framing** (lines 546–547): "the tie carries no information, because there was no relationship to restore." That sentence fights §5.2's own correction that the test restores capability, not a prior relationship. See Finding D.
- **"Integrates nothing" for the dealer (line 411)** still overstates. A dealer integrates inventory, financing, test drives, trade-ins, warranty. The typology's own *partial* cell is the honest label. The overstatement is what makes the node/edge contrast look cleaner than organization theory allows.

**Net on novelty:** not a mere relabel if the edge operation, function-level unit, and strategy output are kept and the App Store demo is repaired. As written, a referee can still dismiss it as substitutability-plus-Hirschman unless Finding A and the strategy overclaim are fixed. Minor-to-moderate, not fatal.

### Finding D — Closed finding #3 half-holds: §5.2 fixed, §5.5 slips

**Closed finding #3 status: holds in §5.2; regresses in §5.5.**

Lines 417–426 correctly state that the counterfactual restores the **capability of direct dealing**, not a prior relationship, and that classification varies by actor. That closes the discovery-by-construction trap for the definitional section.

Lines 544–547 then apply the test to OTA demand aggregation:

> Restore the same tie for a traveler who has never heard of the hotel, and the tie carries no information, because there was no relationship to restore. Demand aggregation survives the bypass, and it is necessary.

"No relationship to restore" is the under-specification the prior round named. After §5.2's repair, the worked case should ask whether that traveler could reach that hotel at comparable cost through search, brand, or other channels if the OTA's exclusivity and parity architecture were lifted — and may still answer "no" for an unknown independent. The verdict can stay; the reason must match the definition. As written, §5.5 teaches the reader the old, circular operation one section after the new one was introduced.

### Finding E — Portfolio still omits tying/leverage

§5.4 remains the chapter's strongest organization-theoretic contribution: one firm, four kinds of mediator; exit against contingent gates, voice against necessary functions; Baldwin modularity and Parmigiani concurrent sourcing as the partial-verdict logic (lines 526–532). Keep all of it.

Still missing: why the interested firm **bundles** necessary and contingent functions. The necessary layer protects contingent rents — classic monopoly leverage / tying. The OTA bundles billboard with parity; the app store bundles distribution with the payment gate. Naming leverage would (a) reconcile firm-level interest (§3) with function-level classification (§5.4), (b) explain portfolio assembly, and (c) connect to §7's remedy logic (attack the contingent gate without pretending the necessary layer dissolves). One paragraph. Not a new theory demand — an unused implication of the chapter's own setup.

### Finding F — Hirschman: weak voice named, loyalty still dropped

§5.3 now names Hirschman's worry that exit can substitute for voice and that under a necessary mediator voice must be supplied by institutions (lines 498–505). That closes the prior "weak voice" gap. Good.

Loyalty is still absent. Lock-in, sunk reputation, and ratings stores are exactly Hirschman's loyalty: they suppress exit against *contingent* mediators, which is why "exit is available in principle" (line 490) so often fails. One sentence would make the contingent case honest. Minor.

## Part 3 — Paste-ready revisions

**R1 — App Store demo (replace lines 452–458).** Prefer the portfolio-honest version:

> The same divergence appears on a platform, which is where it matters. Separate the application store's functions. Distribution to the installed base of a single operating system is, for most developers, integrating work no direct tie reproduces at scale: delete that node and no rival store reaches those users without the platform's cooperation or a regulator's access order, so a node test returns non-substitutable and an edge test that only opens a payment link leaves distribution in place. The payment gate is a different function. Restore the developer-to-user tie for payment — sideloading or a link to an external checkout — and the gate's force disappears, because payment processing was never integrating work the parties could not perform. Node and edge disagree on the gate only if the node test is allowed to treat the platform's own exclusionary rules as fixed features of the world; run honestly, the gate is contingent and distribution is the necessary layer beside it. The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering obligations are the edge test on the gate; its access and sideloading contests are fights over the distribution layer.

**R2 — §5.5 discovery application (replace lines 544–551 reason-clause):**

> Run the counterfactual on each function separately. Restore the capability of direct hotel-to-traveler dealing for a traveler who already knows the hotel exists, and the parity clause does nothing: it was held in place by a contract, and it is contingent. Ask the same question for a traveler who would not have found the independent hotel at comparable cost through other channels, and demand aggregation still does integrating work no open tie reproduces: it is necessary for that booking. The verdict is a split…

**R3 — Novelty claim (replace lines 468–471):**

> Brokerage theory predicts the broker's returns. Transaction cost economics prescribes a governance form to a party that can move its boundary. Essential facilities tells a court when to compel access for an excluded rival. Multihoming and the platform-dependence literature already advise the party inside. What none of them supplies is a function-level sort, run from that party's position, that says which of those advices applies where: exit where the prohibition on direct contact is load-bearing, voice where the mediator's integrating work would survive its removal.

**R4 — Dealer (line 411):**

> A franchised car dealer relays a vehicle from manufacturer to buyer and integrates little the direct tie could not reproduce — inventory and local service are real, and still not what franchise law is protecting.

Or shorter: replace "integrates nothing" with "integrates little that franchise law is needed to protect."

**R5 — Tying after "One firm, four kinds of mediator at once" (line 518–519):**

> The interested firm holds the four together for a reason. Necessary functions are where its position cannot be bypassed; bundling contingent gates to them defends rents the gate alone could not hold. That leverage is why parity traveled with the billboard, and why the payment gate travels with distribution.

**R6 — Loyalty, one sentence after line 490:**

> Hirschman's third term, loyalty, decides whether an actor uses an exit it formally holds. Ratings stores, sunk learning, and habituation are loyalty by another name: the contingent mediator is exitable in principle and often held anyway.

**R7 — Line 460 trim:** delete "and naming it strengthens the claim."

## Verdict

**Minor revisions** (bordering accept-with-nits once R1–R3 land).

The first panel's majors are largely closed: J. D. Thompson present; Selznick earned; factoring separated from bypass; neighbors Burt / Williamson / essential facilities named; OTA anchored to 2015–2021; capability language in §5.2; bearer and cooperativism filing in order. The chapter would survive a volume referee who knows coordination theory **if** the platform node/edge demonstration stops stacking the deck and the "no strategy for the inside party" overclaim is cut.

**Single most important fix:** R1 — rebuild the App Store demonstration so the node test is the neighbors' test (practicable duplication / opportunity structure), not "under the platform's own rules," and so payment-gate contingency sits beside distribution necessity as portfolio facts.

**Biggest genuine strength:** The function-not-firm portfolio (§5.4) plus the exit/voice remedy split, with the OTA case now honest about what the decade can and cannot show. That unit of analysis is more granular than firm-level essential-facilities or make-or-buy runs, and it is the chapter's real organization-theoretic contribution.

**What only the author can supply:** Whether, in the companion paper, the integrated-information measures operationalize §5.1 factoring alone (as the text now implies) or also grade bypass-necessity. The org-theory reader can run §5.2 without IIT either way; the Author (2026) cites should point only at the check the formal paper actually computes.
