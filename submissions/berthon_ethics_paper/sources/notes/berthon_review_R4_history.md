# R4 — Historian of literacy and law

Agent-drafted review. The author has not seen it.

Reviewer seat: R4, historian of literacy and law. Manuscript: `../AUTHOR_DRAFT_v2.md`, section 3 in full, with the historical claims in sections 1, 2.1, 4 and 7.3. Ground truth: the arm's facet files, audits and `../AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md`. Where the arm is silent I checked an official or archival text myself and say so; where I could not check I say that too. Nothing below cites from memory.

## 1. Verdict

The paper argues that five institutional practices of the literacy transition (gate, withholding, record over voice, monopoly, oversold promise) recur in platform mediation, and section 3 is the evidence for the five. It is not ready for *Business Ethics Quarterly*: of about a hundred factual claims I checked in section 3 and its satellites, roughly a third match the arm's sources, a third say more than the source does, and the rest are either contradicted by the source or rest on nothing in the record, including several errors that `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` corrected in the first draft and that have come back (Marshall ¶14, the 1643 petition, "aloud" in the Pragmática, Jomtien's "mandate", the Voting Rights Act as the end of the gate). The single most serious problem is that the section's best-known cases are told from the standard summary rather than from the source the arm read, so the Waitangi count, the Virginia chronology, Graff's data, the Stationers' petition and the 1917 immigration test are each wrong in a way a referee in legal or literacy history will recognise on sight.

## 2. Findings, most serious first

Each finding gives the section and a short quotation, what is wrong, the evidence with its locator, and the fix as an instruction. Counts of the claim-by-claim check are in section 5, and the full table is the appendix.

### F1. Waitangi: the signature count, the enforcement claim and the translator's motive. **Must fix.**

§3.3: "Over 500 chiefs signed the Māori version ... The colonial administration immediately enforced the English text, treating the signatures as a total surrender of territory and law (Waitangi Tribunal, 2014, pp. 355–358). Williams was not a detached linguistic translator; he was an interested institutional intermediary whose textual ambiguities served Crown expansion."

- The Tribunal's count is "more than 500 rangatira" across nine copies and about 50 meetings, of whom "Only 39 rangatira signed the English text" (Wai 1040, p. 386). Five hundred is the total, not the Māori-text count. The arm's own audit struck the same sentence from facet A (`AUDIT_01.md`, row A16, "WRONG x2").
- Pages 355–358 hold Hobson's dispatch ("Williams ... repeated in the native tongue, sentence by sentence") and the speeches of Te Kēmara and Rewa at the 5 February hui (facet A, Group 4; facet G, Group 3). Nothing on those pages concerns what the administration did after signing, and the Stage 1 report "does not contain findings in respect of claims" (p. xxii–xxiii). The enforcement sentence therefore has no source at the locator given.
- Williams's motive is contested in the historiography the Tribunal reviews: Ward ("I do not think any of this is true", p. 414), Head ("an implausible conspiracy to deceive", p. 414), McKenzie (no "will to deceive ... by his choice of terms", p. 417) and Owens ("haste and inexperience", p. 418) against Belich and Moon and Fenton (pp. 415, 417, 421). What the Tribunal itself writes is narrower than the draft: Williams "may have been honest in his choice of 'kawanatanga'" but "must, however, have known that tino rangatiratanga conveyed more than what was set out in the English text" (p. 514, as recorded in facet G and `T_adjudication.md` §8.1). The documented grounds for suspicion are Bishop Broughton's instruction of 10 January 1840 to support cession (p. 506) and Williams's land holdings (p. 417).
- "Henry Williams and his son translated the English text into Māori overnight", "a newly coined transliteration" and the gloss on tino rangatiratanga are not in any page the arm read. I could not verify them this session.

Fix: give the count as the Tribunal gives it (more than 500 in all; 39 on the English text); cut or re-source the enforcement sentence; state Williams's position as the Tribunal states it (instruction, holdings, a contested verdict) and cite p. 514 for the one finding the Tribunal makes; source or drop "overnight", "his son" and "newly coined".

### F2. *R. v. Marshall*: ¶14 is misread, and section 1 inverts the holding. **Must fix.**

§1: "it noted that Mi'kmaq signatories had no practical opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations, allowing Crown scribes to overwrite oral covenants." §3.3: "Justice Ian Binnie noted that the Mi'kmaq signatories were non-literate and lacked the practical capacity to create their own written record."

- ¶14 is a general remark about treaty interpretation: "The Indian parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations." It is not a finding about the Mi'kmaq signatories, and the word "non-literate" does not occur in it (facet A, `marshall1999`; `AUDIT_01.md` A24 confirms the wording). `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` row A8 flagged exactly this in the first draft, and noted the error originated in Claude's own introduction.
- The Court held the opposite of what section 1 implies: the Nova Scotia courts "erred in concluding that the only enforceable treaty obligations were those set out in the written document of March 10, 1760" (¶40). No scribe appears in the judgment; "Crown scribes" is the draft's invention (`AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` A9).
- "Centuries of judicial doctrine gradually recognized this structural injury" has no source. The two cases cited are two years apart (1997, 1999).
- *Delgamuukw*: the "equal footing" language is ¶87, not ¶98; ¶98 is conditional ("I fear that if this reasoning were followed, the oral histories of aboriginal peoples would be consistently and systematically undervalued"), so "held that Canadian common law had systematically erred" overstates it. The terms *adaawk* and *kungax* are not in the paragraphs the arm read (84–87, 94–108); I did not open the judgment to check them.

Fix: attribute ¶14 to treaty-making in general; delete "non-literate" and "Crown scribes"; state ¶40's holding as the Court's correction of the lower courts; move the *Delgamuukw* locator to ¶87 and soften "mandating"; drop "centuries".

### F3. The anti-literacy statutes: chronology and scope. **Must fix.**

§3.2: "In the wake of Nat Turner's revolt in 1831, Virginia enacted sweeping statutes criminalizing assemblies held to teach enslaved or free Black persons reading and writing. Identical measures followed in Alabama, Georgia, and North Carolina."

- The Virginia act in the arm's archival transcription is dated 7 April 1831 (facet C, entry 6, Encyclopedia Virginia transcription of *Acts* 1831, pp. 107–108). The draft places it after Turner's revolt. I could not open a page giving the revolt's date this session (Encyclopedia Virginia returned "page not found"; Wikipedia blocked the request), so I flag the sequence for the author to confirm: if the revolt postdates April 1831, "in the wake of" reverses cause and effect.
- The act is not "sweeping". Section 4 makes assemblies of free Black people for reading or writing unlawful; section 5 fines white persons who assemble with free Black people to instruct them; section 6 fines white persons who teach slaves "for pay or compensation". It does not bar a master teaching his own slave or a parent a child (facet C, entry 6, "Reading"; `T_adjudication.md` §2 row 2: "each narrower than its usual summary").
- "Identical measures followed" is wrong in both words. Georgia's act is assented 22 December 1829, before Virginia's; Alabama's is 16 January 1832; North Carolina's source act is "1830, c. 6". None is identical: South Carolina 1740 bars teaching to *write* only; North Carolina exempts "the use of figures"; Alabama adds "spell" and reaches free people of colour; Georgia sits inside a bill on "printed papers ... calculated to excite disaffection" (facet C, entries 1–5; "Claims the sources contradict", items 1–2). `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C6 made this point in the first draft.
- "Following the Stono Rebellion of 1739, South Carolina enacted its Negro Act of 1740": the statute's own stated ground is "great inconveniences" (s. XLV, p. 413); the Stono link is not in the arm's record. The arm names Rasmussen (2010, *PMLA* 125.1) as the three-page article that would settle it.

Fix: date each statute from the archival copy, order them correctly, describe what each actually prohibits, and source the Stono and Turner connections or state them as the historians' consensus with a citation.

### F4. Jomtien and Dakar were not "mandates to eradicate adult illiteracy". **Must fix.**

§3.5: "The World Conference on Education for All at Jomtien (1990) and the World Education Forum at Dakar (2000) announced explicit mandates to eradicate adult illiteracy ... The targets were roundly missed (UNESCO, 2015)."

- Jomtien's Framework for Action ¶8 lists target dimensions that "countries may wish" to adopt, among them "Reduction of the adult illiteracy rate ... to, say, one-half its 1990 level by the year 2000" (facet F, `wcefa1990world`; `T_adjudication.md` §3 item 1). It is a proposal, not a mandate, and it is halving, not eradication.
- Dakar ¶7(iv) is "achieving a 50 per cent improvement in levels of adult literacy by 2015" (facet F, `unesco2000dakar`). The 2015 Global Monitoring Report says this "was not achieved" (18% to 14%), and also calls EFA "a qualified success" and reports that 17 of 73 countries halved their illiteracy rate (facet F, `unesco2015efa`). By the UIS series, the world rate fell from 25.4% in 1990 to 18.9% in 2000, about half the proposed halving (facet F, line 308). "Roundly missed" is the draft's gloss.
- `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` B10 flagged "explicit, failed mandates" in the first draft. The error has returned in stronger form.
- The UIS figure (739 million in 2024, 63% women) is right (facet F, `uis2026adult`: 739,139,712; 466,257,152 women). The reference list entry is not: the arm's source is the UIS February 2026 data release (indicator `ILLPOP.AG15T99`, SDG World aggregate), not a report titled "Literacy rates among adults and youth: Global report on trends and data". No such report is in the arm's record.

Fix: quote the two targets as written, say what each monitoring report said, and cite the UIS data release with indicator and retrieval date.

### F5. The immigration tests and the end of the voting gate. **Must fix.**

§3.1: "When the United States Congress passed statutory literacy tests for incoming immigrants in 1897 and 1917, successive presidents vetoed the measures ... The literacy gate was ultimately dismantled not by individual test-takers learning to parse the text, but by blunt federal preemption through the Voting Rights Act of 1965."

- Cleveland vetoed on 2 March 1897 and Wilson on 29 January 1917, but Congress enacted the 1917 bill over Wilson's veto: the Immigration Act of 1917 (39 Stat. 874, ch. 29, 5 February 1917) excludes "All aliens over sixteen years of age, physically capable of reading, who can not read the English language, or some other language or dialect" (facet D, entries 10–11). The sentence as written implies the vetoes held. Taft's veto (1913) is in Lake, at second hand.
- The Cleveland quotation is accurate: I opened the veto message on the American Presidency Project (document "veto-message-31", dated 2 March 1897) and it reads "It is said, however, that the quality of recent immigration is undesirable. The time is quite within recent memory when ..." The draft drops "however,". The arm's own Cleveland quotation (the "hundred thousand immigrants" passage, Dillingham *Abstracts* vol. 2, pp. 573–574) is also on that page.
- The 1965 Act suspended tests in jurisdictions caught by the §4(b) formula; the 1970 Amendments barred them nationwide (§201(a), 84 Stat. 315), *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970) upheld that, and the 1975 Act's title makes the ban permanent (facet D, entries 7–9, synthesis F1 "What ended it"). `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C4 said this of the first draft. The paragraph also covers Australia and Latin America, whose gates ended in 1958 (Museum of Australian Democracy page), 1985 (Brazil, EC 25), 1979 (Peru) and 1970 (Chile), none by the Voting Rights Act.

Fix: say the 1917 Act passed over the veto; restore "however,"; give the end of the US test in its three steps and the ends of the other gates in the same paragraph.

### F6. Graff's evidence is misdescribed. **Must fix.**

§3.5: "Analyzing census records and labor markets across nineteenth-century urban Canada and the United States, Graff demonstrated ... Illiterate workers from favored backgrounds routinely secured higher wages and better employment than literate workers from marginalized groups. Industrial employers championed basic literacy not to foster intellectual freedom, but to cultivate a disciplined, punctual, and obedient factory workforce (Graff, 1979, pp. 302–321)."

- Graff's data are three Ontario cities (Hamilton, Kingston, London), 1861–1871 census and assessment rolls, in a society "in excess of 90%" literate (facet L2, 1979 introduction p. 19; facet F via Schudson and Black and Yasukawa). There are no United States labour-market data in the book as read. Alexander (1980, p. 31) says the research "lacked a time dimension and was confined to a society which was already overwhelmingly literate by the 1860s".
- The closest Graff comes to the "favored backgrounds" sentence is p. 320: "The majority of Irish Catholic adults ... were literate ... but they stood lowest in wealth and occupation, as did laborers and servants. Women and blacks fared little better, regardless of literacy." That is literate workers from a disfavoured group doing badly; "illiterate workers ... routinely secured higher wages" is not in the parts read, and "wages" is not Graff's measure there (facet L2, Q3).
- The employers' belief that educated workers were "more moral, loyal, cheerful, and contented as well as more punctual and reliable" is Graff p. 203, quoted in Black and Yasukawa (facet F, `black2014literacy`), not pp. 302–321. Pages 302–321 (chapter 7, section IV) argue that work "seldom, if ever, required the use of reading and writing" for the unskilled and that literacy was "more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most individuals within it" (p. 321). The promoters Graff names in chapter 7 are school reformers and the religious and labour press, who appealed to economic self-interest only "if rarely" (p. 299); Schudson's review makes the same point, that the nineteenth-century promise was moral before it was economic (facet F, `schudson1982literacy`).
- Graff also writes that "Literacy did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it" (1979, p. 19) and that the myth was "partly true" (Galvão et al. 2017, p. 54). The draft's "punctured this historical orthodoxy" omits his own qualification.

Fix: name the three cities and the decade; replace the wages sentence with what p. 320 says; move the employers' sentence to p. 203 and attribute the discipline argument to p. 321; add Graff's qualification.

### F7. The Stationers' Company: date, grantor, decrees, Milton, and the 1643 petition. **Must fix.**

§3.4 and the diagram.

- "In 1557, Queen Mary granted": the charter is Philip and Mary's ("3 & 4 Phil. & Mar."; "in the 3rd and 4th years of our reigns"). Arber's introduction dates it 4 May 1556 in four places; the regnal formula puts 4 May in 1557 (`AUDIT_01.md` B2; facet B, `arber1875transcript`). Say "Philip and Mary" and give both datings or pick one with the reason.
- "no individual in England could operate a printing press unless they were a freeman": the charter's clause continues "or has therefore licence of us ... by the letters patent" (Arber I, pp. xxx–xxxi). The royal-patent route matters, since the 1643 dispute is about patentees.
- "demolish unlicensed presses (Arber, 1875, pp. xxviii–xxxii)": the charter's search clause lets the wardens "seize, take, hold, burn" *books* printed contrary to law (p. xxxi). Press-breaking is not on those pages.
- "the Star Chamber Decrees of 1586 and 1637, which confined all printing in England strictly to the City of London and the two university presses": not in the arm's record. Facet B found the 1586 decree only mentioned in Arber I and did not read it; I searched the OCR text of Arber vol. II this session for the decree and found nothing usable. The arm did read the Star Chamber ordinances of 1566 (Arber I p. 322), which the draft does not mention. Either open the 1586 and 1637 decrees (Arber II, pp. 807ff., and Arber IV) or cut the sentence.
- "Provincial printing was banned": not in the 1662 Act as the arm quotes it; the Act caps master printers at twenty "besides the Kings Printers and the Printers allowed for the Universities" and limits presses per master (Statutes of the Realm vol. 5, 14 Car. II c. 33). The cap is correct; the diagram puts "20 Master Printers" under a date range of 1557–1662, but the number dates from 1662.
- "John Milton delivered his Areopagitica to Parliament": it was an unlicensed printed pamphlet in the form of a speech, "A Speech of Mr. John Milton ... to the Parlament of England" (the draft's own reference list carries the title). Not delivered.
- The quotation: the Gutenberg text (the arm's source) reads "Truth and understanding are not such wares as to be monopolized and traded in by tickets and statutes and standards" (line 961 of the file I opened this session). "Brocards" is not in that text. "p. 18" is not in the arm's record (the Gutenberg file has no pagination); I could not check a 1644 quarto.
- "In their 1643 petition to Parliament, non-elite pressmen denounced the monopolistic patents held by the Company's senior partners ... reducing journeymen printers to wage dependency": the petition in Arber is the Company's own, to the Commons (January 1643), against "a very few Persons of the said Company, but Eleven in number" who were "Pattentees, and Monopolizers of Printing the most vendable Bookes" (Arber I, pp. 582–583). Journeymen, pressmen and wage dependency are not in the record (`AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C16; facet B, Failure 1). Milton himself suspected the 1643 Order was "the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of bookselling", which is the contemporary objection the draft could use.
- The charter's stated ground ("seditious and heretical books", p. xxviii) and the defenders' argument that privilege "enabled the production of worthwhile texts that would never appear at all in other circumstances" (Johns 2002, p. 112) are absent. Johns also writes that licensing was "part and parcel of the regimes of privilege and registration, which were designed to protect good works as well as to hinder bad" (p. 119). Internal critique needs the institution's own stated ground.
- Minor: "Centralized London Guildhall" in the diagram; the Company's hall is not the Guildhall. Check the name.

Fix as itemised above.

### F8. The Pragmática of 1503 and the notaries. **Must fix.**

§3.3: "promulgated by Queen Isabella, overhauled the legal notariat ... Recognizing that scribes routinely defrauded illiterate citizens, the law required notaries to undergo public examination, keep an unalterable bound register (protocolo), and read all legal instruments aloud in the simultaneous presence of the parties and independent witnesses before signing (Almeida Cabrejas, 2019, p. 24; Burns, 2010)."

- What the arm's source says: the Pragmática of Alcalá (7 June 1503) required notaries to enter deeds in a register and to read the notes "presentes las partes e los testigos"; if a party could not sign, a witness or another literate person signed and the notary recorded it (Almeida Cabrejas 2019, p. 24, quoting the law through Marchant Rivera 2015). The arm quotes the law at second hand and its audit removed "aloud" as not in the text (`AUDIT_03.md` G29; `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C12). "Isabella", "public examination", "unalterable", "protocolo" and "independent" are not in the passage.
- "Recognizing that scribes routinely defrauded illiterate citizens": the purpose is not stated in any source read (`AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C14). Facet G found "no petition by an illiterate party against a notary or scrivener" and "no source ... reports a scribe or notary convicted of falsifying a deed for an illiterate client" (G, Q5).
- Burns (2010) is unread. The review the arm has (Andrien 2012, pp. 144–145) reports that Cuzco notaries "used a variety of clever legal strategies to circumvent the intent of the law" and "could manipulate the legal record in the service of a powerful client or on their own account", and that the Crown sold the offices. That cuts against §4.4's "independent public officer, legally bound to remain neutral" and against §7.3. Andrien does supply the one use of "sworn" in the record ("the very documentary record that they were sworn to uphold", p. 145); `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` A3 found no other.
- The reference list entry is wrong. The arm's verified record is: Almeida Cabrejas, B. (2019), "No firmó porque dijo no saber": alfabetizados y analfabetos en documentos notariales de CODEA de los siglos XVI y XVII, *Philologia hispalensis* 33(1), 21–41, doi 10.12795/ph.2019.v33.i01.02. The draft's title ("The evolution of the Spanish notarial institution ...") and journal ("Journal of Legal History and Notarial Studies, 44(1), 18–35") match nothing in the arm; a Crossref query this session returned no such item. Treat the entry as fabricated until a database record is found.

Fix: describe the 1503 rule in the law's own terms; cut the purpose clause or source it; say that Burns is known through reviews and what they report; replace the reference.

### F9. India: no statute, no "Dalit" ban, and what Phule asked for. **Should fix.**

§3.2: "upper-caste hierarchies enforced centuries-old bans on Dalit schooling, prompting ... Phule (1882) to submit his blistering memorandum to the Hunter Commission, demonstrating that statutory and caste-based deprivation of literacy was the foundational mechanism of social domination."

- Phule's deposition (signed Poona, 19 October 1882) says "The Mahars, Mangs, and other lower classes are practically excluded from all schools owing to caste prejudices, as they are not allowed to sit by the children of higher castes", asks "under the promise of the Queen's Proclamation" for separate schools and compulsory primary education to age 12, and complains that teachers are "almost all Brahmins" (facet M5, `phule1882memorial`). It is a complaint that state schools tolerated the exclusion, which M5 calls "withholding by omission"; there is no statute, so "statutory" is wrong for India.
- The dharmaśāstra texts the arm read restrict hearing, reciting and being taught the Veda (Manu 2.66, 2.172, 4.80; Gautama XII.4–6), not schooling or writing (facet C, entry 10; M5, B3). "Centuries-old bans on Dalit schooling" is not what those texts say, and "Dalit" is a later term that Phule's deposition does not use.
- The Hunter Commission volume itself was not opened; the arm read a reproduction.

Fix: say what Phule said and what he asked for; attach the deposition to the Queen's Proclamation as the internal standard (which is the paper's own method); drop "statutory" and "centuries-old bans".

### F10. Knox, the registrars, Kousser and Gaston. **Should fix.**

§3.1: "Knox acknowledged that the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments prohibited direct racial exclusions, but assured the delegates that an administrative literacy requirement, combined with discretionary property and character clauses, would achieve the identical result ... Registrars were empowered to evaluate whether an applicant could read and 'correctly explain' complex clauses ... White registrars posed simple questions to illiterate white applicants while subjecting Black college graduates to impossible legal parsing tests (Kousser, 1974) ... school systems that deliberately withheld literacy from the Black population ... (Gaston County v. United States, 1969)."

- I opened the archive.org OCR of the 1901 *Proceedings* this session and searched pp. 7–12 around the white-supremacy sentence. "Fourteenth" and "Fifteenth" do not occur; Knox's phrase is "within the limits imposed by the Federal Constitution". He reviews other states' devices (Mississippi's "understand when read" clause, Louisiana's and North Carolina's grandfather clauses) and says "The decision as to who are sufficiently intelligent to meet the requirements of the understanding clause is exclusively in the hands of the registrars. But to this plan, the objection has been urged with force that it perpetuates the very form of abuse which we are seeking to escape" (p. 11). He also says "we must establish it by law—not by force or fraud" (p. 9). The paraphrase "assured ... would achieve the identical result" goes beyond the text; the two sentences I quote are a stronger internal-critique hook than the paraphrase, because Knox names the discretion objection himself. "Correctly explain" is not in his address; the clause is "read, or understand when read to them".
- The registrar-discretion sentence is true and belongs to *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. at 312 and nn. 12–13 (the "FRDUM FOOF SPETGH" applicant; the Forrest County registrar who "rejected six Negroes with baccalaureate degrees"). Kousser's book was not read; Holmes's review of it (facet L3, entry 10) does not mention literacy tests and credits the poll tax and the secret ballot. Cite the case, not the unread book.
- *Gaston* speaks of "separate and inferior schools" and of a "segregated and inferior education" (395 U.S. at 293–295). "Deliberately withheld literacy" is not in the opinion (`AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` C2 made the same point about "outlawed").

### F12. Spain, Ukraine and the other withholding cases. **Should fix.**

§3.2.

- Leonard: the orders of 1531, 1536 and 1543 barred *taking* "books of fiction and of secular matters" to the Indies, on the ground that it was "bad practice for the Indians"; only books "relating to the Christian religion and morality" were allowed (Leonard 1992, pp. 81–82). ### F13. Messick, Fricker and the "scribal class". **Should fix.**

§3.3: "Under classical Islamic shari'a rules of evidence, oral witness testimony (shahada) held primacy over uncorroborated written documents, precisely because written documents were recognized as susceptible to forgery and private manipulation (Messick, 1993). In the Western legal tradition, the triumph of the written record over the voice operated as a form of structural testimonial injustice (Fricker, 2007). The epistemic credibility of non-literate communities was systematically devalued, allowing the scribal class to exploit their counterparts' inability to verify the inscription."

- Messick is the one case the arm opened in this group, and it supports the first sentence's core: the manuals "do not consider documents to be potential evidence", "an oral core is complemented by a written surround" (facet M3, `messick1993calligraphic`). Two cautions: Messick's case is highland Yemen from the late nineteenth century, so "classical Islamic" needs his own framing; and the "because susceptible to forgery" clause is not in the arm's quotations. M3's own lesson is the stronger one: record over voice "is a legal choice", not a property of writing, and the paper should say so (M3, synthesis).
- Fricker (2007) is "Not accessible" in the arm's bibliography (known through secondary works). The draft applies her term to the medium rather than to the hearer's prejudice toward a speaker, and applies it to 1760 and 1840 without any contemporary, which is the presentism the paper's own §2.3 forswears.
- The third sentence is contradicted by the arm's reading. Facet A: "No source I read has a medieval contemporary calling the displacement of memory by writing a wrong done to the illiterate"; Harrison's thesis finds "no significant shift towards the use of written evidence" in the manor courts she studied, and Clanchy (via his reviewers) reports serfs using charters by 1300; Nussdorfer: "even people who could not write or read themselves went to notaries"; Baker (1989): Maine deeds were not a "fraudulent 'deed game'"; the Tribunal finds Māori literacy "exaggerated somewhat" but counted in hundreds or more by 1839. Facet G found no scribe convicted of falsifying for an illiterate client. "The scribal class" exploiting non-literate communities is a thesis the record does not carry.

### F14. *Non est factum*, "sworn notaries", "equitable", and §7.3. **Should fix.**

§3.3, §4.4, §7.3, abstract and contribution 3.

- In *Foster v Mackinnon* the person who misdescribed the paper was Callow, "the acceptor of the bill", a counterparty; the defendant was "a gentleman far advanced in years", not illiterate, and the instrument was a bill of exchange, not a deed (facet G, `foster1869mackinnon`; `AUDIT_03.md` G2–G3). §3.3 correctly says "counterparties", but the abstract and contribution 3 say the plea "restrained human scribes", which `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` A4 rejected; §4.4 says "non-literate parties" and §7.3 says "restrained human scribes" again. ## 4. Method

**(a) Case selection and the missing counter-cases.** The arm recorded counter-cases that a historian will raise, and none appears in section 3: Italy's 1882 law, where passing the compulsory elementary course was a route *onto* the roll and the electorate rose from 621,896 to 2,049,461 (facet M5, `ballini0000sistemi`); Chile 1874, where removing the rule multiplied voters (Engerman and Sokoloff, p. 25); the Spanish Indies and Ukrainian bans that leaked (M5, synthesis 2); demand for literacy from below in Johansson's Sweden ("everybody's calling and right", p. 64), Washington's "whole race trying to go to school" (p. 30), and the Sunday-school families who "insistently, sometimes riotously demanded" it (`AUDIT_05.md` M1-13); benefit of clergy, where a reading test spared commoners from hanging (facet A, Blackstone); notaries who served "a powerful client" rather than the illiterate (Andrien on Burns); and Māori literacy counted in hundreds or more by 1839 (Tribunal pp. 258–259). §2.3's last paragraph gestures at demand from below in one sentence and section 3 then proceeds as if every rule narrowed, every ban worked and every scribe cheated. The paper's thesis survives these cases (M5: "a literacy rule can widen or narrow a franchise depending on what it replaces"), but only if it states them.

**(b) "The historical record reveals five distinct practices."** The arm's lead verdict is narrower: "named institutions doing five different things with literacy once standing came to depend on it", each at the scale of a legislature, court, church or company, with "gate with a stated exclusionary purpose" documented for the United States and Natal only and "record over voice" found nowhere outside the colonial treaty cases (`T_adjudication.md` §1–2; M5, Question 8). The draft's section 3 is wider than Anglophone (Brazil, Peru, Spain, Russia, India, Castile, Yemen), but each non-Anglophone case rests on one secondary source read in part, and the sentence "The historical record reveals" claims a survey the paper has not made. Say "five practices, documented in the following cases", and in §4.5 or §7 say where each is thin.

**(c) Review-depth sources cited as read.** See F17. The most consequential is Kousser, cited for a fact that *Katzenbach* supplies at first hand, and Burns, cited three times for a neutrality the reviewer of Burns denies.

**(d) Presentism.** The paper's method (§2.3) is internal critique, and section 3 keeps to it for the gate, the withholding, the monopoly and the promise, where named contemporaries exist (Wilson, Cleveland, Bonifácio, Pedigo; the Douglasses and Burke; Milton and the Company's petition; UNESCO's own 1975–76 assessment). It breaks the rule for record over voice: the only contemporaries in the record are Colenso's question of 6 February 1840 and the chiefs' speeches on land and rank, and the draft instead applies Fricker's 2007 category and two Canadian judgments of the 1990s, which `T_adjudication.md` row 3 notes are "later courts, in their own words". The "affected ignorance" charge against "scribes, administrators, and judges" (§2.3) is asserted without the test Moody-Adams sets, which requires named persons with an interest who declined to ask (facet L3, Question 1). Two further points. Moody-Adams, on whom §2.3 relies, argues that the "standards of their own day" defence "simply presumes without argument" that moral ignorance is cultural (p. 298), so the paper should not say presentism *forces* the three benchmarks; it chooses them (L3, Q1, point 3; `AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` B6). And the vocabulary slips: "Dalit" for 1882, "Commonwealth" for 1901, "oligarchs" and "cartel" for 1643, "disenfranchised ... Afro-descendant majorities" where the source says nothing of race.
