## §2. Terms and the standard

**2.1 Algorithmacy, and why the word is not a metaphor for reading.**
- Claim: algorithmacy names a competence in a relation — coordinating with another person through a
  third party that reads both and commits an outcome binding on both. The paper compares positions
  around a compulsory competence. It does not claim that dealing with an algorithm is a kind of reading.
- Evidence: the definition at `sources/design_ethics_DRAFT.md:97`.
- Answers: Kamperman and Graff's objection that AI literacy uses "literacy uncritically as a metaphor"
  (2026, p. 35) ● [L2].
- Caution: no claim that literacy or mediation restructures cognition (standing ruling; `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §3).

**2.2 The literacy transition, narrowly stated.**
- Claim: particular institutions made reading and writing a condition of legal or civic standing. The
  paper does not claim that societies as wholes came to require literacy.
- Evidence against the broad claim: Graff on 1861 Ontario, where unskilled work "seldom, if ever,
  required the use of reading and writing" (ch. 7, p. 302) ● [L2]; Hunnisett reporting Clanchy, literacy
  "not essential for kings or for such officials as sheriffs" (p. 67) ○ [L3].
- Caution: Clanchy is unread. Do not attribute a require/reward distinction to Graff [L2].

**2.3 What counts as a failure.**
- Claim: a practice counts as an ethical failure on one of three grounds internal to its time — a
  contemporary called it wrong, the actors broke their own professed standard, or outcome fell short
  of promise (ruling 4).
- Evidence: facet X, Q2 (Hunt ●; Butterfield ◐; Levy ●).
- One sentence stating which ground each of the five practices meets (table in `T_adjudication.md` §5).

**2.4 Why the absence of an objector does not clear a practice.**
- Claim: where few contemporaries objected, the paper claims plausibly affected ignorance, and no more.
- Evidence: Moody-Adams (1994) against the "inability thesis" (p. 293); evidence of what agents did
  not do cannot show what they could not do (p. 294); "affected ignorance" is "choosing not to know
  what one can and should know" (p. 296) ● [L3].
- Caution: her case is slavery in ancient Greece. Applying her test to record over voice is Claude's
  reading. The choice not to add a present-day standard is the author's; presentism does not force it.

**2.5 Who failed.**
- Claim: the paper names legislatures, courts, churches, companies and lords. It keeps "the global
  community" for the international campaigns (ruling 3).
- Evidence: Moody-Adams, a culture "cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304) ● [L3]; facet X, Q3 item 6.

**2.6 What literacy delivered.**
- Claim: the charge is against what was done with literacy, never against literacy. People sought it
  and it paid.
- Evidence: Johansson on Sweden, reading as "everybody's calling and right" (p. 64) ●; Washington, "a
  whole race trying to go to school" (p. 30) ◐; Graff, "Literacy did carry certain benefits" (1979
  introduction, p. 19) ● [X, L2].
- Caution: the measured benefits are for years of schooling in the late twentieth century.

## §3. Five things done with literacy

Each subsection runs: what was done, to whom, on what stated ground, who objected, what ended it.

### 3.1 The gate

1. **The device.** A reading test neutral on its face, with the passage and the verdict left to an
   official. *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966), at 310–312 ◐ [D].
2. **The stated purpose.** Knox to the Alabama convention, 1901: "to establish white supremacy in this
   State" (p. 8) ● [D].
3. **The same authority had withheld the schooling.** *Gaston County v. United States* (1969):
   "separate and inferior schools" ● [D]. This is the "broke their own standard" ground.
4. **Effect and intent beyond race.** Holmes reporting Kousser: turnout 64 to 30 percent; black-belt
   Democrats "wanted to eliminate the voting of poor whites as well as blacks" (pp. 270–271) ○ [L3].
   Caution: a review; the figures mix the test with poll taxes and other rules.
5. **The device elsewhere.** Australia's dictation test, 1901–1958 (Act No. 17 of 1901, s. 3(a));
   Brazil's exclusion of illiterates from the roll, 1881–1985 ◐ [D]. Caution: Brazil's fall in the
   roll is not a literacy effect alone.
6. **Who objected.** Wilson's 1917 veto of the immigration literacy test: "a penalty for lack of
   opportunity" ●; Cleveland, 1897; José Bonifácio, 1879–80; Populists, Republicans and Black voters
   (Holmes, p. 271 ○) [D, L3]. Note: Wilson's and Cleveland's vetoes concern immigration, not voting.
7. **What ended it.** The Voting Rights Act of 1965 and its 1970 amendments [D].

### 3.2 The withheld competence

1. **The statutes at their actual scope.** South Carolina 1740 bars teaching slaves to write; Virginia
   1831 bans assemblies and paid teaching; Georgia, Alabama and North Carolina, 1829–1834 ● [C]. Each
   is narrower than its usual summary; say so.
2. **The stated ground.** Auld: learning would "forever unfit him to be a slave" (Douglass, 1845,
   p. 33) ●; Margo reporting Williams, "Fearing insurrection" (p. 590) ○ [C, L3].
3. **Comprehension without leverage.** Douglass, reading gave "a view of my wretched condition,
   without the remedy" (ch. VII) ● [C, X]. Carry this forward to §5.
4. **Who objected.** Frederick Douglass, 1845; Margaret Douglass, 1853 ● [C]; South Carolinians who
   challenged Seabrook (Waters, p. 106 ○; the reviewer does not say they called it a wrong) [L3].
5. **Limits.** No source measures an effect; Margo notes Williams does not test whether the laws
   worked (p. 591). Outside the United States the record is thin.

### 3.3 The record over the voice

1. **The pattern.** The party that wrote the record bound the party that could not make its own.
   *Marshall* (1999), para. 14 and the holding at para. 40 ◐ [A].
2. **Oral evidence discounted.** *Delgamuukw* (1997), oral histories "systematically undervalued"
   (para. 98) ◐ [A].
3. **A disputed text.** Waitangi, 1840: more than 500 signatories in all, 39 on the English text; the
   Tribunal finds the rangatira did not cede sovereignty (Wai 1040, p. xxii) ◐ [A].
4. **The third party.** Henry Williams interpreted "sentence by sentence" (p. 355), under a bishop's
   instruction to support cession (p. 506); the Tribunal: he "must, however, have known that tino
   rangatiratanga conveyed more than what was set out in the English text" (p. 514) ◐ [G]. Deceit is
   disputed among the historians the Tribunal reports; say so.
5. **A second third party.** Cuzco notaries "could manipulate the legal record in the service of a
   powerful client or on their own account" (Andrien reporting Burns, p. 145) ○ [L3].
6. **The safeguards of the same era.** The Pragmática of 1503 (notes read in the presence of parties
   and witnesses; a witness signs for a party who cannot) ◐ at second hand; second interpreters at
   Anglo-Iroquois councils (Hagedorn, p. 61) ◐; the plea of *non est factum* (*Foster v Mackinnon*,
   1869, at 711) ◐ [G]. Carry these forward to §5.
7. **The ground for "failure".** Mostly later courts in their own words; then 2.4. This is the row
   with the fewest contemporary objectors, and the paper says so.

### 3.4 The monopoly

1. **The grant.** The Stationers' Company charter (4 May 1556 in Arber; 1557 by regnal year): no
   printing outside the Company; search and seizure ● [B].
2. **The cap.** The Licensing Act of 1662 (14 Car. II c. 33): twenty master printers ● [B].
3. **Who objected.** Milton, 1644; the Company's junior members, 1643 ● [B].
4. **Limits.** One kingdom; no count of people shut out; Johns argues every state regulated print, so
   the monopoly is no departure from a free baseline (Johns 1998 unread ○). Do not use Innis as a wrong
   done to persons, or print as the cause of dialect loss.

### 3.5 The promise

1. **The only world-scale case.** Jomtien (1990) proposed that countries halve adult illiteracy by
   2000; the world rate fell from 25.4% to 18.9% (UIS) ◐. Jomtien's figure was a proposal "countries
   may wish" to adopt, not a binding target [F].
2. **The missed target.** Dakar (2000): the 2015 monitoring report says the target "was not achieved"
   ◐ (matched against a reproduction of the summary only) [F].
3. **The present count.** 739,139,712 adults could not read and write in 2024; 466,257,152, or 63%,
   were women (UIS, February 2026 release) ● [F].
4. **The myth at smaller scale.** Graff: "literacy was more valuable to the society's goals and needs
   than to those of most individuals within it" (ch. 7, p. 321) ● [L2]. Chapters 1–6 unread.
5. **Who objected.** UNESCO's own experts and the Persepolis symposium, 1975; Freire, 1970 ◐ [F];
   Hodgson (1868), Phillips Thompson and the labour press in Ontario (Graff, ch. 7, pp. 269, 299–301)
   ● [L2].
6. **Prior work.** Kamperman and Graff (2026, p. 36) already carry this failure over to AI literacy.
   Cite them here and in 4.5.

### 3.6 Compulsory participation and one enforced language (not a sixth failure of literacy)

1. **What the reports blame.** Canada's TRC (at least 150,000 children; 3,201 reported deaths); the US
   Department of the Interior (408 schools; at least 973 deaths); *Bringing Them Home* ◐ [E]. They
   blame removal, forced labour, abuse and language suppression. None blames the teaching of reading.
2. **What carries over (ruling 5).** Two properties: attendance was compelled, and one language of
   instruction was enforced.

## §4. The same positions, occupied again

One paragraph per position. Each names the property compared, gives the present-day evidence, and
ends on where the parallel is weak. The readings are Claude's; the evidence is the sources'.

1. **Frame.** The history is evidence for §3; what follows is analogy (ruling 1). Qadri et al. (abstract
   only ○): an analogy must name the property that carries it [X].
2. **The gate.** Property: standing depends on being read by a party the person cannot question.
   Evidence: 4% of applicants rejected by all ten employers sharing a vendor (Bommasani et al., 2026);
   26 million credit-invisible US adults (Brevoort et al., 2015) (`sources/part2_ethics_DRAFT.md:25`).
   Weak point: the present gate is the intermediary's determination, not a test of the person's
   competence; no rule gates standing on AI literacy [Z]. Nearest case: Ohio State's "AI fluent"
   requirement, which binds a curriculum (Kamperman and Graff, p. 35 ●).
3. **The withheld competence.** Property: the party that gains from incompetence controls access to
   the competence. Evidence: fifteen seconds to accept a ride, destination unseen (Rosenblat & Stark,
   2016, p. 3762); scores workers cannot learn from (`part2_ethics_DRAFT.md:25, 83`). Weak point: no
   law forbids learning, and no source in hand shows intent.
4. **The record.** Property: one side holds the binding record of what both agreed. Evidence: template
   replies and appeals that change nothing (`part2_ethics_DRAFT.md:29`). Weak point: those sources were
   not re-verified in this arm.
5. **The monopoly.** Property: few hands hold the means. Evidence: none sourced. The "infrastructural
   enclosure" bullet (`sources/design_ethics_DRAFT.md:116`) cites nothing. Either source it or cut the
   row to one sentence that says the evidence is missing.
6. **The promise.** Property: a competence sold as the remedy for a structural condition. Evidence:
   Kamperman and Graff's Myth #2 (p. 36) ●; Berthon, Pitt and Campbell, "information is necessary,
   but not sufficient to change behavior" (p. 457) ●; Williams, "media literacy" alone (p. 101) ●.
   The Berthon passage is rewritten here from `BERTHON_NOTES.md`. Weak point: Kamperman and Graff's
   own remedy is person-side, so they are not allies on the remedy.
7. **Compulsory participation and the enforced standard.** Property: participation is compulsory and
   one standard of expression is enforced. Evidence: mandated digital participation (Bagger et al.,
   `part2_ethics_DRAFT.md:27`); language models that penalise African American English (Hofmann et
   al., 2024; Sap et al., 2019, `design_ethics_DRAFT.md:118`). Weak point: the historical wrong was
   custody and language suppression, a different order of harm.

## §5. Where the analogy stops

1. **Two likenesses the old table denied.** There was always an owner behind the page (3.4), and
   comprehension did not give leverage where the law withheld it (3.2, Douglass).
2. **The first difference: the medium answers back.** A page does not change with its reader; the
   system updates on the person's behaviour. Yeung (2016) ◐ [X]. Caution: Yeung writes of one user
   and a choice architect, not a triad.
3. **What is not a difference.** A third party who binds two people is old (3.3). So "binding
   outcomes" does not separate the two transitions.
4. **The second difference: what checks the third party.** Then: a named person, read in the parties'
   presence, with witnesses, a second interpreter, a plea for the signer who was misled. Now: appeals
   and template replies. Claude's reading (`T_adjudication.md` §8.1); the present-day cell rests on the
   two drafts.
5. **The table.** The five rows the author accepted (`OUTLINE.md` §5).
6. **What the analogy cannot show.** It cannot show intent in the present case, and it does not
   predict that the earlier harms recur at the earlier scale.

## §6. What the earlier failures ask of this transition

1. **The obligation.** `sources/part2_ethics_DRAFT.md:101`, set against the paper's finding that four
   of the five failures lay with the party holding a position, not with the learner.
2. **Against the gate: structural refusal.** The person can decline the intermediary's reading without
   losing standing (`sources/design_ethics_DRAFT.md:128`).
3. **Against withholding: a declared operating envelope and conditions for learning.**
4. **Against the one-sided record: counter-delegation.** The person keeps their own record and their
   own reader. This is the modern counterpart to the witness and the second interpreter (Claude's
   reading).
5. **The two failures with no paired principle.** Monopoly and overselling. For overselling, the
   answer is in the argument itself: do not offer literacy as the remedy. For monopoly the paper has
   no design answer; it points to policy. Berthon, Pitt and Campbell's "informing, guiding, and
   restricting" (pp. 452, 457) ● is a ready frame for that paragraph, and their finding that
   "public policy solutions are required" (p. 457) is the bridge.
6. **Who bears the remedy.** The designer and the regulator, with the person informed but not left to
   it. Keaney and Berthon (2025) treat "the right to explanation" as the customer's right (pp. 1, 3, 8)
   ●; note that their own reference list includes a paper arguing the GDPR grants none.

## §7. Limits and close

1. **What was read.** Most sources in part; no book whole. Graff chapters 1–6, Clanchy, Stock, Burns,
   Kousser, Keyssar and Williams (*Self-Taught*) unread; ten of the books are known through reviews.
2. **What the cases cover.** Mostly English-speaking jurisdictions and their empires; one world-scale
   case. The gap search was bounded (`literature/facets/Z_gap_search.md`, "Search limits").
3. **What is not shown.** No measurement of any person's algorithmacy; the present-day evidence is for
   credit, hiring and platform work, not for triage or search.
4. **Close.** Return to the positions: the test, the access, the record. End on the question the
   paper leaves for design, which is who checks the third party.
