## 1. The verdict

The round supports a narrower thesis than the one it started from, and a sharper one. The record does
not show a global community failing to spread literacy. It shows named institutions doing five
different things with literacy once standing came to depend on it, and in four of the five a
contemporary said at the time that it was wrong. The comparison to algorithmacy holds best where the
historical wrong lay in the relation between a person and whoever read, recorded or tested them, and
worst where the wrong lay in schooling.

## 3. What does not survive

1. **"As a global community."** Every case in rows 1–4 is the act of a legislature, a court, a church,
   a company or a lord. Only row 5 has world-scale actors (UNESCO, UNDP, UNICEF, the World Bank and
   the signatory states), and there the failure is a missed promise. Even there the Jomtien figure was a proposal that "countries may wish" to adopt, not a binding target [F, X].
2. **Graff's require/reward distinction.** `sources/design_ethics_DRAFT.md:85` attributes it to *The
   Literacy Myth*. Nothing opened in this round states it. Graff himself says the myth's assumptions
   were not "completely false" [F]. The book needs a library copy.
3. **The table at `design_ethics_DRAFT.md:101–106`.** Row 1 says text has no party behind it; the
   Stationers' charter and the draft's own Innis and Johns citations show an owner behind print.
   Row 4 says that under literacy comprehension yielded civic standing; rows 1 and 2 above show
   standing denied to people who could read, and Douglass describes reading as "a view of my wretched
   condition, without the remedy" [C, X]. The disanalogies that survive are that the intermediary
   adapts to the person and that it commits the outcome.
4. **Innis as a wrong done to persons.** "Monopoly of knowledge" describes how a medium is organised,
   not a bar on named people [B].
5. **Print as the cause of dialect loss.** The sources disagree on cause and on whether anyone was
   wronged [B].
6. **A gate in present AI-literacy rules.** No text opened denies an individual a vote, a job or a
   service for failing an AI-literacy test. The EU duty binds firms for their staff [Z].
7. **Clanchy.** Still unread beyond snippets. The one study of manor courts that was read finds no
   general shift to written proof in the courts it examined [A].

## 5. The standard for "failed"

Facet X sets out five standards. Three fit this record without importing present-day morals: a
contemporary named the wrong (rows 1, 2, 4, 5); the actors broke their own professed standard (the
state that tested for a schooling it had withheld; the sponsors who set the targets); and outcome
against promise (row 5). Row 3 rests mostly on what later courts said. Levy's split, cited in X,
lets the paper condemn a practice without blaming persons.

Three results follow. First, the literacy transition did have third parties who wrote or translated
the binding text for people who could not read it, so P13's "binding outcomes" is not a difference
between the two transitions. Second, the record documents safeguards around those third parties
(reading in the parties' presence, witnesses, a second interpreter, a plea in law for the signer who
was misled) more firmly than it documents abuse by them; facet G found no petition by an illiterate
party against a notary or scrivener. Third, only Waitangi meets all three parts of the check, and its
third part is contested. Burns, Nussdorfer (2009), Merrell and *Thoroughgood's Case* were not opened.

The reading that follows is Claude's: the historical third party was a named person, bound by a
public duty and checked by other people present, and the comparison to algorithmacy turns on which
of those safeguards has a present counterpart, not on whether a third party existed.

### 9.1 Kamperman and Graff (2026): what the paper must cite, and what it adds [L2]

Kamperman and Graff apply Graff's literacy myth to "AI literacy". Their second myth is "Literacy is a
certain or guaranteed path to economic security" (p. 36), which is the overselling argument of row 5.
The gap statement of facet Z no longer holds for that row: the paper cites them for it.

They do not discuss a reading test as a gate, a law against teaching, a written record overriding
speech, a monopoly over the medium, or a third party between two people. A full-text search by the
auditor found no voting test, statute, treaty, deed, licence or monopoly. On those four rows, and on
the question of standing, I found no prior work in the sources searched.

Two things in the article bear on this paper's own terms. It faults AI literacy for using "literacy
uncritically as a metaphor" (p. 35), an objection "algorithmacy" has to meet. And its remedy is
person-side with "norms for disclosure" (p. 37), so it does not argue against the remedy of teaching
the person, as §4 guessed it might. Its nearest thing to a gate is its report that Ohio State
"proclaimed all OSU graduates starting with the class of 2029 would be 'AI fluent'" (p. 35), which is
a requirement placed on a curriculum, not a bar on a person.

### 9.2 Graff: the require/reward distinction, and the first premise [L2]

No passage in the two introductions or chapter 7 sets a society requiring literacy against literacy
rewarding the literate. The auditor searched all three files for "require", "reward", "benefit" and
"demand" and found none. Graff's own contrasts are promised against delivered, and society against
individual: "literacy was more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most
individuals within it" (ch. 7, p. 321). He does not say literacy rewarded no one: "Literacy did carry
certain benefits" (1979 introduction, p. 19). Chapters 1–6 are unread.

Chapter 7 also bears on P1, that societies came to require literacy for standing. For unskilled and
semiskilled work in 1861 Ontario, "Doing his or her job seldom, if ever, required the use of reading
and writing" (p. 302), and "Simply, high degrees of literacy were often not required for work or
welfare" (p. 303); the next sentence on p. 302 says artisans needed it more often. Hunnisett's review
of Clanchy adds that literacy was "not essential for kings or for such officials as sheriffs" (p. 67).
P1 therefore holds only for the particular gates the facets document, which is how the map already
qualified it.

Graff gives no one-sentence definition of the literacy myth in the parts read; the formula facet F
quotes is from his 2010 and 2022 papers. Chapter 7 names contemporaries who doubted the promoters
(Hodgson in 1868, Phillips Thompson, the labour press; pp. 269, 299–301), which adds to row 5's
"who named it a wrong at the time" at the scale of one Canadian province.

### 9.4 Moody-Adams (1994) and the standard for "failed" [L3]

Moody-Adams argues against "the inability thesis", that a culture can leave people unable to know a
practice is wrong (p. 293). Evidence of what agents did not do cannot show what they could not do
(p. 294); "affected ignorance" is "choosing not to know what one can and should know" (p. 296); and a
culture "cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304). Her worked case is slavery in ancient Greece.
She does not discuss literacy.

What this does to §5, in Claude's reading:

1. The author's three internal standards stand. She supplies a test of blame (could have known, had
   an interest, did not examine), not a further standard for calling a practice a failure.
2. A present-day standard would not be unfair on her argument. The ruling against adding one is then
   a choice the paper makes, and the paper should not say presentism forces it.
3. For record over voice, where few contemporaries objected, a thin record of objection does not
   clear the actors. The paper can claim plausibly affected ignorance where it shows interest and a
   refusal to examine. It cannot claim proof.
4. Her line on culture supports ruling 3: "the global community" is not an agent that failed.
5. Levy's split between condemning a practice and blaming persons (§5) is not hers. She blames
   persons in degree.

- **The gate.** Holmes on Kousser: in the 1880s 64 percent of eligible Southern voters took part and

## 10. The fourth round: what else to include (2026-10-13)

The author asked for the outline's key citations and for "any other aspects of that literature that
we should include". Facets M1–M5 searched literacy studies, algorithm studies, intermediaries and
records, ethical frameworks, and cases outside the Anglo-American record; `../audit/AUDIT_05.md`
checked 243 items (201 confirmed, 30 wrong and corrected, 12 unverifiable). The results, by section of
the author's outline, are in `../../LITERATURE_TO_ADD.md`. Three bear on verdicts above:

1. §8.1 and the author's 5.3: platforms are not without procedural safeguards. EU law and contract
   law supply some. What has no counterpart is an independent, binding check at the point of decision.
2. §9.1: Kamperman and Graff are not the only prior work. Brandt's "sponsors of literacy" names the
   parties who "regulate, suppress, or withhold literacy and gain advantage by it"; Vee (2013)
   compares mass-literacy history with a computing competence; Edwards and Veale (2017) call
   explanation to the individual a "transparency fallacy". None has the five practices or the triad.
3. §2 row 1: the Saraiva Law of 1881 is the right date for Brazil. The gate is also documented across
   Latin America and in Natal (1897), and a stated exclusionary purpose only for Natal and the US.
