## 1. Sudore et al. (2006) — closed

**What I found.** PMC's HTML page for the article (PMC1831581) carries the full text. Its scanned PDF sits behind a
reCAPTCHA page and Springer sells the article, so I read the HTML and have no printed page numbers. The abstract is on
p. 867 (Springer metadata: pp. 867–873). The page notes a correction at *JGIM* 21(9):1009, which I did not read.
Locators below are section names.

**Exact words.**
- Methods, "The Consent Form": "participants were given a copy of the consent form (in English or Spanish) and were
  asked to read along while the form was slowly read to them verbatim over approximately 10 minutes."
- Methods, "Teach-To-Goal Strategy": "those sections of the consent form corresponding to each missed statement were
  re-read. Missed statements were then repeated." Up to six passes in all, with a dementia screen after the third.
- Methods: seven true/false statements; "I don't know" counted as wrong. "Complete comprehension" means all seven
  right.
- Results: "Study staff approached 329 potential participants … leaving 208 participants." "Only 4 participants who
  engaged in the consent process (2%) could not achieve complete comprehension of consent information within 6
  passes." "The remaining sample (N =204)".
- Results: "Only 57 (28%) participants responded correctly to all of the comprehension statements, thereby requiring
  only 1 pass through the consent process (Fig. 1), 106 (52%) required a second pass, and 41 (20%) required 3 or more
  passes."
- Abstract (p. 867): "With a teach-to-goal strategy, 98% of participants who engaged in the consent process achieved
  complete comprehension." Conclusion: "Using a modified consent process, little additional education was required to
  achieve complete comprehension, regardless of literacy or language barriers."
- Population: patients at San Francisco General Hospital's General Medicine Clinic, aged 50 or over (mean 61), English
  or Spanish speakers, with dementia, deafness and poor vision excluded.

**Hedges the paper imposes.** The 28% is 57 of the 204 who finished; 98% is 204 of the 208 who engaged. Participants
read along on their own copy during the first pass. The "targeted education" was re-reading the sections behind each
missed statement and asking again; the paper describes no other teaching. "Full understanding" means seven true/false
items, which the authors concede did not cover every element of consent. And the authors draw the opposite moral from
v4's framing: little *additional* education was needed.

**Line 105** ("And when modern researchers measured what reading aloud achieves, the answer was little without
teaching. In a study of 204 older adults, 40 percent with limited literacy, a simplified consent form read aloud left
only 28 percent understanding it fully; repeated rounds of questions and targeted teaching brought the figure to 98
percent of those who engaged in the process"): **narrower.** The numbers hold. "Older adults" stretches "aged 50 and
over", "teaching" overstates re-reading, and "understanding it fully" needs its definition.

Smallest correction **[proposed, Claude]**: "And when modern researchers measured what reading aloud achieves, the
answer was little without checking. In a study of 204 hospital patients aged 50 and over, 40 percent with limited
literacy, a simplified consent form read aloud while they followed on their own copy left only 28 percent answering
all seven comprehension questions correctly; re-reading the sections behind each missed question and asking again
brought 98 percent of those who engaged in the process to that standard (Sudore et al., 2006)."

**Line 165** ("a text read aloud left 72 percent of their participants without full understanding at the first pass,
and teaching to a stated goal left 2 percent of those who engaged in the process without full understanding"):
**holds.** 147 of 204 missed at least one item at the first pass; 4 of 208 never reached all seven.

## 2. Willis (2008) — partly closed

**What I found.** The 57-page SSRN working paper, "AGAINST FINANCIAL LITERACY EDUCATION -- 3/20/08 DRAFT", headed "94
Iowa L. Rev. (forthcoming, Nov. 2008)" (Penn Public Law Research Paper 08-10, SSRN 1105384). OpenAlex lists a copy
hosted by FAIR Canada as a location of this work. The Penn repository link now redirects to a search page, and I did
not try SSRN's download (bot check) or HeinOnline (subscription). The published pages are therefore unknown. Willis's
own 2011 SEC comment letter cites the work as "94 IOWA L. REV. 197 (2008)". The draft and that letter spell the title
without the hyphen that v4 uses; I could not see the printed title.

**Exact words** (draft pagination; PDF page = draft page + 2):
- Abstract: "In response, policymakers have embraced financial literacy education as a necessary corollary to the
  disclosure model of regulation." Draft p. 3: "financial literacy education is a necessary corollary to the
  disclosure model."
- Abstract: "For some consumers, financial education appears to increase confidence without improving ability, leading
  to worse decisions." Draft p. 3 hedges further: "potentially leading to worse decisions."
- Abstract and draft pp. 3–4: "When consumers find themselves in dire financial straits, the regulation through
  education model blames them for their plight, shaming them and deflecting calls for effective market regulation."

**Line 191** ("Willis (2008) argues that consumer education is 'a necessary corollary to the disclosure model of
regulation,' that it can 'increase confidence without improving ability, leading to worse decisions,' and that it
'blames them for their plight, shaming them and deflecting calls for effective market regulation.'"): **narrower.** All
three strings are exact against the abstract. But the "corollary" is the policymakers' view that Willis reports and
then attacks, not her own claim, and the second quotation is hedged in the source ("For some consumers … appears to").

Smallest correction **[proposed, Claude]**: "Willis (2008) argues that policymakers have embraced financial-literacy
education as 'a necessary corollary to the disclosure model of regulation,' that for some consumers it 'appears to
increase confidence without improving ability, leading to worse decisions,' and that it 'blames them for their plight,
shaming them and deflecting calls for effective market regulation.'" Pages: cite the published article once read;
until then the quotations are from the abstract.

**Line 23** (Willis cited for curricula that shift responsibility onto the people harmed): **holds** (draft pp. 4,
43–46).

**What I found.** archive.org's search-inside returns 403 for this lending scan, and Google Books redirected to a bot
check, which I did not pursue. Chaining the full-text search found the even-page heads ("20 | THE CENTRAL
CAPABILITIES") and a garbled recto head on p. 21 ("| 21 | CC RAE APF LENG …").

## 9. Cross (1917) p. 552, with Gabel (1928) — partly closed

**What I found.** On p. 552 the clause "though it was an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read
that he might claim his clergy" closes a sentence with no note number. The nearest note, n. 37, cites "Pike, History
of Crime, I. 300". I read Pike (1873) vol. 1 in a public-domain scan (archive.org `historyofcrimein01pikeuoft`):

- **Pike, p. 300:** "If not already, it was very soon afterwards a part of the duty of the judges to enquire whether
  gaolers had aided prisoners to gain a knowledge of letters, and to punish them when detected in the offence."
- **Pike, p. 301:** church tenants "were taught to read in prison and claimed at the trial."
- **Pike, p. 483** (notes): the authority is "the 'Liber Assisarum,' 26 Edward III., … p. 138, where the judges are
  instructed (Article 11) to enquire what gaolers had aided their prisoners to learn letters".

**Gabel** (full-text-search highlights, page unknown, in the reading-test section) puts the offence on the gaoler too.
Of a prisoner taught in gaol by two boys from Appleby, admitted "through the good offices of the gaoler", she writes:
"The offence as regards the pretended literacy was really the gaoler's, for if the prisoner had actually succeeded in
learning to read while in gaol, his claim to clergy would have held,51 though the gaoler might have been punished."
Gabel has no hit for "indictable". Stephen (1883, vol. 1) has no such claim.

**Verdict.** Cross's sentence carries no note of its own, but its likely source is Pike, p. 300, which Cross cites
for the sentence before it. Pike and Gabel both say something narrower than Cross: judges were to find and punish
*gaolers* who helped prisoners learn letters, and a prisoner who truly learned in gaol kept his clergy. I have not read
the Liber Assisarum itself.

**Line 73** ("Cross records, without a source, that it was 'an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to
read that he might claim his clergy' (p. 552)"): **narrower.** Smallest correction **[proposed, Claude]**: "Cross
records, citing no authority at that point, that it was 'an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read
that he might claim his clergy' (p. 552); the source he cites nearby says only that judges were to find and punish
gaolers who 'aided prisoners to gain a knowledge of letters' (Pike, 1873, p. 300)."

**Line 93** ("if Cross's unsourced report is right that teaching a felon to read for his clergy was itself an
offence"): **narrower.** Smallest correction **[proposed, Claude]**: "The neck-verse shows the same instinct in a
different institution: judges were charged with finding and punishing gaolers who helped prisoners learn their letters
(Pike, 1873, p. 300)." This adds Pike to the reference list: Pike, L. O. (1873). *A history of crime in England* (Vol.
1). Smith, Elder.

**Line 111** (Cross p. 555 on the widening of clergy): not re-checked; not on the thin list.
