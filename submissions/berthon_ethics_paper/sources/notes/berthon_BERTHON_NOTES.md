## 2. The mindfulness column

**Berthon and Pitt (2019), "Types of mindfulness in an age of digital distraction", *Business Horizons*
62(2): 131–137.** Read in full in the version of record. Its text is identical to the proof cited in
facet H; proof page N is printed page 130 + N.

- The quotation the draft uses is verbatim, less its first word: "Increasingly, our attention is not
  under our own control. Choices are made for us not necessarily in our interest but in the interest
  of social media companies and the advertisers they serve. In short, business models are designed to
  make us mindless." (p. 136)
- The next paragraph qualifies it: "Now before we get all hot and bothered about nasty companies
  commandeering our minds, it is worthwhile to reflect on how we got to where we are now." (p. 136)
- The paper addresses managers: "it is incumbent upon managers in general and marketers in particular
  to understand the phenomenon" (pp. 131–132). It gives consumers no instruction.
- On which mindfulness suffices: "evaluative (right) mindfulness is needed", and "wise mindfulness or
  mindfulness that leads to insight is essential if we are to see how our minds are undermined by the
  technologies and content we interact with" (p. 136).
- On firms: "challenges to innovate to develop new business models that do not run on attention become
  readily apparent" (p. 136). The paper names no mechanism, regulator or law.

## 3. The policy paper, and the later papers

**Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019), "Addictive De-Vices: A Public Policy Analysis of Sources and
Solutions to Digital Addiction", *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* 38(4): 451–468.** Read in full.

- The diagnosis: the addiction is "both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and
  engineered (designed on purpose)" (abstract, p. 451).
- The three strategies, as the paper defines them (pp. 452, 457): informing is "educate and disclose",
  guiding is "shape and incentivize", restricting is "ban and remove".
- The paper argues for the regulator: "Thus, we argue that public policy solutions are required to
  address digital addiction" (p. 457); "the negative consequences of addiction to digital experiences
  are unlikely to be addressed on the market level at which they occur" (p. 463).
- On the limits of informing: "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior"
  (p. 457, citing Worsley 2002); "Informational campaigns alone have had limited impact" (p. 460).
- On the individual: "Consumers on their own struggle to maintain equanimity with regard to their
  digital experiences" (p. 463).
- Where its remedies sit (Table 2, pp. 458–459). Inform: mandatory warning labels, which it calls
  "one of the least controversial" measures; disclosure duties. Guide: opt-in defaults; the proposal
  to "mandate natural 'stopping points'"; independent review of experiments; teaching digital hygiene
  and mindfulness in schools; a tax on the most addictive offerings, "only a long-term political
  possibility" (p. 461). Restrict: bans on predatory apps and loot boxes; a ban on advertising
  addictive media to children; network blocks and device lock-outs; a ban on freemium models.
- It calls these "our suggestions" (p. 457). Several are put as things regulators "could" do.
- It includes individual-side measures (mindfulness teaching, digital hygiene, support groups) inside
  the regulatory package. So it does not set structural remedies against individual ones.
- Facet H, working from a press release, placed stopping points and taxes under "restricting". The
  paper places both under guiding.

**Keaney and Berthon (2025), *Business Horizons*, in press.** Read in full (proof pp. 1–9). It treats
"the right to explanation" as the customer's legal right (pp. 1, 3, 8) and addresses managers. Its own
reference list includes a paper arguing that no such right exists in the GDPR.

**Berthon, Yalcin, Pehlivan and Rabinovich (2024), "Trajectories of AI technologies", *Business
Horizons* 67(5): 461–470.** Read in full. "What we design shapes who we will become" (p. 468);
regulation appears as a trajectory managers should expect (pp. 464, 467).

**Wang, Berthon and Su (2025), HICSS-58, pp. 5184–5193.** Read in full (facet H). "a sensemaking
perspective puts control over big data and AI firmly back in managers hands; and places the ethical
responsibility squarely on their shoulders." (p. 5191)

**Su, Wang and Berthon (2023), HICSS-56, pp. 4933–4942.** Read in full (facet H).

None of the six texts read in full mentions literacy. The policy paper's analogies are tobacco,
alcohol, gambling and food.

**Claude's reading, for the author to accept or reject:** Berthon's remedy follows his reader. For
policy-makers he argues for regulation; for managers he places responsibility on the firm. In no text
is the individual the sole bearer of the remedy. He is closer to the paper's own position on
"informing is not enough" than the draft passage allows.

## 4. What Williams says about user-side remedies

All from the list of "things we must not do in response to the challenges of the attention economy"
(p. 100). Read: pp. 100–102 only.

- "we must not reply that if someone doesn't like the choices on technology's menu, their only option
  is to 'unplug' or 'detox.'" (p. 100)
- "we must reject the impulse to ask users to 'just adapt' to distraction" (p. 101)
- "education is necessary – but not sufficient – for transcending this problem. Nor will 'media
  literacy' alone lead us out of this forest." (p. 101)
- "We also can't expect companies to self-regulate, or voluntarily refrain from producing the full
  effects they're organizationally structured and financially incentivized to produce." (p. 101)
