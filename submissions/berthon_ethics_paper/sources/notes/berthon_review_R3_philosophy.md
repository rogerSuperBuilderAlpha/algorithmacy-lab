## 1. Verdict

The paper takes the history of literacy as evidence that whoever holds the test, the access and the record dominates the parties who must coordinate through them, and derives three design duties for platform operators from republican non-domination, Walzer's internal critique and Young's social-connection model. It is not ready for *Business Ethics Quarterly*: two of its three business-ethics citations for the republican turn do not say what the draft says (one does not exist as cited), its central Moody-Adams quotation is not in her article, and the philosophical frames are stacked rather than reconciled. The most serious problem is methodological: the paper forbids present-day standards when judging 1740 and 1901, then classifies those same practices as "republican domination" (Pettit 1997), a present-day theory, and grounds the obligations of section 5 on a model (Young's) that cannot by itself assign a duty to one actor.

## 2. Findings, most serious first

### F1. The Moody-Adams quotation at p. 304 does not exist. **Must fix.**

§2.2: Moody-Adams "(1994, p. 304) terms this evasion 'the myth of culture as an engine of moral blindness,' warning that attributing moral culpability to diffuse cultural forces obscures the human decisions that maintain exploitative arrangements."

The phrase is not in the article. I searched the full text layer for "engine", "myth" and "blindness": "engine" and "myth" return nothing; "blindness" occurs only on p. 294 ("culturally induced 'blindness'"; "culturally induced moral blindness"), in her statement of the view she is attacking. What p. 304 says is: "any talk about culture is talk about a useful theoretical abstraction that outlives its usefulness when we attempt to think of cultures as 'forces' or 'causes' that make things happen. A culture—independent of agents who perpetuate culture—cannot be an 'agent' of anything." The draft's paraphrase of her point is fair; the quotation marks and the words inside them are not hers.

Fix: remove the quotation marks and the invented phrase; paraphrase p. 304 or quote the sentence above.

### F2. The definition of affected ignorance (pp. 293–296) is not hers either, and the matrix mislabels what she offers. **Must fix.**

§2.3: affected ignorance is "the deliberate refusal to attend to moral considerations that one has the capacity to know, driven by an unacknowledged desire to preserve one's material advantage."

Her definition, p. 296: "Affected ignorance—choosing not to know what one can and should know—is a complex phenomenon, but sometimes it simply involves refusing to consider whether some practice in which one participates might be wrong." Again at p. 301: "essentially a matter of choosing not to be informed of what we can and should know" (`L3_library_reads.md` entry 1). "Material advantage" and "unacknowledged desire" are the draft's additions. Her Greek case does say the supporters of slavery "could well have embodied their choice to perpetuate an institution that benefited nonslaves in various ways" (p. 296), so interest is in her account as a plausible motive in one case, not as part of the definition.

Three further problems in the same paragraph and the matrix:

- "When scribes, administrators, and judges ignored the obvious comprehension asymmetries of their counterparties, they engaged in affected ignorance." She says the decision between culturally induced blindness and "an unwillingness to consider that some practice might be wrong" "is hardly a simple empirical matter" (p. 294) and that "no empirical considerations alone can decisively resolve the debate" (p. 296). What she licenses is the negative claim: evidence of what agents "did not do" cannot show what they "could not do" (p. 294), so silence does not clear them. She does not license the positive verdict that named officials "engaged in affected ignorance" without the facts her Greek case assembles (an internal perspective, a benefit, a refusal to examine; `L3_library_reads.md`, Question 1). The paper may say "plausibly affected ignorance"; it cannot assert it.
- The matrix row "Moody-Adams's Benchmark: Affected Ignorance" places her beside the three benchmarks as a fourth criterion of ethical failure. She supplies a test of blame for persons, given a practice already judged wrong; she takes slavery as wrong without argument and offers no test of wrongness (`L3_library_reads.md`, Question 1, point 2).
- She argues against the paper's own method. The "standards of their own day" view "simply presumes without argument that our own moral ignorance is best attributed to 'cultural limitations' rather than to our failings as human beings who perpetuate cultures" (p. 298), and "only a misguided cultural relativism could support the view that moral criticism of another culture is never justified" (p. 308). The draft uses her inside a section that adopts internal critique to avoid anachronism. A referee who knows the article will see the contradiction. The earlier fact check already flagged this (`AUTHOR_DRAFT_CHECK.md` B6–B8); the errors returned.

Fix: quote p. 296 for the definition; move her out of the matrix or retitle the row as a condition on blame, not a benchmark of failure; soften the scribes-and-judges sentence to a conditional; add one sentence saying why the paper keeps internal standards although she would allow more.

### F5. Walzer: wrong book, and the three benchmarks are the author's, not Walzer's. **Must fix** (citation) and **should fix** (attribution).

§1, §2.3 and §5 cite "Walzer (1988)" for "the method of internal critique", and the reference list gives *The Company of Critics* (Basic Books, 1988). That book is a set of portraits of eleven twentieth-century critics (Orwell, Camus, Gramsci and others, per the publisher's and catalogue descriptions I found; I did not open it). The statement of the method is in the Tanner lectures of 1985, published as *Interpretation and Social Criticism* (Harvard, 1987): "the local judge, the connected critic, who earns his authority, or fails to do so, by arguing with his fellows" (p. 33); "Social criticism, for such people, is an internal argument" (p. 34); the question whether "standards [are] available to him that are internal to the practices and understandings of his own society, and at the same time properly critical" (p. 34); and "The same men and women who act badly create and sustain the standards by which (at least sometimes) they know themselves to act badly" (p. 42). The arm read that text (`M4_ethical_frameworks.md` §5).

The matrix heading "Walzer's Internal Benchmark" with criteria 1–3 presents the author's three tests as Walzer's. Walzer gives none of them as a list. Criterion 2 (actors broke a professed standard) is the closest to p. 42; criterion 1 (a contemporary condemned it) is the connected critic by another name; criterion 3 (outcomes fell short of promises) is not in the lectures at all. The three are a reasonable operationalisation and the paper should own them.

Is the method applied consistently? No, and this matters beyond attribution. In §3 the past is judged by its own standards. In §5 "Applying Walzer's internal critique to contemporary technology companies reveals an irreconcilable ethical contradiction": firms profess non-maleficence (Jobin et al.) and maintain asymmetries. That is a charge of hypocrisy (criterion 2), and it is the only one of the three criteria §5 applies. A hypocrisy charge has a known weakness: a firm that withdrew its ethics guidelines would escape it. The three design duties are not derived from the firms' professed standards; they are derived from Young and from the historical safeguards. So §5 announces Walzer's method and then uses a different argument. Walzer himself flags the risk: the critic's "hatred for the hypocrisy of his fellows may well outmeasure hypocrisy itself" (p. 42).

Fix: cite *Interpretation and Social Criticism* (1987) with the page numbers above; retitle the matrix column so the three criteria are the paper's; in §5 either apply all three criteria to the present (the arm proposed how: `M4_ethical_frameworks.md` §8 item 2) or say openly that the obligations rest on a different ground.

### F6. Young's model is reported accurately, but the conclusion drawn from it does not follow. **Should fix.**

§2.2 and §5: the social-connection model "assigns forward-looking political responsibility to all actors who participate in and profit from the structural processes that produce injustice"; therefore platform intermediaries "bear an unavoidable, prospective obligation to redesign"; §5: "an unavoidable, affirmative duty to redesign the architectures they control."

Young's abstract (2006, Crossref record): the model "says that all agents who contribute by their actions to the structural processes that produce injustice have responsibilities to work to remedy these injustices"; five features distinguish it from the liability model: "it does not isolate perpetrators; it judges background conditions of action; it is more forward looking than backward looking; its responsibility is essentially shared; and it can be discharged only through collective action." The draft's contrast is close enough, with one slip: the liability model is not confined to "a distinct, localized tort"; it is the general fault model of moral and legal responsibility (McKeown 2018, p. 2: "backward-looking and isolating, seeking to attribute blame or liability to particular agents for harm that has already occurred").

Three consequences the draft does not draw:

1. *Shared, including the users.* On Young's view the victims "can be called to a responsibility they share with others in the structures" (McKeown, p. 12, quoting Young); responsibility differs by "power, privilege, interest or collective ability" (p. 13). The model gets operators to the front of the queue by position; it does not assign them "an unavoidable ... duty" by themselves, and it does not relieve users. The draft's "all actors who participate in and profit" quietly adds "profit" as the selector; Young's parameters are four, and interest is one of them, held most strongly by the exploited.
2. *Discharged through collective action.* A duty on a single operator to redesign its own architecture is the liability model's shape (an identifiable agent, a specific remedy), not the connection model's. The paper needs the liability model for the obligations of §5, or it needs to say that the three duties are what collective action by operators, regulators and users should produce.
3. *Non-blaming versus blaming.* Young's political responsibility "does not entail blame" (McKeown, p. 12). Moody-Adams is a theory of blame. The draft uses Moody-Adams to blame scribes and judges (§2.3) and Young to oblige platforms (§2.2, §5) without saying it is running two models. The arm already recommended the split (`M4_ethical_frameworks.md` §2; `LITERATURE_TO_ADD.md` §3 item 3): liability for the historical bodies, where intent is in the record; connection for the present, where the paper does not prove intent. Say so in one paragraph.

### F7. Pettit and the republican remedy. **Should fix.**

§2.2's account is accurate in outline. "Protected against the arbitrary power of another", "capacity to interfere ... at will", the benevolent master, and "tracked interests" all match the Stanford entries: freedom to the extent that no one has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (Pettit 1999, 165, as quoted in Lovett, SEP "Republicanism" §1.2); "to have a master with an exceptionally benevolent disposition is ... to have a master nonetheless" (ibid. §2.1, citing Pettit 1997, 73–4); Pettit earlier held that arbitrary power "fails to track the 'interests' of those subject to it" (SEP "Domination" §4.1). Four points:

- *Formulation.* "Tracked interests" is the 1997 formulation. Pettit "now favors the language of control or the absence of control (2012: 57–58)" (SEP "Domination" §4.1); the SEP "Republicanism" calls the change "neither a real change in view nor the addition of a new condition" (§2.2). It matters here only for the remedy: on the control formulation the question is whether the affected party controls the interference, which the paper's three duties mostly do not deliver (next point). Since Pettit was not opened, the sentence "As Pettit notes, a benevolent slaveholder ..." needs a page (SEP gives 1997, 73–4) or a citation to the encyclopedia.
- *Remedy mismatch.* Two accounts of non-arbitrary power are on offer: procedural ("reliably controlled by effective rules, procedures, or goals that are common knowledge to all persons or groups concerned") and democratic ("directly or indirectly controlled by the concerned persons or groups themselves") (SEP "Republicanism" §2.2). Declared operating envelopes (§5.3) are the procedural answer, but a rule the operator declares and can redeclare is not a check in the required sense; the entry says "Just because one is better able to cope with arbitrary power, it does not follow that one's domination is any less." Structural refusal (§5.2) is exit, which the republican literature the arm read treats as the weak answer (Hoeksema 2023, abstract, in `M4` §3). Counter-delegation (§5.4) is the nearest thing to contestation, but as drafted it produces an independent record, not a forum with power to bind. The section that comes closest to the republican remedy is §5.5 (law, antitrust, utility regulation), which the draft presents as the fallback when design fails. A republican would make it the main line: external checks that "remove or replace the interference option" (SEP "Domination" §3.1).
- *Is platform power arbitrary in the technical sense?* The draft asserts it; it does not show it. Law, contract and competition constrain platforms to some degree, and the arm's own file lists binding constraints: GDPR Art. 22(3), the Platform Work Directive Arts. 10(5) and 11, the DSA Art. 20(6), unconscionability doctrine (`LITERATURE_TO_ADD.md` §2 item 1). The argument the paper needs is that these checks are not "common knowledge" rules that reliably control the specific interferences it names (pay changes, deactivation), or that the reviewer under them is the platform itself. Muldoon and Raekstad supply the material: remuneration "altered by algorithms in real-time, without their ability to contest or control whether or how this occurs" (pp. 597–598), accounts "deleted by an algorithm without warning and with no right to contest the decision" (p. 599). Cite those sentences for the arbitrariness claim rather than asserting it. Their claim about "throttle order allocations" I did not find in the pages I read. Note also that they place the dominator in "the owners and managers of a company" acting as "a group agent" (p. 594, per `M4` §3), not in the algorithm; the draft's "the platform retains unilateral ... power" is compatible but should name the firm.
- *"Illiberal".* The abstract and §1 call platform power "illiberal". The republican argument of §2.2 is that liberal non-interference is the wrong test, because it finds platform arrangements "benign or consensual". A power that passes the liberal test cannot be condemned as illiberal without changing the meaning of the word. The venue's special issue uses "illiberalism" in a political sense (anti-democratic rule); the paper's charge is domination. Either use "dominating" or "arbitrary", or argue that domination by private platforms is a species of illiberalism in the special issue's sense, which the paper does not now do.

### F8. "Structural testimonial injustice" conflates Fricker's two categories. **Should fix.**

§3.3: "the triumph of the written record over the voice operated as a form of structural testimonial injustice (Fricker, 2007)".

In Fricker's central case, testimonial injustice is "an identity-prejudicial credibility deficit" (2007, 28, as quoted in IEP), done by a hearer to a speaker. Hermeneutical injustice is the one with "no agent"; it is "purely structural" (2007, 159, as quoted in IEP). "Structural testimonial injustice" is not a Fricker category; it joins the adjective of one wrong to the noun of the other. The arm did not open the book (`M4` §1). The paper has two routes: show the identity prejudice behind the written-text rule (the arm found only an unverified lead, Hibbitts 1992), or call the Waitangi translation gap hermeneutical and the Marshall/Delgamuukw treatment of oral evidence testimonial, with a sentence for each. Symons and Alvarado (2022) is the published bridge to the algorithmic case (`M4` §1).

### F9. Negative liberty is misdescribed, and the "no formal injustice" claim is unfair to liberalism. **Should fix.**

§2.2: "Classical liberal ethics centers on negative liberty—the absence of actual, physical interference with an individual's choices. ... So long as no explicit coercion or overt fraud can be proven, negative liberty identifies no formal injustice."

Berlin's negative liberty is freedom "to the extent that their choices are not interfered with", and "there are many variations ... depending on how exactly one wants to define 'interference'" (SEP "Republicanism" §1.1); none restricts it to physical interference, and coercion by threat is the paradigm case. "Physical" should go. The second sentence also sets up a straw opponent: liberal contract and tort law have doctrines for exactly the cases the draft describes, and the arm lists them (*Williams v. Walker-Thomas*, 1965, unconscionability; *Specht v. Netscape*, 2002, notice; the UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 62 and 68; `LITERATURE_TO_ADD.md` §2 item 1). The republican point survives in a narrower form: those doctrines police particular transactions, and non-domination targets the standing capacity to interfere. State it that way.

### F10. Section 6 changes the theory of obligation. **Consider**, with one factual **should fix**.

After non-domination, internal critique and social connection, §6 adds Martela and Steger's meaning-in-life, Winner and Verbeek, and Vallor's virtue ethics. Two questions a referee will ask. First, does the paper need five frameworks? §6.1–6.2 describe harms and mechanisms, not grounds of duty; they could be evidence for the republican diagnosis rather than new frames. Second, §6.3 shifts the locus of obligation from the structure to the character of designers and executives ("engineers and corporate executives" must cultivate humility; designers "must practice" attention). That is an agent-centred ethics, and it is the one §5.1 rejects when it says platforms "must be restrained through mandatory design duties" rather than trusted to be benevolent. Green (2021) names the move as the field's standard failure: a "myopic focus on individual engineers" (`M4` §6). Republican non-domination says explicitly that the remedy is "not to make our master a better person ... but to render him less of a master" (SEP "Republicanism" §2.1). Virtue ethics and non-domination can coexist if the virtues are assigned to the designers of the checks, not to the dominators; the draft does not draw that line.

Factual point: "Moral Attention" is listed as one of "Vallor's virtues". Her twelve technomoral virtues are honesty, self-control, humility, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, perspective, magnanimity and technomoral wisdom; "perceptual attention to moral salience" is one of her seven practices of moral self-cultivation, "either constitutive of the virtues, or necessary to their development" (NDPR review of Vallor 2016, citing her pp. 64 and 120; book not opened). Aristotle is in the reference list (Bartlett and Collins translation) and nowhere in the text; remove the entry or cite it.

### F11. The organising claim of §3 uses the present-day standard the method forbids. **Must fix** (see §5 below).

§3 opens: "five distinct practices through which dominant institutions used written competence to exercise republican domination". §2.3 says practices count as failures "only when" one of three internal benchmarks is met, to avoid "anachronism and whiggish historical distortion (Butterfield, 1931)". Republican domination is a theory of 1997. The arm's recommendation was to use domination as the *description* of what the practices share and keep the three benchmarks as the *test* (`M4` §3 point 7, §9(i)). The draft runs them together: contribution 1 says each practice "operated as a mechanism of republican domination", and the matrix and §3 headings make domination the verdict. A one-paragraph separation (description versus test) resolves it; without it, the Butterfield citation indicts the paper's own section headings.

## 5. The strongest objection the paper does not anticipate

As a referee would put it:

"The paper's method forbids the paper's thesis. Section 2.3 rules that a historical practice counts as an ethical failure only by standards internal to its own time, and invokes Butterfield against anachronism. Section 3 then presents five practices as 'modes of republican domination', a classification drawn from Pettit (1997). Either the classification is doing normative work, in which case the paper is judging 1740 and 1901 by a 1997 theory and the Butterfield citation condemns it; or it is merely descriptive, in which case nothing in section 3 establishes that the practices were wrongs rather than regrettable arrangements, and the three internal benchmarks are carrying the whole argument—benchmarks that the paper attributes to Walzer but that are its own, one of which (hypocrisy) is the only one applied to the present in section 5. The obligations of section 5 are then grounded on Young, whose model is shared, non-blaming and discharged only collectively, and so cannot by itself place an 'unavoidable, affirmative duty' on one class of actor. The author needs to choose: judge both periods by non-domination and defend that against the presentism charge (Moody-Adams, whom the paper cites, would help, since she rejects the 'standards of their own day' defence at p. 298); or judge both periods by the three internal benchmarks and show that all three, not just hypocrisy, hold for platforms today. The draft does neither, and uses each framework where it is convenient."
