# Read to Them and Bound: Disclosure, Burden, and the Missing Scaffold from Oracy to Algorithmacy

## Abstract

On 10 October 2026 Microsoft's chief executive, Satya Nadella, published a short essay proposing that organizations treat frontier AI models as insider risks. The essay is among the most careful statements yet of how a deploying organization should govern systems it cannot fully understand, and its ethical design toward people stops at legibility: every safeguard it offers is either a control held by the organization or a text for someone to read, and the people its systems act on appear once, as recipients of a disclosure after failure. I argue that this design repeats three mistakes of the passage from oracy to literacy, when standing, proof, and agreement moved from speech to writing. It places the burden of understanding on the person who must read; it keeps that person in a literacy-based experience when the medium has changed; and it does not scaffold the person's growth into the new competence, which I call algorithmacy. Using internal critique, I trace each mistake through the Spanish Requerimiento, first carried to the Americas in 1514, the common law's duty to read, the reading test of benefit of clergy, and treaties made in speech and bound in writing, and I trace the slower record of what institutions did when they took the danger seriously: they checked understanding, shifted risk to the drafter, changed the medium, and taught. From that record I derive three duties for the operators of algorithmic systems and answer the objection that scaffolding is only another way of shifting responsibility to the user.

**Keywords:** AI governance; disclosure; transparency; responsibilization; scaffolding; history of literacy; algorithmacy

## 1. Introduction

On 10 October 2026 Satya Nadella, Microsoft's chief executive, posted a short essay titled "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era" (Nadella, 2026). By 12 October it had been viewed about thirteen million times (Nadella, 2026, as captured that day). Its premise is candid. The frontier models now being given "access to our most sensitive data" cannot be traced to a code path the way older software could, and "A model provider's assurances do not relieve us of that responsibility" (paras. 2–3). Its remedy is an architecture of containment: treat each model the way a firm treats a powerful employee who might err or be compromised, keep every control outside the model, and "separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it" (paras. 5, 7–8, 11). Its closing line turns the ordinary logic of trust around. The most trustworthy system, Nadella writes, is not the one with the model we trust most but the one that lets us trust the model least (para. 21).

Read for what it asks of people, the essay offers two kinds of safeguard. The first kind are controls that the deploying organization holds: model diversity, independent controls over what a model may access and do, continuous testing, and an emergency brake that "An authorized person" can pull mid-task (paras. 14, 16–17, 19). The second kind are texts. Chain-of-thought transparency is to be "non-negotiable," although Nadella himself calls it, on its own, "not sufficient or dependable" (para. 9). Every meaningful model action "must leave tamper-proof human readable evidence" (para. 15). And when systems fail, "we need timely disclosure to those affected" (para. 20). The essay's "we" throughout is the deploying organization. The people on whom its systems act appear once, in that last clause, as recipients of a disclosure after the failure. Nothing in the essay builds anyone's capacity to understand or to act on what the systems do.

Microsoft's product documentation shows where that leaves the person at the other end. The current note for Microsoft 365 Copilot tells end users to "always review and verify AI-generated responses," and on the risk of overreliance it says: "we help mitigate this risk by adding disclaimers to our products but users should still make sure to review the accuracy of the answers" (Microsoft, 2026). The firm's 2024 transparency report calls responsible use "a shared responsibility between application developers and application users" (Microsoft, 2024, p. 34). In this design the disclosure is the firm's part, and the reading is the user's.

This paper asks what ethical design owes a person when a new competence becomes a condition of standing and the person does not yet have it. The question is old. When agreements, title, and proof moved from speech to writing, institutions faced it repeatedly, and their characteristic answer is preserved in a document drafted by the Castilian jurist Palacios Rubios and carried ashore by a Spanish expedition at Santa Marta on 14 June 1514 (Las Casas, 1876, pp. 156–157; Fernández de Oviedo, 1853, p. 29). The Requerimiento was a text to be read aloud, in Spanish, to Indigenous peoples before a Spanish force attacked them. It told its hearers that the papal grant of their lands was contained "in certain writings which passed upon the subject as aforesaid, which you can see if you wish" (Helps, 1855, p. 380). It asked them to "take the time that shall be necessary to understand and deliberate upon it" (p. 381). It declared that "the deaths and losses which shall accrue from this are your fault," and it ended by asking the notary present for written testimony that it had been read (p. 382). Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, who carried the paper on the expedition, wrote that showing it to the Indians "poco hacia al caso"—did little good (Fernández de Oviedo, 1853, pp. 27–28).

Nadella's essay is not the Requerimiento. It threatens no one, and its author asks for more care than most of his industry offers. But it shares the Requerimiento's structure at three points, and the history of the passage from speech to writing shows that each is a mistake. I argue, first, that the essay places the burden of understanding on the person who must read: the staff member who must make sense of "human readable evidence" after the fact, and the person affected, who receives a disclosure. Second, it keeps that person in a literacy-based experience—a regime of records, notes, and notices—when the thing to be understood is an adaptive process that no record captures. Third, it does nothing to scaffold the person's growth into *algorithmacy*, the competence of coordinating with others through a system that reads all parties and acts on them. Each of these mistakes has a long record in the transition from oracy to literacy, and so does each of the remedies that eventually corrected it.

Two qualifications shape the argument. The common law of that transition did not treat disclosure as sufficient; it treated the signature as sufficient, and the duty to read a document aloud to someone who could not read it arrived there later, as a partial reform. The insider-risk essay therefore stands not at the beginning of the history but at its middle, where institutions had learned to disclose and had not yet learned to check understanding, to move the risk of misunderstanding onto the drafter, or to teach. And the scaffolding the paper asks for is not a curriculum handed to users. Firms already offer those, and critics have shown how such curricula shift responsibility for a platform's or a market's harms onto the people it harms (Docherty & Valderrama Barragán, 2026; Willis, 2008). What the record supports is a narrower duty: support built into the system, owed by its operator, withdrawn as the person becomes able, and paired with the operator's own accountability.

The paper makes three contributions to normative business ethics. First, it gives the critique of disclosure-based AI governance a historical ground. Critics have shown that notice and consent burden individuals (Solove, 2013) and that transparency assumes an observer who already knows enough to use it (Selbst & Barocas, 2018); the history shows the same structure operating for centuries, names the institutions that ran it, and records the objections contemporaries raised. Second, it distinguishes three wrongs that the critique usually runs together—where the burden falls, what medium the safeguard takes, and whether anyone builds capacity—and shows that they were corrected separately and at different times. Third, it derives from the corrections three duties for the operators of algorithmic systems, and it states the condition under which a duty to scaffold does not collapse into responsibilization.

Section 2 defines algorithmacy, the three wrongs, and the standard by which I judge them. Section 3 traces the three wrongs through the passage from speech to writing and records what institutions did when they took the danger seriously. Section 4 reads the insider-risk essay and Microsoft's practice against that record. Section 5 derives the duties and answers four objections; Sections 6 and 7 state the limits and conclude.

## 2. Framework: A Competence, Three Wrongs, and a Standard

### 2.1 Algorithmacy

Policy talk about AI usually pictures one person and one machine. Most consequential algorithmic systems stand instead between people who need something from each other. A screening vendor stands between an applicant and an employer, a ride-hail platform between a driver and a rider, and—in the case Nadella addresses—an agent acting on an organization's behalf (paras. 2–3) between that organization and the people its actions reach. I use *algorithmacy* for the competence this arrangement demands: the capacity to coordinate with others through an automated third party that reads all of them and commits outcomes that bind them. The word borrows the suffix of literacy and numeracy to mark a competence on which standing comes to depend, not to claim that dealing with an algorithm is a kind of reading.

The difference matters for what follows. Literacy is a competence with fixed objects. A page reads the same to every reader, and a person who learns to read it gains a skill that accumulates. The objects of algorithmacy move. An adaptive system updates on the behavior of the people it reads, which Yeung (2017) calls the "networked, continuously updated, dynamic and pervasive" character of algorithmic choice architecture, and a skill aimed at it cannot be acquired by reading a description of what it did last week. Collins, Brown, and Newman (1987, pp. 2–4) designed cognitive apprenticeship for the parallel problem in teaching reading: "standard pedagogical practices render key aspects of expertise invisible to students," so that a teacher must externalize "processes that are usually carried out internally." The processes of an adaptive model are invisible to everyone but its operator. Whoever holds them holds the means of making the competence learnable.

### 2.2 Three wrongs

The argument turns on three wrongs. They are distinct, and each can be present without the others.

The first is *placing the burden on the person*: assigning the work of understanding to the party least able to do it, and treating the opportunity to understand as if it were understanding. Solove (2013, p. 1893) puts the modern form exactly: privacy self-management "cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals," and is "being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities" (p. 1880). The wrong is not that people are asked to understand. It is that the institution's duty ends when the text has been offered.

The second is *keeping the person in a literacy-based experience*: offering, as the safeguard against a process, a text about the process. Disclosure, logs, notes, and explanations are all of this kind. They work for a reader who already knows enough to see what a disclosure means. Selbst and Barocas (2018, p. 1094) observe that "The process of moving from observation to knowledge to accountability cannot be assumed; in many cases, the ability to leverage observations for accountability requires preexisting knowledge that allows observers to appreciate the significance of a disclosure." A text offered to someone who lacks that knowledge leaves them where they were. The wrong lies in choosing the medium of the old competence for a safeguard that must operate in the new one.

The third is *not scaffolding the person's growth*. Scaffolding has a precise meaning in the learning sciences, and I hold to it. Belland (2017, p. 5) defines it as "interactive support that leverages what students already know to help them meaningfully participate in and gain skill at tasks that are beyond their unassisted abilities." Three exclusions follow. "Simply telling students what to do or how to do it does not qualify as scaffolding" (p. 6); "A job aid does not meet the definition of scaffolding" (p. 19); and "Support that continues indefinitely does not meet the scaffolding definition either" (p. 18). Scaffolding is contingent on what the person can now do, and it fades. On this definition a disclosure is not a scaffold, nor is a record, nor an emergency brake held by someone else.

The three wrongs come apart. An institution may move the burden off the person—by checking understanding itself, say—and still offer only texts. It may change the medium without teaching anyone anything. And it may teach in a way that leaves the burden exactly where it was, which is the objection Section 5.4 answers.

### 2.3 The standard

By what standard did the institutions of the oral-to-written transition fail? Importing a present-day theory to condemn 1514 would invite the charge Butterfield (1931) made against history written as the ratification of the present. I use internal critique instead. Walzer (1988, p. 42) observes that "The same men and women who act badly create and sustain the standards by which (at least sometimes) they know themselves to act badly." Following v3 of this argument, I count a practice as an ethical failure if contemporaries condemned it in the moral vocabulary of their time, if the institution broke a standard it professed, or if the outcome fell systematically short of what its sponsors promised. Moody-Adams (1994, p. 296) adds that the ignorance which excuses past actors is often affected—"choosing not to know what one can and should know"—so a thin record of objection does not show that no one could have known. The same three benchmarks apply to the present: the operators of AI systems are judged here by the standards they profess, the objections their contemporaries raise, and the promises made for their products.

Republican political theory supplies a description of the position these institutions held, though not the standard of wrongness. On Pettit's account a person is unfree to the extent that another has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (Pettit, 1999, p. 165, as quoted in Lovett, 2026). Lovett (2026) adds the point that matters most here: "Just because one is better able to cope with arbitrary power, it does not follow that one's domination is any less." A person who has learned to read the notice has gained a skill. Whether she has gained standing depends on what the notice's author may still do.

### 2.4 Institutions, not minds

The comparison between speech and writing carries a hazard. A long tradition, associated with Goody and Watt and with Ong, held that writing changes how people think, and that oral and literate societies are divided by a cognitive gulf. Finnegan (1973) attacked that "great divide," and Street (2003, p. 77) showed how the assumption that literacy has effects "regardless of the social and economic conditions" served literacy campaigns as much as scholarship. Goody himself later wrote that the division between "literacy" and "orality" "is never a question of crossing a single frontier, a simple binary shift" (Goody, 1987, p. 106). Ong's own book says it treats "the differences in 'mentality' between oral and writing cultures" (Ong, 1982/2002, p. 3). I make no claim of that kind. The three wrongs are claims about institutions: who was assigned the work of understanding, which procedure was kept, and who built whose capacity.

Two objections to writing survive without any claim about minds, and Plato stated both. In the *Phaedrus*, the Egyptian king Thamus tells the inventor of letters that his pupils "will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things, when they are for the most part ignorant" (Plato, 1914, 275a–b), and Socrates adds that a written word "always needs its father to help it" (275e). The first is the objection to texts without scaffolding. The second is the objection to a text that cannot answer the person who must act on it. Both bear on the institutions of the transition that followed, and both bear on the insider-risk essay.

## 3. The Three Wrongs in the Passage from Speech to Writing

The cases below come from the long period in which agreement, title, and proof moved from the spoken word and the remembered witness to the signed and recorded text. They are not a survey. I chose them because each shows an institution deciding, in a recorded act, what a person who could not read was owed. For each I ask who was bound by the writing, whether it was read or explained to them, whether that was treated as enough, who bore the burden, and whether anyone built the person's capacity.

### 3.1 The burden

The Requerimiento is the purest case. Its procedure was ordinary Castilian law before it was colonial law: "the presentation of a demand or warning to an addressee and its official recording" (Israeli, 2022, p. 39). Its legal effect lay in the record. "Once a requerimiento had been conducted, the addressee could not pretend to be ignorant of the demand, and could be considered as having been given a fair opportunity to make amends," and "the lack of a testimonio could render the entire procedure meaningless" (p. 46). The text read in the Americas kept that structure. It disclosed its grounds by reference to writings the hearers might inspect; it asked them to understand; and it assigned them the consequences of not doing so: "y protestamos que las muertes y daños que dello se recrecieren sea á vuestra culpa" (Las Casas, 1876, p. 156). What the procedure produced was a notary's signed testimony that the text had been read, not any evidence that it had been understood.

Contemporaries condemned it in their own vocabulary. Las Casas argued that the law already denied force to a command addressed to people who did not know its language, and he asked what anyone could say in defence of those who read it "á quien ni palabra del entendían, más que si fuera en latin referido ó en algarabía" (Las Casas, 1876, pp. 162–163). He recorded raiding parties that halted a league from a village and "entre sí leian el requerimiento á los árboles" before attacking at dawn, after the captain had asked the notary for testimony that the village had been summoned and had refused (pp. 198–199). Oviedo, who was there, put the point more drily. The governor handed him the text "as if I understood the Indians, or as if we should find any one there who would make them understand it" (Helps, 1855, p. 387, translating Fernández de Oviedo, 1853, pp. 27–28). The text promised its hearers "el tiempo que fuere justo" to understand; Oviedo promised to tell later what time the captains gave, "atando los indios después de salteados, y en tanto leyéndoles toda aquella capitulaçion del requirimiento"—tying up the Indians once seized, and meanwhile reading them the whole of it (Fernández de Oviedo, 1853, p. 32). When Oviedo later asked Palacios Rubios whether the Christians' consciences were satisfied by it, the jurist said that they were, "si se hiçiesse como el requirimiento lo diçe"—if it were done as the text said (p. 31). The author of the disclosure judged it by its form.

The common law reached the same result by a different road. It did not ask whether a text had been read to the person who signed it. It asked whether the person had signed. In *L'Estrange v F. Graucob Ltd* (1934), the seller's agent testified that he had read the whole order form aloud to the buyer, small print included; she said he had not. The court did not resolve the dispute, because on the rule Scrutton L.J. stated nothing turned on it. "When a document containing contractual terms is signed, then, in the absence of fraud, or, I will add, misrepresentation, the party signing it is bound, and it is wholly immaterial whether he has read the document or not" (p. 403). Maugham L.J. found it "an irrelevant circumstance that the plaintiff did not read, or hear of, the parts of the sales document which are in small print" (p. 407). The American rule, stated in *Upton v. Tribilcock* (1875, p. 50), made the signer's failure to read "his own fault," and courts applied it to people who could not read at all. In *Washington Mutual Finance v. Bailey* (2004), borrowers who could not read told the lender so and asked what they were signing; they were told it was "insurance and financial paperwork," an answer the court found "not plainly misleading" (slip op. pp. 11–12). The Fifth Circuit reversed a holding that the lender had owed them an oral explanation, adopting a district court's statement that "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him" (p. 10, quoting an earlier district court decision). *Morales v. Sun Constructors* (2008, slip op. p. 6) extended the rule to a worker who could not understand English: absent fraud, his inability to "read, write, speak, or understand the English language is immaterial," and an incomplete translation was "due to Morales' failure to request any explanation or translation" (p. 8 n. 2).

The burden could also be set, and raised, by the party who administered the text. For centuries English criminal law spared from hanging a first offender who could claim "benefit of clergy," and after an ordinance of 1350 the claim came to be tested by reading. A verse of the Psalter, usually from the fifty-first Psalm, became known as the "neck-verse," and "if the accused was able to 'read like a clerk'" he escaped the gallows (Cross, 1917, p. 552). The examiner chose the passage. In 1640 a yeoman named Osmund Gibbs petitioned that, falsely accused, he had been "put to read for his life," and that the judge, incensed against him, made "a clear bar … to prevent promptings" and "turned him unto one of the hardest verses to read" (p. 554). Cross records, citing no authority at that point, that it was "an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read that he might claim his clergy" (p. 552); the source he cites nearby says only that judges were to find and punish gaolers who "aided prisoners to gain a knowledge of letters" (Pike, 1873, p. 300). Parliament removed the reading condition under Anne, on what Cross calls the "rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered criminals" (p. 555), and abolished the privilege for felony in 1827 (Criminal Law Act 1827, s. 6).

A broader version of the same shift ran through medieval England, though its losers were not the unlettered. Under Edward I the Crown's attorneys argued in many of the *quo warranto* proceedings that the only sufficient warrant for a franchise was a written charter, a claim that, as Clanchy (1979, pp. 3, 27) shows, the Crown abandoned in the 1290s by conceding tenure "from time out of mind." The episode shows the burden of producing a writing placed on the holder of a remembered right. It also shows who gained from the change: the monasteries, the experts in writing, were in Clanchy's account also the greatest forgers (Clanchy, 1979).

### 3.2 The medium

The second wrong concerns not who must understand but what they are given. Where an agreement was made in speech and bound in writing, the written text could say less than the spoken one, and the people who had heard the promise had no way to check the page.

The Canadian courts have recorded the result. In *R. v. Marshall* (1999, para. 14) the Supreme Court observed of eighteenth-century treaties that "The Indian parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations," and held that enforceable obligations were not confined to "the written document of March 10, 1760" (para. 40). In *R. v. Badger* (1996, para. 52) it held that "The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and they did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement." Treaty 6 of 1876 shows the gap in a single clause. Treaty commissioner Alexander Morris told the Cree at Fort Carlton that "A medicine chest will be kept at the house of each Indian agent, in case of sickness amongst you" (Morris, 1880, p. 218). The written treaty provides for a medicine chest "for the use and benefit of the Indians, at the discretion of such Agent" (p. 355). The treaty was signed, its text records, "the same having been first read and explained" by three named interpreters (p. 357). The disclosure was made. The text it disclosed was not the promise.

The Treaty of Waitangi of 1840 shows the same gap with a translator in it. The missionary Henry Williams made the Māori text and interpreted "sentence by sentence"; more than 500 rangatira signed, 39 of them the English text (Waitangi Tribunal, 2014, pp. 343, 355, 386). The English text ceded sovereignty. The Tribunal found that the rangatira who signed the Māori text "did not cede their sovereignty" (p. xxii).

Israeli's account of the Requerimiento names the mechanism. In fifteenth-century Castile the procedure "did have an important dialogical component": the addressee was asked whether he would comply, and "the notary was obliged to document the addressee's response and incorporate it into his testimony" (Israeli, 2022, p. 47). Moved to people who could not understand the text and had no way to answer it, the procedure, as its sixteenth-century critics argued, kept its form and lost the conditions it needed to work (p. 47). The record was still made. It recorded only one side.

The form persists. Under the English civil procedure rules, an affidavit by a person who cannot read must carry a certificate that the person administering the oath "read the affidavit to the deponent," that "the deponent appeared to understand it," and that the deponent signed or made a mark (Ministry of Justice, 1998, para. 7.1). The test of understanding is the certifier's impression. The read-over is a disclosure, made by a person independent of the parties (para. 9.2) and recorded in the words of a prescribed form (Annex 1), and its test of understanding is an impression.

### 3.3 The missing scaffold

The third wrong concerns capacity. The institutions that bound people by writing rarely built their ability to read it, and some forbade others to try.

The anti-literacy statutes of the American slave states are the starkest case. South Carolina's Negro Act of 1740 fined anyone who taught an enslaved person to write, and between 1829 and 1834 Georgia, Virginia, Alabama, and North Carolina passed acts of their own (South Carolina, 1740/1840, s. 45; General Assembly of Georgia, 1830; General Assembly of Virginia, 1831; General Assembly of Alabama, 1832; General Assembly of North Carolina, 1831). Frederick Douglass recorded the rationale as his master gave it: learning "would forever unfit him to be a slave" (Douglass, 1845, p. 33). The neck-verse shows the same instinct in a different institution: judges were charged with finding and punishing gaolers who helped prisoners learn their letters (Pike, 1873, p. 300). Oviedo wished the text had been made understood first, and his one proposal for doing so was a jest. Oviedo suggested to the governor that the text be kept "until we have one of these Indians in a cage, so that he may learn it at leisure and the lord bishop may make him understand it" (Fernández de Oviedo, 1853, p. 31, my translation); the governor, Oviedo reports, took it with much laughter.

Capacity was built, but mostly by people the institutions did not employ. In 1866 an inspector of the Freedmen's Bureau schools reported that "in very many places I have found what I will call 'native schools,' often rude and very imperfect, but there they are, a group, perhaps, of all ages, trying to learn," taught by "Some young man, some woman, or old preacher, in cellar, or shed, or corner of a negro meetinghouse, with the alphabet in hand, or a torn spelling book" (Alvord, 1866/1905, p. 145). Booker T. Washington remembered "a whole race trying to go to school" (Washington, 1901, p. 30). Where states and churches did scaffold at scale, the scaffold served the sponsor. In Lutheran Scandinavia reading was held to be "everybody's calling and right," and people were held to it by worldly compulsion and church discipline (Johansson, 1985, p. 64). Cuba's campaign of 1961 sent 268,420 tutors to live in the homes of adults they taught in groups of two or three, and declared 707,212 people literate at about the level of a first-grade reader (Bhola, 1984, pp. 92, 97–98, 100); its primer taught "Fidel is Our Leader" (p. 100). Hannah More, who taught the English poor to read in her Sunday schools, explained in 1801, "I allow of no writing for the poor" (Roberts, 1835, p. 72). Brandt (1998, p. 166) named this pattern: the sponsors of literacy "enable, support, teach, model, as well as recruit, regulate, suppress, or withhold literacy—and gain advantage by it in some way." Scaffolding, in the history, was possible at scale. It was also never neutral.

### 3.4 What institutions did when they took the danger seriously

Against these three wrongs the record holds a slower history of correction. It is real, it came late, and it rarely went as far as teaching.

Some institutions checked understanding instead of recording a reading. Castile's Pragmática of Alcalá of 1503 required notaries to read their notes to the parties and witnesses present and required a witness to sign for a party who could not (Almeida Cabrejas, 2019, p. 24). Modern Spanish notarial regulation defines a complete reading by what it communicates: a reading counts as complete only when the notary has conveyed the instrument's content "con la extensión necesaria para el cabal conocimiento de su alcance y efectos, atendidas las circunstancias de los comparecientes"—to the extent needed for full knowledge of its scope and effects, given the circumstances of the persons appearing (Reglamento Notarial, 1944, art. 193). English probate law requires that before a will "which appears to have been signed by a blind or illiterate testator" is admitted, the registrar "shall satisfy himself that the testator had such knowledge" of its contents (Non-Contentious Probate Rules, 1987, r. 13). The burden of the check sits with the institution, not the testator.

Others moved the risk of misunderstanding onto the party that drafted the text. In *Jones v. Meehan* (1899, pp. 10–11) the United States Supreme Court held that a treaty with an Indian nation "must be construed, not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers, but in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians," because the treaty was drawn up by the United States "in their own language," and the signatories' "only knowledge of the terms in which the treaty is framed is that imparted to them by the interpreter employed by the United States." *Badger* (1996, para. 52) adopts the same rule for Canada's treaties. At Anglo-Iroquois councils, when either side doubted an interpreter, "others were employed, formally or informally, to 'assist' and check the accuracy of his translations" (Hagedorn, 1988, p. 61). And English law allowed a plea of *non est factum* to a person who could not read and had "a written contract falsely read over to him," provided he was not negligent (*Foster v. Mackinnon*, 1869, p. 711).

Some changed the medium. In 2021 Spain extended the 1862 notarial law to allow parties with disabilities easy-read text, pictograms, multimedia devices, and interpreters (Ley del Notariado, 1862, art. 25, as amended by Ley 8/2021)—159 years after the same article first made reading the deed to the parties, or letting them read it, the notary's duty. And when modern researchers measured what reading aloud achieves, the answer was little without checking. In a study of 204 hospital patients aged 50 and over, 40 percent with limited literacy, a simplified consent form read aloud while they followed on their own copy left only 28 percent answering all seven comprehension questions correctly; re-reading the sections behind each missed question and asking again brought 98 percent of those who engaged in the process to that standard (Sudore et al., 2006).

### 3.5 Four stages, and what cuts the other way

Taken together, the cases describe four stages, not two. At the first, the signature or the recorded reading was sufficient, and the person bound bore the whole burden of understanding (*L'Estrange*; the Requerimiento; *Bailey*). At the second, the institution disclosed: it read the text aloud, explained it, or translated it, and certified that it had done so (the Treaty 6 interpreters; the affidavit jurat; California Civil Code § 1632, which requires a translation of certain consumer contracts negotiated in another language). At the third, the institution checked understanding or moved the risk of misunderstanding to the drafter (art. 193; probate r. 13; *Jones v. Meehan*). At the fourth, someone built the person's capacity, and the scaffold had a sponsor whose interests it served. The history is not a march through these stages; most institutions never left the first two.

Three facts cut the other way, and the argument must carry them. Writing also widened standing: benefit of clergy saved people who could read, and in the end everyone convicted of a clergyable felony, from the gallows (Cross, 1917, p. 555). Intermediaries often served the unlettered well; the Castilian notarial records are full of witnesses signing at the request of parties who could not (Almeida Cabrejas, 2019, p. 29). And Treaty 6's signatories asked for capacity themselves. The Cree requested "missionaries and school teachers" (Morris, 1880, p. 185), and the treaty promised schools "as to her Government of the Dominion of Canada may seem advisable" (p. 353). What Canada's schools became is a separate wrong, which the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) attributes to removal and the suppression of language, not to the teaching of reading. The demand for capacity came from below. The decision about its form stayed with the sponsor.

## 4. The Echoes in the Insider-Risk Essay

### 4.1 What the essay asks for, read fairly

Nadella's essay deserves a careful reading, because the charge against it is not that it is careless. Its principal moves are controls, and they are good ones. No one model, the essay says, should "become the sole dependency for an important outcome or be responsible for verifying its own work" (Nadella, 2026, para. 14). "Orgs should be able to independently determine what a model can access and what actions it can take" (para. 17). "No single model should control both a system's behavior and the evidence required to determine whether that behavior is aligned with the original intent" (para. 18). The essay draws these from an information-security tradition that dates, it says, to the 1970s (para. 11), and it does not treat disclosure as sufficient: it calls chain-of-thought transparency "not sufficient or dependable, because we don't yet know how to make model outputs themselves consistently faithful or transparent" (para. 9). On one point it asks for more than the European Union's AI Act, which sends reports of serious incidents involving high-risk systems to market surveillance authorities (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, art. 73), while the essay asks for "timely disclosure to those affected" (para. 20).

Microsoft's own published standard asks more still. Its Responsible AI Standard of 2022 requires teams to identify "stakeholders who are subject to decisions informed by the system" and to evaluate "whether each stakeholder who will make decisions or be subject to decisions based on the behavior of the system can interpret the relevant system responses reasonably well" (Microsoft, 2022, p. 9, requirements T1.1 and T1.3). It requires evaluation of "whether each oversight or control function can be accomplished by stakeholders in realistic conditions of system use" (p. 8, A5.4). The standard tests whether people can do what the design asks of them. The insider-risk essay does not ask.

So the charge must be stated precisely. The essay's ethical design toward people is exhausted by legibility. Its controls are held by the deploying organization and exercised by "An authorized person" (para. 19); its safeguards for everyone else are texts—a chain of thought, "tamper-proof human readable evidence," a disclosure after failure. The person who must read them appears nowhere in the essay as someone whose capacity is the designer's concern. In the terms of Section 3.5, the essay stands at the second stage: it discloses, and it records that it has disclosed.

### 4.2 The burden: evidence for whom?

"Every meaningful model action must leave tamper-proof human readable evidence. If it can't be observed, it can't be trusted!" (para. 15). The principle presupposes a reader. The essay does not say who that is, and its context makes it someone inside the deploying organization reconstructing what an agent did. Human-factors research has studied that reader for forty years. Bainbridge (1983, p. 776), writing of operators set to monitor automated plants, concluded that "The human monitor has been given an impossible task," because the system acts faster and on finer information than the monitor can follow, and because the job is "very boring but very responsible, yet there is no opportunity to aquire or maintain the qualities required to handle the responsibility." Green (2022, p. 1) finds that policies requiring human oversight of algorithms "rest on an uninterrogated assumption: that people are able to effectively oversee algorithmic decision-making." And when oversight fails, responsibility can settle on the nearest person. Elish (2019, p. 40) calls that person the moral crumple zone, who "bears the brunt" of a failure in a system she had little power to control.

The first substantive commentary on the essay that I found made the point from inside the practice it describes. Writing on the day the essay appeared, Sudjianto (2026), writing as a practitioner, accepted Nadella's architecture and observed that "An audit trail tells us what the agent did, not whether it should have done it," and that "An experienced model validator does not become experienced by reading validation reports." His concern was that firms are automating "the very work through which people develop the expertise needed to independently verify AI." On his account, the records the essay demands will be read by people whose training ground the same systems are removing.

For the people outside the organization, the burden is plainer: they receive a disclosure after failure (para. 20). Inside it, at the point of use, Microsoft's documentation tells users to verify. Its 2026 note for Copilot lists, under "End-users should," that they "always review and verify AI-generated responses," and it states that users "need to be trained to effectively interact with and benefit from the service" (Microsoft, 2026). The disclaimer is the firm's discharge. The review is the user's duty. In the structure of the duty-to-read cases, the document has been offered, and the person who does not read it with care has only herself to blame.

The echo of the Requerimiento lies in what the record proves. The notary's testimony established that the text had been read, and with it, as in the Castilian procedure from which the text descended, that the addressee "could not pretend to be ignorant of the demand" (Israeli, 2022, p. 46). A log that an agent's action was recorded in human-readable form, and a disclaimer that the user was told to verify, establish the same kind of fact. They prove that understanding was made possible, and they say nothing about whether anyone understood.

### 4.3 The medium: a text about a process

The second echo concerns what the person is given. The essay's safeguards for people are all written: a chain of thought, an evidence log, an incident report. Each describes a process that is not itself a text—an adaptive model acting in a changing environment—and each reaches the reader after the process has moved on.

Evidence from the study of AI-assisted decisions shows that the form of a safeguard can decide whether it helps. Vasconcelos and colleagues (2023) asked people to check an AI's solutions to mazes of varying difficulty. On the hardest mazes a visual explanation reduced overreliance on wrong answers; in an exploratory comparison, the same information written out as text ("left, up, right, up…") did no better than no explanation at all, and in a small exploratory study a salient visual marker of the error brought overreliance to zero (pp. 129:11, 129:15, 129:18–129:21). Their experiments, they conclude, suggest that overreliance "is not merely an immutable inevitability of cognition but at least, in part, a strategic choice" (p. 129:32): people engage with an explanation when engaging costs less than the task. In their study, a text that had to be read and interpreted was the costlier form. Calo (2012), the most careful defender of notice, concedes the textual case. "Studies show only marginal improvement in consumer understanding where privacy policies get expressed as tables, icons, or labels, assuming the consumer even reads them" (p. 1033), and he saves notice by moving it beyond sole reliance on "language or its symbolic equivalent" and into the experience of using the product (pp. 1033, 1071–1072). The best argument for disclosure is an argument for moving beyond the literacy-based form.

The privacy literature measures what the form costs. Reading every privacy policy a person meets in a year would take about 201 hours on one estimate and 244 on another (McDonald & Cranor, 2008). In a controlled sign-up, 74 percent of participants skipped the policy, and 98 percent missed clauses that would have shared their data with the National Security Agency and taken their first-born child as payment (Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch, 2022, p. 4784, restating their 2020 study). Solove (2013, p. 1888) lists the failures in order: people do not read the policies; if they read, they do not understand; if they understand, they often lack the background to choose; and if they have it, their choices might be skewed. Edwards and Veale (2017, p. 67) carry the result into algorithmic governance: "Individual data subjects are not empowered to make use of the kind of algorithmic explanations they are likely to be offered."

One objection must be met here. Conversational AI speaks; is it not a return to orality, so that the comparison with the oral-to-written transition runs backwards? Commentators have argued so (see Marz, 2026, who reports and rejects the claim). Ong himself answered it in advance. His "secondary orality" is "essentially a more deliberate and self-conscious orality, based permanently on the use of writing and print," and the computer "maximizes commitment of the word to space" (Ong, 1982/2002, p. 133). Linguists have long classed typed online exchanges as conceptually oral but written in medium (Heyd, 2021, p. 133), and some call machine-generated text "a new form of literacy, tertiary literacy" (Arató & Balázs, 2024, p. 607), though the same authors also posit a tertiary orality (p. 609). Whatever the interface, the governance is written. The chat may talk; the safeguard is a log, a note, and a disclosure.

### 4.4 The missing scaffold

The third echo is an absence. Nothing in the essay builds the capacity of anyone—staff or affected—to understand or act on what the systems do. Its words for people are "authorized person," "those affected," and "us"; the words "skill," "user," "people," and "literacy" do not occur in it.

Microsoft does invest in competence, and the form of the investment repeats the history's pattern. In July 2025 its president announced more than $4 billion in donations of cash and technology to schools and nonprofits and a programme that "will help 20 million people earn an in-demand AI skilling credential ranging from foundational fluency to advanced technical training" (Smith, 2025). These are courses beside the product, not support within it. The 2022 Standard's own route to interpretation runs through "the system UX, features, reporting functions, and educational materials" (Microsoft, 2022, p. 9, T1.2)—a route that runs largely through texts about the system. Its research arm has built tools that would count as scaffolds, among them "an Excel add-in prototype that helps users assess AI-generated code" (Microsoft, 2024, p. 34). The report presents them as prototypes.

The law repeated the pattern and then retreated from it. Article 4 of the AI Act required providers and deployers to "take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, art. 4). The duty ran to staff. The people on whom the systems are used appear only as persons to be considered, and the duties the Act owes them directly are chiefly notices and explanations (arts. 26(11), 50, 86). In July 2026 the Digital Omnibus replaced the article with a duty to "support the development of AI literacy," adding that "This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual," on the stated ground that the original duty "creates an additional compliance burden, particularly for smaller enterprises" (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, art. 1(5) and recital 8). The duty to build competence was weakened in the summer before the essay appeared, and the duties to disclose were left in place, though some were deferred (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, art. 1(40)).

Nadella has used the word himself. In December 2025 he listed, among what "we still need to get right," "A new concept that evolves 'bicycles for the mind' such that we always think of AI as a scaffolding for human potential vs a substitute," and called it "the product design question we need to debate and answer" (Nadella, 2025, para. 4). The insider-risk essay is where he answered a different question, about containing models, and its answer contains no scaffold. Plato's Thamus described the result of letters offered without one: readers who "will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things" (Plato, 1914, 275a–b). A staff member with a complete log and no training in reading it, and a user with a disclaimer and no means of checking the answer, are in that position.

## 5. What Ethical Design Owes Instead

### 5.1 The ground

The history in Section 3 does not show that disclosure was useless. It shows that disclosure was the second of four stages, and that the institutions which went further did three things: they took on the burden of checking understanding or moved the risk of misunderstanding to the drafter; they changed the medium in which the person met the text; and, rarely, they built the person's capacity. Each of these answers one of the three wrongs. I state them as duties of the operator—the firm that designs and runs the system, or deploys it with authority over others—because the operator holds the positions the history describes. It writes the record, chooses the medium, and holds the means of making the competence learnable.

The duties rest on the three benchmarks of Section 2.3. Operators profess a standard the disclosure-only design breaks: Microsoft's own standard asks whether people "can interpret the relevant system responses reasonably well" (Microsoft, 2022, p. 9) and whether oversight can be performed "in realistic conditions" (p. 8). Contemporaries object, from Sudjianto's practitioner's warning to the scholars who find that human-oversight policies rest on an "uninterrogated assumption" (Green, 2022, p. 1). And the promise falls short: disclosure is offered as the means by which people stay in control, and Section 4 showed that the people offered it are poorly placed to use it.

### 5.2 Against the burden: check understanding and carry the risk

The first duty is to take back the burden the disclosure-only design assigns. It has two parts, both drawn from the record.

The operator should check understanding, not certify a reading, wherever a person is to be bound by a system's output or is to oversee it. The probate registrar must "satisfy himself" that a testator who could not read knew the will's contents (Non-Contentious Probate Rules, 1987, r. 13); the Spanish notary must convey a deed's scope and effects "atendidas las circunstancias" of the persons before him (Reglamento Notarial, 1944, art. 193). For AI systems the analogue is the test Microsoft's standard already describes and the essay omits: evidence, gathered by the operator, that the people who must oversee a system can do so in realistic conditions, and that the people subject to its decisions can interpret what it tells them. Green (2022) proposes that agencies justify any reliance on human oversight with exactly such evidence before deployment, and that "The default assumption should be that human oversight is likely to be ineffective, unless proven otherwise" (p. 15). Sudore and colleagues' result is the measure of what is at stake: a text read aloud left 72 percent of their participants without full understanding at the first pass, and teaching to a stated goal left 2 percent of those who engaged in the process without full understanding (Sudore et al., 2006).

Where understanding cannot be secured, the operator should carry the risk of its absence. *Jones v. Meehan* (1899) construed a treaty "in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians" because the United States had drafted it in its own language and supplied the only interpreter. The same reasoning applies to an operator who writes the terms, the log, and the disclosure. Helberger, Pierson, and Poell (2018) put the modern version: platforms "have an obligation to create the conditions that allow individual users to comply with their responsibilities," and users can be held to those responsibilities only to the extent that platforms and public authorities have done so (pp. 3, 11). A duty to verify AI output, assigned to a user whom the operator has not equipped to verify it, inverts that order.

### 5.3 Against the literacy-based experience: change the medium

The second duty is to deliver safeguards in the medium in which the person meets the system. A log written for later reading cannot do this, because the person acts before the log is read. What can is support that operates at the point of use and costs less to engage with than the task it checks. The experimental record points the way: in a small exploratory study, salient, visual markers of a system's error reduced overreliance where written explanations did not (Vasconcelos et al., 2023), and Calo's (2012) "visceral notice" moves the information into the experience of using the product, not only into language about it.

A second form of the duty concerns what a person needs to know to act. One form is a *declared operating envelope*: advance, binding limits on what a system may do to the person, stated in terms the person can act on without understanding the model. A driver need not understand a pricing model to know the minimum she will be paid for a trip of a given time and distance. The envelope changes the medium of knowledge from a description of the process to a commitment about its outcomes. It remains a text, but it is a text that does not need the process to be understood.

### 5.4 Against the missing scaffold: build the competence in the system

The third duty is to scaffold the person's growth into algorithmacy. The definition in Section 2.2 fixes what that means. The support must be interactive, contingent on what the person can now do, and withdrawn as the person becomes able (Belland, 2017, pp. 5–6, 18–19). It must operate where the competence is exercised, which for an algorithmic system means inside it. And it must externalize the processes the person cannot otherwise see, which is the problem cognitive apprenticeship was built to solve for reading, writing, and mathematics (Collins et al., 1987, pp. 3–6).

Systems built this way exist, at small scale. Kulesza and colleagues (2015) designed a classifier that "explains the reasons for its predictions to its end user, who in turn explains corrections back" (author's version, p. 1); participants with no more than introductory programming experience who used it scored 52 percent higher on a test of their mental model of the system than a control group (p. 9). Bainbridge (1983, p. 777) proposed the industrial form more than thirty years earlier: let the operator "use hands-on control for a short period in each shift," or, if that is "laughable," provide simulator practice, because "the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, … may need the greatest investment in human operator training." Sudjianto's (2026) recommendation that firms "redesign junior roles around developing judgment, not merely supervising AI outputs" is the same duty stated for the essay's own setting.

The evidence that such support changes outcomes is real but modest. A meta-analysis of 76 randomized trials of financial education found effects of about a tenth of a standard deviation, small but larger than earlier estimates, and noted that one of the main findings of an influential earlier review "is not confirmed" (Kaiser et al., 2022, working paper p. 16; on the earlier review, Fernandes et al., 2014). The modesty is itself an argument for where the duty should sit. Courses taken once and far from the decision decay; the earlier review found no significant effect of instruction at delays of twenty months, and its authors suggested that "'just-in-time' financial education may have promise, alone and embedded in decision support systems" (Fernandes et al., 2014, working paper pp. 14, 22).

Scaffolding without an avenue to act does not give a person a capability. Nussbaum (2011) distinguishes the internal capability a person develops from the conditions that let her exercise it, and observes that a society "might do quite well at producing internal capabilities but might cut off the avenues through which people actually have the opportunity" to use them (p. 20). A worker who has learned how a platform's dispatch works, and has no way to contest what it does, has gained a skill and no standing—the point Lovett makes about coping with arbitrary power, and the point Douglass made in 1845, that reading "had given me a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy" (Douglass, 1845, p. 40). The three duties therefore stand together. The scaffold builds the person's competence; the check and the shift of risk keep the operator answerable while the competence grows; and the envelope, joined to an independent party able to hear disputes, gives the competence something to act on.

### 5.5 Four objections

*The essay is for enterprises, not consumers.* It is, and its "we" is the deploying organization. But the organization is not a reader. Its staff are, and the essay's principles assign them the task Bainbridge called impossible without building their ability to do it. And the organization's systems act on people outside it, whom the essay addresses once (para. 20). An ethics of AI governance written for enterprises still owes something to the people the enterprises' systems act on, and the essay's answer is a disclosure.

*Disclosure respects autonomy.* Calo (2012, p. 1049) states the defence well: "Notice purports to respect the basic autonomy of the consumer or citizen by arming her with information and placing the ultimate decision in her hands." Solove (2013, pp. 1899–1900) concludes that privacy self-management "should not be abandoned," and he finds the paternalistic alternatives troubling. Disclosure also disciplines the discloser: Solove suggests its greatest practical effect may be on the companies required to write it (p. 1900). Nothing here asks operators to stop disclosing. The argument is that disclosure is the second of four stages and cannot be the last. Berthon, Pitt, and Campbell (2019, p. 457) reached the same verdict about digital addiction: "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior." They file education with disclosure under "informing" (p. 452). The history suggests separating them. A disclosure informs a person who can already read; a scaffold builds the ability to read.

*Scaffolding is responsibilization by another name.* This is the strongest objection. Willis (2008) argues that policymakers have embraced financial-literacy education as "a necessary corollary to the disclosure model of regulation," that for some consumers it "appears to increase confidence without improving ability, leading to worse decisions," and that it "blames them for their plight, shaming them and deflecting calls for effective market regulation." Docherty and Valderrama Barragán (2026, p. 571) find that Meta's digital-literacy curricula, by "placing on individuals the burden of living well on platforms," arrange "users and technology in relationships of responsibilized control." Martin (2019, p. 845) notes that on her own account, a firm that gives the people who use an algorithm tools to revisit how it works moves toward a traditional product handoff, with "(minimal)" responsibility of its own. And the history in Section 3.3 shows that every scaffold had a sponsor. The objection defines the conditions the third duty must meet. A scaffold that is a curriculum beside the product, offered voluntarily, and paired with no change in who is answerable, is what Willis and Docherty describe. A scaffold built into the system, owed rather than offered, withdrawn only as the person becomes able, and joined to the operator's duty to check understanding and carry the risk of its absence, leaves the operator answerable while the competence grows. Business ethics has already asked firms to take on a duty of this kind. Scherer, Neesham, Schoeneborn, and Scholz (2023, pp. 428–429) ascribe to business "a responsibility to contribute to organizing individual and collective maturity" and offer, among their illustrations of what it could mean, "informing and training citizens," and Vredenburgh (2022, accepted manuscript pp. 18, 22) argues that people cannot be expected to use explanations across the institutions that affect them "absent support," so that "states and companies may need to invest more resources in providing individuals with free experts." What the history adds is the order in which the duties must be discharged.

*The history is analogy.* It is evidence about institutions and an analogy for the present, and the analogy is bounded at the point Section 2.1 marked: the objects of literacy stay still, and the objects of algorithmacy move. That difference strengthens the argument. A person can, with time, learn to read a fixed text without anyone's help; the freedpeople's native schools show how far people went to do so. A person cannot learn an adaptive system that only its operator can see into without the operator's help. The duty to scaffold falls on the operator because no one else can discharge it.

## 6. Limits

The historical cases are narrow. They come from Castile and its empire, England, the United States, Canada, and New Zealand, with Cuba and Scandinavia as checks, and they are stronger for some wrongs than others. The burden is documented with statutes, judgments, and contemporary condemnation; the medium rests on treaty cases and later courts; the missing scaffold rests on withholding laws and on scaffolds built by sponsors with interests of their own. Several sources are known to me in part: Clanchy's and Goody's books through excerpts, the consent study and Willis's article through their abstracts, Nussbaum's book through search snippets, and the financial-education studies through their working papers. The Requerimiento's Spanish text and its contemporary critics I read in nineteenth-century editions.

The present-day evidence is also narrow. The essay is one text by one executive, and Microsoft's practice is read from its published standards, reports, and product notes, not from its products in use. The experimental evidence on explanation and overreliance comes mostly from one person working with one system at decision time; none of it tests the after-the-fact reading of agent logs that the essay's principle of observation requires. Whether the scaffolds proposed here would work at the scale of a workforce or a customer base is not yet shown.

The analogy compares positions and practices. It does not show intent. Nothing here suggests that Nadella or Microsoft means to shift blame onto users as the Requerimiento's authors shifted it onto its hearers; the comparison concerns the structure of what is offered and what is recorded. And the argument does not claim that the people bound by writing thought differently from those who wrote, or that the people who meet AI systems lack any capacity they could not acquire. Its claims concern who was assigned the work, what form the safeguard took, and who built whose capacity.

## 7. Conclusion

Nadella's essay ends on a reversal: the most trustworthy system is the one that lets us trust the model least. The reversal is right about models, and the essay builds an architecture to support it—controls outside the model, diversity among models, a brake in human hands. What the essay does not ask is what would let the people its systems act on, and the staff who must read its evidence, need to trust the model less because they understand it more. Its answer to that question is a text.

The passage from speech to writing shows where that answer leads. A text read aloud in a language its hearers did not know made them answerable for a war. A signature bound a buyer to terms read, or not read, to her. A reading test set by the court decided who hanged. The institutions that did better learned, slowly, to check what a person understood, to carry the risk when they had written the text, to change the form the text took, and sometimes to teach. The operators of AI systems are at the start of that learning, and they have the means, which no notary had, of building the competence their systems demand into the systems themselves. Whether they will use those means, and who will require them to, are the questions the essay leaves open.
