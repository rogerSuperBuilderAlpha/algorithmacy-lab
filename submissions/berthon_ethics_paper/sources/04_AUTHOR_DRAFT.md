Algorithmacy: Refactored Abstract, Introduction, and Structural Outline
Abstract
Current AI ethics frameworks routinely prescribe "AI literacy" and transparency as the primary safeguards against algorithmic harm. Yet historically, when reading and writing became mandatory conditions for civic, legal, and economic standing, the deepest injuries did not arise from an uneducated public. They were inflicted by the institutions that controlled the test, the access, and the record. This paper examines the transition to literacy to theorize algorithmacy—the competence demanded when two human parties coordinate through an opaque algorithmic intermediary that observes both and commits binding outcomes. Assessing the historical record against standards internal to their eras, I analyze five institutional practices: gating standing on arbitrary tests, withholding competence by statute, elevating the written text over oral pacts, monopolizing the means of reproduction, and overselling economic returns. Mapping these practices onto platform coordination reveals that educational remedies fundamentally misdiagnose structural extraction. Contemporary intermediaries do not merely repeat these abuses; they amplify them because the medium itself adapts dynamically to the person it evaluates, all while lacking the procedural safeguards (such as sworn notaries, independent interpreters, or procedural pleas like non est factum) that historically restrained human scribes. The paper derives three architectural obligations for system designers: structural refusal, declared operating envelopes, and infrastructural counter-delegation.
Introduction
Across credit allocation, hiring, and gig labor, people increasingly coordinate through algorithmic systems that evaluate their behavior and enforce binding decisions. When scholars examine the ethics of these systems, they consistently converge on a baseline duty: do no harm. Jobin, Ienca, and Vayena (2019) reviewed 84 sets of AI-ethics guidelines and found non-maleficence prioritized in 60 and beneficence in 41. The standard institutional remedy derived from this duty focuses on informing the subject—mandating disclosure, generating explanations, and training the user under the banner of "AI literacy." Yet disclosure rarely restructures power. As Berthon, Pitt, and Campbell (2019, p. 457) observe regarding digital harms, "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior." Williams (2018, p. 101) arrives at a similar ceiling in the attention economy, warning that neither education nor media literacy can resolve structural capture. Kamperman and Graff (2026, p. 36) locate the historical root of this impasse: the AI literacy movement merely resurrects the foundational myth that individual literacy guarantees civic standing and economic security. While the parallel between acquiring literacy and learning to live with algorithms is already established, current critiques treat it as little more than an overpromised skill.

The historical failure of the literacy transition, however, went far beyond empty promises. The deepest harms were committed by the authorities who governed the test, managed access, and controlled the ledger once textual competence became compulsory. When the Alabama Constitutional Convention met in 1901 to establish a voter literacy test, its president openly declared their mandate: "to establish white supremacy in this State" (Alabama Constitutional Convention, 1901, p. 8). Decades earlier, Hugh Auld ordered his wife to halt Frederick Douglass's reading lessons because literacy would "forever unfit him to be a slave" (Douglass, 1845, p. 33). Centering the written record also functioned as an instrument of dispossession: in R. v. Marshall (1999, para. 14), the Supreme Court of Canada observed that Mi'kmaq leaders signing an 1760 treaty had no practical ability to produce their own written records, allowing colonial interpretations to overwrite oral covenants. These were not innocent educational deficits. They were deliberate deployments of institutional leverage made possible because literacy had been transformed from a private skill into a mandatory gate. Contemporary scholarship focuses heavily on the user's need to learn, leaving the institutional positions that dictate the terms of participation largely unexamined.

This oversight is costly because those same institutional positions have been reoccupied. When an applicant, borrower, or platform worker engages an employer, bank, or rider, they coordinate through a third-party intermediary that observes both actors and enforces binding terms. Navigating this triadic relationship requires a distinct competence: algorithmacy. But the intermediary holds the test: when employers share automated hiring models, applicants rejected by one risk algorithmic exclusion across an entire sector (Bommasani et al., 2026). It commands access: a ride-hail driver must accept a fare within fifteen seconds, often without knowing the destination or compensation (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). And it controls the ledger: workers contest automated determinations through closed ticketing systems that offer standardized refusals. If historical harms arose from how institutions wielded the test, the gate, and the record, then an ethical model that focuses primarily on upskilling the user directs its obligations to the wrong party.

This paper interrogates the historical transition to literacy to document what institutions did with written competence once civic and economic standing depended on it. I evaluate historical practices using three internal criteria: contemporary objection, breach of an institution's own professed standards, or systemic failure to deliver promised civic benefits. Five institutional practices meet these criteria: gating legal standing through discretionary reading tests, barring acquisition of the skill by law, allowing written texts to override oral agreements, monopolizing print technology through exclusive charters, and overselling individual social mobility. Treating this history as empirical evidence and its algorithmic counterpart as a bounded analogy, I isolate the mechanisms that map onto digital platforms—and pinpoint the exact boundaries where the historical parallel fails.

The argument advances three contributions to organizational and design theory:

Disaggregating Systemic Failure: It replaces abstract notions of collective societal failure with five concrete, documented institutional practices, identifying the specific legal and economic mechanisms used to exploit compulsory competence.
Reframing the Ethical Subject: It redirects the analogy between literacy and algorithmacy away from individual user deficits toward structural intermediary positions. Educational interventions cannot resolve harms rooted in asymmetries of testing, access, and record-keeping.
Isolating Structural Divergence and Design Obligations: It identifies the critical boundary where the analogy breaks down: historical texts were passive, whereas algorithmic intermediaries learn and adapt dynamically against their subjects. Furthermore, platforms operate stripped of the historical procedural safeguards—such as sworn notaries, dual interpreters, and common-law doctrines like non est factum—that once restrained human scribes. From this divergence, the paper establishes three design principles for algorithmic coordination: structural refusal, declared operating envelopes, and infrastructural counter-delegation.

Restructured Outline
§2. Theoretical Foundations: Algorithmacy, the Literacy Transition, and Evaluative Criteria
2.1 Defining Algorithmacy Beyond the Literacy Metaphor
Core Claim: Algorithmacy is not a metaphor for reading code or interpreting digital dashboards; it is a relational competence demanded by an irreducible triadic structure—two parties coordinating through a computational intermediary that observes both and renders binding outcomes.
Theoretical Anchor: Rebuts Kamperman & Graff's (2026) critique that modern technology scholarship adopts "literacy" uncritically. The comparison here operates across institutional positions within compulsory mediation, not across cognitive mechanisms.
Analytical Boundary: Rejects the premise that mediation fundamentally restructures human cognitive wiring, focusing strictly on institutional and relational power.
2.2 Bounding the Historical Literacy Transition
Core Claim: The transition to literacy was neither uniform nor universal; it occurred through specific institutional interventions that transformed reading and writing into legal prerequisites for standing.
Evidence: Graff (1979) on nineteenth-century Ontario, demonstrating that manual and trade labor functioned effectively without literacy until institutions introduced textual hurdles; Clanchy (1979) on early administrative states where oral oaths bound kings and magistrates long before text became compulsory.
Analytical Distinction: Distinguishes between literacy as an optional, rewarding capability and literacy as a mandatory institutional gate.
2.3 The Internal Standard of Ethical Failure
Core Claim: Historical practices cannot be judged by contemporary standards; an institutional practice qualifies as an ethical failure only when evaluated against benchmarks internal to its historical period.
Three Evaluative Benchmarks:
Contemporary Objection: At least one recorded contemporary voice publicly condemned the practice as unjust.
Internal Incoherence: The institution violated explicit values or procedural standards it claimed to uphold.
Unrealized Promise: The intervention demonstrably failed to deliver the civic or material returns promised by its sponsors.
2.4 Affected Ignorance and the Limits of Historical Acquiescence
Core Claim: The historical absence of vocal protest does not exonerate an exploitative practice; it frequently signals deliberate institutional suppression.
Theoretical Grounding: Moody-Adams (1994) on "affected ignorance"—the active, institutional choice to ignore readily available evidence of exploitation when doing so protects procedural dominance.
Application: Used specifically to interrogate legal transitions where bureaucratic text erased longstanding oral covenants without formal objection from illiterate parties who were denied standing to protest.
2.5 Locating Institutional Responsibility
Core Claim: Blame cannot be dissolved into vague abstractions like "the global community" or "society at large"; ethical failures must be traced to specific courts, parliaments, chartered monopolies, and administrative bodies.
Theoretical Anchor: Mobilizes Moody-Adams's critique that cultures cannot act as ethical agents. Collective responsibility is reserved exclusively for formal international campaigns (such as UNESCO target frameworks) with explicit, failed mandates.
2.6 The Real Value of Historical Literacy
Core Claim: Critiquing what institutions did with literacy does not deny the material value of learning to read. People actively pursued literacy because it provided real economic and intellectual agency.
Evidence: Johansson (1981) on parish reading campaigns in Sweden; Washington (1901) on the post-emancipation push for education across the American South.
Conceptual Guardrail: Distinguishes the genuine empowerment of acquiring a competence from the institutional misuse of that competence as a barrier to participation.
§3. Five Institutional Abuses of the Textual Transition
3.1 The Gate: Facial Neutrality and Administrative Discretion
The Device: Discretionary literacy examinations presented as neutral civic standards, with evaluation left to local administrative gatekeepers (South Carolina v. Katzenbach, 1966).
Professed vs. Actual Intent: The Alabama Constitutional Convention of 1901 framing tests around civic competence while leadership explicitly designed them to secure white supremacy (Knox, 1901).
Internal Incoherence: The same state authorities administering literacy tests systematically underfunded or outlawed schools for the populations being tested (Gaston County v. United States, 1969).
Comparative Scope: The Australian Immigration Restriction Act of 1901 (dictation tests in arbitrary European languages); nineteenth-century Brazilian franchise exclusions (Saraiva Law of 1881).
Contemporary Resistance & Resolution: Presidential vetoes by Cleveland (1897) and Wilson (1917) condemning tests as penalties for lack of educational opportunity; legislative dismantling via the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
3.2 The Withheld Competence: Criminalizing Acquisition
The Statutory Landscape: South Carolina's 1740 slave code outlawing writing; antebellum statutes in Virginia, Georgia, Alabama, and North Carolina criminalizing assembly for literacy instruction.
Institutional Rationale: Formal declarations that education rendered enslaved persons ungovernable and fostered rebellion (Douglass, 1845; Williams, 2005).
Consequence: Structural awareness without agency—Douglass's observation that learning to read illuminated his subjugation without providing the legal leverage to change it.
Contemporary Resistance: Narrative resistance by Frederick Douglass; deliberate civil disobedience by educators like Margaret Douglass (1853).
3.3 The Record Over the Voice: The Textual Erasure of Oral Pacts
The Mechanism: The party controlling the written word drafting binding instruments that systematically overwrote oral negotiations with illiterate counter-parties.
Judicial Recognitions: Canadian jurisprudence in R. v. Marshall (1999) acknowledging that Indigenous signatories lacked the means to record their understanding, and Delgamuukw (1997) noting the systematic discounting of oral histories in contract interpretation.
The Biased Intermediary: The Treaty of Waitangi (1840), where British translators drafted Maori versions (tino rangatiratanga) that obscured the sovereign concessions written into the English text.
Historical Procedural Safeguards: Early institutional checks designed to curb scribe dominance—the Castilian Pragmática of 1503 (requiring public readings of instruments before witnesses); dual-interpreter requirements at Anglo-Iroquois treaty councils; and common-law relief under non est factum (Foster v. Mackinnon, 1869).
3.4 The Monopoly: Enclosure of the Means of Reproduction
The Apparatus: Royal charters creating corporate print cartels—the Stationers' Company charter of 1556 granting exclusive printing rights and unilateral search-and-seizure authority.
Statutory Caps: The Licensing Act of 1662 capping England's master printers at twenty, concentrating textual reproduction in a state-aligned guild.
Contemporary Resistance: Milton's Areopagitica (1644) challenging pre-publication licensing; petitions from journeymen printers against guild monopolies.
Analytical Boundary: Clarifying that early modern print was defined by state and corporate enclosure, not an open public square.
3.5 The Oversold Promise: Literacy as a Structural Panacea
The Institutional Myth: International campaigns promoting individual literacy as a self-sufficient engine of economic development while ignoring structural poverty.
Empirical Shortfalls: The Jomtien Declaration (1990) and Dakar Framework (2000), which missed adult literacy targets by wide margins; UNESCO (2026) data showing hundreds of millions of illiterate adults globally, heavily concentrated among women.
Historical Precedents: Graff (1979) demonstrating that nineteenth-century urban literacy rates frequently outpaced job requirements, serving social discipline rather than upward mobility.
Contemporary Critique: Paulo Freire (1970) and the 1975 Persepolis Declaration condemning functional literacy initiatives that treat systemic disenfranchisement as personal ignorance.
3.6 Boundary Case: Compulsory Enclosure and Linguistic Erasure
Historical Analysis: Indian Boarding Schools in the United States and Residential Schools in Canada (TRC Report, 2015).
Distinction: The fundamental crimes were forced child removal, abuse, and systematic cultural and linguistic erasure—not the teaching of reading itself.
Translatable Dynamics: Isolate two structural mechanisms relevant to contemporary platforms: compulsory participation and the violent enforcement of a single administrative language.

§5. The Breakdown of the Analogy: Dynamic Plasticity and the Void of Safeguards
5.1 Where the Historical Analogy Holds
Shared Structural Positions: In both transitions, institutional gatekeepers controlled the terms of engagement, while individual learners bore the personal costs of mediation.
The Fallacy of Pure Comprehension: Just as Frederick Douglass discovered that reading alone could not break legal subjugation, achieving high algorithmic comprehension gives a platform worker zero leverage over a closed optimization model.
5.2 The First Divergence: The Plasticity of the Intermediary
The Static Page vs. The Adaptive Model: A printed contract or statute is physically fixed; its letters do not rearrange themselves based on who is reading.
Dynamic Recalibration: Algorithmic intermediaries adjust their behavior, pricing, and evaluation parameters in real time based on the subject's actions (Yeung, 2016). The target shifts precisely as the user adapts.
5.3 The Second Divergence: The Total Collapse of Procedural Buffers
Historical Safeguards Around Human Scribes: The early modern transition built formal legal protections around fallible scribes—the requirement that notaries read instruments aloud before witnesses, the institutional use of dual translators, and common-law relief under non est factum when an illiterate party was deceived.
The Modern Procedural Void: Platform mediation strips these protections away. The intermediary replaces the sworn notary with a terms-of-service checkbox and automated dispute workflows that lack human discretion or independent review.
5.4 Limits of Historical Inference
Analytical Boundary: Historical parallels expose structural patterns, but they cannot prove subjective malicious intent among modern platform designers, nor can they forecast identical historical outcomes.
§6. Architectural Obligations for Algorithmic Coordination
6.1 Redirecting the Locus of Ethical Obligation
Core Principle: Because the deepest harms of mediation stem from who controls the test, the access, and the record, ethical obligations belong to the designers and operators of the intermediary, not to the individuals trying to navigate it.
Reframing Interventions: Replaces consumer-side "AI literacy" initiatives with direct architectural and regulatory limits on platform intermediaries.
6.2 Countering the Gate: The Right to Structural Refusal
Design Mandate: Systems must provide viable alternative pathways for participation and coordination that do not penalize users who opt out of algorithmic evaluation.
Mechanism: Denying an intermediary the power to gate civic, legal, or economic access behind opaque behavioral scoring.
6.3 Countering the Withheld Competence: Declared Operating Envelopes
Design Mandate: Intermediaries must explicitly state their operational constraints, optimization metrics, and performance boundaries to both parties prior to coordination.
Mechanism: Eliminates dynamic information asymmetries (e.g., hidden ride destinations, shifting pay algorithms), creating predictable conditions where users can learn and respond.
6.4 Countering the Asymmetric Record: Infrastructural Counter-Delegation
Design Mandate: Providing users with independent algorithmic agents capable of inspecting, auditing, and recording platform transactions.
Mechanism: The contemporary operationalization of the notary and the second interpreter: personal software agents that maintain independent, verifiable audit logs of the intermediary's inputs and determinations.
6.5 The Limits of Pure Design: Structural Monopoly and Regulatory Boundaries
Design Mandate: Acknowledging where architectural choices hit their limit. Monopolized compute and infrastructure cannot be designed away; they demand policy interventions, public utilities, and antitrust enforcement (Berthon et al., 2019).
§7. Boundary Conditions and Conclusion
7.1 Methodological and Empirical Boundaries
Scope Limits: Focuses primarily on Anglo-American and colonial legal traditions; findings directly map to platform work, consumer lending, and automated hiring, but require further verification in search and administrative triage.
Data Limits: Evaluates institutional practices and statutory frameworks rather than quantifying individual cognitive algorithmacy.
7.2 Conclusion: The Scribe's Ghost in the Machine
Synthesis: Reintegrates the core insight: literacy became dangerous not when people failed to learn it, but when institutions weaponized the positions that governed it.
Final Horizon: The fundamental ethical challenge of generative and algorithmic systems is not educating users to survive structural asymmetry, but constructing verifiable procedural safeguards that restrain the automated intermediaries standing between them.
