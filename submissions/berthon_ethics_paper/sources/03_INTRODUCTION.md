**[Common ground]** People now reach credit, work and care through systems that read them and decide,
and the scholars who write on the ethics of those systems agree on a first duty: do no harm. Jobin,
Ienca and Vayena (2019) reviewed 84 sets of AI-ethics guidelines and found non-maleficence named in 60
of them and beneficence in 41. The remedy that follows most readily from that duty is to inform the
person — disclose the system, explain its output, teach its user — and the teaching now has a name,
"AI literacy." The same scholars agree that this remedy has a limit. Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019)
write of digital addiction that "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior"
(p. 457). Williams (2018) writes of the attention economy that education is necessary but not
sufficient, "Nor will 'media literacy' alone lead us out of this forest" (p. 101). Kamperman and Graff
(2026) reach back to the history of reading for the reason: among the myths the AI-literacy movement
repeats is that "Literacy is a certain or guaranteed path to economic security" (p. 36). The
comparison between learning to read and learning to live with algorithms is, in short, already on the
table, and Kamperman and Graff draw it through one failure, the overselling of a competence.

**[Complication]** Overselling is the smallest part of what went wrong when reading became a
condition of standing. The parties who held the test, the access and the record did the larger part, and they
did it with literacy. The president of Alabama's constitutional convention of 1901 told the delegates
who would write a literacy test into the franchise what they had met to do: "to establish white
supremacy in this State" (Alabama Constitutional Convention, 1901, p. 8). Hugh Auld forbade his wife
to teach Frederick Douglass to read because learning would "forever unfit him to be a slave"
(Douglass, 1845, p. 33). And the Supreme Court of Canada, reading a Mi'kmaq treaty of 1760 in 1999, observed
of treaty-making in general that "The Indian parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the
opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations" (*R. v. Marshall*, 1999, para. 14). None of these is a
broken promise. Each is the use of a position that opened once a competence became compulsory — the
examiner's, the teacher's, the record-keeper's. Kamperman and Graff do not treat these positions, and
in the sources I searched (Crossref, Consensus, arXiv and the open web) I found no work that asks who
occupies them now.

**[Concern]** The question matters because the positions are occupied. A person who deals with an
employer, a lender or a rider through a platform coordinates with that other person through a third
party that reads both and commits an outcome binding on both; I call the competence this demands
algorithmacy. The third party holds the test: when employers share a screening vendor, 4% of applicants
to ten positions were rejected by all ten (Bommasani et al., 2026). It holds the access: a ride-hail
driver has about fifteen seconds to accept a ride without seeing its destination or fare (Rosenblat &
Stark, 2016, p. 3762). And it holds the record of what the two parties agreed. If the lesson of the
literacy transition were only that promoters oversold reading, then better teaching and more honest
promises would answer it. If the lesson is that the wrongs were done by whoever held the test, the
access and the record, then an ethics that addresses the person who must learn addresses the wrong
party.

**[Course of action]** I therefore read the record of the literacy transition for what named
institutions did with literacy once standing depended on it. I count a practice as an ethical failure
on one of three grounds, each internal to its time: a contemporary called it wrong, the actors broke a
standard they themselves professed, or the outcome fell short of the promise. Five practices meet
those grounds — gating standing on a reading test, withholding the competence by law, letting a
written record override what was said, holding the means of print by monopoly, and overselling
literacy's returns. I treat this history as evidence and the step to algorithmacy as an analogy, and
for each practice I name the property the analogy rests on and the point at which it gives way.

**[Contribution]** The paper makes three contributions. It replaces the single charge that "the
global community" failed to spread literacy with five documented practices and their actors, and it
keeps the collective charge only for the international campaigns, where world-scale actors set targets
and missed them. It moves the comparison between literacy and algorithmacy from the competence to the
positions around it, which is where four of the five failures lie and where Kamperman and Graff's
argument does not reach. And it identifies what differs this time: the intermediary adapts to the
person it reads, and the safeguards that once surrounded a human third party — a notary who read the
instrument in the parties' presence, a second interpreter, a plea in law for the signer who was
misled — have no settled counterpart. From those differences the final section derives what the
earlier failures ask of the people who design the intermediary.
