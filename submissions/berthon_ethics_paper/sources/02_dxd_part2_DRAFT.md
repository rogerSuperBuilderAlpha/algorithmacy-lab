# Decision Experience Design Ethics

*Terms and conditions apply*

Part 1 argued that deciding through an opaque, adaptive intermediary calls for a competence of its own, algorithmacy, modeled on numeracy — a measurable, domain-specific skill whose effect on judgment and decision tasks, such as susceptibility to framing, is not due to general intelligence (Peters et al., 2006) — rather than on the rewiring of consciousness that Ong (1982/2002) claimed for writing and Street (1984) dismantled. It closed on a design preference, structural intervention over visual legibility, and on four falsifiable propositions. A competence claim carries an ethical question that Part 1 did not take up. If the quality of a decision made through an intermediary depends on a skill that can be learned, then someone owes someone the conditions for learning it, and the person who owes them is whoever designs the point at which the decision is made. This essay asks what that debt consists of, why the prevailing answer in design ethics (disclose, arrange the options, let the person choose) does not pay it, and what a design that did would contain.

## Where Part 1 Stopped

Part 1 defined algorithmacy as the competence to decide through an intermediary that reads several parties and commits outcomes that bind them: to interpret the intermediary, to specify intent through it, and to keep track of it as it changes. Coordination of this kind is triadic in the sense Part 1 gave the word — a third party reads both human parties and commits an outcome binding on both — and I will mostly say "coordination through an intermediary" from here on. Numeracy earns its standing by predicting judgment and decision performance after general intelligence is controlled (Peters et al., 2006; Reyna & Brainerd, 2023), and the claim for algorithmacy is the same kind of claim. Nothing in that claim says the intermediary restructures the mind.

The design conclusion Part 1 drew from the human-factors record was that legibility is the wrong target. Explanations of a model's internals raise acceptance of its outputs without raising decision quality (Bansal et al., 2021), and a tool that revealed a hidden feed filter raised awareness, felt control, and engagement, though nobody measured decision quality (Eslami et al., 2015). The alternatives came from the design-ethics arm of this project: counter-delegation, which gives the human party an agent of their own inside the coordination; structural refusal, which lets the person reject the mediation without a crippling penalty (Zong & Matias, 2024); and operational predictability, a declared operating envelope with backward-compatible updates in place of post-hoc explanation (Bansal, Nushi, Kamar, Weld, et al., 2019; Bainbridge, 1983). Those three are the principles this essay assumes. What it adds is an account of why a designer is obliged to supply them, and what a fourth element, the conditions for acquiring skill, has to do with the first three.

The question is ethical because the prevailing answer is ethical. Design ethics, in its regulatory and practitioner forms, mostly answers "what does the designer owe the user?" with a version of informed choice: tell the person what the system does, arrange the options without foreclosing any, and let an informed chooser decide, including the decision not to take part. That answer is coherent. It is also conditional on a fact about the person it addresses, which is that the person can leave.

## Mandatory Participation: What Disclosure Assumes

Consent presupposes refusal. A notice no one can act on is a record, not a notice, and the condition under which notice-and-consent could ever have worked is that the reader could decline. Nissenbaum (2011) made the structural version of this argument before generative systems existed: online, "there is considerable agreement that transparency-and-choice has failed" (p. 35), because a notice cannot describe a recursive, indefinitely extensible practice, and "burdening individuals with the full weight of protecting their privacy online through notice-and-choice is unlikely to yield success" (p. 43). Her argument does not depend on exit: disclosure fails even where refusal is free, because reading it does not confer the capacity it presumes. Edwards and Veale (2017) reach the same point from the law of explanation rights: "individuals are mostly too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise to meaningfully make use of these individual rights" (p. 67), and consent online has become "a mere husk of its former self" (p. 66). Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2020) measured the husk: of 543 people joining a fictitious social network, 74% skipped the privacy policy and 97% agreed to it.

So there are two arguments against disclosure, and the pitch this essay grows from ran them together. The first holds everywhere: disclosure distributes information, not capacity. The second holds only where participation is mandatory: consent presupposes that refusal is possible, and refusal is not always possible. The second argument is the one that changes the designer's obligation, and it needs Hirschman's vocabulary.

Hirschman (1970) distinguished two ways a member of a declining organization can respond. Exit is leaving the relation; voice is acting on it from inside. Where exit is open, members use it, and its easy availability starves voice of the members who would have exercised it. Where exit is closed, everything falls on voice: "The voice option is the only way in which dissatisfied customers or members can react whenever the exit option is unavailable" (p. 33), and with "exit wholly unavailable, voice must carry the entire burden of alerting management to its failings" (p. 34). Voice has preconditions of its own. It presupposes that the members who stay are "endowed with some capacity to articulate this discontent" (p. 34), and it is "costly and conditioned on the influence and bargaining power customers and members can bring to bear" (p. 40). Those two preconditions — a capacity to articulate and some power to make articulation count — are where an obligation to build competence attaches. If a person cannot leave, the only remaining channel runs through what they can say and whether anything answers. One caution travels with the frame: Hirschman's firm wanted to recover, so voice could reach it, and an intermediary pursuing an objective its users did not set has no such interest. Hirschman (1993) later showed that exit and voice can also reinforce each other, so the frame is a vocabulary, not a law.

Whether exit is in fact closed is an empirical question, and the pitch overstated the answer. "There is no exit" is false as a sentence about credit, health care, hiring, and platform work. About 26 million U.S. adults, 11% of the adult population, had no credit record in 2010 and 19 million more could not be scored, at the documented cost of much reduced access to credit (Brevoort et al., 2015); half the respondents in a randomized vignette study chose a human specialist over an AI clinic (Robertson et al., 2023); ride-hail drivers who cannot leave build off-platform businesses that use the app to find repeat customers, a response Maffie (2023) calls independence and distinguishes from both exit and voice. The defensible claim is that exit is unavailable or ruinously priced. A driver has about fifteen seconds to accept a ride without seeing its destination or fare and risks deactivation for cancelling (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016, p. 3762), and drivers were typically not permitted to opt out of pooled rides (Calo & Rosenblat, 2017, p. 1663). When employers share a screening vendor, leaving one employer does not leave the screen: 4% of applicants to ten positions were rejected by all ten, and applicants "would need to apply widely in order to ensure their applications are considered by a human" (Bommasani et al., 2026). In the clinical case the patient was never asked; the algorithm Obermeyer et al. (2019) dissected, one of the largest of a class of tools that by industry estimates are applied to roughly 200 million people a year, sorted patients by predicted cost. For emergency triage and for search I found no study that prices non-participation.

The public-sector literature already names the condition. Bagger et al. (2023) describe Danish schools and hospitals in which "opting out of digital and datafying systems is generally not perceived as an option" and conclude that "the datafied welfare state is not just cultivating digital resignation but also mandating digital participation" (p. 3). I will use the plainest term, mandatory participation, for the fact, and "binding" for the structural property behind it, that the intermediary commits an outcome for both parties at once. The pitch's "coopted" is dropped, because mandatory participation and experienced coercion are not the same thing: remote gig workers in Wood et al.'s (2019) survey reported high autonomy alongside low pay and exhaustion, and Cameron (2024) shows ride-hail drivers producing their own consent through "constant and confined choices." A person can be bound and feel free, and the design question is what the binding permits them to learn and to say.

Voice channels exist on these platforms, and they are weak. The crowdwork platforms Gegenhuber et al. (2021) studied gave workers "microphones, not megaphones": a say on task-level problems, no co-determination of platform decisions. Drivers who wrote to support received template replies (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016, p. 3771). The one experiment I read on designed appeals found that no appeal design, human- or machine-reviewed, "significantly improve[d] user perceptions of fairness compared to a no appeal baseline condition" (Vaccaro et al., 2020, §5.1). A channel that is open but unanswerable does not restore the condition Hirschman named; it mimics it. That is the limit of disclosure under mandatory participation: it informs a person about a system they must use regardless, and consent collapses into ritual. If the person cannot leave, the next thing to check is the ethics of the options they are given.

## Choice Architecture as an Ethics, and Its Limit

Choice architecture has an ethics, and it is more careful than the pitch allowed. Thaler and Sunstein's (2008) libertarian paternalism defends the arrangement of options on two conditions. The first is avoidability: a nudge must be "easy and cheap" to avoid (2008, p. 6, as quoted by Schmidt & Engelen, 2020, p. 5; Hansen, 2016, p. 156, locates the same test in a footnote on p. 8, and I did not open the book to settle it). The second is an identifiable architect who arranges the options to serve the chooser's own ends and can be held to account, which Schmidt and Engelen (2020) say the tradition secures with transparency and democratic control. Hansen (2016) adds a distinction the pitch missed: a nudge works through "cognitive boundaries, biases, routines and habits" and independently of information provision (p. 158), so a disclosure is not a nudge, and the critiques of the previous section target disclosure. Nor is choice architecture "passive and static," as the pitch had it. Yeung's (2017) hypernudge is a choice architecture reconfigured continuously and per person from the target's own behavior, and Susser, Roessler, and Nissenbaum (2019) describe platforms as "dynamic, interactive, intrusive, and adaptive choice architectures" (p. 7). The best-known critique of choice architecture in the data-driven setting is about its dynamism.

The two conditions locate the failure. Avoidability fails first, and the nudge literature supplies its own instruments for saying when. Lemken et al. (2024), reviewing 251 food-choice interventions, make the effort to opt out "the first criterion" for judging intrusiveness and describe "nudge stacking," where several nudges at once "can sum up to a 'shove'" (p. 10). Mandatory participation is the limiting case: the opt-out that costs a credit history, a livelihood, or a place in the queue. The second condition fails in the way Part 1 described. Mills and Sætra's (2024) autonomous choice architect is an optimizer selecting interface configurations against a platform objective, and Schmidt and Engelen (2020) concede in their own research agenda that machine learning could even lead to "black boxes in choice architecture where we do not even understand afterwards why an algorithm presented choices" (p. 10). An architect who cannot say why an option appeared cannot be held to account for it. The levers also run in reverse, as the dark-pattern taxonomy (Gray et al., 2018) and the audit of shopping sites (Mathur et al., 2019) show, and a chooser who cannot leave is a chooser whose options can be arranged with no check from the market in exits. The effect sizes, finally, are too small and heterogeneous to carry an ethics on their own: after correction for publication bias the average nudge effect is indistinguishable from zero (Maier et al., 2022) or much smaller, d = -0.01 to 0.08 (Szaszi et al., 2022).

One thing in the tradition survives intact. Thaler and Sunstein's inevitability argument holds that "intentional nudging can often be avoided, but choice architecture cannot" (Schmidt & Engelen, 2020, p. 4): every interface arranges something. Dorrestijn and Verbeek (2013) press the point from inside the philosophy of technology, arguing that by suggesting nudges a person could entirely avoid, "Thaler and Sunstein in fact fail to appreciate how fundamental choice architecture is to every technical design" (p. 52), and that under technical mediation "total transparency with regard to the influences of technology or the possibility to opt out cannot function as fundamental ethical conditions that should always be assured and respected" (p. 53). Their replacement is to "equip users with the means to develop a creative relationship with the technologies that affect their lives" (p. 53). That is the turn this essay makes. It extends Verbeek's position to intermediaries that rewrite their prescriptions at run time and to people who cannot leave, and it says what "equip" would have to mean in an interface.

The behavioral tradition has a competence-first branch of its own. Boosting aims "to foster people's competence to make their own choices," and its test is that the effect "should persist once (successful) intervention is removed" (Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff, 2017, pp. 973–974). That is the right aim and the right test. But the fullest map of boosts for adaptive digital environments still targets attention, information evaluation, and resistance to manipulation (Kozyreva et al., 2020), which is information literacy, and I found no boosting study aimed at operational competence with an intermediary. Boosting names the aim; nobody has yet pointed it at this skill. The industry's own remedy for algorithmic complexity was neither a boost nor a nudge. It was a chat box.

## The Conversational Interface: Adoption Bought with Verification

A conversational interface lowers the cost of entry and leaves the cost of evaluation where it was, or raises it. Hutchins, Hollan, and Norman (1985) defined the two gulfs a direct-manipulation interface must bridge — the gulf of execution, from a goal to the actions that achieve it, and the gulf of evaluation, from the system's state back to the goal — and said both bridges aim "to minimize cognitive effort" (p. 318). Subramonyam et al. (2024) apply the frame to prompting: the first gulf nearly closes, since anyone can type an intention, while "the gulf of evaluation may increase" because the output must be read, interpreted, and checked against a probabilistic process (pp. 2-3). They add a gulf of envisioning, between an intention and a prompt that correctly anticipates what the system can do, which is the user's side of what Part 1 called the declared operating envelope. Their paper tests none of this, and the hedge "may" is theirs. I keep it.

What the record shows is narrower and more useful. Prompting by non-experts is opportunistic and socially miscalibrated: the ten people in Zamfirescu-Pereira et al.'s (2023) study generalized from single observations, most declared success after one improved reply, and none used the systematic testing tool they were given. Tankelevitch et al. (2024) give the burden its name in cognitive psychology, metacognitive monitoring, and note that the demands "are exacerbated by GenAI's non-determinism and model flexibility" (p. 6). The burden has a measured size, so far only for programming assistants: "verifying suggestion" was the largest single activity at 22.4% of session time among the 21 programmers who labeled their own recorded sessions for Mozannar et al. (2024). "Unpaid" is my gloss; nobody has measured the burden as a cost shifted from provider to user, and that normative claim is this essay's.

Overreliance follows the cost structure. "Overreliance is not an inevitability of cognition but a strategic decision where people are responsive to the costs and benefits" (Vasconcelos et al., 2023, p. 129:3): when checking costs more than accepting, people accept. Spatharioti et al. (2025) randomized shoppers to a search engine or an LLM answer tool; on four routine comparisons accuracy was equal and the LLM users faster and happier, and on a fifth built so the model would err, accuracy fell from 93% with search to 47% with the LLM, and no participant who received an implausible number asked again. Fok and Weld (2024) supply the theory: explanations help "to the extent that they allow a human decision maker to verify the correctness of the AI's prediction," and most decision tasks do not admit a cheap check whatever the explanation. Explanation text makes things worse in a specific way. Bansal et al. (2021) found that "explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness," and He et al. (2025) found conversational explanation raised felt understanding and trust, and in its LLM-agent condition "over-reliance was further reinforced" and objective understanding fell (§6.1). Anderl et al. (2024) isolated the form: with content held constant, people were worse at telling accurate from inaccurate information delivered by a voice assistant in both of two experiments, and by typed chat in one of the two.

Two findings cut the other way, and the essay carries them. Kazemitabaar et al. (2024) built structured, editable alternatives to a chat baseline for data analysis; participants felt significantly more in control, but "there was no difference in task success, completion time, or number of required verification hints" (§6.4), at a sample of eighteen. Fan et al. (2026), in the one study that compares inline, chat, and structured prompting under a single model, report chat ahead on correctness for harder items at no time cost; I read only its abstract. So the claim that chat "maximizes the gulf of evaluation" is not demonstrated, and the cleanest structured-versus-chat test to date is null on every objective measure. What the evidence licenses is a hypothesis stated as one, plus a narrower fact: conversational delivery lowered discrimination of accurate from inaccurate content, fluent explanation lowered checking without raising accuracy, and the verification that remained took a measurable share of the work.

The alternative to text is not an explanation of the model. It is a display of what the model will and will not do, and the argument for displaying it visually is old: a single separable visual feature can be found without serial attention while a conjunction cannot (Treisman & Gelade, 1980), which gives a display that encodes one property of the system's state in one pre-attentive feature (Healey & Enns, 2012) a reason to be read faster than a paragraph. Shneiderman (1983) built direct manipulation on continuous representation, reversible actions whose effect is immediately visible, and a layered path to expertise, and listed among its benefits that users "can predict system responses" (p. 65). Predictability, not transparency, was the design goal in the founding text, and the same text warns that "using spatial or graphic representations of the problem does not necessarily improve performance" (p. 64). The uncertainty-visualization literature has since catalogued how a graphic misleads: viewers treat data inside a drawn boundary as more alike than data across it, and the error of reading an uncertain forecast as a certain one "was not observed with textual representations of the same data" (Padilla et al., 2018); instructions reduced the pull of a drawn hurricane track on damage estimates "but importantly, did not eliminate it" (Padilla et al., 2020). Text can carry an envelope too: a first-person hedge in an answer lowered agreement and raised accuracy (Kim et al., 2024). So the ethical claim does not turn on pixels against words. It turns on what is displayed.

The strongest evidence for displaying the envelope is a study of plain sentences. Cabrera et al. (2023) showed participants "behavior descriptions," statements of how an AI performs on subgroups of cases, and distinguished them from explanation: "Explainable AI attempts to describe why an AI system produced a certain output, while behavior descriptions describe what patterns of output a model tends to produce" (p. 2). The descriptions raised team accuracy in two of three tasks, by letting people override the system where it failed and rely on it where it did not, and in those two tasks accuracy rose across rounds as well, the only learning slope tied to a display in anything I read. Spatharioti et al.'s (2025) second experiment is the visual counterpart: color-highlighting the low-confidence numbers in an LLM answer raised accuracy on the hard task from 26% to 53–58%, mostly because flagged users asked again. Both are declared envelopes in Part 1's sense. Neither explains the model.

The ethical point can now be stated without the pejoratives of the pitch. An interface that bought adoption by placing fluent text between the user and the system's operating envelope transferred the cost of coordination to the user as verification labor; the labor is real, people skip it when it costs more than acceptance, and the fluent form lowers their ability to tell when it is needed. What it gave back as predictability was nothing the user could see; what it gave back as skill depends, two sections on, on the design rather than the medium. The remedy is not to make the user a better reader of the model but to show the user the envelope and give them a cheap way to test it. Readers have met an interface that made them sound everything out before.

## The Scribes' Remedy: An Analogy, Stated Carefully

The history of the written page supplies an analogy for what design can do, and nothing more. The chronology, as the palaeographers give it, runs as follows. Early Greek and Latin inscriptions and Latin books separated words with interpuncts, points placed at mid-height in the line, without extra space (Saenger, 1997, pp. 9, 26). The Romans then gave up the interpunct for the Greek convention of continuous script; Saenger dates the end of interpunct-separated Latin to before the end of the second century CE and the adoption of scriptura continua to the late second or third (pp. 29, 69). Aids existed throughout: grammarians marked texts for reading aloud, scribes inserted marks to link or separate words and signs for the length of pauses, and pointed codices survive from the fifth century (Parkes, 1992, pp. 12–13); pagination or foliation in Greek codices written in scriptura continua is documented as early as the third century (Saenger, 1997, p. 77). Irish scribes of the seventh century separated words by space, Irish and Anglo-Saxon missionaries carried the practice to new foundations, and Carolingian scribes extended it (Saenger, 1997, p. 34; Parkes, 1992, ch. 3); the convention spread to the Continent over the following centuries. Parkes's own name for what the scribes built is the "grammar of legibility," a complex of conventions — "word separation, features of layout and punctuation" — that "were developed to make it easier for readers to extract the information conveyed in the written medium" (p. 1), and the motive he gives is that where Latin was a foreign language, readers needed conventions that made it easier to read. The pitch's "visual syntax" is a coinage, and Parkes's term replaces it.

Two things the pitch asserted about this history are false, and the analogy has to be built without them. Word separation did not invent silent reading. Knox (1968) showed from Euripides, Aristophanes, Cicero, Suetonius, and Augustine himself that silent reading of letters, oracles, and short documents was taken for granted in classical Athens and ordinary in Rome, and concluded that "ancient books were normally read aloud, but there is nothing to show that silent reading of books was anything extraordinary except the famous passage from Augustine's *Confessions*, and that is countered by the phrase of Cicero" (p. 435). Gavrilov (1997) and Burnyeat (1997) closed the question for classicists, and Johnson (2000) states the result: "there was no cognitive difficulty when fully literate ancient readers wished to read silently to themselves" (p. 593). Saenger concedes the narrow point himself: Ambrose read silently, only "he could not have read rapidly in the manner of the modern silent reader" (1997, p. 8). The second false claim is cognitive. Unspaced text is read about 40–70% more slowly than spaced text (Mirault et al., 2019, p. 22, citing Rayner et al., 1998, whose abstract reports a drop in reading rate of approximately 50%), and Mirault et al. (2019, p. 22) remark anecdotally on "the ease with which skilled readers can read text presented without spacing between words". Spaces lowered a cost. They did not create a capacity, and they did not restructure the reader.

What the scribes did was a design finding, and the inference it licenses is exactly one. They did not tell readers to listen harder. They changed the page, and they changed it for a particular population, learners for whom Latin was foreign, at a time when the older convention served a readership that did not want widening: "the notion that the greater portion of the population should be autonomous and self-motivated readers was entirely foreign to the elitist literate mentality of the ancient world" (Saenger, 1997, p. 11). Havelock (1963) made the same point about Greece, that the binding variable was "the supply of readers" (p. 39) and not the script. The inference for decision experience design is that an interface can make an operational skill ordinary by giving the system's structure a visible form, and that whether it does depends on who is taught and why (Street, 1984), not on the artefact alone. That is the whole of the analogy. It says nothing about consciousness; the history of word separation, read by the classicists, does not support Ong's claim even for reading. The pitch's "sounding out" survives only here, as the thing a reader of continuous script did with a text and a user of a fluent answer does with an output: serial decoding where a structured display would have made the check cheap. The analogy also bounds what a complaint about deskilling can mean, since the scribes' new readers lost nothing a later reader would miss and gained a competence whose absence had kept them out.

## The Deskilling Debate, Re-posed

Deskilling, in Braverman's (1974) sense, is the removal of skill from work by the design of its tools and tasks, in the service of managerial control; Vallor (2015) extends the term to moral skill, where a technology removes the occasions on which judgment is practiced. The labor-process debate that followed Braverman settled little as fact and one thing as method. Spenner (1983) showed that the answer depends on the research design, Attewell (1987) catalogued the strong thesis's methodological weaknesses, and what survives is that technology as such does not deskill. Adler and Borys (1996) argue that the same formalization enables or coerces depending on whether it helps employees "better to master their tasks" or serves control, and Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) showed that computerization displaces tasks that follow explicit rules and complements non-routine judgment. The unit is the task and its organization, and that is the ground on which this essay treats deskilling as a property of design.

The pitch called the deskilling worry "a nostalgic distraction," and as stated the evidence contradicts it. The losses researchers have measured are not losses of essay-writing. They are losses of the supervisory and verification skills that a person still accountable for the outcome is later asked to exercise, which are the skills algorithmacy consists of. Bainbridge (1983) made the structural case: an operator who has been monitoring an automated process "may now be an inexperienced one" (p. 775), cognitive skill "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness" (p. 775), and a monitor asked to check in real time a computer applying more criteria than a human can follow has "an impossible task" (p. 776). The cases follow her. Airline pilots in a simulator kept most of their manual control skills but showed "more frequent and significant problems" on the cognitive tasks of manual flight (Casner et al., 2014); at four Polish endoscopy centers the adenoma detection rate of unaided colonoscopy fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after AI detection tools arrived, an observational result whose authors say exposure "might reduce" unaided performance (Budzyń et al., 2025); in an accounting firm, Rinta-Kahila et al. (2023) model reliance breeding complacency and eroding "activity awareness, competence maintenance, and output assessment," unnoticed by workers and managers alike. Rinta-Kahila et al. also draw the distinction the pitch needed: they accept the loss of obsolete skills and name as the problem the loss of expertise "if workers remain accountable for tasks for which they lack sufficient understanding." A skill that has become obsolete can go. A skill required to check or steer a system one must use cannot, and the measured losses are of the second kind.

The archetype is Socrates on writing, and it is instructive because the feared loss was real and the new competence was worth having. In the *Phaedrus*, Thamus tells Theuth that writing "will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory," that it is "an elixir not of memory, but of reminding," and that it offers "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom" (275a, Fowler trans.); Socrates adds that a written text, questioned, will "always say only one and the same thing" (275d). Each complaint has a modern counterpart. Cheap offloading lowered later memory in Grinschgl et al.'s (2021) experiments, while a goal to learn "could almost completely counteract" the harm; the appearance of wisdom is the illusion of competence that Fernandes et al. (2026) measured in people who overestimated their AI-assisted performance and that Bastani et al. (2025) found in students who "do not perceive any reduction in their learning." The third complaint inverts: the text that could not answer has become an interface that answers everything, fluently. Nothing in the dialogue says that writing changed the structure of consciousness, and nothing in this essay does either.

The recent evidence on generative systems is mixed, and the mix is the finding. Performance studies inside and outside a model's capability frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026) say nothing about retained skill; the studies that measure unaided skill afterward point both ways. Bastani et al. (2025), in a preregistered field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school students, found that unguarded ChatGPT-style access raised practice scores by 48% and lowered later unaided exam scores by 17%, that students used the tool "as a 'crutch' by asking for and copying solutions," and that a hint-giving version removed the harm without adding a gain, "though we still do not observe a positive effect." Shen and Tamkin (2026) report a 17% lower skill score among 52 developers learning a library with a chat assistant, with learning preserved under the interaction patterns that kept the person engaged (a preprint whose authors are at Anthropic, which the author of this essay should weigh). Against these, novice programmers in a structured curriculum showed no loss on manual tasks (Kazemitabaar et al., 2023), two preregistered lab experiments found no overall effect, while exploratory analyses and a field study found the sign depending on whether students substituted or complemented their own work (Lehmann et al., 2025, preprint), and Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's (2025) customer-support agents kept part of their productivity gain during outages when the recommender was unavailable. Confidence in the tool predicts self-reported disengagement from critical thinking (Lee et al., 2025), and Risko and Gilbert (2016) supply the construct, cognitive offloading, under which all of this sits. I do not rely on Gerlich (2025), which is correlational, or on Kosmyna et al. (2025), an unrefereed preprint with an equally unrefereed critique posted and eighteen participants in its key session.

Two conclusions survive the mix. First, the effect on unaided skill depends on what the interaction asks of the person: delegating the answer harms, hints and conceptual questions do not, and people cannot see the difference from the inside. Second, and against the pitch, the harm is not pinned to chat as a form: Bastani's harmful and harmless arms were both chat interfaces, and Brynjolfsson's durable gain came from a system that pushed suggested replies into the agent's workflow, though the authors call it a conversational assistant. So "deskilling" and "upskilling" are properties of the design of the interaction, and the criteria that sort designs are already in hand. Vallor's test asks whether a tool "obviated the need for us to employ our own skillful moral judgment, rather than aiding us in its successful exercise" (2015, p. 113); Kahneman and Klein (2009) require, for intuitive expertise to form, a predictable environment and the opportunity to learn its regularities. An interface that returns a fluent answer, varies without notice, and offers no cheap test fails both. Automation research adds measurable versions: harm to failure performance rises most when automation crosses from supporting information analysis to selecting the action (Onnasch et al., 2014), and exposure to failures during training reduces complacency "even though it might not avoid it completely" (Bahner et al., 2008). The ethical question, then, is not whether people keep the literacy they had but whether the design affords the competence mandatory participation now requires. If deskilling is a design property, upskilling is a design problem, and that problem has a literature.

## Learning Design Under Mandatory Participation

The learning sciences know how people acquire operational skill with complex systems, and the knowledge transfers on conditions. Three results hold up. Early restriction speeds learning and transfers: Carroll and Carrithers's (1984) training-wheels word processor, which made the common error states unreachable, produced faster learning and better comprehension, and its learners were later better at the full system's advanced functions than learners trained on the full system (Catrambone & Carroll, 1987). Computer-based scaffolds help: Belland et al.'s (2017) meta-analysis of 144 experiments gives g = 0.46. Support that tracks the learner's growing competence beats support that stays fixed: Nückles et al. (2010) found permanent prompts lost their advantage by term's end and gradual, adaptive fading beat them, and Leutner (2000) found slow fading of guidance best in thirty hours of CAD training. Reiser (2004) adds a distinction the earlier sections needed, between scaffolds that structure a task and scaffolds that problematize it, adding difficulty because the difficulty teaches. The learning sciences already separate friction that teaches from friction that only taxes, and a verification burden is the second kind.

The evidence for fading is weaker than the pitch's "progressive elevation" assumed, and I state it at its measured strength. Belland et al. (2017) found that whether scaffolds changed did not moderate the effect; Tawfik et al. (2018) found sustained prompts better than faded ones on one measure; Leutner's second experiment found the approach less effective when the interface already showed its functions. Van de Pol, Volman, and Beishuizen (2010) define scaffolding by contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility, which makes a uniform on-screen display a standing support rather than a scaffold. So fading is supported where it is tied to demonstrated competence in a domain with stable rules, and not as a schedule. (Wood, Bruner, and Ross, 1976, originated the term; I could not read them and do not attribute fading to that paper.) The risk on the other side is the expertise reversal effect, assistance that helps novices and hurts experts: a meta-analysis of 60 studies gives d = 0.505 for high assistance among low-prior-knowledge learners and d = −0.428 for high assistance among high-prior-knowledge learners, who learn better from low assistance (Tetzlaff et al., 2025). The effect is asymmetric. Giving novices assistance does more good than withholding it from experts does harm, which makes a permanent, dismissible envelope display cheap insurance and makes adaptive removal a design to test rather than a default.

Two models the outline hoped to borrow transfer badly. Productive failure, problem-solving before instruction, has a pooled effect of g = 0.36 (Sinha & Kapur, 2021) and fosters learning only when specific design features, contrasting cases or instruction built on student solutions, are implemented (Loibl et al., 2017), and the meta-analysis favors instruction-first for domain-general skills. A person who meets an intermediary has no consolidation phase unless the design supplies one. Legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991) presupposes an old-timer who holds the competence and a community in which the newcomer moves toward it; Beane (2019) shows what happens when technology removes the approved route: only a minority of robotic-surgery trainees reached competence, through unapproved "shadow learning." Rahman (2021) is the platform counterpart: when an opaque score replaced a visible rating, workers could not "decipher or learn from" the criteria for success, and some experimented with tactics while others constrained their activity on the platform. Participation does not teach by itself, and under mandatory participation nobody is the old-timer.

The transfer conditions are the ones Kahneman and Klein (2009) named, and the intermediary violates them by default. Evaluating an intuitive judgment "requires an assessment of the predictability of the environment in which the judgment is made and of the individual's opportunity to learn the regularities of that environment." The mental-models literature says what has to be predictable. A device model helped learners only when it "support[ed] direct and simple inference of the exact steps required to operate the device" (Kieras & Bovair, 1984); understanding the underlying technology was "insufficient" for an accurate model of an AI teammate without analysis of its behavior (Gero et al., 2020); and people learn an error boundary when it is parsimonious and non-stochastic, systems of identical accuracy produce different team performance, and an update that raises accuracy can lower team performance by breaking what the person had learned (Bansal, Nushi, Kamar, Lasecki, et al., 2019; Bansal, Nushi, Kamar, Weld, et al., 2019). Kulesza et al. (2012) complicate a blunt "no explanation" rule, since explaining a recommender's reasoning helped users steer it; the reconciliation is that an explanation helps when it lets a learner infer how to act. And the environment moves: Speekenbrink and Shanks (2010) call learning under unsignalled change under-researched, though their participants tracked both abrupt and gradual change in a stylized cue-learning task, and no scaffolding or fading study I found uses a rule set that moves, so the requirement that updates be backward-compatible rests on the human-AI team evidence and on Bainbridge, not on learning-sciences evidence about fading.

The obligation can now be written as a list, each item tied to its evidence. First, display the declared operating envelope — the system's behavior on the cases it handles and the cases it fails — as a behavior description rather than an explanation (Cabrera et al., 2023; Bansal, Nushi, Kamar, Lasecki, et al., 2019). Second, give a low-cost direct channel for intent: letting people modify an algorithm's output by a few percent substantially reduced their aversion to it, the existence of the channel mattering more than its width (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2018), and the contestability literature's "sandboxing" lets users change inputs and see what changes (Alfrink et al., 2023). Third, make tests cheap: failure exposure in training reduced complacency (Bahner et al., 2008), and a randomized study of 1,368 students found prompting and source-checking skills teachable through modules with embedded practice and feedback (Connell Pensky et al., 2026). Fourth, let the support track competence: permanent and dismissible by default, with removal tied to demonstrated skill rather than a calendar (Nückles et al., 2010; Tetzlaff et al., 2025). Fifth, keep updates backward-compatible, so that what the person learned yesterday is still true today (Bansal, Nushi, Kamar, Weld, et al., 2019), and act at a pace and by criteria the person can follow "even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (Bainbridge, 1983, p. 777).

Skill alone does not discharge the obligation, and the human-factors literature says why. Automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), and Bahner et al.'s training reduced complacency without removing it. The competence interventions that exist help unevenly: Chiang and Yin's (2022) tutorials reduced overreliance only among people who already performed well, and Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos's (2021) cognitive forcing functions worked best for people high in need for cognition and were the designs people liked least. Training wheels are a restriction, and under mandatory participation the party that decides what is locked is the intermediary's owner. This is why the five items above are paired with, and not substitutes for, counter-delegation — an agent of the person's own inside the coordination, adapted to a single relation from the collective, relational data-governance proposals of Viljoen (2021) and Delacroix and Lawrence (2019) — and structural refusal, a way out that does not cost what the opt-outs catalogued earlier cost (Zong & Matias, 2024). A scaffold without standing teaches a person to work a system that still binds them unilaterally. The reason to call the whole arrangement an obligation rather than a product feature is that what it protects is meaning.

## An Ethics of Meaning for Decision Experience Design

Martela and Steger (2016) distinguish three things people mean by meaning in life: "Coherence means a sense of comprehensibility and one's life making sense. Purpose means a sense of core goals, aims, and direction in life. Significance is about a sense of life's inherent value and having a life worth living" (p. 531). The first is predictability-shaped in their own account: coherence "centers on the perception that stimuli are predictable and conform to recognizable patterns" (p. 534). Mekler and Hornbæk (2019), who carried the construct into HCI, make the restriction explicit — coherence is "about understanding 'what' one is doing and experiencing rather than 'why'" (p. 5) — and list designs that "help foster predictability" among their open questions (p. 10). That is a mapping, not a finding of either paper, and the one experiment behind "meaning as coherence," in which patterned stimuli raised reported meaning (Heintzelman, Trent, & King, 2013), failed a preregistered direct replication (Ratner, Burrow, & Thoemmes, 2016). The coherence leg is a conceptual argument: a system a person must use, whose behavior they cannot anticipate, attacks comprehensibility at the point where comprehensibility is defined, and disclosure does not repair it, because disclosure answers "why" and coherence asks "what."

Purpose and significance map onto the other two obligations with a similar caveat. A channel for intent, and the skill to use it, is what purpose requires of an interface: Lukoff et al. (2021) found that specific intentions call for more control, and Peters, Calvo, and Ryan (2018), from self-determination theory, say that devices which "do not in turn demand actions from users without their assent" support autonomy (p. 5). Significance, that one's participation counts, is met by standing. Here the empirical literature points somewhere the tripartite did not: Costin and Vignoles (2020) found across three studies that a sense of mattering predicted later judgments of meaning while coherence and purpose did not, and Martela and Steger (2023) themselves find significance and mattering overlapping, possibly sub-facets of one dimension. If experienced meaning tracks mattering, the weight of the obligation falls on whether the person's participation registers — whether they can decline, steer, and be answered — rather than on predictability alone. The tripartite also has no competence leg; the home for algorithmacy as an acquired skill is the competence need of self-determination theory, "feeling able and effective" (Peters et al., 2018, p. 2).

Vallor supplies the reason the affordance is a duty rather than a feature. Moral skills, in her account, are "acquired in specific practices which, under the right conditions and with sufficient opportunity for repetition, foster the cultivation of practical wisdom and moral habituation" (2015, p. 109), and a technology deskills when it does the judging for us rather than "with us" (pp. 118–119). The 2016 book names the design ideal that removes the practice, "frictionless interactions" (Vallor, 2016, p. 161): "a frictionless world where every social bond and duty was conditional upon the ongoing ability of others to keep us stimulated and pleased would diminish us all. In particular, it would render meaningless technomoral virtues such as care" (p. 164). Her criterion is on practices and their norms, not on a system's declared limits, so a duty to display the envelope extends her argument rather than reading it. The extension is short: a design that is frictionless, unpredictable, and unsteerable removes the occasions on which the judgment she describes could be practiced, for a person who cannot go elsewhere to practice.

Why the designer, and not the user, carries the duty follows from what a designed object is. Akrich (1992) called the end product of design work a "script" or "scenario" (p. 208): the designer's hypothesis about the roles its users will play, realized or defeated only when actual users take up the roles or invent others. Latour (1992) elaborated the vocabulary and drew the moral: "Prescription is the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms" (reprint p. 157). Verbeek took the concept into design ethics, where a speed bump has the script "Slow down when you approach me" (2011, p. 10), and Tromp, Hekkert, and Verbeek (2011) give the lineage in a sentence: "The concept of script had been introduced by Akrich to describe the 'implicit manuals' that products embody, and Latour elaborated on this concept" (p. 4). The pitch's "Verbeek's invisible scripts" is therefore corrected to Akrich's and Latour's scripts, taken up by Verbeek; I found no Verbeek text that uses the phrase. The designer materializes a morality whether or not one was intended, so the only question is which script gets written, and Latour's own example is already a declared envelope: the photocopier whose messages escalate from "ADD PAPER" to "DO NOT OPEN — DANGER, HIGH VOLTAGE," each addressed to a narrower audience of permitted actors (reprint pp. 161–162). A displayed envelope is a prescription about who may act, which is why it needs standing beside it.

The obligation can be stated. Where participation is mandatory, a design that offers disclosure without predictability, a channel for intent, and standing fails the person it mediates even if every disclosure is accurate, because what it withholds is not information but the conditions under which a person could acquire the competence to decide through it, make their intent count, and refuse it. The obligation is not paternalist: it asks the designer to supply predictability, a channel, and an exit, and to teach a skill the person may decline to use; it does not choose for them. These are claims that can be wrong, and the last section says how.

## Propositions, Agenda, and Limitations

The propositions continue the numbering of Part 1, whose four concerned incremental validity, the triadic penalty, bounding over explaining, and channel existence over bandwidth. Each is stated so that a named design could refute it.

**Proposition 5 (assisted performance against retained skill).** Holding the model and the task constant, an interface that returns free-form conversational answers will produce higher assisted performance and lower unassisted performance afterward than an interface that displays the system's declared operating envelope and requires an independent attempt before assistance. Adoption, the choice to use the tool when both are offered, is recorded separately; if unassisted skill and adoption rise together under the conversational design, the frictionless-adoption critique fails. Bastani et al.'s (2025) three-arm design is the template, with a learning-gain measure in the control, since their hint-giving arm removed harm without producing gain.

**Proposition 6 (support that tracks competence).** Support removed on demonstrated competence will yield higher retained decision quality than support that is permanent or absent. The null is live: if permanent, dismissible support equals faded support, as Belland et al. (2017) would predict, the cheaper design wins and the fading requirement is dropped.

**Proposition 7 (scaffold without standing).** An envelope display and a feedback loop without a channel for intent and without a refusal path will raise measured algorithmacy without raising willingness to decide through the intermediary or perceived autonomy. This extends Part 1's fourth proposition and the finding of Vaccaro et al. (2020) that an unanswerable appeal does not restore perceived fairness; Dietvorst et al. (2018) predict the direction.

**Proposition 8 (meaning).** Scores on items derived from Martela and Steger's (2016) coherence, purpose, and significance will track, respectively, the predictability of the declared envelope, the existence of a channel for intent, and standing, and none will track the volume of disclosure. The mattering result (Costin & Vignoles, 2020) predicts that the significance item will carry most of the variance in overall meaning, which would move the weight of the obligation toward standing.

**Proposition 9 (failure exposure).** Exposure to an adaptive intermediary's failure modes during use will reduce complacency, measured as how much of the available information the person samples before accepting an output, relative to equal-length exposure without failures. This is Bahner et al.'s (2008) result carried to a two-sided intermediary at larger N.

The limits are these. The history is analogy and bears no explanatory weight; its chronology rests on Saenger and Parkes read at page-snippet depth and on classicist papers read in their opening pages. The learning-sciences transfer is untested on adaptive intermediaries: no scaffolding or fading study I found uses a rule set that moves, and no study compares a conversational and a structured interface on adoption and skill with the same envelope held constant, so Proposition 5 is an inference. The verification burden has a measured size for programming assistants only. Several deskilling results are preprints or self-report, one preprint's authors are at the company whose model drafted this text, and the one clinical result is observational. The ethical claims are normative; the propositions test their empirical presuppositions, not the norms. And the exit argument is documented for credit and platform dispatch, partly for hiring, and not at all for emergency triage or search.

The pitch closed with a question for a cohort of designers, and it can be asked in the terms this essay has used. In the tools you use every day, where does the work of deciding turn into the work of verifying — reading and checking generated output before you can act, with no way to see in advance where the system is reliable and where it is not? And what would that tool look like if it displayed its operating envelope, let you register what you wanted through a direct channel, told you when it changed, and let you refuse it without penalty?
