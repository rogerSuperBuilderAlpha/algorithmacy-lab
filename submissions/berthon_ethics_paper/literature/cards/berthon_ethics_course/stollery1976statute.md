## Summary
A study paper for the Alberta Institute of Law Research and Reform, written by a 1976 law graduate, on the writing requirements of the Statute of Frauds 1677 and Lord Tenterden's Act as they stood in Alberta. It opens with why the statute was passed: in 1677 parties and interested persons could not testify, so a merchant whose signature was forged "had to sit by, silent and unheard"; jury verdicts were practically unappealable; and perjury was thought rife after the Civil War and Restoration (pp. 222-223). It notes that statutes of 1844-1854 let parties testify, "removing this rationale" (p. 222). After analysing the requirement of writing and the devices courts used to avoid it, it concludes under the heading "The Act Causes Injustice", quoting the English Law Revision Committee of 1937, which recorded Lord Campbell's view that the Act "promotes more frauds than it prevents" and Fitzjames Stephen's that it enables a man "to break a promise with impunity, because he did not write it down with sufficient formality" (p. 254).

## Key facts it relies on
- P. 222: "In 1677, parties to an action, their husbands or wives, and persons with an interest in the result of the action could not be witnesses."
- P. 222 (quoting Lord Bowen): "the merchant whose name was forged to a bill of exchange had to sit by, silent and unheard, while his acquaintances were called to offer conjectures and beliefs as to the authenticity of the disputed signature".
- P. 222: "A series of statutes between 1844 and 1854 permitted litigants to give evidence on oath, removing this rationale for the provisions of the Statute of Frauds."
- P. 223: "Subordination and perjury evidently were rife" (a block quotation; n. 6 attributes it to Drachsler, "The British Statute of Frauds", (1958-1960) 3-4 Am. Bar Assoc. Section of International and Cooperative Law Bulletin, 24 (as the PDF text prints it); "Subordination" is as printed, presumably for "subornation").
- P. 254 (quoting the Law Revision Committee, Sixth Interim Report, Cmd. 5449, 1937, at 7, per n. 330): "'promotes more frauds than it prevents.' True, it shuts out perjury; but it also and more frequently shuts out the truth. It strikes impartially at the perjurer and the honest man who has omitted a precaution, sealing the lips of both."
- P. 254 (same source): "A and B contract: A has signed a sufficient note or memorandum, but B has not. In these circumstances, B can enforce the contract against A, but A cannot enforce it against B."

## Critical notes from the literature
- A student study paper, not a historical monograph; its history rests on Holdsworth, Bowen, Thayer and Franklin as quoted. Use it for the 1937 Committee's words until the Committee's report is read directly (facet V2, leads).
- It does not identify any social class as bearing the statute's cost; its loser is the party who did not obtain a signature.
