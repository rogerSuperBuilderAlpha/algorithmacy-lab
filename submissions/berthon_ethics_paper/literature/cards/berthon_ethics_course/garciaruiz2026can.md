## Summary
García-Ruiz and Rocchi ask whether work can be meaningful under algorithmic management, which they define
with Baiocco et al. (2022) as "the use of computer-programmed procedures for the coordination of labour
input" (p. 5). Using MacIntyre's goods-virtues-practice-institution scheme, they hold that work is
meaningful when it has practice-like features (abstract, p. 3). Algorithmic management, they argue, does
three things: it matches consumers and providers, monitors workers, and rates them (pp. 5-6). It brings
information asymmetry, automated communication, sanctions "without prior notice", cryptic reasons and
appeals that "are hardly accepted" (p. 6), and it threatens meaningful work through deskilling, task
fragmentation, surveillance and dependency (p. 23). The authors set out seven normative conditions that
organisations should meet (p. 24, summarising Table 3). Where organisations will not meet them, workers
resist: through unions and guilds, such as the FairTube initiative, which asked YouTube for "an appeals
process overseen by an independent board" (p. 21, n. 4); through cooperatives; and through individual
strategies. Cameron's (2022) ride-hail drivers play a "relational game" and an "efficiency game" to make
the work meaningful (pp. 22-23). The authors conclude that workers can "creatively use the affordances"
of the system even in adverse settings (p. 23).

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (p. 3): organisations "need an appropriate framework to enable meaningful work when adopting algorithmic management systems"; the article explores "strategies of resistance".
- p. 4: "there is no systematic normative study of whether meaningful work is still possible under algorithmic management".
- p. 5: algorithmic management defined as "the use of computer-programmed procedures for the coordination of labour input in an organisation" (quoting Baiocco et al. 2022, 5); Uber drivers "do not know the passenger's destination before the pickup".
- p. 6: "The algorithm has the power to assess, classify, reward, or sanction, to the point of firing, even without prior notice. Platforms are not required to provide reasons for those actions."; "the reasons given are invariably cryptic"; "Appeals are hardly accepted".
- p. 21: besides "demands for legislative regulation", collective initiatives include the Online Merchants Guild and the YouTubers Union.
- p. 21, n. 4: FairTube (2019, affiliated with IG Metall) asked YouTube "to put in place an appeals process overseen by an independent board"; the platform "refrained from establishing changes to its coordination and reward system".
- p. 22: such collective attempts "are proving insufficient" (citing Schor 2021).
- pp. 22-23: drivers in the "efficiency game" "distrust the algorithm", "keep their own financial records", and "feel trapped in a game they cannot win" (quoting Cameron 2022, 245).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet N5 (`literature/facets/N5_prior_work_gap.md:130, 183, 216`) read pp. 3-6 and 21 with n. 4 and used FairTube as evidence that the demand for an independent third party is not new. It proposed the citation for "Contemporaries condemn the practice", as "the drivers' and creators' own voice".
- AUDIT_06 (P5-N08, `literature/audit/AUDIT_06_new.md:59`) agreed with the reading and set the locator to "p. 21 n. 4" (minor).
- From my reading: the paper is not a condemnation. It treats algorithmic management as having "advantages" (p. 4), and some of Cameron's drivers "see the app as a facilitator of their work" (p. 22). Its normative aim is meaningful work, not fair process.
- From my reading: FairTube is a group of YouTubers, not drivers. The drivers in the paper (pp. 22-23) adapt and distrust; they do not, in the pages read, condemn the practice.
