## Summary
Claassen proposes a "double-fiduciary theory" of the corporation (abstract). On a concession theory of markets, states authorise markets, in society's name, to create wealth; firms therefore owe the state/society a fiduciary duty to create wealth, and corporate law can be read as having made shareholders the "proximate beneficiaries" of that duty. Because the pursuit of wealth breeds domination, corporations owe a second fiduciary duty, also to society/state, "not to dominate others in the economy" (abstract; introduction). He distinguishes three forms of corporate power: firm power over employees, market power, and political power over the rules of the game (section on the duty not to dominate). He compares his view with three rivals: duties to shareholders, to "the corporation" or its purpose, and to multiple stakeholders. Against critics who call duties to conflicting parties impossible, he reports that "the political literature on fiduciary relations" allows fiduciaries to serve "multiple beneficiaries, who have competing interests" under a duty of "reasonableness" and "fairness" (Fox-Decent, 2011). He then says the stakeholder construal leaves justification, specification and implementation unresolved. He closes by answering objections that the theory is too timid or too radical, and calls for new accountability mechanisms toward stakeholders or citizens.

## Key facts it relies on
- Thesis: corporations have "a fiduciary duty to the state/society to create wealth" and need "a second fiduciary duty, i.e. not to dominate others in the economy" (abstract).
- Shareholders as "'proximate beneficiaries'", the state as "'ultimate beneficiary'" (abstract; introduction).
- Three rival views: duties to shareholders (Friedman), to "the corporation" or the charter's purpose, and to "multiple stakeholders (multi-fiduciary stakeholder theories)" (section comparing construals).
- The critics' objection: "it is impossible to have fiduciary duties to several (conflicting) parties. Being a fiduciary implies being partial" (Marcoux; Heath) (same section).
- The reply: "However, the political literature on fiduciary relations argues partiality is not at all a necessary feature. Fiduciaries can have fiduciary relations to multiple beneficiaries, who have competing interests. In these cases, they have a duty of 'reasonableness' and 'fairness' in balancing their interests (Fox-Decent, 2011, pp. 34–37)" (same section).
- The stakeholder construal still needs justification, specification of a "'reasonable' and 'fair' distribution", and an answer to the charge that a multi-fiduciary structure "is unworkable in practice for corporate boards" (same section).
- Three forms of corporate power: firm power, market power, political power (section "The duty not to dominate").
- PMC header: "2022 Aug 18;27(3):317–338", "Collection date 2024".
