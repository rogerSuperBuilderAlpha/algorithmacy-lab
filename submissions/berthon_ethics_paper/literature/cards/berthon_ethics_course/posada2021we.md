## Summary

Posada, Weller and Wong argue that datafication, the conversion of the world into stored digital data, is a "constitutive shift" in social and political relations, and that the printing press is the best analogy for it (abstract, §1, pp. 1–2). Both technologies first decentralised power and then "triggered a recentralization of power by new actors better adapted to leveraging the new technology" (p. 1). The press moved power from Church and state to private actors such as printers and publishers (p. 2); their print history draws on Eisenstein, Anderson, Deibert and Ruud's account of Russian printers in 1905 (pp. 4–5). Datafication has likewise moved power to a few firms: "The biggest players in AI are just nine firms" (p. 5). Google, they write, "serves a public purpose in monopoly fashion" while answering to advertisers, and with the social-media and app gatekeepers it decides "what we know, how we know it, and if we know it" (p. 5). The internet's early infrastructure was publicly financed and later privatised; Big Tech grew through acquisitions, scale and platform control, "having created the systems that others must use" (pp. 5–6). They close by calling for new legal and regulatory infrastructure and by asking "who holds the power in a digital world, why" (p. 7).

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract (p. 1): datafication and AI "(and previously, the printing press) both disrupted extant power arrangements, leading to decentralization, and triggered a recentralization of power by new actors better adapted to leveraging the new technology."
- p. 1: "Literacy as a minimal qualification was replaced with access – to devices, to platforms, to the Internet."
- p. 2: the press "shifted power from the Church and state as the keeper of knowledge to private actors … empowering publishers and printers."
- p. 5: "The biggest players in AI are just nine firms based in China and the US."
- p. 5: "even as it serves a public purpose in monopoly fashion, Google responds to market incentives: its advertisers"; with other gatekeepers "they effectively gatekeep what we know, how we know it, and if we know it."
- p. 5: the early ARPANET infrastructure "was a public good, financed by the United States government"; privatisation followed in the 1980s and early 1990s.
- p. 5: concentration came from regulators "not addressing the increasing size and power of these companies", from "a 'winner-take-all' form of competition", and from the platform model; "These dynamics make oligopolies more likely."
- pp. 5–6: "Big Tech sits at the nexus of datafication, having created the systems that others must use"; "the corporations have seized power by controlling the currency of power: data."
- p. 7: policy should "move away from thinking about datafication as just a faster printing press."

## Critical notes from the literature

- Facet N5 (`literature/facets/N5_prior_work_gap.md`, l. 70–71): their print history "do[es] not discuss licensing monopolies, literacy tests, anti-literacy law or records," and they "claim the recentralization the paper declines to establish, so the open row should cite them as a claim the paper does not adopt." The v3 "but see" follows that advice.
- Their print story runs from state to private actors (p. 2), the reverse of the manuscript's Stationers, who held printing by a public grant. They do not address whether present infrastructure is held by grant or licence.
- The concentration claims rest on secondary sources (antitrust filings, platform studies, Abdalla and Abdalla on funding), not on a market study of computing or data. They are argued, not measured, in this paper.
- Facet N5 (l. 175, 180) lists Posada et al. as prior work the paper must cite for its print-to-AI comparison on power grounds, which exists "at the level of the medium, not the positions."

## Where the manuscript uses it

- **Line 149**, "Whether the infrastructure on which present intermediaries run is held in a comparable way is an empirical question about markets in computing and data that this paper has not examined (but see Posada et al., 2021)". Takes a published claim that power over data infrastructure has recentralised in a few firms. Abstract (p. 1); pp. 5–6. **holds** as a "but see": Posada et al. assert concentration and control of "the systems that others must use," which bears on the open question. They do not discuss holding by public grant, the comparison the sentence draws with the Stationers' letters patent, and their evidence is secondary.
