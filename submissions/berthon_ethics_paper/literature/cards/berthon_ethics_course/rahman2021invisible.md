## Summary
From the abstract: earlier research shows people experience third-party evaluations as control because they
align their behaviour with the evaluations' criteria to win resources and recognition, but that research
studied transparent criteria, and algorithmic evaluations increasingly are not transparent. Drawing on "over
three years of interviews, archival data, and observations as a registered user on a labor platform", Rahman
studies how freelancers contend with "an opaque third-party evaluation algorithm". The platform "implemented an
opaque evaluation algorithm to meaningfully differentiate between freelancers' rating scores". Freelancers
"experienced this evaluation as a form of control but could not align their actions with its criteria because
they could not clearly identify those criteria." Their responses diverged: some experimented to raise their
scores, others "constrained their activity on the platform", depending on performance, dependence on the
platform, and whether they had suffered score drops. He calls the result an "invisible cage": "a form of control
in which the criteria for success and changes to those criteria are unpredictable", which shapes gig workers'
access to clients "while undermining their ability to understand and respond to factors that determine their
success."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Ledger P4-052, P4-068–070 grade every Rahman claim "unsupported" at abstract depth and ask for the full article
  (`review/v3/ledger_parts/P4.md:92`).
- **Dissertation, not the article (Rahman 2019, ch. 4.2, printed pp. 42–43 (verifier-corrected from p. 42), `rahman2019.txt:2268-2316`):** the
  platform, pseudonymised "StarWorks", "previously used a transparent five-star rating algorithm"; to address
  rating inflation it "officially moved to a new algorithm ... called the Work Success Score (WSS)"; "When the
  WSS was first introduced, no explanation for the score was given"; and "a summarized, public 5-star rating was
  now completely removed from the main search results page." This supports "replaced a visible rating" for the
  dissertation's account. Whether the 2021 article keeps the same description must be checked in the article.
- The dissertation uses "discipline" when summarising prior studies of platform work: "the platform's
  algorithms leveraged the information they collected to discipline workers' behaviors" (`rahman2019.txt:1878`).
  The abstract's word is "control", not "discipline".
- Facet M2 (`literature/facets/M2_algorithm_studies.md:49-52`) records Cameron and Rahman (2022), whose
  ride-hail workers "can influence and manipulate algorithms in ways that platforms cannot always observe"
  (confirmed against the Crossref abstract, AUDIT_05 M2-27). It complicates any claim that opacity leaves workers
  no leverage.
- The study is one platform. The abstract reports divergent reactions, not uniform submission.
