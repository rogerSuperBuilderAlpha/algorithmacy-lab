## Summary
Selbst and Barocas ask what makes machine-learning decisions hard to explain and separate two properties: "inscrutable" models, whose rules are too many and too interdependent to inspect, and "nonintuitive" models, whose rules may be inspectable but whose basis defies intuition (abstract, pp. 1085-1086). Laws such as FCRA, ECOA and the GDPR, and most explanation techniques, address inscrutability; this "may not help if the goal is to assess whether the basis for decision-making is normatively defensible" (p. 1086). They conclude that "one must seek explanations of the process behind a model's development, not just explanations of the model itself" (p. 1086). On p. 1094, discussing secrecy and expertise before turning to inscrutability, they quote Ananny and Crawford's account of the "chain of logic" behind transparency and argue that the step from observation to accountability needs knowledge the observer must already have. They add that "the problem of expertise—like the problem of secrecy—is not unique to algorithms." This card rests on the abstract and p. 1094 with its notes; the doctrinal Parts II-IV were not read this session (facet M2 read other parts).

## Key facts it relies on

- p. 1086: existing law and techniques address inscrutability; doing so "may not help if the goal is to assess whether the basis for decision-making is normatively defensible."
- p. 1086: "one must seek explanations of the process behind a model's development, not just explanations of the model itself."
- p. 1094: without specialised knowledge of source code, "disclosure may offer little value to affected parties and regulators."
- p. 1094, quoting Ananny and Crawford (n. 48, "at 974"): "Transparency concerns are commonly driven by a certain chain of logic: observation produces insights which create the knowledge required to govern and hold systems accountable."
- p. 1094: "The process of moving from observation to knowledge to accountability cannot be assumed; in many cases, the ability to leverage observations for accountability requires preexisting knowledge that allows observers to appreciate the significance of a disclosure." (n. 49 cites Burrell at 4.)
- p. 1094: "Transparency of systems of decision-making is important, but incomplete."
- p. 1094: "the problem of expertise—like the problem of secrecy—is not unique to algorithms."
