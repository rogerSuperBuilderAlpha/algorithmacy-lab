## Summary
Donald John Marshall Jr., a Mi'kmaq, admitted catching and selling 463 pounds of eels without a licence, with a
prohibited net, in the close season; his only defence was a treaty right under the Treaties of 1760–61
(headnote). The written treaty of 10 March 1760 contained a restrictive trade covenant (trade only at British
truckhouses) and nothing on a right to fish or trade. The majority (Binnie J., for Lamer C.J. and L'Heureux-Dubé,
Cory and Iacobucci JJ.; Gonthier and McLachlin JJ. dissenting) allowed the appeal and entered an acquittal. It
held that extrinsic evidence may show a written document does not contain all the terms (para. 10), that
historical and cultural context may be received without ambiguity (para. 11), and that where a treaty was
concluded orally and written up by the Crown it would be unconscionable to rely on the written terms while
ignoring the oral ones (para. 12). Because "the Indian parties did not ... have the opportunity to create their
own written record", treaty interpretation is flexible (para. 14). The evidence showed "the inadequacy and
incompleteness of the written memorial" (para. 35), and the Nova Scotia courts erred in treating it as the whole of the obligations
(para. 40). The surviving right is to trade for "necessaries", a moderate livelihood, subject to justified
regulation (headnote). McLachlin J., dissenting, accepted the trial judge's finding that the Mi'kmaq understood
the trade clause (para. 89).

## Key facts it relies on
- Facts and holding: acquittal on all charges; Gonthier and McLachlin JJ. dissenting (headnote).
- Para. 12: "where a treaty was concluded verbally and afterwards written up by representatives of the Crown,
  it would be unconscionable for the Crown to ignore the oral terms while relying on the written terms" (citing
  *Guerin*).
- Para. 14, after quoting *Badger* para. 52 on treaties drafted in English and not translated: "The Indian
  parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the opportunity to create their own written record of the
  negotiations."
- Para. 35: the evidence "demonstrates the inadequacy and incompleteness of the written memorial of the treaty
  terms".
- Para. 40: "the Nova Scotia judgments erred in concluding that the only enforceable treaty obligations were
  those set out in the written document of March 10, 1760"; effect must be given to "their common intention in
  1760 – not just the terms of the March 10, 1760 document".
- Para. 41: the written text is incomplete, so terms are found from "the fragmentary historical record" and the
  parties' objectives.
- Headnote: the right is limited to "necessaries" (a moderate livelihood) and is subject to regulation
  justified under *Badger*.
- Dissent, para. 89: the trial judge found "no misunderstanding or lack of agreement" and that the Mi'kmaq
  understood the terms; French missionaries served as interpreters; some Mi'kmaq spoke English.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Ledger P4-053 flags the dissent (McLachlin J., para. 89) as the point a referee may raise: on the trial
  judge's findings the Mi'kmaq understood the written trade clause, which cuts against reading the case as a
  record imposed on parties who could not read it.
- The para. 14 sentence is the Court's general statement about treaty-making, made after quoting *Badger* (on
  treaties with Cree and Dene signatories, para. 14); it is not a finding about the 1760 Mi'kmaq negotiations as such
  (ledger P1-027 notes the manuscript now states it generally).
