## Summary
Wilson's veto message of 29 January 1917 returns H.R. 10384, the immigration bill, to the House without his
signature. It is two paragraphs long, and I read it whole in the American Presidency Project transcription,
which gives House Documents, 64th Congress, 2nd Session as its source. Wilson accepts "most of the provisions"
but rejects the literacy test as "a radical change in the policy of the Nation which is not justified in
principle." The test, he writes, "is not a test of character, of quality, or of personal fitness, but would
operate in most cases merely as a penalty for lack of opportunity in the country from which the alien seeking
admission came." Immigrants often come to gain an education, and "the illiterate immigrant is" not "as such an
undesirable immigrant." He draws a line: "Tests of quality and of purpose can not be objected to on principle,
but tests of opportunity surely may be." His second objection concerns the bill's exemption for those fleeing
religious persecution. Applying it would force an immigration officer to pass judgment on a foreign
government's laws, an "invidious function" that risks "very serious questions of international justice and
comity." Either objection, he says, is enough to withhold assent "in its present form."

## Key facts it relies on
- Dated "THE WHITE HOUSE, January 29, 1917" and addressed "To the House of Representatives" (para. 1, close).
- The bill is H.R. 10384, "An act to regulate the immigration of aliens to, and the residence of aliens in,
  the United States" (para. 1).
- The literacy test is "a radical change in the policy of the Nation which is not justified in principle"
  (para. 1).
- "It is not a test of character, of quality, or of personal fitness, but would operate in most cases merely
  as a penalty for lack of opportunity in the country from which the alien seeking admission came." (para. 1.)
- "Tests of quality and of purpose can not be objected to on principle, but tests of opportunity surely may
  be." (para. 1.)
- Second ground: the religious-persecution exemption would make an officer "pass judgment upon the laws and
  practices of a foreign Government" (para. 2).
- The message does not record the override; the override is in the statute (39 Stat. 874: House two-thirds;
  Senate two-thirds, 5 February 1917; `pdfs/D/st39_full.txt` ll. 1660-1680), not in this source.
