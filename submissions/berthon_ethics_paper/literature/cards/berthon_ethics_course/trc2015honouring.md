## Summary
The Summary condenses the Commission's multi-volume final report, after six years of hearings and more than
6,000 witnesses (p. v). Its central finding is that Canada's Aboriginal policy for over a century aimed to
eliminate Aboriginal peoples as distinct peoples through assimilation. It calls the residential schools, a
central element of that policy, "cultural genocide" (p. 1). It records that children were separated from their
families "not to educate them, but primarily to break their link to their culture and identity". It quotes
Macdonald in 1883 that a child at home might "learn to read and write" yet remain "a savage who can read and
write" (p. 2). At least 150,000 children passed through the system, which lasted until the late 1990s (p. 3).
The Summary documents harsh discipline, suppressed languages, physical and sexual abuse, and chronic
underfunding (pp. v, 3-4, 59-60). It describes forced student labour meant to make the schools self-supporting
(p. 59), and an attendance regime that was voluntary by regulation in 1894, enforced without legal authority,
and made compellable by the 1920 Indian Act amendment (pp. 60-62). It also documents English-only directives and
punishment for "speaking Indian" (pp. 80-81).

## Key facts it relies on
- "Children were abused, physically and sexually, and they died in the schools in numbers that would not have
  been tolerated" (pp. v-vi).
- Residential schools were part of a policy "best described as 'cultural genocide'" (p. 1).
- Macdonald, 1883: "He is simply a savage who can read and write"; the TRC's gloss: "This was done not to educate
  them, but primarily to break their link to their culture and identity" (p. 2). Langevin, 1883: "they may know
  how to read and write, but they still remain savages" (p. 58).
- "Aboriginal languages and cultures were denigrated and suppressed" (p. 3); children "discouraged—and often
  prohibited—from speaking their own languages" (p. 4).
- "The government believed that between the forced labour of students and the poorly paid labour of
  missionaries, it could operate a residential school system on a nearly cost-free basis" (p. 59).
- 1894 regulations: attendance "voluntary", but agents could order children placed and retrieved by warrant
  (pp. 60-61); Indian Affairs enforced attendance "for which it had no legal authority" (p. 62).
- "In 1920, the Indian Act was amended to allow the government to compel any First Nations child to attend
  residential school. However, residential school was never compulsory for all First Nations children"; in
  1944-45, 31.1% of school-aged Aboriginal children were in residential school (p. 62).
- Directives of 1883-1893 for "reading, writing and speaking the English language rather than that of Cree"
  (p. 80); Onion Lake taught "read and write both Cree and English" as "one of the few exceptions" (p. 81).
