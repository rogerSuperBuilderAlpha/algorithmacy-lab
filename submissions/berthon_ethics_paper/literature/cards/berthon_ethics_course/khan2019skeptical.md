## Summary
Khan and Pozen challenge the consensus behind Jack Balkin's proposal to treat online platforms such as
Facebook, Google and Twitter as "information fiduciaries" owing users duties of care, confidentiality and
loyalty (pp. 497, 499-500). Balkin's idea had "garnered remarkably broad support and essentially zero critical
pushback" (p. 497). Part I asks "Fiduciaries for Whom?" Facebook and Balkin's other main examples are
Delaware corporations, whose officers and directors already owe fiduciary duties "to the corporation and its
stockholders" and must "treat stockholder welfare as the only end" (p. 503). "A fiduciary with sharply
opposed loyalties teeters on the edge of contradiction" (p. 504): directors could be forced to breach duties
to stockholders to honour duties to users. Part II.A notes that Balkin "has never squarely addressed the issue
of crosscutting loyalties" and tests four reconciliations (p. 508). Part V offers better analogies: platforms
as infrastructure, disciplined by common-carrier rules, line-of-business limits, interoperability and
antitrust; and data collection as pollution, met by prohibitions, Pigouvian taxes and liability
(pp. 538-540). The authors conclude that the fiduciary idea invites "an enervating complacency" about
structural power (p. 502) and should play no "significant role" (p. 541).

## Key facts it relies on
- Balkin proposes duties "of care, confidentiality, and loyalty" for platforms vis-à-vis end users, "more
  limited" than for professionals (p. 500).
- Facebook, Google, Twitter and Uber are Delaware corporations (p. 503, nn. 26-27).
- Delaware officers and directors "must ... treat stockholder welfare as the only end", quoting *Frederick Hsu
  Living Trust v. ODN Holding* (Del. Ch. 2017) (p. 503, n. 28).
- "A fiduciary with sharply opposed loyalties teeters on the edge of contradiction" (p. 504, n. 32 citing
  Miller).
- Directors could face "the untenable position of having to violate their fiduciary duties (to stockholders)
  ... in order to fulfill their fiduciary duties (to end users)" (p. 504).
- "Balkin has never squarely addressed the issue of crosscutting loyalties" (p. 508).
- Alternatives: infrastructure regulation and a pollution model with "clear prohibitions and economic
  disincentives, rather than morally laden standards" (pp. 538-540).
- The fiduciary framework risks "an enervating complacency about issues of structural power" (p. 502).

## Critical notes from the literature
- AUDIT_05 M3-22 (`literature/audit/AUDIT_05.md:412`): the loyalty conflict Khan and Pozen describe is
  stockholders against users, not user against user. A duty of impartiality between two users (driver and
  rider) is the facet's and the paper's own extension, and should be marked as such.
- Facet M3 (`literature/facets/M3_intermediaries_records.md:17, 258`): neither Balkin nor Khan and Pozen
  reaches a duty owed to two principals at once, which the paper treats as its own ground.
- Piety's response (cited at p. 504, n. 31) argues that Delaware law has not deterred misconduct, an added
  reason for skepticism. I did not open it.
- Kim and Routledge (2022, pp. 99-100), read this session for their own card, propose the opposite analogy:
  boards could owe "fidelity to the interests of data subjects" as they do to shareholders. They do not
  answer the conflict that Khan and Pozen raise.
