## Summary
Holmes reviews Kousser's study of disfranchisement in the eleven ex-Confederate states, 1880-1910. Kousser's view parallels Woodward's: an open, competitive post-Reconstruction politics gave way to a harsher, restrictive system. In the 1880s, 64 percent of eligible voters took part and 25 percent backed Republicans or independents. After the disfranchisement statutes, in the first decade of the twentieth century, only 30 percent of the electorate voted, and the polity became a "broadly-based oligarchy". Kousser's method is Goodman's ecological regression on election returns. His findings: the poll tax was among the most effective devices; middle-class whites used the secret ballot to disfranchise illiterates; white primaries held the one-party system. Black-belt Democrats, especially the affluent, led disfranchisement and "wanted to eliminate the voting of poor whites as well as blacks", while "Populists, Republicans, and blacks consistently opposed the new laws". Against V. O. Key, Kousser holds that the laws themselves mattered and estimates that they cut turnout by an average of 37 percent. Holmes dissents on several points. Key's thesis "still has much validity", since earlier election laws and violence (Texas, Mississippi) had already reduced Black voting. Hill and black-belt Democrats held similar views, which tempers the class reading. He still calls the book "indispensable".

## Key facts it relies on
- Scope: the "disfranchisement movement that prevailed in the eleven ex-Confederate states" (p. 270).
- 1880s: "64 percent of the eligible voters participating in elections", with 25 percent supporting Republicans or independents (p. 270).
- First decade of the twentieth century, "after the enactment of disfranchisement statutes": "only 30 percent of the electorate voted"; 10 percent supported Republicans (pp. 270-271).
- Method: Goodman's ecological regression (p. 271).
- The poll tax was "one of the most effective devices". Middle-class whites used the secret ballot "to disfranchise illiterates" (p. 271).
- Black-belt Democrats "wanted to eliminate the voting of poor whites as well as blacks. Populists, Republicans, and blacks consistently opposed the new laws" (p. 271).
- The suffrage laws "caused an average reduction in voter turnout throughout the South of 37 percent" (p. 271).
- Holmes's dissent: "Key's thesis still has much validity". "In Texas and Mississippi violence played a more decisive role". "Democrats in the hills and black belts appear to have held similar views" (pp. 271-272).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The review itself carries the main critique, set out above (pp. 271-272). Holmes disputes how much of the decline belongs to the laws rather than earlier violence and election laws. He also disputes the class reading of who supported disfranchisement.
