## Summary
A public-policy analysis arguing that addiction to digital experiences is "both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and engineered (designed on purpose)", and that marketers are complicit (p. 451). The authors review what addiction is and how cues, rewards and missing end cues build it (pp. 452–453), then trace marketing's role through the four Ps: product design is "the heart" (notifications as variable-ratio reinforcement, streaks, infinite scroll, unsupervised experimentation on users), and advertising, distribution and "free" pricing deepen it (pp. 454–456). Industry self-help, such as Apple's and Google's usage dashboards, is "a minimum first step", and competition makes it insufficient, so "public policy solutions are required" (p. 457). Remedies are sorted as informing (educate and disclose), guiding (shape and incentivize) and restricting (ban and remove) for each P (p. 457; Table 2, pp. 458–459): labels and disclosure of design mechanisms, stopping points, independent review of experiments, advertising limits, friction billing, taxes, bans on loot boxes and predatory freemium models (pp. 457–461). They warn that information alone is "necessary, but not sufficient" (p. 457) and that campaigns alone have had "limited impact" (p. 460), and close that public policy intervention is "inevitable" (p. 463).

## Key facts it relies on
- Addiction to digital experiences is "both akratic ... and engineered" (abstract, p. 451).
- Three strategies: "informing (educate and disclose), guiding (shape and incentivize), and restricting (ban and remove)" (pp. 452, 457).
- On usage dashboards: "There's literally no evidence that knowing how much you consume changes how you consume" (Tsukayama 2018, quoted p. 457); "empirical research suggests that information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior (e.g., Worsley 2002)" (p. 457).
- "Thus, we argue that public policy solutions are required to address digital addiction, just as in other areas, from nutrition to gambling" (p. 457).
- Mandatory labelling is "one of the least controversial public policy strategies" (p. 457); restriction is "The most extreme public policy intervention" (p. 459).
- "Informational campaigns alone have had limited impact on people's behavior in other health areas", but "awareness and knowledge are first steps" (p. 460).
- Companies run experiments on users "unencumbered by institutional review boards" (p. 455); Table 2 proposes "Independent oversight (IRBs) of experimentation on consumers" (p. 458).
- "Consumers on their own struggle to maintain equanimity with regard to their digital experiences, and companies are competitively incentivized to learn how to make their offerings as addictive as possible. Thus, public policy intervention is likely to be both unavoidable and beneficial to society." (p. 463).
