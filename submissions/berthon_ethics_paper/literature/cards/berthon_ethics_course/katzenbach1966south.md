## Summary
South Carolina asked the Court to declare key sections of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 unconstitutional and to
enjoin the Attorney General from enforcing them. The Court denied the request, holding that the sections before
it were an appropriate means of enforcing the Fifteenth Amendment (p. 308). To show why Congress chose "sterner"
remedies, the opinion summarises the legislative record: from 1890 seven Southern states enacted literacy and
related tests "specifically designed to prevent Negroes from voting", alongside alternate tests that let white
illiterates register (pp. 310-311); case-by-case litigation failed; and discriminatory administration by
registrars became "the principal method used to bar Negroes from the polls" (p. 312). The Court's footnotes give
examples from federal suits (p. 312, nn. 12-13). On the remedy, the Court accepted that literacy tests are not
in themselves unconstitutional (*Lassiter*), but found that in most covered states the tests had been instituted,
framed and administered to disenfranchise Black citizens, so suspending them for five years was legitimate
(pp. 333-334). It also upheld federal examiners and preclearance of new rules (pp. 334-337).

## Key facts it relies on
- Seven states "beginning in 1890 ... enacted tests still in use which were specifically designed to prevent
  Negroes from voting" (p. 310); Tillman's 1895 statement quoted at p. 310, n. 9.
- In 1890 "more than two-thirds of the adult Negroes were illiterate while less than one-quarter of the adult
  whites were unable to read or write"; alternate tests assured "white illiterates would not be deprived of the
  franchise" (p. 311).
- Before the Civil War "most of the slave States made it a crime to teach Negroes how to read or write"
  (p. 311, n. 10).
- Discriminatory administration "has been found in all eight Alabama cases, in all nine Louisiana cases, and in
  all nine Mississippi cases which have gone to final judgment" (p. 312).
- White applicants "have often been excused altogether ... or have been given easy versions, have received
  extensive help from voting officials"; Black applicants "have typically been required to pass difficult
  versions of all the tests, without any outside assistance and without the slightest error" (p. 312).
- n. 12: "FRDUM FOOF SPETGH"; n. 13: the Panola County "Chickasaw School Fund" question and the six rejected
  graduates in Forrest County (p. 312).
- The good-morals requirement is "so vague and subjective that it has constituted an open invitation to abuse"
  (p. 313).
- Tests "have been instituted with the purpose of disenfranchising Negroes, have been framed in such a way as to
  facilitate this aim, and have been administered in a discriminatory fashion" (pp. 333-334).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The Court's account of administration rests on Justice Department suits in three states (Alabama, Louisiana,
  Mississippi), not on all covered states (`review/v3/ledger_parts/P3.md`, P3-004).
- *Lassiter* (1959), which the Court here reaffirms on the point, held literacy tests not in themselves contrary
  to the Fifteenth Amendment; facet D sets that holding against *Katzenbach*'s finding of design and
  administration (`literature/facets/D_literacy_as_gate.md`:535).
- Facet D notes that Kousser, as reported by Holmes, has black-belt Democrats wanting to disenfranchise poor
  whites as well; that is a tension between Kousser and the Court's race-only framing, not one the Court raises
  (`literature/audit/AUDIT_04.md`:281; facet D, F1).
- Tillman and the Mississippi and South Carolina conventions are quoted at one remove through n. 9 (facet D:53,
  554; bib note).
