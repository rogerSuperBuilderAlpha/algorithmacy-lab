New key, opened to trace the source of Cross's (1917, p. 552) unsourced claim that teaching a felon to read was
indictable. Cross cites "Pike, History of Crime, I. 300" in n. 37, the note nearest that claim. Pages are fixed by the
OCR's running heads ("CHAP, IV.] FOR BENEFIT OF CLERGY. 301"; "REFERENCES AND NOTES. 483").

## Summary
In his chapter on the fourteenth century Pike describes how benefit of clergy widened from the tonsured to those who
could read. He notes that one judge held the tonsure necessary "as late as the year 1352", but that "the
qualification of being able to read must, in the usual practice, have been held sufficient" (p. 300). He adds that
judges were soon charged with finding and punishing gaolers who taught prisoners letters (p. 300), and that church
tenants learned to read at a monastery or "were taught to read in prison and claimed at the trial" (p. 301). Only
later could the judges compel the ordinary to accept reading as proof of clerkship (p. 301).

## Key facts it relies on
- P. 300: "If not already, it was very soon afterwards a part of the duty of the judges to enquire whether gaolers had
  aided prisoners to gain a knowledge of letters, and to punish them when detected in the offence."
- P. 301: "Many tenants of the church lands, no doubt, availed themselves of the opportunity to learn letters at the
  nearest monastery, or, if they were in favour with the superior, were taught to read in prison and claimed at the
  trial."
- P. 483 (notes to ch. IV): the paragraph rests on "the 'Liber Assisarum,' 26 Edward III., p. 122 ... the same book,
  p. 138, where the judges are instructed (Article 11) to enquire what gaolers had aided their prisoners to learn
  letters".

## Critical notes from the literature
- Pike's claim is about gaolers and an article of inquiry in the 1350s. Cross (1917, p. 552) states the broader rule
  that it was "an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read". The primary record (Liber Assisarum,
  26 Edw. III, p. 138, art. 11) has not been read.
