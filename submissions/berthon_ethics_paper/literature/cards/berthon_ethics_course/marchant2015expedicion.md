## Summary

Marchant Rivera traces the Castilian notarial register from the abbreviated note of the Alfonsine legislation to the full-text protocol of the early modern period. Under Alfonso X the notary had to keep his notes in books, but a note remained a summary, often shortened by "etcétera" clauses, and later notaries struggled to expand it into a deed (pp. 1–4, 8). The Catholic Monarchs' Pragmática of Alcalá (7 June 1503), later collected in the Novísima Recopilación, installed the literal protocol: a bound register in which each deed was written out in full, read to the parties and witnesses, and signed (pp. 8–10). The chapter prints the operative clauses from the Novísima (1831 edition) and argues that the Catholic Monarchs compiled and renewed older rules rather than inventing notarial regulation (p. 11). Under Carlos I regulation became more frequent and more economic. Málaga serves as the case: royal provisions of July 1532 ordered the city's notaries to write deeds in the register and not to hand blank documents to parties for signature, yet the notaries "continuaban actuando al margen de la legalidad notarial" and expected no punishment (pp. 12–13). A graphic appendix of protocol pages closes the chapter; I did not read it.

## Key facts it relies on

- p. 8: "La Reina Católica … el 7 de junio de 1503 dio en Alcalá de Henares la famosa Pragmática, que luego se recogió en la Novísima Recopilación"; footnote 13 gives the source edition (Novísima Recopilación, Méjico/París 1831, tít. XXIII, lib. X, pp. 544–549).
- p. 9: the notary must keep "un libro de prothocolo enquadernado de pliego de papel entero" and write each deed "por estenso".
- p. 9: "los dichos escrivanos las lean presentes las partes e los testigos. E sy las partes las otorgaren, las firmen de sus nombres. E sy no supieren firmar, fyrme por ellos qual quiera delos testygos, o otro que sepa escrivir".
- p. 9: no deed may be issued under the notary's sign unless the parties and witnesses were present when the note was granted, and the issued copy may neither add nor remove a word from the register.
- pp. 9–10: penalty: "so pena que la escritura que de otra manera se diere sygnada sea en sy ninguna, e el escrivano que la fiziere pierda el officio … e sea obligado a pagar ala parte el interesse."
- p. 10: copies are due within three days (eight if long), on pain of paying "ala parte el ynteresse e daño" and 100 maravedís a day.
- p. 11: the Catholic Monarchs "recopilaron y renovaron la antigua estructura legal sobre escribanos públicos", against the common belief that notarial regulation came almost wholly from them.
- pp. 12–13: in Málaga (1532) the notaries kept acting outside notarial law; a regidor reported they were sure that "aunque no guardaran las provisiones, no serían castigados", and the group "seguía actuando impunemente."

## Critical notes from the literature

- The chapter is diplomatic history of the register, not a study of the notary's duty to the parties. Its law text is a secondary print (Novísima Recopilación, 1831), and I read the repository version, whose pagination is its own. The print pages (pp. 331–347, and p. 335 for the clause) come from Almeida Cabrejas (2019) and were not checked against the book (`review/v3/FIX_CHECK_A.md` l. 59). The RIUMA record types the item as "journal article", which does not match the book chapter details.
- The penalty runs to "la parte", the injured party, and the loss of office runs to the Crown. `review/v3/FIX_CHECK_C.md` (l. 42) reads this as liability to whichever party is harmed and so as support for "answerable to both sides"; the law itself never states a duty to both sides.
- Marchant's own Málaga evidence (pp. 12–13) shows the rule unenforced in practice a generation later. The manuscript's "force" of the office is a fact about the law as written.
- `review/v3/resolutions/C1_historical.md` (l. 207–210): Rojas García (2012) was blocked; Marchant was used because she prints the law's text.
