## Summary
Brandt asks how individual literacy development relates to "the large-scale economic forces that set the routes
and determine the wordly worth of that literacy" (p. 166). Her answer is the concept of sponsors of literacy:
agents who enable, teach or withhold literacy "and gain advantage by it in some way" (p. 166). Sponsors "set the
terms for access to literacy and wield powerful incentives for compliance and loyalty" (pp. 166-167); their
interests and those of the sponsored "do not have to converge" and may conflict (p. 166). The concept rests on
more than 100 interviews with Americans born between about 1900 and 1980 (p. 167). She ties sponsorship to
patron-client systems and argues that literacy, "like land", is a commodity, which is "why the powerful work so
persistently to conscript and ration the powers of literacy" (p. 169). Case sections address stratified access
(Raymond Branch and Dora Lopez), the escalation of standards, and appropriation (Dwayne Lowery, Carol White,
Sarah Steele) (pp. 169-183). She ends with writing teachers as "conflicted brokers between literacy's buyers and
sellers" and with the line that "The history of literacy is a catalogue of obligatory relations" (p. 183).

## Key facts it relies on
- Definition: sponsors "are any agents, local or distant, concrete or abstract, who enable, support, teach,
  model, as well as recruit, regulate, suppress, or withhold literacy—and gain advantage by it in some way"
  (p. 166).
- Sponsors "set the terms for access to literacy and wield powerful incentives for compliance and loyalty"
  (pp. 166-167).
- Literacy learning "has always required permission, sanction, assistance, coercion, or, at minimum, contact
  with existing trade routes" (p. 167).
- Evidence base: "more than 100 in-depth interviews" with people "born roughly between 1900 and 1980" (p. 167).
- The concept covers relations from "benign sharing" to "euphemized coercions in schools and workplaces" and
  "the most notorious impositions and deprivations by church or state" (p. 168).
- "the powerful work so persistently to conscript and ration the powers of literacy" (p. 169).
- Three issues: stratification of opportunity, sponsors' part in "the literacy crisis", and appropriation
  (p. 169).
- "The history of literacy is a catalogue of obligatory relations." (p. 183.)

## Critical notes from the literature
- Wording of the definition differs across three versions: the 1997 CELA report ("teach, or model ... withhold
  literacy and gain advantage"; `AUDIT_05.md:38`, M1-1), the 2001 book as Young quotes it ("teach, and model ...
  withhold, literacy—and gain"; `AUDIT_05.md:39`, M1-2), and the journal ("teach, model ... withhold
  literacy—and gain", p. 166, this card). The manuscript's wording is the report's.
- `M1_literacy_studies.md:76, 187` (confirmed with qualification in `AUDIT_05.md:48`, M1-11): Brandt works from
  learners' recollections of acquisition; the test-giver, court and record-keeper do not appear as sponsors, so
  the concept pre-empts "nobody named the positions" but not the triad.
- `N3_literacy_history.md:54, 132, 145`: Jerasa and Jones (2025) already treat platform algorithms as Brandtian
  sponsors that gatekeep access; the paper's contrast with Brandt must rest on the triad and the binding outcome,
  not on the claim that sponsors teach.
- `AUDIT_05.md:64` (M1-27): Vee (2013) takes the "ratchet" of rising standards from Brandt, so the two are one
  source on that point.
