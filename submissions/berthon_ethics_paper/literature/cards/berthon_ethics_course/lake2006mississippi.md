## Summary
Lake traces the literacy test as a "technology of racial exclusion" that white men's countries exchanged
across borders between 1890 and 1910 (pp. 209-213). The test's target and form changed as it travelled: "from
the requirement to write one's name, to demonstration of the comprehension of the constitution, to the
ability to fill out an application form in English to a dictation test in any European language" (p. 213).
Mississippi's 1890 convention first used an "education test" to strip Black voters; other Southern states
followed, and the Cape Colony copied it in 1892 (pp. 215-216). Lodge and the Immigration Restriction League
built an immigration literacy test, passed in 1896 and vetoed by Cleveland (pp. 217-219). Natal's Premier
founded his 1897 Bill "on the American Act" but went "one step further", because Natal's targets were
"perfectly well able to read and write in their own language"; the final Act required "any European language"
(pp. 220-221). Chamberlain then recommended the Natal model to Australia. The 1901 Immigration Restriction Act
set a dictation test of fifty words in any European language, framed to let officers fail anyone, and it
"remained in immigration legislation until 1958" (pp. 225-228). Lake concludes that the test "was the
instrument of whiteness par excellence" (p. 229).

## Key facts it relies on
- Mississippi's 1890 constitution required a voter to read, understand or interpret a section of the
  Constitution (p. 215).
- The American immigration bill, passed in 1896, was vetoed by Cleveland (p. 219). The Natal Premier quotes its
  test as reading and writing "in their own language or in some other language" (p. 221).
- Natal Premier, Legislative Assembly, March 1897: the Bill "is founded on the American Act. But it goes one
  step further" (p. 221, n. 44: Legislative Assembly Debates, Natal, 25 March 1897, CO 179/198, pp. 30-1).
- Natal's targets were "perfectly well able to read and write in their own language" (Lake's quotation of the
  Premier, p. 221).
- "The final legislation actually specified that the application must be written in 'any European
  language'" (p. 221).
- Chamberlain recommended the Natal Act "as a model to the Australians in 1901" (p. 221).
- Australia's dictation test: "any prescribed passage of fifty words in any European language" (p. 226).
- "The dictation test remained in immigration legislation until 1958" (p. 228).
