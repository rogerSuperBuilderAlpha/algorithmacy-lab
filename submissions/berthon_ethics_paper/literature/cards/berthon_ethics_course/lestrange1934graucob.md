## Summary
Miss L'Estrange, who ran a café in Llandudno, signed an order form for a cigarette slot machine. The form carried, in small print, a clause excluding "any express or implied condition, statement, or warranty, statutory or otherwise". The machine failed. She sued for breach of the implied warranty of fitness and won in the county court. The judge found that the sellers "did not do what was reasonably sufficient to bring these conditions to the notice of the plaintiff". On appeal, Scrutton and Maugham L.JJ. reversed. A signed document binds "whether he has read the document or not", absent fraud or misrepresentation. The ticket cases on reasonable notice apply only to unsigned documents. The evidence was disputed. The sellers' supervisor swore that he read the whole form, small print included, aloud to her, and she denied that anyone read it to her. The court held the point immaterial. Maugham L.J. said he regretted the decision and wished the contract were "in a simpler and more usual form". He held it irrelevant that she "did not read, or hear of" the small print. Judgment was entered for the sellers on claim and counterclaim.

## Key facts it relies on
- Headnote, p. 395: "Held, by the Divisional Court (Scrutton and Maugham L.JJ.)". It is not reported as a Court of Appeal case.
- Headnote, p. 395: the buyer replied "that at the time when she signed the order form she had not read it and knew nothing of its contents, and that the clause excluding warranties could not easily be read owing to the smallness of the print."
- Evidence, pp. 398-399 (page heads uncertain in OCR): the plaintiff "said that she did not read the brown document and that the defendants did not read it to her or tell her to read it".
- Evidence, same pages: Mr Page "said that the interview on February 7, 1933, lasted for over two hours, that he sat near the plaintiff and read to her the whole of the brown paper document including the small print, that the plaintiff asked no question about the small print".
- Scrutton L.J., p. 402: the county court judge found "that the defendants did not do what was reasonably sufficient to bring these conditions to the notice of the plaintiff."
- Scrutton L.J., p. 403: "When a document containing contractual terms is signed, then, in the absence of fraud, or, I will add, misrepresentation, the party signing it is bound, and it is wholly immaterial whether he has read the document or not."
- Scrutton L.J., p. 403: the notice cases (railway tickets and unsigned documents) "have no application when the document has been signed."
- Maugham L.J., p. 405: "I regret the decision to which I have come, but I am bound by legal rules and cannot decide the case on other considerations." The terms were "in regrettably small print but quite legible".
- Maugham L.J., p. 407: "it is, in my view, an irrelevant circumstance that the plaintiff did not read, or hear of, the parts of the sales document which are in small print". He adds, "I could wish that the contract had been in a simpler and more usual form."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Kessler (1943) n. 3 (`kessler1943contracts`; local `C2_law/kessler1943.txt` l. 79-82) cites the case as the English authority that signers "will not be heard to say that they have not read it ... provided the document is legible however small the print."
- The case concerns a literate shopkeeper, not an illiterate signer. It shows that the rule makes the signature, not reading or hearing, the point of commitment. For illiterate signers see `washington2004bailey`, and for non-English speakers `morales2008sun` (facet V3).
- Court: often cited as a Court of Appeal decision. The report's headnote says Divisional Court. Cite "[1934] 2 KB 394 (DC)" or simply the report.
- Page locators for the evidence passage are inferred: the OCR drops some page numbers. The two judgment passages (403, 405, 407) sit between clear margin numbers.
