## Summary
Hagedorn treats the interpreters at Anglo-Iroquois councils between 1740 and 1770 as cultural brokers who mediated much more than language. More than a hundred men and women served as interpreters north of Virginia, about a third of them among the Iroquois (p. 62). Council protocol entitled each side to hear speeches in its own tongue. Because the Iroquois kept their record in memory, accurate translation mattered greatly (p. 63). The paragraph-by-paragraph method gave interpreters "considerable discretionary power" to soften or steer speeches. William Johnson admitted to "softening" translations (p. 65). In 1758 Weiser and Montour declined to translate a denunciation in open council (p. 71). Interpreters also wrote speeches, chose wampum, advised governors on protocol and engaged Indian speakers (pp. 66-71). Hagedorn rejects the view that interpreters were "generally unscrupulous individuals" who worked "without any checks". She calls it "too harsh": when doubts arose, other interpreters were brought in "to 'assist' and check the accuracy of his translations" (p. 61). Disputed renderings were sometimes settled jointly with the Indians (pp. 65-66). Written records were kept only by the Europeans, so the evidence mostly concerns interpreters serving the English (p. 71).

## Key facts it relies on
- The council needed "a Friend to go between them": a person of "Ability and Integrity" in whom both sides could "place a Confidence" (p. 61).
- "When doubts about an interpreter's integrity or ability arose on either the Indian or the English side, others were employed, formally or informally, to 'assist' and check the accuracy of his translations" (p. 61, n. 9).
- Note 9 lists the reasons trust was doubted ("greed and self-interest to ethnic background or intoxication"). It adds that "Teedyuscung, the Delaware chief, and other Indians sometimes had their own private interpreters present at conferences" (pp. 75-76).
- Between 1740 and 1770 "more than one hundred men and women" served as interpreters (p. 62).
- In February 1756 Davison and Weiser disagreed over a Seneca speech. They "met with the Indians and settled between them the 'true Interpretation'" (pp. 65-66).
- In 1757 Governor Denny asked the Indians to give their speeches to the interpreters in advance (p. 66).
- The paragraph-by-paragraph method gave the interpreter "considerable discretionary power". Johnson "softened" speeches (p. 65).
- At Easton in 1758 Weiser and Montour refused to translate Nichas's denunciation of Teedyuscung in open council (p. 71).
- "written records were kept only by the European participants" (p. 71).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Hagedorn's n. 8 cites Jennings (1984, p. 63) for the harsher "generally unscrupulous" view, and she does not quote him. The arm knows Jennings's position only through Ghere's (1984) abstract (facet G, :236, :261, :283).
- Facet G (:236, :242, :251) and ledger P6-015 point out that the checking interpreters were "employed" by the councils or governments. Illiterate individuals did not hire them, so the article does not show a party bringing its own check. Note 9's "private interpreters" (pp. 75-76) is the closest the article comes to that.
- Ledger P1-002 finds nothing in the article that gives the second interpreter binding authority. In 1756 the dispute was settled by agreement with the Indians present (pp. 65-66).
- Hagedorn's 1995 W&M thesis exists but has not been opened (facet G, :283).
