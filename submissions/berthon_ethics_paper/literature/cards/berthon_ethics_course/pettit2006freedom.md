## Summary

Pettit asks how the market looks from the republican conception of freedom as non-domination, not from the liberal conception of freedom as non-interference (pp. 131–132). The article has four sections: freedom, property, exchange and regulation. Social freedom belongs to the chooser, not the choice: a person is free to the extent that she is shielded against interference by others over which she has no control, whether by her own resources or by law and norms that give everyone "antipower" (pp. 132–133). On property, a regime that contingently allows domination, especially through poverty or inequality, justifies redistribution or restriction so that inequality in non-domination is minimised (pp. 140–141). On exchange, he argues under "extremely idealistic assumptions" (no preventable arbitrary interference, a discipline of nondiscrimination, and a ban on contracts that accept domination) that republicans can look on market exchange with complacency. In a well-functioning labour market no one would depend on a particular master (p. 142). A market offer, unlike a threat, adds an option and removes none, so it does not interfere (pp. 142–144). Section 4, which opens on p. 144, defends state regulation against libertarian objections; I did not read past its opening.

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract (p. 131): the republican conception "argues for important normative constraints on property, exchange, and regulation, without supporting extremes to the effect that 'property is theft' or 'taxation is theft'."
- p. 133: people may be shielded from interference "by measures that can empower all citizens equally: a suitable rule of law or a regime of protective norms … a certain degree of 'antipower'."
- p. 141: "economic redistribution or restriction will be supported under a republican political theory, so far as material poverty or inequality is productive of non-domination."
- p. 142, §3 assumptions: no "predatory pricing, insider trading, market manipulation"; "a discipline of nondiscrimination"; and exchanges in which a party "accepts or risks domination by the other in return for some good (this, as in the slave contract) are prohibited."
- p. 142: "Can republicanism look with complacency, under these assumptions (these extremely idealistic assumptions), on market exchanges? I shall argue that it can."
- p. 142: "in a well-functioning labor market (and, of course, it may be very difficult to establish such a market), no one would depend on any particular master and so no one would be at the mercy of a master: he or she could move on to employment elsewhere in the event of suffering arbitrary interference."
- p. 143: "The offer of a market reward is always the offer of a reward that you are allowed to refuse."
- p. 144: only a threat, not a regular offer, affects "the accessibility of the options, and thereby deprive[s] you of some control."

- Facet N1 (l. 47) also reports, from p. 146 (not read this session), that Pettit routes non-domination in private relations through state regulation under the community's control, not through control by those subject to a firm.
