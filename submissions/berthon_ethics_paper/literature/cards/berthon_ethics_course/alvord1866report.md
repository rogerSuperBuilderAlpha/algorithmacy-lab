## Summary
John W. Alvord, the Freedmen's Bureau's inspector of schools, reported on 1 January 1866 on schools he had
visited across the South (the extract names Beaufort and New Orleans). I read only the extract that Walter L. Fleming reprinted in a 1905 syllabus for the New York
State Education Department. Alvord describes freedpeople of every age seeking schooling: adults "gladly avail
themselves of the evening and Sabbath schools", study "fragment[s] of a spelling book" between work, and in colored
regiments "nearly every man can now read" where few had known their letters, with officers and chaplains teaching
(p. 144). Daily attendance at colored schools in the District of Columbia was 75 per cent against 41 per cent at
white schools (p. 144). Most important for this facet, he found "native schools" that the freedpeople had set up
themselves, "often rude and very imperfect", taught by "some young man, some woman, or old preacher" in a cellar,
shed or meetinghouse corner "with the alphabet in hand, or a torn spelling book" (p. 145). He closes with a
legislator's disbelief that a school for Black children could exist (p. 145).

## Key facts it relies on
- Adults "gladly avail themselves of the evening and Sabbath schools" and study "with some fragment of a spelling
  book in their hands" (Fleming p. 144).
- In colored regiments, "where but few knew their letters at first, nearly every man can now read, and many of
  them write"; officers and chaplains served as teachers (p. 144).
- District of Columbia daily attendance: white schools 41 per cent, colored schools 75 per cent (p. 144).
- "in very many places I have found what I will call 'native schools,' often rude and very imperfect, but there they
  are, a group, perhaps, of all ages, trying to learn" (p. 145).
- "Some young man, some woman, or old preacher, in cellar, or shed, or corner of a negro meetinghouse, with the
  alphabet in hand, or a torn spelling book, is their teacher" (p. 145).
- Source line in Fleming: "Report of J. W. Alvord, inspector of bureau schools, Jan. 1, 1866, in Sen. Ex. Doc. ...
  39th Cong, 1st Sess." (p. 145; document number illegible in the OCR).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Fleming's syllabus selects documents for its own narrative. The Alvord text is quoted, but the selection is his
  (Fleming's historiographical stance is not documented in this arm's record).
- Williams (2005), read only as snippets, quotes the same "native schools" passage and records that freedpeople
  "paid tuition when they could afford to, and frequently operated completely self-sustaining schools"
  (`V5_scaffolding.md`, Q3); Margo (2005, p. 590; card `margo2005williams`) reports that Black teachers were
  slightly more than half of those teaching ex-slaves in 1868.
- The original Senate document should be read before citing; the OCR also has errors.
