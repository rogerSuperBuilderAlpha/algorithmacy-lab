## Summary
Jobin, Ienca and Vayena ask whether a global agreement on "ethical AI" is emerging. They ran a scoping review of grey literature: link hubs, a Google keyword search, and citation chaining, with monitoring to 23 April 2019. They found 84 documents with AI ethics principles or guidelines, 88% of them released after 2016. Private companies issued the most (n=19, 22.6%), followed by governmental agencies (n=18, 21.4%) and academic institutions (n=9). Content analysis yields eleven principles. Five appear in more than half of the documents: transparency (73/84), justice and fairness (68/84), non-maleficence (60/84), responsibility (60/84) and privacy (47/84). Beneficence follows with 41/84. Transparency is "the most prevalent principle" and is chiefly presented "as a way to minimize harm". References to non-maleficence outweigh those to beneficence by a factor of 1.5. Behind the numerical convergence, the authors find "substantive divergence" in how principles are interpreted, why they matter, whom they concern and how to implement them. They also find a "gap" between formulating principles and putting them into practice. Public and private sectors issued "nearly equivalent" shares. Africa, South and Central America and Central Asia are underrepresented. The limitations they name are grey-literature retrieval, a possible English-language bias and qualitative coding.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Mittelstadt (2019), in facet M4 (:153) and AUDIT_05 (M4-46): principles alone fail because AI development lacks common aims and fiduciary duties, professional norms, "proven methods to translate principles into practice", and "robust legal and professional accountability mechanisms".
