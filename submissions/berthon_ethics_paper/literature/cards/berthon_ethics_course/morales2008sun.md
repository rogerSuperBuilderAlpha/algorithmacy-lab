## Summary
Juan Morales, a Spanish-speaking welder in St Croix, was hired by Sun after an orientation "conducted entirely in English". He signed a 13-page English employment agreement, nearly eight pages of which concerned arbitration. Sun's orientation leader asked Hodge, a bilingual fellow applicant, to explain what was said and help Morales fill out the papers. Hodge did not explain the arbitration clause. After Morales was fired, the district court refused to stay his suit, holding that he never assented to arbitration. The Third Circuit reversed. Under the objective theory of contract, one "ignorant of the language in which a document is written, or who is illiterate" is bound "if the person does not require the document to be read to him". The court held that Morales's inability to understand English was "immaterial" absent fraud, and that it "was Morales' obligation to ensure he understood the Agreement before signing." Judge Fuentes dissented. He read the precedents to bind only the negligent illiterate signer, and found that Sun had assigned a translator who left out the clause.

## Key facts it relies on
- Slip p. 2: the agreement "was written in English, a language Morales cannot understand".
- Slip p. 3: the arbitration paragraphs "covered nearly 8 of the 13 pages". Langner "asked Jose Hodge ... to explain to Morales what Langner was saying and help him fill out the documents". Hodge "did not specifically explain the arbitration clause to Morales."
- Slip p. 5: quotes *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. at 50. Then quotes Williston: one "who is ignorant of the language in which a document is written, or who is illiterate ... is bound, in the absence of fraud, if the person does not require the document to be read to him".
- Slip p. 6: "In the absence of fraud, the fact that an offeree cannot read, write, speak, or understand the English language is immaterial to whether an English-language agreement the offeree executes is enforceable."
- Slip pp. 6-7 (citing *Wilkisius v. Sheehan*, Mass. 1927): the failure was due "to their own inability to read, write, speak or understand the English language, and to the incapacity of the interpreter".
- Slip p. 7: "It was Morales' obligation to ensure he understood the Agreement before signing. Morales did not ask Hodge to translate the document word-for-word or ask to take the Agreement home and have it translated".
- Slip p. 8 n. 2: "the 'incomplete translation' was due to Morales' failure to request any explanation or translation."
- Dissent, slip p. 12: Sun "assigned Hodge, a coworker who himself was not fluent in English, to translate the document for Morales" and Hodge "neglected to translate the arbitration clauses".

## Critical notes from the literature
- The case binds a non-English speaker by the rule built for the literate (*Upton*). California's Contract Translation Act (`california2025civil`) takes the opposite course for consumer contracts negotiated in five named languages. Morales's contract was an employment contract in the Virgin Islands, outside §1632.
- The majority and dissent disagree over whether a translator supplied by the stronger party shifts the risk. That is the same issue §1632(h) answers by excluding interpreters made available by the business.
