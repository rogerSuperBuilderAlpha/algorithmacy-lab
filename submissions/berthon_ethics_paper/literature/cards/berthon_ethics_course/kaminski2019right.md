## Summary
Kaminski argues that the GDPR does contain a right to explanation and that critics who dismiss it misread a layered system. The pages read this session answer Edwards and Veale's "transparency fallacy" directly: to summarise the right and its accompanying measures "as a 'transparency fallacy'—palliative measures requiring mere icons or simplistic explanations—is to both misrepresent their actual substance and mischaracterize the GDPR's overall transparency regime" (p. 19). The GDPR comes closest to Pasquale's "qualified transparency", "a system of targeted revelations of different degrees of depth and scope, aimed at different recipients", including internal company oversight, regulators, third parties and affected individuals (p. 19). Individual rights aim at "individual understanding of information of a meaningful depth, so that an individual subject of algorithmic decision-making can invoke her rights", and they sit inside "other forms of systemic transparency that in fact goes substantially deeper"; "Individuals might not have access to source code or data sets, but other parties do" (p. 23). Facet N2 recorded her caveat that heavy reliance on collaborative governance without significant public or third-party oversight "is not a known recipe for success, at least as far as protecting individual rights is concerned" (p. 25). The card rests on a preprint with its own pagination; the journal pages are not checked.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 19: the GDPR approximates "qualified transparency": "targeted revelations of different degrees of depth and scope, aimed at different recipients."
- p. 19: transparency "includes putting in place internal company oversight, oversight by regulators, oversight by third parties, and communications to affected individuals."
- p. 19: calling the measures a "transparency fallacy" is "to both misrepresent their actual substance and mischaracterize the GDPR's overall transparency regime."
- p. 18, n. 112: cites Edwards and Veale at 64, 67, "Individuals are mostly too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise".
- p. 23: "Throughout, the emphasis is on individual understanding of information of a meaningful depth, so that an individual subject of algorithmic decision-making can invoke her rights."
- p. 23: "Individuals might not have access to source code or data sets, but other parties do."
- p. 25 (facet N2; reread by verifier): "Its heavy reliance on collaborative governance in the absence of significant public or third-party oversight is not a known recipe for success, at least as far as protecting individual rights is concerned."
