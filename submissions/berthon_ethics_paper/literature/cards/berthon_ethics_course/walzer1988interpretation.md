## Summary
Walzer's Tanner Lectures offer "a philosophical framework for the understanding of social criticism as a
social practice" (p. 3). Lecture I contrasts three paths in moral philosophy, discovery, invention and
interpretation, and favors interpretation as the one that "accords best with our everyday experience of
morality" (p. 4). Lecture II, "The Practice of Social Criticism," which I read from its opening and then pp. 32-45, defends
interpretation against the charge that it binds the critic to the status quo. Walzer sets aside the detached
critic, likened to "an imperial judge in a backward colony" (p. 33), in favor of "the local judge, the
connected critic, who earns his authority ... by arguing with his fellows" (p. 33); for such critics "Social
criticism ... is an internal argument" (p. 34). He then answers whether "standards [are] available to him that
are internal ... and at the same time properly critical" (p. 34). Ruling classes must present themselves as
universal, which "sets standards that the rulers will not live up to" (p. 35; Marx, Gramsci, Silone); the
critic exploits these, as with equality (pp. 36-38). He distinguishes conversion by an outside missionary from
criticism, an "inside job" (pp. 38-39). Moral standards are social artifacts open to critical
interpretation, and "The same men and women who act badly create and sustain the standards by which (at least
sometimes) they know themselves to act badly" (p. 42). Section 3 takes up critical distance (pp. 42-45).
