## Summary
Borrowers who said they were illiterate signed loan and credit-insurance papers with WM Finance, including a separate arbitration agreement. When they sued in state court, WM Finance petitioned to compel arbitration. The district court found the agreement procedurally unconscionable because the borrowers were illiterate and the lender, knowing that, did not tell them orally that they were signing an arbitration agreement. The Fifth Circuit reversed. Under Mississippi law, illiteracy is not a ground to avoid a contract, and a party must "read it or have it read to him". The court quoted the rule that "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him". It found "no authority" that illiteracy removes the duty or puts a duty of oral explanation on the other side. The agreement was in a separate document under a prominent all-caps heading. "Had the Illiterate Appellees had someone read the document to them, the nature of the document they were signing would have been clear."

## Key facts it relies on
- Slip p. 3: the question is "the effect of an individual's illiteracy on the enforcement of an arbitration agreement, which the individual admits he signed, but because of his illiteracy, denies he understood."
- Slip p. 3: "The district court held that the individual's illiteracy, coupled with a lack of oral disclosure, rendered the agreement procedurally unconscionable. We conclude the district court erred and REVERSE."
- Slip p. 9 (quoting *Mixon v. Sovereign Camp*, Miss. 1930): "there cannot be two separate departments in the law of contracts, one for the educated and another for those who are not".
- Slip p. 9 (quoting *J. R. Watkins Co. v. Runnels*, Miss. 1965): "[a] person cannot avoid a written contract which he has entered into on the ground that he did not read it or have it read to him."
- Slip p. 10 (quoting *Dixon v. First Family*, S.D. Miss. 2003): "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him".
- Slip pp. 10-11: the court rejects the holding that the agreement fails because the lender "failed specifically to inform the Illiterate Appellees that they were signing an arbitration agreement after having been made aware of the Illiterate Appellees' inability to read", and finds "no authority supporting the district court's assertion that illiteracy removes this affirmative obligation from a signatory."
- Slip p. 13: misunderstandings "would indisputably have been cleared up had the Illiterate Appellees simply complied with their legal obligation to read the contract or have it read to them."
- Slip pp. 11-12: the borrowers "informed WM Finance that they could not read and inquired as to the nature of the documents they were signing"; the questions "were met with a general response: you are signing insurance and financial paperwork. This response is not plainly misleading."
- Slip p. 13: the agreement "was printed on a separate document headlined by the following phrase in prominent, all-caps print: 'ALTERNATIVE DISPUTE RESOLUTION AGREEMENT.'"

## Critical notes from the literature
- The holding applies Mississippi law. *Morales* (`morales2008sun`) does not show a different rule: the Third Circuit majority applied the same rule under the Restatement, and only the dissent (Fuentes J.) would have relieved a signer who relied on the other party's translator.
- Slip p. 6: the court did not decide whether the borrowers were in fact illiterate. It held that illiteracy "even if established, was insufficient". The case is therefore a statement of law, not a finding about the borrowers.
- The court decided the misrepresentation claim itself rather than sending it to the arbitrator (slip p. 12, n. 4 and text). The borrowers said they told WM Finance they could not read and asked what they were signing, and were told it was insurance and finance paperwork. The court held that this "general response" was "not plainly misleading" (slip pp. 11-12). The case does not hold that a lender may misdescribe a document to an illiterate borrower; it holds that a generic oral answer is not a misdescription.
