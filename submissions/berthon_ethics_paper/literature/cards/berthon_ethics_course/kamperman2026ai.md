## Summary
Kamperman, a writing-studies scholar, and Graff, a historian of literacy, argue in a six-page magazine essay
that "AI literacy" initiatives in higher education "use literacy uncritically as a metaphor to promote
technology for technology's sake" (p. 35). They cite UNESCO's 2024 framework, the OECD's 2025 "AILit"
framework, and programmes at Florida, Ohio State and DeVry as initiatives that barely mention reading or
writing (p. 35). Computer literacy in the 1980s and digital literacy around 2000 followed the same pattern
(p. 35). Their central claim is historical: technological literacy campaigns "repeat many of the conceptual
errors of 19th- and early 20th-century print literacy campaigns, reiterating two harmful myths" (p. 36).
Myth #1 holds that literacy is a set of basic skills that form "an unshakeable foundation"; Myth #2, "Literacy
is a certain or guaranteed path to economic security" (p. 36). Drawing on Graff's literacy myth, they add that
the belief makes failure to read "the fault of the individual" (p. 36). AI literacy also lets technology
companies and states "recruit consumers at an early age" (p. 36). Their remedy is an "AI and Literacy"
curriculum (Table 1, p. 37) that teaches the history and political economy of literacy technologies, has
students write with and without AI, and sets "norms for disclosure" (pp. 37-38).

## Key facts it relies on
- "too many of today's 'AI literacy' initiatives use literacy uncritically as a metaphor to promote technology
  for technology's sake" (p. 35).
- UNESCO's AI Competency Framework "barely mentions reading or writing"; OECD's AILit names four competencies
  without relating them to basic literacy (p. 35).
- Ohio State "proclaimed all OSU graduates starting with the class of 2029 would be 'AI fluent'" (p. 35).
- Technological literacy campaigns "repeat many of the conceptual errors of 19th- and early 20th-century print
  literacy campaigns, reiterating two harmful myths" (p. 36).
- Myth #2: "Literacy is a certain or guaranteed path to economic security" (a section heading, p. 36).
- Earlier tech-funded "computer literacy" programmes "did no more than thrust laptops and tablets into the
  hands of marginalized teens" and drew charges of surveillance (p. 36).
- The literacy myth leads to beliefs that "failure to learn to read and write by a certain age is the fault of
  the individual" (p. 36).
- Table 1 sets out four areas (historical context; political and economic context, "including corporate
  interests"; reading and writing with and without AI; research with and without AI), plus "Establish norms
  for disclosure" (p. 37).
