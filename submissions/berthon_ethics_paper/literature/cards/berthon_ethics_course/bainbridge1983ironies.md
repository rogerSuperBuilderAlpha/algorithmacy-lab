## Summary
A five-page brief paper arguing that automating a process does not remove the human operator's problems and may make them worse. The designer who tries to eliminate the operator "still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate" (p. 775). Monitoring is the worst of these tasks: the automatic system is installed because it does the job better than the operator, "but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively" (p. 776); where the computer uses more dimensions and finer criteria than a person can, "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly", and "The human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776). Skills decay without use, and the deskilled job is "very boring but very responsible" with no chance to build the qualities the responsibility requires (p. 776). Her remedies are practice and design for the operator: hands-on control each shift or simulator practice, systems that "fail obviously", and computer decisions made "at a rate, which the operator can follow" (p. 777). Her final irony is that the most successful automated systems "may need the greatest investment in human operator training" (p. 777). The paper reviews 1970s process-control and flight-deck work and reports no new data.

- p. 776: operators come in "quarter to half an hour" early to get a feel for the process; vigilance decays.
- p. 776: "the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively."
- p. 776: "There is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly."
- p. 776: "The human monitor has been given an impossible task."
- p. 776: "it is very boring but very responsible, yet there is no opportunity to aquire [sic] or maintain the qualities required to handle the responsibility."
- p. 777: "One possibility is to allow the operator to use hands-on control for a short period in each shift. If this suggestion is laughable then simulator practice must be provided."
- p. 777: "automatic systems should fail obviously"; the computer should decide "using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically."
- p. 777: "it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training."
