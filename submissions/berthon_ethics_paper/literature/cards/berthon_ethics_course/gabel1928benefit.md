## Summary
Gabel's monograph studies benefit of clergy in England in the later Middle Ages; its record citations in the snippets are to gaol delivery rolls ("G. D."). The snippets show its treatment of the reading test: the test was introduced gradually as a way of ascertaining "clergy" and "towards the close of the century" (which century the snippet does not show) it "was commonly administered by the court"; any passage might be set, but the first verse of Psalm 51 became known as the "neck-verse" from its common use; and the rolls record prisoners who could not read or spell out syllables yet knew passages by rote, prisoners who learned to "read" in gaol before execution, and one illiterate layman taught in gaol by two boys from Appleby. One record has a judge hand the book upside down to a prisoner, who "read" as before. The snippets also show the Ordinary (the bishop's officer) deciding whether to claim a prisoner as a clerk.

## Key facts it relies on
- Snippet (index places "Neck-verse" at p. 72): "Although any passage might be chosen at random, the first verse of the fifty-first Psalm came to be known as the 'neck-verse,' no doubt because of its common use for the purpose."

- **The Appleby case, continued** (assembled from eleven overlapping highlights; page unknown, apparently in the reading-test section that the book's own cross-reference puts at "pp. 64 et seq."): the jury "took an inquest, the findings of which are curious. The prisoner was discovered to have been a layman and illiterate at the time of his arrest, but had been taught what he knew by two boys from Appleby, who had been admitted to the gaol through the good offices of the gaoler. He was pronounced guilty of the charges and remitted to prison ad dietam, as a layman refusing to stand to the common law of the realm. The offence as regards the pretended literacy was really the gaoler's, for if the prisoner had actually succeeded in learning to read while in gaol, his claim to clergy would have held,51 though the gaoler might have been punished. Literacy thus received by the middle of the fourteenth century" (sentence continues past the highlight).
- **Bearing on Cross.** Gabel puts the offence on the *gaoler* who let teachers in, says a prisoner who truly learned to read in gaol kept his clergy, and says only that the gaoler "might have been punished" (her n. 51, text not seen). That agrees with Pike (1873, vol. 1, p. 300; card `pike1873history`) and is narrower than Cross's general "indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read". Gabel does not support Cross's wording.
