## Summary
Chapter 29 of the 64th Congress, second session, is the general immigration statute of 1917. The scan dates it 5 February 1917. Section 1 defines "alien" and "United States" and extends the Act to the Philippines. Section 2 levies a head tax of eight dollars on every alien entering, with exceptions. Section 3 lists the excluded classes. Besides the long-standing medical, moral and political classes, it adds the "Asiatic" barred zone, defined by lines of latitude and longitude. It also adds the illiteracy test: "All aliens over sixteen years of age, physically capable of reading, who can not read the English language, or some other language or dialect, including Hebrew or Yiddish". Inspectors use printed slips of thirty to forty words in ordinary use. The alien chooses the language or dialect of the test. A resident or citizen may bring in certain older or female relatives whether or not they can read. The test does not apply to religious refugees, returning five-year residents, or aliens in transit. The Act does not print as an ordinary approved law. The closing attestations on p. 898 record that the President returned the bill with his objections. The House passed it again on 1 February 1917, and the Senate on 5 February 1917, each by two-thirds.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet D (entry 10 and l. 238): the Act states no ground for the test. Facet D found no override vote count and no source for when the 1917 clause ended (l. 481, l. 553).
- Card `cleveland1897veto` (paras. 2-3, 11): the 1896-97 bill that Cleveland vetoed excluded those who "can not read and write", and its test was to read and then write twenty to twenty-five words of the Constitution. Facet D (l. 469) calls it simply "a reading test". The 1917 test is reading only, in a language the alien chooses. So the manuscript's "the same device" (L73) is the same in kind, not in its terms.
- The 1917 test leaves the choice of language to the alien. The Natal and Australian tests left it to the officer (Keith 1912, p. 1082; Immigration Restriction Act 1901, s. 3(a)). This bears on L71's remark that "whoever chooses the language and the passage holds the gate". There the US statute is the counter-case.
