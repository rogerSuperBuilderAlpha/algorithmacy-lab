## Summary
Collins, Brown and Newman propose adapting the methods of craft apprenticeship to the teaching of reading, writing
and mathematics. Schools, they argue, convey facts and concepts but leave "key aspects of expertise invisible to
students", so knowledge stays inert (p. 2). Drawing on Lave's study of West African tailors, they describe
apprenticeship as observation, coaching and practice, which from the teacher's side they call modelling, coaching
and fading. Coaching includes scaffolding, "the support, in the form of reminders and help, that the apprentice
requires", after which the master "reduces his participation (fades)" (pp. 2-3). Craft apprenticeship works
because the process is external and visible; cognitive skills require "the externalization of processes that are
usually carried out internally" (p. 4). Their worked cases include Palincsar and Brown's reciprocal teaching of
reading, in which students "get as much support as they need to carry out the task, but no more", and hints are
gradually faded (p. 7). This technical report is the source of the 1989 chapter usually cited; I did not read the
chapter.

## Key facts it relies on
- "standard pedagogical practices render key aspects of expertise invisible to students" (p. 2).
- Apprentices learn by "observation, coaching, and practice, or what we, from the teacher's point of view, call
  modelling, coaching, and fading" (p. 2).
- "A key aspect of coaching is the provision of scaffolding, which is the support, in the form of reminders and
  help, that the apprentice requires to approximate the execution of the entire composite of skills" (pp. 2-3).
- "Once the learner has a grasp of the target skill, the master reduces his participation (fades)" (p. 3).
- Craft apprenticeship teaches processes that are "external and thus readily available to both student and
  teacher for observation" (p. 4).
- Cognitive apprenticeship "requires the externalization of processes that are usually carried out internally"
  (p. 4).
- In reciprocal teaching, "students get as much support as they need to carry out the task, but no more. Hints and
  modelling are then gradually faded out" (p. 7).
