## Summary
Three Cree men with status under Treaty 8 (1899) were charged under Alberta's Wildlife Act for hunting on privately owned land. The Court held that the treaty right to hunt for food continued on private land not put to a visible use incompatible with hunting, and then asked whether the provincial licensing scheme infringed it. Its interpretive reasoning is the part this facet uses. Treaties are to be construed liberally, ambiguities resolved in favour of the Indians, and in their context (¶ 52). The written treaties recorded agreements already reached orally and did not always record their full extent; they were drafted in English, not translated into Cree or Dene, and must be read as the Indians would naturally have understood them at the signing (¶ 52). The verbal promises made for the federal government, recorded in the commissioners' reports and in interpreters' and officials' affidavits and diaries, are of "great significance" (¶ 55); the Treaty 8 nations' oral history "reveals a similar understanding" (¶ 56). Badger's and Kiyawasew's appeals were dismissed and Ominayak's allowed.

## Key facts it relies on
- ¶ 52: "The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and they did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement" (citing Morris 1880, pp. 338-42).
- ¶ 52: "The treaties were drafted in English by representatives of the Canadian government who, it should be assumed, were familiar with common law doctrines. Yet, the treaties were not translated in written form into the languages (here Cree and Dene) of the various Indian nations who were signatories."
- ¶ 52: the words "must not be interpreted in their strict technical sense nor subjected to rigid modern rules of construction. Rather, they must be interpreted in the sense that they would naturally have been understood by the Indians at the time of the signing."
- ¶ 54: the geographic limit is "consistent with the oral promises made to the Indians at the time the Treaty was signed, with the oral history of the Treaty No. 8 Indians".
- ¶ 55: "The Indian people made their agreements orally and recorded their history orally. Thus, the verbal promises made on behalf of the federal government at the times the treaties were concluded are of great significance in their interpretation."

## Critical notes from the literature
- ¶ 52 also says that even translated texts would not likely have been "understood … any differently" by people "who had a history of communicating only orally". That is a court's own great-divide assumption; cite the holding, not that clause (facet V2, Case 5).
- The case concerns Treaty 8; for Treaty 6 the facet uses Morris 1880. *Marshall* (1999) and *Delgamuukw* (1997) carry the same doctrine for other treaties and for oral evidence (facet A).
