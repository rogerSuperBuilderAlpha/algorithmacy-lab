## Summary
Dubal draws on a multi-year ethnographic study of organizing on-demand workers, chiefly ride-hail drivers, and names "algorithmic wage discrimination". The term covers the use of granular data to produce "unpredictable, variable, and personalized hourly pay", which imports price discrimination from consumer markets into labor (abstract, p. 1929). The abstract says the practice "runs afoul of both longstanding precedent on fairness in wage setting and the spirit of equal pay for equal work laws". Parts I and II, which I know only from the abstract and the table of contents, treat wage law and workers' experience of the practice: "calculative unfairness", "trickery" and "gamblification". Part III, which I read closely, evaluates the two responses workers have built. The first is transparency: drivers used GDPR Articles 15 and 22 against Uber and Ola, and an Amsterdam appellate court ordered Uber in April 2023 to explain its upfront pricing (pp. 1982-1984). Dubal concludes that transparency requests are "by themselves ineffectual" absent a ban or collective bargaining. The second is the data cooperative: Driver's Seat and WeClock "countercollect" data and have helped some workers contest erroneous suspensions, but they leave the economic relationship untouched (pp. 1985-1986). She proposes instead "a statutory or regulatory nonwaivable ban on algorithmic wage discrimination", including a ban on digitalized piece pay (p. 1989).

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (p. 1929): based on "a multi-year, first-of-its-kind ethnographic study of organizing on-demand workers". The Article "proposes a nonwaivable restriction on these practices".
- Roadmap (p. 1940): attempts at transparency "may not mitigate the objective or subjective harms". The remedy is "a narrowly structured, nonwaivable peremptory ban on the practice".
- p. 1982: efforts "to gain transparency over—and even to 'reverse engineer'—the labor management structures that produce algorithmic wage discrimination have yet to change firm practices."
- p. 1983: Uber and Ola argued that "the safety and security of their platform may be compromised if the logic of such data processing is disclosed to their workers."
- p. 1984: the Amsterdam appellate court (April 2023) held that Uber "must explain how driver personal data and profiling is used in Uber's upfront, dynamic pay and pricing system."
- p. 1984: "Absent a ban on algorithmic wage discrimination under Article 22 of the GDPR or through collective bargaining agreements, transparency requests are by themselves ineffectual. Knowledge alone will not end or mitigate the precarities of digitalized pay." Also: transparency "does not by itself shift the power dynamics".
- p. 1985: data cooperatives "have been useful for workers who have been able to contest unfair suspensions or terminations based on errors in facial recognition or in geolocation checks", but "most U.S. workers do not have the option to make such contestations".
- p. 1986: "This, then, is primarily a structural problem, not necessarily one that is rooted in control over and legibility of data." Cooperatives do "not fundamentally intervene in the economic relationship".
- p. 1989: "a statutory or regulatory nonwaivable ban on algorithmic wage discrimination, including, but not limited to, a ban on compensation through digitalized piece pay."

## Critical notes from the literature
- AUDIT_05 C3 (M2-24): "ineffectual" is said of transparency requests, conditional on there being no ban or bargaining agreement, and not of knowledge in general. "Knowledge alone will not end or mitigate ..." is the general sentence (`audit/AUDIT_05.md`:253, :296-297, :353).
- AUDIT_05 C2 (M2-19): the "most frequently proposed policy reforms" sentence opens Part III.A on p. 1979, not pp. 1980-1981 (`audit/AUDIT_05.md`:248).
- Facet M2 reads Dubal as conceding that counter-data helps contest errors without changing the relationship, and as making prohibition the stronger remedy. A paper that proposes design duties has to answer that (`facets/M2_algorithm_studies.md`:67, :153, :155, :185).
- Ledger P6-018 separates the two mechanisms. Error-contesting is Dubal's finding about data cooperatives (p. 1985), not about GDPR data requests (p. 1984). "Have yet to change firm practices" (p. 1982) is her assessment of transparency efforts.

Issue 7 rests on R1, not on this copy (`review/v3/ledger_parts/P6.md`:89). The bib DOI resolves to the SSRN record, not the journal.
