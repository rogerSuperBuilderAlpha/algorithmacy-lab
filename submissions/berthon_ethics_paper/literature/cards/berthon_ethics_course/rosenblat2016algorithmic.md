## Summary
Rosenblat and Stark report a nine-month study (December 2014 to September 2015) of Uber drivers, built from
about 1,350 archival items from five driver forums, e-mail with Uber's customer-service representatives (CSRs),
and seven semi-structured interviews (pp. 3759-3760). They argue that Uber, while presenting itself as a neutral
technology intermediary and its drivers as free entrepreneurs, exercises "soft control" through design choices
and information asymmetries (pp. 3758, 3761). Their cases are blind ride acceptance (drivers see neither
destination nor fare before accepting and risk deactivation for cancelling), minimum fares and unilateral rate
cuts (pp. 3762-3765), Uber's claim to adjudicate fare disputes with its own data (p. 3765), surge pricing and
predictive "nudges" (pp. 3765-3771), a narrow communication channel staffed by CSRs who send template replies
(p. 3771), and passenger ratings that act as middle management (pp. 3772-3776). They conclude that the app
produces "the equivalent effects" of a managed labour force while the rhetoric of platforms and algorithms
masks that role (p. 3777). The authors state that forum posters may not represent drivers generally
(p. 3760).

## Key facts it relies on
- Data: five forums, ~1,350 archival items, CSR e-mail, seven interviews; drivers' and forums' names were
  changed (pp. 3759-3760, n. 3).
- Representativeness caveat: forum drivers "may be more strongly opinionated than other drivers" (p. 3760).
- Uber deducts a commission "between 20% and 30% for uberX" plus fees (p. 3760); it calls itself "a technology
  services provider that does not provide transportation service" and frames UK drivers as "customers"
  (p. 3761).
- Drivers "have about 15 seconds to accept it or reject it" and "are not shown destination or fare information
  before they accept a ride" (p. 3762).
- Uber "does not disclose the criteria" for hourly guarantees; selection rests on "opaque criteria"
  (pp. 3763-3764).
- Uber claims its data are "akin to an objective, third-party witness, and thus that its interpretation should
  be conclusive"; drivers "need their own data to hold Uber accountable for the wages they are owed" (p. 3765).
- "Drivers can make inquiries and usually receive template responses, but they are not empowered to negotiate
  the terms of their work"; some CSRs are outsourced to the Philippines or staffing agencies (p. 3771).
- Drivers report being "unable to protest successfully" to remove low ratings (p. 3775).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Muldoon and Raekstad (2023) build their republican account of platform domination partly on this article
  (pp. 3758, 3762, 3763), so the paper's republican frame and this evidence share a lineage (`literature/audit/
  AUDIT_05.md`, M4-27; `literature/facets/M4_ethical_frameworks.md`:80).
- The template-reply passage (p. 3771) concerns inquiries in general, answered by human CSRs, some outsourced. It
  is not about inquiries "about a decision" and says nothing about who holds a record (`review/v3/ledger_parts/
  P1.md`, P1-032; `P4.md`, P4-055).
- My reading: p. 3765 is closer to the "record" claim than p. 3771. There Uber claims its own trip data settle
  disputes "conclusive[ly]", and drivers keep dash-cam footage and logs to contest it. The author may want that
  page for lines 17 and 137 (and as a candidate for the unsourced line 145 flagged at P4-071).
- Scope: US ride-hail, 2014-2015, self-selected forum posters; the authors flag that rates and fees change
  constantly (p. 3762, n. 8).
