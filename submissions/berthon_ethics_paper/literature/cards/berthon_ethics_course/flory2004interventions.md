## Summary
Flory and Emanuel reviewed controlled trials, from MEDLINE 1966 to March 2004, that compared research participants' understanding after a standard consent process with understanding after an intervention. Thirty studies described 42 trials. Multimedia interventions improved understanding in 3 of 12 trials. Enhanced consent forms did so in 6 of 15, but 5 of those 6 were of limited quality. Extended discussion improved understanding significantly in 3 of 5 trials, and the other 2 showed trends. Test/feedback improved understanding in all 5, but may have measured rote memorisation. Lower education was associated with less understanding. No intervention reduced satisfaction or willingness to enrol. The authors conclude that efforts through multimedia and better forms "have had only limited success". Having "a study team member or a neutral educator spend more time talking one-on-one to study participants appears to be the most effective available way" to improve understanding, though they call for more research.

## Key facts it relies on (all from the abstract)
- "Thirty studies described 42 trials that met inclusion criteria."
- "Of 12 trials of multimedia interventions, 3 showed significant improvement in understanding."
- "Of 15 trials of enhanced consent forms, 6 showed significant improvement in understanding (all P<.05), but 5 of 6 trials were of limited quality".
- "Of 5 trials of extended discussion, 3 showed significant improvement in understanding (all P<.001) and 2 showed trends toward improvement (P=.054 and P=.08)."
- Test/feedback: all 5 improved understanding but "may have mistaken rote memorization for improvement in understanding."
- "Some demographic factors, particularly lower education, were associated with less understanding."
- Conclusion: "Having a study team member or a neutral educator spend more time talking one-on-one to study participants appears to be the most effective available way of improving research participants' understanding; however, further research is needed."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Later reviews partly revise the ranking. Nishimura et al. (2013, abstract, BMC Medical Ethics) found enhanced consent forms (SMD 1.73) and extended discussion (SMD 0.53) both significant in meta-analysis. Glaser et al. (2020, abstract) found verbal discussion with teach-back effective in 3 of 3 studies and written interventions in 6 of 14. The direction (interaction beats text) holds across these reviews. The size of the text effect varies.
- The sister arm's verified finding is that disclosure evidence is mixed in direction (`wt-dxd-foundation/.../facets/L_disclosure_and_choice.md`). This review fits that finding. It does not show that people "review badly". It shows that understanding follows interaction more than text.
