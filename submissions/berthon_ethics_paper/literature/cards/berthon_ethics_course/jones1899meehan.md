## Summary
The case concerned title to land reserved for the Chippewa chief Moose Dung by article 9 of the 1863 Old Crossing treaty, and whether he could lease it. Before construing the treaty, Gray J. set out how treaties with Indian tribes must be read. The United States negotiated through representatives "skilled in diplomacy, masters of a written language", with "an interpreter employed by themselves", and drew the treaty "in their own language". The Indians "have no written language" and knew the terms only through "the interpreter employed by the United States". So the treaty "must therefore be construed, not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers, but in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians." The Court supports this with Marshall C.J. in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832). There Marshall noted that every Cherokee chief "makes his mark", that the treaty was probably "interpreted to them", and that people who "could not write, and most probably could not read" could not be held to distinctions such as "allotted" against "marked out". The Court held that the reservation passed an alienable fee title to the chief. The canon is framed through wardship ("pupilage").

## Key facts it relies on
- Syllabus, p. 1: "A treaty between the United States and an Indian tribe must be construed, not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers, but in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians."
- P. 10: Marshall's description that "they are in a state of pupilage" and that "their relation to the United States resembles that of a ward to his guardian" (citing *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*).
- Pp. 10-11: negotiations are conducted by the United States "by representatives skilled in diplomacy, masters of a written language, ... and assisted by an interpreter employed by themselves; that the treaty is drawn up by them and in their own language".
- P. 11: the Indians "are a weak and dependent people, who have no written language and are wholly unfamiliar with all the forms of legal expression, and whose only knowledge of the terms in which the treaty is framed is that imparted to them by the interpreter employed by the United States".
- P. 11, quoting *Worcester*, 6 Pet. at 551-552: "every one makes his mark; no chief was capable of signing his name. It is probable the treaty was interpreted to them."
- P. 11, quoting *Worcester*: "Is it reasonable to suppose that the Indians, who could not write, and most probably could not read, who certainly were not critical judges of our language, should distinguish the word 'allotted' from the words 'marked out'?"
- P. 11, quoting McLean J. in *Worcester*: "The language used in treaties with the Indians should never be construed to their prejudice."
- Decided October 30, 1899 (p. 1); "Mr. Justice Gray delivered the opinion of the court" (p. 2).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The canon is a rule of construction after the fact, and it puts the risk of the text on the drafter. It does not require that the text be understood when signed.
- Its premise is paternalist ("pupilage", "weak and dependent"). It sits alongside later cases that let Congress override treaties. I did not read those, and this card claims nothing about them.
- v3's Waitangi material (`waitangi2014declaration`; facets G and T) shows the same structure from the other side: a treaty in two languages, with the drafter's interpreter supplying the text the signatories heard.
