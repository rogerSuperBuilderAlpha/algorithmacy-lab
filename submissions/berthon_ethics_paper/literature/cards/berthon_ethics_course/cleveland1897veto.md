## Summary
Cleveland returns House bill No. 7864, "An act to amend the immigration laws of the United States", without
approval (para. 1). The bill's first section would exclude "All persons physically capable and over 16 years of
age who can not read and write the English language or some other language", with family exceptions (paras.
2-3); the test is to read and write twenty to twenty-five words of the Constitution (para. 11). He calls it "A
radical departure from our national policy" (para. 4). He answers the case for restriction point by point:
overcrowding is not claimed (para. 7); the charge that recent immigrants are undesirable was once made of those
"now numbered among our best citizens" (para. 8); urban concentration and unemployment have other remedies
(paras. 9-10). It is "infinitely more safe to admit a hundred thousand immigrants who, though unable to read and
write, seek among us only a home and opportunity to work" than one literate "unruly agitator", and literacy is "a
misleading test" and "the pretext for exclusion" (para. 13). He finds the family exceptions inadequate (paras.
14-15) and the fourth and fifth sections, which criminalize habitual cross-border labour, "illiberal, narrow, and
un-American" and vague (paras. 16-23). He closes that the provisions are "unnecessarily harsh and oppressive"
(para. 24).

## Key facts it relies on
- Dated "March 02, 1897"; addressed "To the House of Representatives"; returns "House bill No. 7864" (header,
  para. 1).
- The literacy clause: exclusion of persons over 16 "who can not read and write the English language or some
  other language" (para. 3).
- The test: read and then write "not less than twenty nor more than twenty-five words of the Constitution"
  (para. 11).
- "It is said, however, that the quality of recent immigration is undesirable. The time is quite within recent
  memory when the same thing was said of immigrants who, with their descendants, are now numbered among our best
  citizens." (para. 8.)
- "infinitely more safe to admit a hundred thousand immigrants who, though unable to read and write, seek among
  us only a home and opportunity to work than to admit one of those unruly agitators" (para. 13).
- Literacy "affords ... a misleading test of contented industry"; causes other than illiteracy "should be dealt
  with directly, instead of making illiteracy the pretext for exclusion" (para. 13).
- The veto also rests on the bill's sections 4-5 (border labour), called "illiberal, narrow, and un-American"
  (para. 21).
- The message does not record when Congress passed the bill or what followed the veto.

## Critical notes from the literature
- `literature/facets/D_literacy_as_gate.md:250, 479` quotes the "hundred thousand immigrants" sentence from the
  Immigration Commission's *Abstracts*, vol. 2, pp. 573-574; `AUDIT_01.md:119` (D11) confirmed it there, and
  `AUDIT_01.md:120` (D12) could not find the 2 March date in that passage. The APP page supplies the date.
- `D_literacy_as_gate.md:541`: Cleveland and Wilson both hold that illiterate immigrants are not as such
  undesirable; the Commission (1911) argued oversupply of unskilled labour and assimilability.
- Cleveland's objection is partly prudential: he prefers illiterate labourers to literate "agitators" (para. 13).
  It is a condemnation of the test, not a claim of a right to enter.
- Ledger P3-013 notes the Natal Premier modelled his 1897 bill on this "American Act", the Lodge bill that
  Cleveland vetoed.
