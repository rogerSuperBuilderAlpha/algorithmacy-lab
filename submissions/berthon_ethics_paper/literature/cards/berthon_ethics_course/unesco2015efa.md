## Summary
The Summary of the 2015 EFA Global Monitoring Report assesses whether the world met the six Education for All
goals set at Dakar in 2000, and whether partners kept their commitments. It reports progress on each goal and
finds that most were missed (pp. 5-9). Goal 4, a 50 per cent improvement in adult literacy, was not achieved:
illiteracy fell from 18% to an estimated 14%, and only 17 of 73 countries with literacy below 95% halved their
illiteracy rate (pp. 6-7, 29). The Goal 4 chapter says reductions in illiteracy "partly reflect the entry of
younger, more educated cohorts into adulthood". Cohort analysis shows "almost no real gains in adult literacy
since 2000" in most of 30 countries (pp. 29-30). It attributes slow progress to ambiguous global commitment,
ineffective campaigns, limited mother-tongue programmes and weak demand (pp. 30-31). It also warns that
campaigns portraying illiteracy as a "social illness" to be "eradicated" can stigmatise it (p. 31). The Summary
concludes that EFA was "a qualified success" (p. 13).

## Key facts it relies on
- Goal 4 text: "Achieving a 50 per cent improvement in levels of adult literacy by 2015, especially for women"
  (pp. 6, 29).
- "There are about 781 million illiterate adults. The rate of illiteracy dropped slightly, from 18% in 2000 to an
  estimated 14% in 2015, which means the Dakar target of halving illiteracy was not achieved" (p. 6).
- "Only 17 out of the 73 countries with a literacy rate below 95% in 2000 had halved their illiteracy rate by
  2015" (p. 7; also p. 29, "projected").
- "Reductions in adult illiteracy rates partly reflect the entry of younger, more educated cohorts into
  adulthood, rather than improvement within cohorts of adults who were past school age" (p. 29).
- In Malawi the literacy rate of women aged 20-34 rose from 49% to 63% (2000-2010), but the same cohort "remained
  constant at 49%" (p. 30).
- Campaigns "can raise unrealistic expectations"; their language "often portrays illiteracy as a 'social illness'
  that can be 'eradicated'", which "can stigmatize illiteracy"; campaigns since 2000 "have not had a major impact
  globally" (p. 31).
- "In the end, the EFA movement can be characterized as a qualified success" (p. 13).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet F and the ledger flagged that the quotation rested on a secondary host and that the official PDF should
  be found before print (`literature/facets/F_promise_and_campaigns.md`:208-212; `review/v3/ledger_parts/P4.md`,
  P4-020). The official Summary now confirms the wording at p. 6.
- The same Summary calls EFA "a qualified success" and counts 17 of 73 countries that halved illiteracy; the
  manuscript reports neither (P4-020; F:212).
- The ledger judged that the Summary documents a missed target, not the premise that literacy is a cure
  (P4-059). The official text adds p. 31: the GMR itself criticises the "eradication" language of campaigns.
  That gives line 139 a closer anchor, though the GMR describes the framing as a risk rather than adopting it.
- The Summary's cohort finding (pp. 29-30) cuts in two directions: it supports "the condition persists", and it
  shows that part of the fall in the world rate that line 109 reports is a schooling effect, not adult learning.
