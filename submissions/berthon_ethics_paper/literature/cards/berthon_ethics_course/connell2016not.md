## Summary
Connell asks why *non est factum*, the plea that a signed document is "not my deed", belongs in contract law, and argues that it applies the objective principle of *Smith v Hughes*. Along the way he gives a compact history and a statement of the present law. The plea began as protection for "blind and illiterate persons who, without being careless, relied on another party's seriously flawed explanation of the document". It expanded when "a large proportion of the population was unable to read". Courts then limited it by requiring a fundamental difference between the document and what the signer thought, and no carelessness, which the signer must prove. Lord Wilberforce in *Saunders v Anglia* explained the doctrine as a compromise between protecting innocent third parties who rely on signed documents and relieving signers who did not consent. In *Saunders* the widow Mrs Gallie lost. Her spectacles were broken and she relied on Lee's false explanation, but the document was not radically different and she had been careless. The building society that lent on the deed was protected.

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract, p. 245: "In its early days, non est factum was available only to blind and illiterate persons who, without being careless, relied on another party's seriously flawed explanation of the document."
- P. 249: "The first major expansion of non est factum occurred at a time when a large proportion of the population was unable to read, and transactions involving deeds were commonplace. The plea was extended to blind and illiterate persons who had relied on someone else's explanation of the deed."
- P. 249, n. 18, quoting Lord Reid in *Saunders* [1971] AC 1004 at 1016: the plea should logically extend to those "unable through no fault of their own to have without explanation any real understanding of the purport of a particular document, whether that be from defective education, illness or innate incapacity". Also: "In general I do not think he can be heard to say that he signed in reliance on someone he trusted."
- P. 250, n. 19, quoting Lord Wilberforce at 1025: a "residue of difficult cases" including "illiterate or senile persons who cannot read, or apprehend, a legal document".
- P. 251: the plea "is available only to a signer who has not been careless, and it is up to the signer to prove that they have not been careless" (citing *Saunders* at 1019).
- P. 251: the "compromise explanation" balances "the protection of innocent third parties who rely on the validity of apparently legitimate signed legal documents" against "the desire to provide relief to signers who did not actually consent" (citing *Saunders* at 1023).
- P. 253: Mrs Gallie signed when "her spectacles were broken and she relied on Lee's incorrect explanation". The Lords found she "failed to establish her plea ... both in terms of the document not being radically different from what she thought and because she had been careless."
- P. 253, quoting Viscount Dilhorne: "she cannot relieve herself of the consequences at the expense of the respondents who advanced money on the faith of the document."
