## Summary
Kessler argues that the common law's single, individualist doctrine of contract cannot handle the standardized mass contract. He sees freedom of contract as the legal counterpart of free-enterprise capitalism: the law "delegate[s] legislation to the contracting parties" (pp. 629-630). Mass production made the standard contract inevitable, and it lets firms calculate and exclude risks, including the "juridical risk" of a sympathetic jury (pp. 631-632). Because firms with strong bargaining power use these forms, the weaker party's "contractual intention is but a subjection more or less voluntary to terms dictated by the stronger party". Such contracts are "frequently contracts of adhesion" (p. 632). Courts protect adherents covertly, by "interpretation" against the drafter or, in the insurance-delay cases, by tort liability, while professing that contract law is "of one cloth". The result is confused law (pp. 633-639). Kessler urges courts to ask openly what the weaker party could reasonably expect (p. 637). In his conclusion, freedom of contract lets enterprisers "legislate by contract … in a substantially authoritarian manner", imposing "a new feudal order of their own making" (p. 640). Freedom of contract "must mean different things for different types of contracts", depending on the drafter's degree of monopoly (p. 642).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 629: the law "has to delegate legislation to the contracting parties. As far as they are concerned, the law of contract has to be of their own making."
- p. 631: standard contracts emerged with "mass production and mass distribution"; they let firms "select and control risks".
- p. 632: "Standard contracts are typically used by enterprises with strong bargaining power. The weaker party … is frequently not in a position to shop around for better terms … His contractual intention is but a subjection more or less voluntary to terms dictated by the stronger party, terms whose consequences are often understood only in a vague way, if at all. Thus, standardized contracts are frequently contracts of adhesion; they are à prendre ou à laisser."
- p. 632, n. 11: the term "contract of adhesion" was introduced by Patterson (1919), 33 Harv. L. Rev. 198, 222.
- p. 633: in reinsurance "parties of equal skill and bargaining power are dealing with another". There a standard form is not adhesive.
- p. 637: courts should determine "what the weaker contracting party could legitimately expect by way of services according to the enterpriser's 'calling'".
- p. 640: "Freedom of contract enables enterprisers to legislate by contract and, what is even more important, to legislate in a substantially authoritarian manner without using the appearance of authoritarian forms." Standard contracts could impose "a new feudal order of their own making upon a vast host of vassals".
- p. 642: "freedom of contract must mean different things for different types of contracts. Its meaning must change with the social importance of the type of contract and with the degree of monopoly enjoyed by the author of the standardized contract."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet M3 (l. 145, 302) paired Kessler with Rakoff (1983), "Contracts of Adhesion: An Essay in Reconstruction", as the core adhesion literature a contracts referee expects. Rakoff remains record only in the arm.
- Ledger P5-033 marked the v2 sentence "unsupported" (no citation). Resolution C2 (P5-033) supplied Kessler, p. 632, and the v3 text adds it.
- Kessler's adhesion is a matter of degree and context. Standardized contracts are "frequently", not always, adhesive (p. 632), and reinsurance is his counter-example (p. 633). The adhesive character comes from bargaining power and monopoly, not from standardization alone (p. 642).

contracts of adhesion, standard-form contracts, freedom of contract, bargaining power, insurance law, judicial interpretation against the drafter, tort and contract, private legislation, monopoly, legal realism
