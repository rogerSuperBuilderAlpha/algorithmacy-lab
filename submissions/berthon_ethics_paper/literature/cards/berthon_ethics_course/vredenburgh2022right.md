## Summary
Vredenburgh argues, on an interest-based theory of rights, that people subject to opaque institutions, algorithmic
or bureaucratic, have a right to explanation grounded in "informed self-advocacy": "a cluster of abilities to
represent one's interests and values to decision-makers and to further those interests and values within an
institution" (AAM p. 5). Without it a person cannot conform to rules or contest mistaken decisions, and people
"can reasonably reject institutional set-ups" that deny it (AAM p. 5). She proposes rule-based and
population-level causal explanations. She then concedes intelligibility: "It is neither realistic nor desirable
to expect individuals to use explanations to engage in informed self-advocacy within all the institutions that
affect them, absent support", so "Individuals require epistemic support" (AAM p. 18). Rights holders "should be
provided with access to free experts and advocates", like welfare caseworkers who once explained a "failure to
cooperate" notice (AAM p. 19); free experts "could be AI systems designed to generate explanations in response to
user queries" (AAM p. 21). Her conclusion: "states and companies may need to invest more resources in providing
individuals with free experts – human or algorithmic" (AAM p. 22).

## Key facts it relies on
- Definition of informed self-advocacy (AAM p. 5).
- Explanation unusable "absent support"; "Individuals require epistemic support in order to engage in tolerably costly informed self-advocacy" (AAM p. 18).
- "Rights holders should be provided with access to free experts and advocates" (AAM p. 19), with Eubanks's Indiana welfare example.
- "knowledge hoarding is a mechanism by which dominant group members maintain a monopoly on positions of power" (AAM p. 19).
- "free experts could be AI systems designed to generate explanations in response to user queries" (AAM p. 21).
- "states and companies may need to invest more resources in providing individuals with free experts" (AAM p. 22).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Grote and Paulo (2025, abstract) argue her explanations are "intellectually over-demanding" for laypersons and shift the burden of scrutiny to them, and propose public trust as the goal instead. Cordasco and Véliz (2026, JBE, abstract, in N5) argue the self-advocacy interest cannot by itself ground a general right.
- Her remedy is an intermediary expert; it does not aim to build the person's own competence (facet V6, Q1c).
