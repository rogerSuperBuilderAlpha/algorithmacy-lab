## Summary
Kim and Routledge seek a philosophical foundation for the claim that algorithmic decision-making "gives rise
to a 'right to explanation'" (p. 75). They start from the GDPR debate over whether such a legal right exists
(p. 76) and set it aside to ask what would justify a moral right. The usual view treats informed consent as a
static, complete transaction, which machine learning defeats: data are used in "secondary, noncontextual, and
unpredictable" ways that cannot be explained in advance (p. 75). They reconceive informed consent "as an
assurance of trust for incomplete transactions" (p. 75). Their argument runs in two premises (p. 77): consent
in algorithmic contexts is incomplete without an assurance of trust, and a firm's readiness to give ex post
explanations uniquely provides that assurance. A right to ex post explanation therefore "uniquely helps to
complete informed consent". The trustors are "data subjects (users, employees, or applicants)" who consent
through terms of service (p. 88). In replies to objections, they compare the firm to a physician as trustee
(p. 99) and to a board owing fidelity to shareholders (pp. 99-100). They concede that the right cannot
"fundamentally solve" surveillance and manipulation built into business models. It is only "a useful starting
point" (p. 101).

## Key facts it relies on
- The aim is "a philosophical foundation for the claim that algorithmic decision-making gives rise to a 'right
  to explanation'" (p. 75).
- Informed consent reconceived "as an assurance of trust for incomplete transactions" entails "a right to ex
  post explanation" (p. 75).
- Premise 1: consent "is incomplete without an assurance of trust"; Premise 2: readiness to respect ex post
  explanation "is unique in helping give an assurance (or evidence) of trust to users" (p. 77).
- Under the GDPR reading, data subjects include "service users, customers, employees, or applicants" (p. 76).
- Data subjects "can reasonably place trust in data processing companies only when the relationship is
  predicated on the companies' readiness/commitment to be accountable" (p. 88).
- Consent is sought from "data subjects (users, employees, or applicants through a 'terms of service and
  privacy policy')" (p. 88).
- "When data subjects entrust data processing firms with the management of their personal information, firms
  likewise become trustees" (p. 99).
- "We do not believe that a right to ex post explanation can fundamentally solve such problems" (p. 101).

## Critical notes from the literature
- R2 BEQ referee report (`review/R2_beq_referee.md:234-241`): an earlier draft misread the paper as resting on
  autonomy restoration; the ground is trust via informed consent, and a critique must meet that ground.
- Facet N5 (`literature/facets/N5_prior_work_gap.md:100-101`) records Cordasco and Véliz (2026, *JBE*,
  abstract only): explanations for self-advocacy must be "reliable" and "verifiable (open to independent
  check)", and that interest "cannot by itself ground a general right".
- Facet N2 (`literature/facets/N2_platform_law.md:97-109`): the CJEU in Case C-203/22, *Dun & Bradstreet
  Austria* (27 February 2025), para. 57, held that GDPR art. 15(1)(h) gives "a genuine right to an
  explanation". The legal debate Kim and Routledge set aside (p. 76) has since moved.
- The authors' own limit (p. 101) supports the manuscript's next question: explanation lets a user "alter the
  level of trust", not change the decision.
- Ledger P5-001 rated line 153 "unsupported" on abstract depth only; this card reads the full text, which
  names the trustors (p. 88).
