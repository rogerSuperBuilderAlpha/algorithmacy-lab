## Summary

Milton addresses the Parliament of England and asks it to revoke its Order regulating printing, which required that "no book, pamphlet, or paper shall be henceforth printed, unless the same be first approved and licensed". He leaves alone the Order's provisions that protect each man's copy and provide for the poor. He traces licensing to its "inventors". The Popes extended their dominion "over men's eyes" by prohibiting books, and "the Council of Trent and the Spanish Inquisition engendering together brought forth, or perfected, those Catalogues and expurging Indexes". Their "last invention" was to license every book before printing. He argues that licensing insults the nation's learning and treats knowledge as a staple to be stamped "like our broadcloth and our woolpacks". In practice it is a servitude like being sent to "twenty licensing forges". He proposes instead the earlier rule that a book carry its printer's and author's names, with punishment after publication. He suspects that the Order owed its passage to "the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of bookselling". His tolerance has limits: "popery, and open superstition" is to be "extirpate". The Gutenberg text carries no date and no page numbers. I have not collated it against the 1644 edition.

## Key facts it relies on

- The Order Milton attacks: "that no book, pamphlet, or paper shall be henceforth printed, unless the same be first approved and licensed by such, or at least one of such, as shall be thereto appointed" (Licensing Order passage).
- He exempts the parts that preserve "every man's copy to himself, or provides for the poor" (same passage).
- "the Council of Trent and the Spanish Inquisition engendering together brought forth, or perfected, those Catalogues and expurging Indexes, that rake through the entrails of many an old good author" (history of licensing).
- "Truth and understanding are not such wares as to be monopolized and traded in by tickets and statutes and standards. We must not think to make a staple commodity of all the knowledge in the land, to mark and licence it like our broadcloth and our woolpacks" ("twenty licensing forges" paragraph).
- His alternative: the earlier Order "that no book be printed, unless the printer's and the author's name, or at least the printer's, be registered" (closing passage).
- "it may be doubted there was in it the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of bookselling; who under pretence of the poor in their Company not to be defrauded ... brought divers glossing colours to the House" (closing passage).
- The limit of his tolerance: "popery, and open superstition ... should be extirpate" (near the close).
