## Summary

A consumer, OQ, was refused a loan after SCHUFA, a German credit information agency, passed a negative score to the lender. SCHUFA told her the score and outlined its method but, citing trade secrecy, refused to disclose the elements and their weighting, and said its contractual partners made the actual decisions (paras 14–16). The supervisory authority rejected her complaint, and the Wiesbaden Administrative Court asked whether the agency's automated scoring is itself a "decision" under Article 22(1) GDPR (paras 17–27). The Court held that it is, where the third party "draws strongly" on the score to establish, implement or terminate a contract (para. 73; operative part). Article 22(1) confers a right that "lays down a prohibition in principle" (para. 52). Automated decisions are permitted only under the three exceptions in Article 22(2), with safeguards, including human intervention, attached to the contract and consent exceptions (paras 53–54, 64). In a three-party case, a restrictive reading that treats the score as preparatory would let the scorer escape Article 22 and leave the data subject unable to obtain the logic from anyone (paras 61–63). National law relying on Article 22(2)(b) must also satisfy Articles 5 and 6 (paras 65–72).

## Key facts it relies on

- Para. 16: SCHUFA "informed OQ of her score and outlined, in broad terms, the methods", but "referring to trade secrecy, it refused to disclose the various elements taken into account … and their weighting", and said "it was those contractual partners which made the actual contractual decisions."
- Para. 43: three cumulative conditions for Article 22(1): a "decision", "based solely on automated processing, including profiling", producing legal or similarly significant effects.
- Paras 46, 48–50: "decision" is broad enough to cover a creditworthiness score; an insufficient score leads "in almost all cases" to refusal, so the score's establishment is itself a decision with significant effects.
- Para. 52: Article 22(1) "lays down a prohibition in principle, the infringement of which does not need to be invoked individually by such a person."
- Paras 53–54: automated decisions are authorised only in the three Article 22(2) cases; "In the cases referred to in points (a) and (c) … the data controller is to implement at least the right of the data subject to obtain human intervention, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision."
- Para. 61: "in circumstances such as those at issue in the main proceedings, in which three stakeholders are involved, there would be a risk of circumventing Article 22 … and, consequently, a lacuna in legal protection" if only the lender's act counted as a decision.
- Para. 63: the third party "would not be able to provide that specific information because it generally does not have it."
- Para. 66: safeguards include "at least the right for the data subject to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to challenge the decision."
- Operative part: automated scoring by a credit information agency "constitutes 'automated individual decision-making'" where a third party "draws strongly on that probability value to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship."

## Critical notes from the literature

- Facet N2 (`literature/facets/N2_platform_law.md`, l. 62, 99, 265) reads SCHUFA as a strong legal statement of the paper's triad (the intermediary's reading is the decision) and as a challenge to any claim that no rule stands at the point of decision: Article 22(1) now prohibits ex ante. The facet adds that the rule is enforced after the fact by supervisory authorities and courts, and that the human it guarantees is still "on the part of the controller" (para. 66).
- Facet N2 (l. 181–185): Wachter, Mittelstadt and Floridi (2017) read the earlier German SCHUFA judgments as a "loophole"; this judgment (para. 52) and *Dun & Bradstreet Austria* have since gone the other way on prohibition versus objection.
- The holding is conditional. It applies where the third party "draws strongly" on the score, a factual question left to the referring court (paras 48, 72), and whether German § 31 BDSG supplies an Article 22(2)(b) basis was also left to that court (paras 71–72).
- The Court did not answer the second question (para. 74).

## Where the manuscript uses it

- **Line 167**, "The General Data Protection Regulation gives a person a right not to be subject to a solely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects and, where such a decision rests on a contract or her explicit consent, the right to obtain human intervention and to contest it (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 22(1)–(3); on a credit bureau's score, *OQ v Land Hessen*, 2023, paras. 52, 61)." Takes the reading of Article 22(1) as a right and prohibition, and its extension to an intermediary's score in a three-party case. Paras 52 and 61; paras 53–54 confirm the contract and consent conditions for the human-intervention safeguard. **holds**: para. 52 states the prohibition in principle and para. 61 applies Article 22 to the scorer where three stakeholders are involved. The Court's holding requires that the lender "draws strongly" on the score; the manuscript's parenthesis does not state that condition, though it does not contradict it.
