## Summary
Martin argues that algorithms are value-laden: they "create moral consequences, reinforce or undercut ethical
principles, and enable or diminish stakeholder rights and dignity", and they shape "the delegation of roles and
responsibilities" in a decision (abstract, p. 835). The current conversation "absolves firms of responsibility":
developers call algorithms neutral, users say they cannot understand them (p. 836). Against the view that
transparency is a precondition of accountability, she attributes accountability "regardless of the level of
algorithmic transparency designed"; "'It's complicated' or 'I do not know how it works' turns out to be an
unsatisfying response" (p. 837). Because the firm "is knowledgeable as to the design decisions and is in a unique
position to inscribe the algorithm with the value-laden biases as well as roles and responsibilities", it is
responsible for designing "who-does-what" (p. 844). Inscrutable design "precludes users from taking
responsibility" and so raises the designer's accountability (p. 844). Conversely, if the algorithm's role is minimised "by providing tools to allow users to revisit
how the algorithm works", the firm "would have more of a traditional handoff of a product with associated (minimal)
responsibility" (p. 845). Transparency "may be neither feasible nor desirable" in full and should be targeted to
purpose (p. 846).

## Key facts it relies on
- Firms "accountable for designing how large a role individual will be permitted to take in the subsequent algorithmic decision" (abstract, p. 835).
- "if an algorithm is designed to preclude individuals from taking responsibility within a decision, then the designer of the algorithm should be held accountable" (abstract, p. 835).
- Users "claim algorithms are difficult to identify let alone understand, therefore excluding users of any culpability" (p. 836).
- "The design of the algorithm not only scripts what users can do but also the reasonable expectations of users to take responsibility" (p. 844).
- Grounds: the firm's knowledge and "unique position" (p. 844); willing entry into the decision context (p. 844, via N4).
- Tools that let users revisit the algorithm reduce the firm's responsibility toward "(minimal)" product liability (p. 845).
- "full transparency may be neither feasible nor desirable"; gaming risk; transparency "targeted for a specific purpose" (p. 846).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Her "users" are the deploying decision-makers (e.g. judges using COMPAS), not the people decided about (p. 844); N4 notes she does not use Young.
- N5 (§3b) holds that the paper's operator duties extend Martin's who-does-what to the affected parties' roles.
- V6 (O3) notes that on Martin's own model, scaffolding users' understanding shifts accountability to them (p. 845): a JBE source for the responsibilization objection.
