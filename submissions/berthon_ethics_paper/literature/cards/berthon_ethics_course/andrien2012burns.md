## Summary
A two-page review of Burns's *Into the Archive* (Duke, 2010). This card rests on the review, not on Burns's book, which the arm has not opened. Andrien reports that Burns "historicizes" the colonial archives of Cuzco in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries by studying notaries as legal intermediaries who turned clients' needs into notarised instruments. Against Lockhart's generation, which read notarial records as a plain view of social practice, Burns argues that notary offices were "sites of negotiation, where the greed and trickery of both notaries and their clients" shaped the record (p. 144). Notaries had a reputation as "rogues and scoundrels"; the Crown regulated them with approved forms and manuals, sold the offices, and the notaries left routine work to assistants (pp. 144–145). They used fake contracts (*confianzas*) and counter-protests (*exclamaciones*), and "could manipulate the legal record in the service of a powerful client or on their own account" (p. 145). Andrien finds Burns convincing on the record's lack of transparency but weak on how two centuries of change altered notarial work (pp. 144–145).

## Key facts it relies on
- Book: Burns, *Into the Archive*, Durham: Duke University Press, 2010, 247 pp. (p. 144).
- Scope: Cuzco's colonial archives, sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (p. 144).
- Notary offices were "sites of negotiation, where the greed and trickery of both notaries and their clients helped to shape the production of notary account books (protocolos)" (p. 144).
- Notaries as "ventriloquists" with a reputation "as rogues and scoundrels" (p. 144, citing Burns p. 2).
- The Crown regulated notaries with "approved documentary forms and procedures"; self-help manuals appeared by the mid-sixteenth century (p. 144).
- The Crown sold notarial offices and allowed transfer to family or associates for a fee (p. 145).
- "notaries could manipulate the legal record in the service of a powerful client or on their own account to shelter assets, disguise debts, sell merchandise, control land, or engage in a host of other shady ventures" (p. 145).
- Burns warns against assuming that "indigenous notaries spoke for their communities" (p. 145, quoting Burns p. 145).
