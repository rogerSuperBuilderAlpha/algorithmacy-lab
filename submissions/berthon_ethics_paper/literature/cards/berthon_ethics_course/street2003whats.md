## Summary
Street surveys recent critiques of the New Literacy Studies (NLS) and what they mean for practice. He restates
NLS's founding distinction (p. 77). The "autonomous" model assumes that literacy in itself has effects on
cognition, economic prospects and citizenship "regardless of the social and economic conditions" behind
illiteracy, and so disguises cultural and ideological assumptions as neutral and universal. The "ideological"
model treats literacy as a social practice, always contested and rooted in relations of power (pp. 77-78). He
then answers theoretical critiques. To Brandt and Clinton's "limits of the local" he replies that "distant"
literacies are hegemonic, not autonomous (pp. 79-80). He also takes up Collins and Blot, and Bartlett and
Holland (pp. 80-82). He turns to applications in schooling, numeracy and development, including the Community
Literacies Project Nepal, and notes that the more ethnographers show literacy lacks the effects "the rhetoric
has suggested", the harder it is to fund programmes (pp. 82-85). He criticises the policy turn to "scientific"
evidence and systematic reviews (pp. 85-87). He concludes that NLS must keep contesting what counts as literacy
while linking it to wider social theory (p. 87).

## Key facts it relies on
- NLS asks "whose literacies" are dominant and whose are marginalised (p. 77).
- The autonomous model: "Introducing literacy to poor, 'illiterate' people, villages, urban youth etc. will have
  the effect of enhancing their cognitive skills, improving their economic prospects, making them better
  citizens, regardless of the social and economic conditions that accounted for their 'illiteracy' in the first
  place. I refer to this as an 'autonomous' model of literacy" (p. 77).
- The model "disguises the cultural and ideological assumptions that underpin it" (p. 77).
- Particular versions of literacy reflect "a desire for that view of literacy to dominate and to marginalize
  others" (p. 78).
- "Distant" literacies are no more autonomous than local ones, "only 'distant', 'new', or hegemonic" (p. 80).
- Ethnographers show literacy "does not necessarily have the effects that the rhetoric has suggested--improved
  health, cognition, empowerment" (p. 85).
- Critique of "evidence-based" literacy policy (NRP, What Works Clearinghouse, National Literacy Strategy)
  (pp. 86-87).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Street's own critics, reported in this article: Brandt and Clinton say NLS "veers too far in a reactive
  direction" and that literacy practices "are not typically invented by their practitioners"; Collins and Blot
  call ethnographic cases "necessary but insufficient" (pp. 79-81). The quotations are second-hand
  (`literature/facets/M1_literacy_studies.md`:82-83, 177).
- A cut against the paper: Street's remedy is to study literacies as local social practices; the paper's is a
  design duty on the intermediary. A Street-trained referee may ask whether "algorithmacy", a competence noun,
  is itself an autonomous-model term (M1:95, 211). The manuscript's line 33 addresses this directly.
- The 1984 book behind the distinction was not opened; Street's claim reaches the arm through this article,
  Horner and Vee (M1:90).
