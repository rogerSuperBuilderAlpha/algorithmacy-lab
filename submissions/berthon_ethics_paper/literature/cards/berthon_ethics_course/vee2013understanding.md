## Summary
Vee argues that computer programming should be understood as "computational literacy" and that literacy
studies can explain its social, technical and cultural dynamics (abstract, p. 42). She defines a literacy as
facility with an infrastructural symbolic technology that is "necessary for everyday life"; by that test
programming is still a "material intelligence," not yet a literacy, but is becoming one as code becomes
infrastructural (pp. 44-46). Her history has two transitions (p. 49). In medieval England, texts entered
government, contract law and publishing, land law came to favor written contracts over witnesses (p. 50), and
"those who could not read text began to be recast as 'illiterate' and power began to shift towards those who
could" (p. 51). In the long nineteenth century, institutions "were built on the assumption that a majority of
citizens could read and write" (p. 49), and literacy created demand for more literacy (p. 52). Code, she
argues, follows the same path from government to business to domestic life (pp. 50-51). Citing Graff, she
grants that literacy alone does not lift people out of poverty, but holds that illiteracy impedes people where
text is infrastructural; so too with code, where "the power balance is once again shifting toward those who are
skilled" (pp. 54-55). She concludes that writing teachers should bring programming into composition and widen
"transformative access" to it beyond computer science's narrow identities (pp. 55-60).

## Key facts it relies on
- The programming-literacy parallel dates to the 1960s (Perlis, Kemeny); van Rossum's 1999 DARPA application
  compared "mass ability to read and write software with mass literacy" (epigraph and p. 43).
- Vee reports Cynthia Selfe as calling the invocation of literacy "rhetorically opportunistic because it is a
  trigger for funding," and argues it is "more than just rhetorical flourish" (p. 43).
- Programming is "a material intelligence, not yet a literacy" (p. 46).
- Interfaces built for consumption mean "the programmers and software designers (or the companies they work
  for) still call the shots" (p. 49).
- Two transitions: medieval England, and the long nineteenth century when institutions "were built on the
  assumption that a majority of citizens could read and write" (p. 49).
- By the late 13th century "land laws had begun to favor written contracts over personal witnesses" (p. 50).
- "power began to shift towards those who could [read]" (p. 51).
- Graff: possessing literacy does not, by itself, lift people out of low income or class (p. 54).
- "the power balance is once again shifting toward those who are skilled in this new literacy technology"
  (p. 55, PDF p. 14; PDF p. n = printed 41+n).
- Remedy: integrate computational literacy into writing courses; seek "transformative access" (Banks)
  (pp. 55, 59).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet M1 (`literature/facets/M1_literacy_studies.md:104-105`) judges that Vee pre-empts the claim that the
  historical comparison is new and that her parallel "is institutional, not only about promises." Her book
  *Coding Literacy* (2017) was not opened (metadata and Byrd's review only).
- AUDIT_05 (`literature/audit/AUDIT_05.md:62, 192`): the contrast "Vee sees power shifting to the skilled; the
  paper sees it shifting to position-holders" is "too tidy," because Vee's pp. 49 and 54 name programmers,
  designers and firms. The real difference is that Vee does not treat the position as a wrong.
- AUDIT_05 M1-20 (`:57`) corrected an earlier misattribution: "rhetorically opportunistic" is Selfe's
  characterization as Vee reports it, not Vee's own.
- Gap search (`literature/facets/Z_gap_search.md:318`) names Kamperman and Graff (2026), not Vee, as the
  nearest candidate precedent.
