## Summary
Cross, an American historian of England, surveys benefit of clergy from its medieval origin to its abolition, with most space on the eighteenth century. Originally limited to ordained clerks, the privilege came after a 1350 ordinance to mean "all who could read", tested by a Psalter verse, usually Psalm 51, the "neck-verse" (p. 552). A statute of Henry VII branded lay readers and limited them to one claim (p. 552); peers were admitted "though he cannot read" (p. 553); women were admitted under William and Mary (p. 554). He gives the 1640 petition of Osmund Gibbs, whose judge barred prompting and set him "one of the hardest verses" (p. 554), and Jeaffreson's Middlesex figures showing the share of capital convicts who could read and plead clergy rising from 8.54 per cent under Edward VI to 38.83 per cent under James I (p. 555). Parliament removed the reading condition under Anne, which Cross presents as a response to "the rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered criminals" (p. 555). The rest of the article, which I skimmed, traces how clergy and the punishments substituted for it (among them the house of correction, p. 556) shaped the eighteenth-century capital code.

## Key facts it relies on
- P. 552: "This was apparently interpreted to mean all who could read … A verse in the Psalter was commonly selected (usually the 51st Psalm) which came to be known as the 'neck-verse', and if the accused was able to 'read like a clerk' he was handed over to the ordinary, though it was an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read that he might claim his clergy."
- P. 552: the Henry VII statute (n. 39: "4 Hen. VII., c. 13"), dated by Cross "in 1487", branding lay readers "with an M on the brawn of his left thumb" for murder, "for any other felony with a T".
- P. 553: peers to be held as clerks convict "'though he cannot read'"; n. 47 (Pike, printed at the foot of p. 554): "a Peer who could or who could not read might still have robbed one church or committed one highway robbery with impunity, though an ignorant peasant would have been hanged."
- P. 554: Gibbs (1640) "was found guilty and put to read for his life. Petitioner desired to read the Psalm of Mercy, but Farwell so incensed the judge against him, that there was not only a clear bar made to prevent promptings, but the judge turned him unto one of the hardest verses to read".
- P. 555: "8.54 per cent. out of 117 convicted of capital offenses could read and pleaded their clergy: in the reign of James I. there was an amazing increase to 38.83 per cent. out of 1725 cases."
- P. 555: "At length Parliament came to realize the rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered criminals, so in the reign of Anne the reading test was taken away"; the Act: "shall not be required to read, but without any reading shall be allowed, taken and reported to be and punished as a clerk convict." N. 55: "6 Anne, c. 9, s. 4" in *Statutes of the Realm*, "usually cited as 5 Anne, c. 6".

## Critical notes from the literature
- Cross gives no source in the text I read for the claim that teaching a felon to read was indictable (p. 552); treat it as Cross's assertion until a primary source is found.
- Cross dates 4 Hen. VII c. 13 to 1487; *Statutes of the Realm* vol. 2 p. 538 heads the page "A.D. 1488-9", and the volume's note on the Parliament (p. 524) says chapters X-XXIV were passed in the session of 25 January-27 February 1489/90, so c. 13 dates to early 1490 (new style). Cross's 1487 is wrong on either reading (`statute1489clergy`).
- "Rank injustice" is Cross's 1917 characterisation of Parliament's reasoning, not a quotation from the Act or the debates; Blackstone gives the ground as learning being "no extenuations of guilt" (facet A).
- Benefit of clergy was a mercy literacy unlocked, not a penalty on illiteracy (facet A); the wrong is unequal mercy.

## Second-pass notes (2026-10-15)
- **No note on the sentence.** Re-read p. 552 with its footnotes (local PDF, sha256 unchanged). The clause "though it was an indictable oYfense [offense] at common law to teach a felon to read that he might claim his clergy" closes a sentence that carries no note number. The notes nearest it are n. 37 (after "such grew to be the usage of the courts") and n. 38 (after the next sentence, on Henry VI). N. 37 reads: "However, one judge as late as 1352 held that tonsure was necessary for a successful assertion of benefit of clergy, though the ordinary was willing to go further. Pike, History of Crime, I. 300. Cf. Makower, Church of England, p. 403, note 32." N. 38: "Ibid., I. 460; Makower, p. 405; Pike, House of Lords, p. 261".
- **Probable source: Pike (1873), vol. 1, p. 300**, the page Cross cites in n. 37. I read it in the public-domain scan (archive.org `historyofcrimein01pikeuoft`, djvu text; local `berthon_ethics_local/pdfs/v4thin/pike/pike1.txt`, sha256 9e6fe8b5…). On p. 300, directly after the 1352 tonsure case Cross also takes from him, Pike writes: "If not already, it was very soon afterwards a part of the duty of the judges to enquire whether gaolers had aided prisoners to gain a knowledge of letters, and to punish them when detected in the offence." On p. 301 he adds that tenants of church lands "were taught to read in prison and claimed at the trial". Pike's note (vol. 1, p. 483, "References and Notes" to ch. IV) gives the authority: "the 'Liber Assisarum,' 26 Edward III., p. 122 ... the same book, p. 138, where the judges are instructed (Article 11) to enquire what gaolers had aided their prisoners to learn letters".
- **What this changes.** Pike's claim is narrower than Cross's: an article of inquiry, from the 1350s, directing judges to find and punish *gaolers* who helped prisoners learn letters. Cross generalises it to "an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read". I have not read the Liber Assisarum (26 Edw. III) itself, so the primary record is still unseen. Stephen (1883, vol. 1, ch. XIII; archive.org `historyofcrimina01stepuoft`) has no such claim (full-text search for "teach", "taught", "indict" near "read"/"clergy").
- v4 line 73 ("Cross records, without a source, that ..."): **narrower than accurate** — Cross gives no note *at that sentence*, but the adjacent note cites Pike, whose p. 300 is the likely source and says something narrower. v4 line 93 ("if Cross's unsourced report is right that teaching a felon to read for his clergy was itself an offence"): **narrower**; see `v4/V4_THIN_SOURCES.md` for the smallest corrections.
