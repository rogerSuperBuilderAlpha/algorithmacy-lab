## Summary
Washington's autobiography, first serialized in the *Outlook* (Preface) and published by Doubleday, Page in
1901, narrates his life from slavery in Virginia to the founding of Tuskegee. I read chapter II, "Boyhood Days"
(pp. 23-32 of the 1901 printing). It opens "After the coming of freedom" (p. 23) and describes the freed
family's move to Malden, West Virginia, his work in a salt furnace, where he first learned a figure from the
barrel mark "18" (pp. 26-27), and his "intense longing to learn to read" with a Webster "blue-back" speller
(p. 27). The community hires and boards a teacher for its first school (p. 29). Washington then generalizes:
"This experience of a whole race beginning to go to school for the first time" (p. 29) was one in which "it was
a whole race trying to go to school. Few were too young, and none too old" (p. 30); the older people hoped
"to learn to read the Bible before they died" (p. 30). He describes night school and moving the furnace clock
to reach day school on time (pp. 31-32). The text is a retrospective memoir by a school founder, written
decades after the events, and the passage I read gives no year.

**Edition question resolved.** The page cited in the arm's record came from the 1937 Sun Dial printing
(`washington1937sundial_upfromslaveryaut00wash.txt`, imprint "THE SUN DIAL PRESS, INC. ... 1937", lines
76-86). This session I opened the 1901 Doubleday, Page scan (title page: "NEW YORK / DOUBLEDAY, PAGE & CO. /
1901", "Copyright, 1900, 1901"). The 1937 printing reproduces the 1901 page breaks here: in both, the head
"30 UP FROM SLAVERY" falls directly before "can form any exact idea ... As I have stated, it was a whole race
trying to go to school." So p. 30 is correct for the 1901 Doubleday, Page edition that the manuscript cites.

## Key facts it relies on
- Chapter II opens "After the coming of freedom", fixing the passage after emancipation (p. 23).
- The freed community organizes and pays for its own first school; each family pays a monthly sum and the
  teacher boards round (p. 29).
- "This experience of a whole race beginning to go to school for the first time, presents one of the most
  interesting studies ..." (p. 29).
- "As I have stated, it was a whole race trying to go to school. Few were too young, and none too old, to make
  the attempt to learn." (p. 30.)
- Day schools and night schools filled; older people hoped "to learn to read the Bible before they died"
  (p. 30).
- His stepfather kept him at the furnace, and he got lessons at night and later day school for a few months
  (pp. 30-31).
- Imprint: Doubleday, Page & Co., New York, 1901; copyright 1900, 1901 (title page and verso of the 1901 scan).
