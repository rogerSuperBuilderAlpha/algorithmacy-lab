## Summary
This card rests entirely on secondary texts; the book was not opened. As Lovett's entry reports it, Pettit
develops the republican conception of freedom as the condition of not being subject to the arbitrary or
uncontrolled power of another, and treats domination as the capacity for such interference whether or not it
is exercised (SEP §1.2). Two secondary sources give the book's core definition with a page: someone dominates
another to the extent that "(1) they have the capacity to interfere (2) on an arbitrary basis (3) in certain
choices that the other is in a position to make" (Gädeke 2020, quoting Pettit 1997b, p. 52; O'Shea 2019, p. 3,
n. 8, "Pettit, Republicanism, 52"). Lovett reports Pettit (1997, 73–4) arguing that a benevolent master leaves
one reasonably secure against interference "but it is to have a master nonetheless" (SEP §2.1). On institutions,
Lovett reports Pettit (1997, 186–7) requiring that decision-makers give reasons, that contestation be open to
all, and that there be impartial "courts of appeal" (SEP §4.2, per facet N1). Facet N1 also records p. 62, via
Lindblom (2019, p. 199): domination holds "whether or not it was consensual in origin".

## Key facts it relies on
- Freedom as non-domination: "a person or group enjoys freedom to the extent that no other person or group has
  'the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis'" (SEP §1.2, citing Pettit **1999**, p. 165;
  1997 given only as "cf.").
- Book's definition of domination, p. 52, three clauses: capacity to interfere, on an arbitrary basis, in
  certain choices (Gädeke 2020, n. 3; O'Shea 2019, n. 8).
- Domination without interference: the slave lacks freedom "because he is vulnerable to the arbitrary power of
  his master; whether his master happens to exercise that power is neither here nor there" (SEP §1.3, Lovett's
  words).
- Benevolent master, pp. 73–4: "to have a master with an exceptionally benevolent disposition is to be
  reasonably secure ... but it is to have a master nonetheless" (SEP §2.1, Lovett paraphrasing Pettit 1997).
- Consent no defence, p. 62: "Whether a relationship sprang originally from a contract or not, whether or not
  it was consensual in origin, ..." (as quoted in Lindblom 2019, p. 199; verifier opened `lindblom2018.txt` and confirmed the
  quotation, with Pettit's "(1997, 62)", on p. 199).
- Contestation, pp. 186–7: reasons, equal openness, impartial "courts of appeal" (SEP §4.2, per facet N1:28).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet N1 (`literature/facets/N1_republican_domination.md:22`, item 1a) found the year error and recommends
  either citing "Pettit, 1999, p. 165, as quoted in Lovett, 2026" and adding the chapter, or finding the wording
  in the 1997 book. The 1997 p. 52 definition is close in sense but different in wording (it has "certain
  choices", not "their affairs").
- O'Shea (2019, p. 3) notes that "arbitrary" is "the most slippery term" and that Pettit later preferred
  "exposure to another's power of uncontrolled interference" to avoid its connotations.
- Lovett's entry records the rival view that non-domination is just secure non-interference (Goodin 2003;
  Carter 2008; Kramer 2008; Moen 2024), which Pettit (1997, 73–4) is cited against (SEP §2.1).
- Facet N1 (item 1c) warns that the manuscript elsewhere merges the procedural (Lovett) and democratic (Pettit)
  accounts of what makes power non-arbitrary; that bears on §5.1, not on this card's citing line.
- Facet N1 (item 1d) supplies primary Pettit wording for the kindly master from Pettit (2006, p. 134), "the
  slave of the rich and kindly master may enjoy an enormous range of choice without having any social freedom
  whatsoever", the one Pettit primary text the arm opened.
