## Summary
An essay, from the 2015 McClatchy Lecture, that seeks to reconcile privacy regulation of data businesses with the First Amendment. Balkin argues that "many online service providers and cloud companies who collect, analyze, use, sell, and distribute personal information should be seen as information fiduciaries toward their customers and end-users" (p. 1186). Like doctors and lawyers, fiduciaries owe a duty of care and, "in many ways more important", a duty of loyalty, including a duty not to create conflicts of interest (pp. 1207–1208). Because the obligation arises from a relationship of trust, not from the content of speech, the First Amendment permits more regulation of information fiduciaries than of others (pp. 1186, 1209). Balkin then narrows the claim: the analogy is "not an identity", and the duties of companies such as Uber, Facebook and Google "may be different and sometimes considerably narrower" than those of traditional professionals (p. 1225). They may monetise data, but may not use it "in unexpected ways to the disadvantage" of users (p. 1227). In the "Algorithmic Society", where firms use data to decide about people who are not their end-users, the fiduciary model "can only take us so far" (pp. 1232–1234).

## Key facts it relies on
- Thesis: online service providers and cloud companies "should be seen as information fiduciaries toward their customers and end-users" (p. 1186).
- "Generally speaking, a fiduciary is one who has special obligations of loyalty and trustworthiness toward another person" (p. 1207).
- "Fiduciaries have two basic duties. The first is a duty of care. ... The second, and in many ways more important duty, is the duty of loyalty" (pp. 1207–1208).
- Definition: "An information fiduciary is a person or business who, because of their relationship with another, has taken on special duties with respect to the information they obtain in the course of the relationship" (p. 1209).
- The new relationships "may not require the same degree of obligation, loyalty, and protection that applied to older forms of professional relationships" (p. 1221).
- Digital fiduciaries' duties "may be different and sometimes considerably narrower" (p. 1225); "'Fiduciary' does not mean 'not for profit'" (p. 1227).
- Data as compensation "creates an inherent potential for conflicts of interest between the digital company and the end-user" (p. 1226).
- Where algorithms affect non-users, "we can no longer rely on the notion of special fiduciary relationships between individuals and companies"; the focus shifts to "obligations of trustworthiness and fair play with respect to the general public" (pp. 1233–1234).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Khan and Pozen (2019, p. 504) answer that "A fiduciary with sharply opposed loyalties teeters on the edge of contradiction", since officers owe duties to stockholders; their Part II.A says "Balkin has never squarely addressed the issue of crosscutting loyalties" (p. 508, not read by the arm; facets/M3_intermediaries_records.md:167; AUDIT_05.md M3-22).

- Balkin himself concedes the narrower scope (p. 1225) and the limit at non-users (pp. 1232–1234); a reader of the manuscript's objection will find part of it in Balkin.
