## Summary
Act No. 670 of 1740 is the colony's comprehensive slave code, printed in McCord's 1840 edition of the
statutes (vol. 7, "containing the acts relating to Charleston, courts, slaves, and rivers"; title page). The
preamble declares that enslaved people are property, that the owners' power should be "settled and limited by
positive laws", and that "the slave may be kept in due subjection and obedience" (p. 397). Section XLV recites
that "the having of slaves taught to write, or suffering them to be employed in writing, may be attended with
great inconveniences". It fines anyone who teaches a slave to write, or who uses a slave "as a scribe in any
manner of writing whatsoever", one hundred pounds current money for each offence (p. 413). The surrounding
sections regulate renting houses to slaves (s. XLII), travel in groups of more than seven without a white
person (s. XLIII), hours of labour (s. XLIV), plantations without a white resident (s. XLVI) and flight to
Florida (s. XLVII) (pp. 412-414). The act was temporary: it ran for three years and to the end of the next
session (s. LVIII, p. 417). It was assented to in May 1740 (p. 417), and Act No. 702 of 1743, which begins on
the same page, revived it.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet C (`C_literacy_withheld.md`, Common misreadings 1, l. 274): the 1740 act is often summarised as barring
  teaching slaves to "read and write". The text bars teaching to *write* and using a slave as a scribe, and says
  nothing of reading. South Carolina added reading only in Act No. 2639 of 1834 (same volume, pp. 468-469;
  facet C entry 2; AUDIT_01 C1 confirmed).
- Facet C entry 24: Rasmussen, "'Attended with Great Inconveniences': Slave Literacy and the 1740 South Carolina
  Negro Act", *PMLA* 125.1 (2010): 201-203, would confirm or correct this reading. The arm could not access it.

  pp. 397-417, A. S. Johnston and "(Original work published 1740)", so both faults are fixed.
