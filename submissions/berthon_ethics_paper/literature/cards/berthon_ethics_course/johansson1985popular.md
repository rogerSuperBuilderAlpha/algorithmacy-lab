## Summary
Johansson, of the Umeå demographic database, gives a short interpretive essay on the "old reading tradition"
of Scandinavia from about 1600 to 1900. A note says it first appeared in Swedish at the 1981 Nordic historical
congress (p. 64, n. 1). The abstract frames it as a study of Sweden, but the body covers Denmark, Norway,
Sweden, Finland, Iceland and Greenland, organised around the image of a growing tree (pp. 60-62). His thesis
is that popular reading grew out of the Lutheran church, long before the industrial revolution (abstract,
p. 60). Denmark and Norway taught through rural schools; Sweden and Finland put the duty on the home, under
the clergy's supervision, visitations and Catechetical Examination Registers (pp. 62-63). The rural population
resisted "costly school systems" because reading "had no obvious practical use" (p. 63). Everyone could be
tested in reading at confirmation, communion, marriage or migration, and confirmation "opened the road to
marriage and other social privileges" (p. 64). People were "held to the book" by "worldly compulsion and
church discipline". Johansson calls the programme "often distorted", "nothing but coercion and legalism", yet
says reading could "bear its real fruit" (p. 64). He closes on the theology: reading the Word "was everybody's
calling and right according to the Lutheran accentuation of the priesthood of every man" (p. 64). It gives
no tables.

## Key facts it relies on
- The reading tradition was religious in origin and predates industrialisation (abstract, p. 60).
- Sweden and Finland placed instruction in the home, with the clergy as the parish "teacher" and control by
  visitation and Catechetical Examination Registers (p. 63).
- Rural resistance to "costly school systems": reading "had no obvious practical use" and "should not cost
  anything" (p. 63).
- More than three hundred religious titles, mostly translated from German and printed before 1800, "could at
  that time be found in the homes" of Västerbotten county; some books appear in about 80 per cent of "all the
  inventories of the deceased" (p. 63).
- "All Scandinavians could be tested in reading at the time of confirmation, communion, marriage, or
  migration" (p. 64).
- "confirmation and Holy Communion opened the road to marriage and other social privileges" (p. 64).
- "it was often both by worldly compulsion and church discipline that the people were held to the book";
  "There was nothing but coercion and legalism" (p. 64).
- Reading the Word "was everybody's calling and right according to the Lutheran accentuation of the
  priesthood of every man" (p. 64).
