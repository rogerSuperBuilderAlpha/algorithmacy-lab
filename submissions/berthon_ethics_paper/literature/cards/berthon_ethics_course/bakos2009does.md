## Summary
An empirical test of the "informed minority" hypothesis in contract law and economics: the claim that a small share of term-conscious buyers can discipline sellers' boilerplate. Bakos, Marotta-Wurgler and Trossen use clickstream data on 45,091 US households (from a panel of 92,411, January 2007) and track visits to the websites of 66 software companies, 56 that sell software and 10 freeware providers, all of which post their end-user license agreement (EULA) as an optional link before purchase (pp. 2–3, 17–19). They find that "only one or two out of every thousand retail software shoppers chooses to access the license agreement", and that those who do stay a median 29 seconds, far too short to read a typical EULA of about 2,277 words (abstract; pp. 26–27). Across definitions of a shopper, the access rate for retail software runs from 0.05% to 0.17% (p. 36). They conclude that an informed minority of useful size is implausible in this market, and that mandated or increased disclosure is unlikely by itself to create one: "Shoppers do not access EULAs regardless of how accessible they are" (pp. 4–5, 37).

## Key facts it relies on
- Panel: 45,091 households, 66 online software companies (abstract; p. 3); raw data from 92,411 households, January 2007 (p. 17).
- Final sample: 56 retail and 10 freeware companies, after excluding firms that force click-through assent (p. 19).
- Headline: "only one or two out of every thousand retail software shoppers chooses to access the license agreement" (abstract, p. 1).
- Retail visits (2+ pages): EULAs accessed in 55 of 120,545 visits (0.05%); median time on the EULA 29 seconds (p. 26).
- "access" means a EULA view of at least one second, chosen to give "a conservatively high number" (p. 26).
- Mean retail EULA length 2,277 words; a full read would need 8–10 minutes (p. 26).
- "the highest fraction of readers among retail shoppers across all shopper and session definitions is 0.17%" (p. 28).
- Policy implication: "our findings call into question the effectiveness of policies attempting to prevent potential market failures by requiring increased or mandatory disclosure" (p. 4).

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is the 2009 working paper; the published version is *Journal of Legal Studies* 43(1) (2014) 1–35, which the arm has not opened (references.bib bakos2014does). The sample may differ between versions (bib note on bakos2009does).
- Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011, p. 671 and n. 127) cite a different 2009 version (NYU Law & Economics Working Paper No. 195) and summarise the finding as "roughly 1 in 1000 people actually scrolls through online boilerplate".
- The quotation the manuscript attaches to "that research" is Marotta-Wurgler's later conclusion, not this paper's (ledger P6-009; Ben-Shahar and Schneider n. 129–130).
- The authors limit the result to one contract and market (EULAs, software online) and note that reputation may still discipline sellers (p. 4).
