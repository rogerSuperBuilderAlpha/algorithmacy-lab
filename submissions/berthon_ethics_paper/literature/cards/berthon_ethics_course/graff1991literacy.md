## Summary
Graff studies literacy in three mid-nineteenth-century Ontario cities: Hamilton, London and Kingston (p. 17). The society he studies was more than 90% literate (p. 19). He argues that a "literacy myth" surrounds modern thought (p. 2): a set of assumptions that literacy brings individual advancement, economic development and social order, which his evidence does not bear out. Literacy "did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it", but it was "neither neutral, unambiguous, nor radically advantageous or liberating". It also served as an element of the structure of inequality, alongside ethnicity, class, sex and age (p. 19). Chapter 7 turns to the quality and uses of literacy. Most unskilled and semiskilled work "seldom, if ever, required the use of reading and writing". Skilled and nonmanual work needed it more, though Graff warns that those demands are easily exaggerated (p. 302). Promoters set literacy's moral and hegemonic purposes above its skills. Graff concludes that literacy "was not the benefit to individuals that it was promised to be". It was "more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most individuals within it" (p. 321). He enlists the contemporary critic W. B. Hodgson as an early voice against the overselling (p. 301).

## Key facts it relies on
- The study centres on Hamilton, London and Kingston, which Graff calls typical nineteenth-century commercial cities with good surviving literacy data (pp. 17-18).
- "A literacy myth surrounds us" (p. 2). This is the book's framing claim.
- "Literacy did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it, although its possession often signified attributes other than the abilities of reading and writing" (p. 19).
- The society was literate "with rates of literacy in excess of 90%", yet literacy "contributed regularly as an element of the structure of inequality" (p. 19).
- Graff quotes Hodgson: "It ought never to be forgotten that the power to read does not in the least determine the use to which it is put" (p. 301, n. 47). Graff's note 25 dates *Exaggerated Estimates of Reading and Writing as a Means of Education* to London, 1868.
- Unskilled and semiskilled work "seldom, if ever, required the use of reading and writing". At the artisanal level literacy "was more often needed", with "differential access to this occupational level and to its rewards". The evidence "forces us to evaluate it cautiously", and "Much skilled work consisted of practical knowledge" (p. 302).
- Nonmanual workers "often, if not always, had a more pressing and instrumental need for literacy skills" (p. 302).
- "social realities contradicted the promoted promises of literacy" (pp. 320-321). Literacy "was not the benefit to individuals that it was promised to be". It "was more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most individuals within it" (p. 321).
