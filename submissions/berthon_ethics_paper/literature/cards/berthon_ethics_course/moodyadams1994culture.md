## Summary

Moody-Adams argues that the link between culture and agency does not exempt anyone from responsibility. Her target is the "inability thesis": the claim, in Slote, Donagan and Wolf, that upbringing in a culture can leave people unable to know that its practices are wrong (pp. 292–293). The thesis is empirically weak, she argues. Its defenders "base hypotheses about what some agent(s) could not do solely on evidence of what the agent(s) did not do" (p. 294). Where a society maintains an internal perspective on its rules, as Greek slavery's legal and religious supports show, inability is hard to prove. The surviving record is skewed, since it is "principally the product of those who had reason to support the institution" (p. 296). Ancient slaveholders more plausibly show affected ignorance, "choosing not to know what one can and should know" (p. 296). The commonplace that it is "only fair" to judge people by "the standards of their own day" presumes without argument that our own ignorance is cultural rather than culpable (p. 298). She finds affected ignorance in Milgram's subjects (pp. 299–302). She proposes a "forgiving moralist's" model that keeps blame and allows forgiveness (pp. 302–303). Finally she rejects "cultural insanity" defences: a culture apart from the agents who perpetuate it "cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304).

## Key facts it relies on

- Definition of the target: "the inability thesis about cultural impediments", the claim that one's upbringing "simply renders one unable to know that certain actions are wrong" (p. 293).
- "a particular weakness of the theories under consideration is their tendency to base hypotheses about what some agent(s) could not do solely on evidence of what the agent(s) did not do" (p. 294).
- On Greek slavery, "the literature that does survive is principally the product of those who had reason to support the institution" (p. 296, citing Finley).
- "Affected ignorance—choosing not to know what one can and should know—is a complex phenomenon, but sometimes it simply involves refusing to consider whether some practice in which one participates might be wrong" (p. 296).
- "it is a commonplace of everyday moral reflection that it is 'only fair' to judge people by 'the standards of their own day'"; that view "simply presumes without argument that our own moral ignorance is best attributed to 'cultural limitations'" (p. 298).
- Four varieties of affected ignorance: euphemism (torturers), wanting to "know nothing" (the executive), asking no questions, and refusing to acknowledge fallibility (pp. 301–302).
- Blame "in some degree", asked as a question with "might" (p. 302); the forgiving moralist's model (p. 303).
- "A culture—independent of agents who perpetuate culture—cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304; the original puts "agent" in double quotation marks).

## Critical notes from the literature

- Fricker (2010) holds that structural, cultural-historical ignorance is non-culpable and so disagrees with Moody-Adams on the point the paper's benchmark rule turns on. Fricker does not cite her (`literature/facets/M4_ethical_frameworks.md` lines 130, 181, 218; `literature/audit/AUDIT_05.md` M4-11, M4-12).
- Mason (2015) reads Moody-Adams as holding slaveholders culpable through "a complex state of self-deception". Mason's own verdict on similar cases is mixed: "all three categories of blame reaction are merited" (`literature/facets/X_counter_case.md` entry 15; `AUDIT_02.md` X18).
- Peacock (2011), "Inability, culpability and affected ignorance: reflections on Michele Moody-Adams", is a direct response that the arm knows only by abstract (`references.bib`, `peacock2011inability`). Isaacs (1997, *Ethics*) is a likely reply in the same journal, known by record only (`N4_responsibility_intermediaries.md` line 77).
- `AUDIT_04.md` (L3-9 and the L3 summary): her p. 302 blame sentence is a question with "might", and her p. 294 point is narrower than "absence shows nothing". Summaries should keep her modality.
