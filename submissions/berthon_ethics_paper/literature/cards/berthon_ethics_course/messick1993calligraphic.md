## Summary

Messick studies how writing and authority were bound together in highland Yemen, from a late-nineteenth-century manuscript culture to the changes brought by print, new-method schools and new ideas of the state. He calls this order "the calligraphic state" (Introduction, p. 1). "Textual domination" names "the interlocking of a polity, a social order, and a discursive formation" (p. 1). I read the opening of the Introduction and pp. 205–210, the core of the evidence chapter. There Messick shows that Shafi'i doctrine, as stated in the manuals of al-Nawawi, Abu Shuja' and al-Ghazzi, made oral testimony the ideal form of evidence. Documents were "written out of the evidence doctrine". The manuals discuss witnessing support for documents but never treat documents as evidence in themselves. Practice ran the other way: the courts "in actuality relied heavily on the services of the written word" (p. 209). Formularies (the *shurut* literature) supplied model documents even though they subverted the formal rules. Only the Maliki school grafted the certified "reliable witness" onto a notarial profession. Shafi'i doctrine forbade relying "only on designated witnesses" (p. 208). The Ottoman Majalla (Art. 1736) gave documents cautious recognition. Under Ottoman rule Yemen introduced court registration, which the imamate dropped after 1918 (pp. 205–206).

- "As was noted earlier, the manuals discuss witnessing support for documents, but they do not consider documents to be potential evidence" (p. 209).
- A judge's letter to another judge "is not accepted ... except after the testimony of two witnesses" (Abu Shuja', p. 210).
