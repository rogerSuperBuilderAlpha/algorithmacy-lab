## Summary
Chapter XXXIX, passed on 7 April 1831 and in force from 1 June 1831, amends Virginia's general law on slaves and free Black people. Sections 1-3 deal with free Black people who remain in the state "contrary to law". They are to be sold publicly by the sheriff, the superior courts get jurisdiction, and revenue commissioners must list them for the grand juries each year. Sections 4-6 deal with schooling. Section 4 makes any meeting of free Black people "at any school-house, church, meeting-house or other place for teaching them reading or writing, either in the day or night, under whatsoever pretext" an unlawful assembly. A justice may disperse it and punish attendees with up to twenty lashes. Section 5 fines any white person who assembles with free Black people to teach them reading or writing up to fifty dollars, with up to two months' prison at a jury's discretion. Section 6 fines any white person who, "for pay or compensation", assembles with slaves and teaches a slave to read or write, and anyone who hires such a teacher, ten to one hundred dollars. Sections 7-8 order the act given in charge to grand juries and set its start date. The act does not reach unpaid, individual teaching of a slave by an owner or anyone else.

## Key facts it relies on
- Heading: "CHAP. XXXIX.—AN ACT to amend the act concerning slaves, free negroes and mulattoes. [Passed April 7, 1831]" (p. 107).
- s. 1: free Black people convicted of remaining contrary to law "shall be publicly sold off by the sheriff or sergeant" (p. 107).
- s. 4: "all meetings of free negroes or mulattoes, at any school-house, church, meeting-house or other place for teaching them reading or writing, either in the day or night, under whatsoever pretext, shall be deemed and considered as an unlawful assembly"; corporal punishment "not exceeding twenty lashes" (p. 107).
- s. 5: a white person who assembles with free Black people "for the purpose of instructing such free negroes or mulattoes to read or write" is fined up to fifty dollars and may be imprisoned up to two months (p. 107).
- s. 6: "if any white person, for pay or compensation, shall assemble with any slaves for the purpose of teaching, and shall teach any slave to read or write", that person and anyone "contracting with such teacher" is fined ten to one hundred dollars (p. 108).
- s. 7: judges and prosecutors must give the act in charge to grand juries (p. 108).
- s. 8: "This act shall be in force from the first day of June next" (p. 108).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet C (entry 6 and "Claims the sources contradict" 2) reads the act as I do. It bans teaching in assemblies of free Black people and paid teaching of slaves. It does not ban a master teaching his own slave or a parent teaching a child.
- Facet C (contradiction 3): Goodell (1853), quoting Kent, dates a Virginia statute to "1830". The act opened here is dated 7 April 1831.
- Facet C (contradiction 4): the Mayor of Norfolk gave the penalty in 1853 as "a fine of one hundred dollars and imprisonment for six months" (Douglass, 1854). That differs from ss. 5-6 here, so a later codification governed Margaret Douglass's case. Facet C did not open it.
- Facet C: the Virginia act of 2 March 1819 that other summaries cite could not be opened.
- The act states no ground for ss. 4-6.
