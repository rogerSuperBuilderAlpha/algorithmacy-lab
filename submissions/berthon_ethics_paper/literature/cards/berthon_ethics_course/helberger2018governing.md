## Summary
Helberger, Pierson and Poell argue that the public values at stake on online platforms (transparency,
non-discrimination, civility, media diversity) cannot be secured by allocating responsibility to one central actor,
as law usually does under the host-editor dichotomy (pp. 1-2). Because users partly drive the outcomes, the problem
is one of "many hands" (Thompson 1980; p. 3). Their answer is "cooperative responsibility": a division of labour
among platforms, users and governments (p. 3). Platforms have duties of their own and "an obligation to create the
conditions that allow individual users to comply with their responsibilities" (p. 3); following Fahlquist (2009),
users can fairly be held responsible only in proportion to their capacity and to what institutions have done first
(p. 3). Because architecture "shapes how users fulfill their role responsibly", design choices and terms of use allocate
responsibility (p. 4). Three cases follow, which I only skimmed: transparency and non-discrimination in peer-to-peer
mobility services, contentious content on social media, and diversity of content (listed p. 2). The conclusion assigns design
responsibility to platforms, "active participation, empowerment, and real responsibility" to users, and
framework-setting to governments (p. 11).

## Key facts it relies on
- "The power relationship between users and platforms is unequal, not least because of the opacity of platform
  operations" (p. 2).
- "Neither the platform nor the users can be held fully responsible. The solution, we will argue, lies in shared
  responsibility and a division of labor between the platform and users, or what we will refer to as cooperative
  responsibility" (p. 3).
- "Only when users have sufficient capacity, knowledge, and freedom would it be fair to expect that users take some
  kind of backward-looking responsibility" (p. 3).
- "we would like to argue that platforms have an obligation to create the conditions that allow individual users to
  comply with their responsibilities. In other words, platforms must cooperate with and empower users (Pierson
  2012)" (p. 3).
- Fahlquist (2009, 119), quoted: "The greater the extent to which institutional agents have taken their
  forward-looking responsibility, the greater the extent to which it is reasonable to ascribe both backward-looking
  and forward-looking responsibility to individuals" (p. 3).
- "how platform architectures are designed, shapes how users fulfill their role responsibly"; terms of use are
  where "the platform allocates the responsibility between users and platform" (p. 4).
- "Users can only be expected to take some backward-looking and forward-looking responsibility if the necessary
  conditions have been created by platforms and authorities for them to take up this role" (p. 11).
- Note 3: the section is "not so much to develop arguments about which concrete responsibilities these ought to be"
  as how to reach a fair arrangement (p. 11).
