## Summary
Bhargava and Assadi ask why organisations still interview when interviews are costly and poor at prediction. They name a twofold threat to the traditional view that interviews exist to predict performance and fit: the behavioural threat (people are bad at predicting performance and judging fit) and the algorithmic threat (algorithms already predict better in several domains) (pp. 201–203). Together these pose an "interview puzzle." Their answer, built on T. M. Scanlon's value of choice, is that choosing generates three kinds of value: instrumental, representative and symbolic (p. 214). Interviews may fail on instrumental value, yet the representative value (the choice expresses who chose) and the symbolic value (being trusted to choose marks one's standing) survive both threats (pp. 215–216). The chooser is the firm and its members choosing colleagues: "when firms conduct interviews, they are making choices about whom to employ" (p. 215). They conclude that interviews "should thus not be entirely replaced by algorithms, however sophisticated algorithms ultimately become" (p. 201). The candidate appears mainly in the future-research section, which notes that algorithmic hiring "deepens the informational asymmetry between candidates and employers" (p. 221). I read the abstract, introduction, section 3 and sections 5–6 in the Cambridge open-access PDF.

- Three values of choice, after Scanlon: instrumental, representative, symbolic (p. 214).
- "Returning to the context of interviewing, when firms conduct interviews, they are making choices about whom to employ" (p. 215).
- Symbolic value "is what is lost when a person for whom it would be appropriate (in a given social context) to make a certain decision is precluded from making that decision" (p. 216).
- "substituting interviews with algorithms can result in a loss of symbolic value even if ... an algorithm may arrive at a better assessment of a candidate's expected performance and fit" (p. 216).
- Future research: little is written on "what is owed to prospective employees"; algorithms in hiring deepen the "informational asymmetry between candidates and employers" (p. 221).
