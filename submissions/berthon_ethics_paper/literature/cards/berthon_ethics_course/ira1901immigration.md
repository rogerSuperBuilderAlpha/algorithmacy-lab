## Summary
The Act restricts immigration into the Commonwealth and provides for removing "prohibited immigrants". Section 3 lists the prohibited classes. The first, s. 3(a), is the dictation test: a person who, when asked by an officer, "fails to write out at dictation and sign in the presence of the officer a passage of fifty words in length in an European language directed by the officer". The other classes are likely public charges, the insane, people with certain diseases, certain convicts, prostitutes, and contract labourers, subject to exceptions (s. 3(h)-(n)). A person may be given the test on entry, if found after evading an officer, or within one year after entry (s. 5). A person who fails only the test may enter on a £100 deposit pending a certificate of exemption (s. 6). Unlawful entry carries up to six months' imprisonment and deportation (s. 7). Non-British convicts of violent crime may be set the test after serving their sentence and deported if they fail (s. 8). Ship owners face penalties (s. 9), and the Governor-General may make regulations empowering officers to determine who is prohibited (s. 16). An annual return to Parliament must count those refused, those who passed the test, and those admitted without being asked to take it (s. 17). The marginal notes cite the Natal Act 1897 and the Western Australian and New South Wales Acts as models.

## Key facts it relies on
- s. 1, short title: "Immigration Restriction Act 1901". Assented 23rd December 1901 (preamble; Schedule).
- s. 2: "Officer" means any officer appointed under the Act, or any Officer of Customs.
- s. 3(a): "Any person who when asked to do so by an officer fails to write out at dictation and sign in the presence of the officer a passage of fifty words in length in an European language directed by the officer". Margin: "See Natal Act 1897, No. 1, s.3"; "W.A. 1897, No. 13, s.2".
- s. 5(2): any immigrant may be asked to take the test "at any time within one year after he has entered the Commonwealth".
- s. 6: a person prohibited under s. 3(a) only may enter on depositing "One hundred pounds" and must obtain a certificate of exemption within thirty days.
- s. 7: up to six months' imprisonment, and liability to deportation.
- s. 8: a non-British convict of a violent crime may be required to take the dictation test after imprisonment, and deported on failure.
- s. 16: regulations may empower "officers to determine whether any person is a prohibited immigrant".
- s. 17: the annual return counts "the number of persons who passed the test" and "the number of persons admitted … without being asked to pass the test".
- Marginal notes throughout cite "Natal ib." (for example ss. 3(h), 6, 7, 9, 12-15).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet D (:278-282), from the Museum of Australian Democracy item page (`foundingdocs0000immigration`): the mechanism "could not be overtly based on race as this was opposed by Britain and … Japan. Instead, the basis was literacy". The test was given 805 times in 1902-03 with 46 passes, and 554 times in 1904-09 with six passes. After 1909 no one passed. Facet D (:284): "The Act, frequently amended, remained in force until 1958." The Act's own text cannot show this.
- Facet D (:347), via Lake (2006): the test "was so framed as to give Customs Officers maximum flexibility in ensuring that all undesirable immigrants would fail."
- AUDIT_01 (D13) confirmed s. 3(a), the Natal marginal note, s. 5(2), s. 6 and the assent date against this transcript. It also records a 1905 amendment to "any prescribed language", known from the Museum page, not from an amending Act.
