## Summary
The D.C. Circuit (Bazelon C.J., Danaher and Wright JJ.) heard two consolidated appeals by installment buyers
of a District furniture store. Each printed form contract "purported to lease the item" and contained a
pro-rata clause that kept a balance due on every item until all were paid, so default on one purchase let the
store repossess everything bought since the first transaction (at 447). Mrs. Williams, supporting seven
children on a $218 monthly stipend that the store knew of, bought a $514.95 stereo, defaulted, and faced
replevin of all items since 1957 (at 447-448). The lower courts held they had no power to refuse enforcement.
Writing for the majority, Judge Skelly Wright disagreed. Treating the point as one of first impression and
taking the newly enacted UCC § 2-302 as persuasive authority, the court held "that where the element of
unconscionability is present at the time a contract is made, the contract should not be enforced" (at 449).
Unconscionability includes "an absence of meaningful choice" together with terms "unreasonably favorable to
the other party"; the manner of contracting bears on meaningful choice, including whether a party, "considering
his obvious education or lack of it," had "a reasonable opportunity to understand the terms" (at 449). Because
no findings had been made, the court remanded (at 450). Judge Danaher dissented, urging caution and
legislative remedy (at 450-451).

## Key facts it relies on
- Form contract "purported to lease the item"; the pro-rata clause secured each new debt on all prior items,
  a provision the court calls "rather obscure" (at 447).
- The store knew of Williams's $218 monthly stipend and seven children (quoting the D.C. Court of Appeals,
  at 448).
- Holding: "where the element of unconscionability is present at the time a contract is made, the contract
  should not be enforced" (at 449).
- Definition: "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms
  which are unreasonably favorable to the other party" (at 449).
- "Did each party to the contract, considering his obvious education or lack of it, have a reasonable
  opportunity to understand the terms of the contract, or were the important terms hidden in a maze of fine
  print and minimized by deceptive sales practices?" (at 449).
- When a party of little bargaining power signs "with little or no knowledge of its terms, it is hardly likely
  that his consent, or even an objective manifestation of his consent, was ever given to all the terms"
  (at 449).
- Disposition: remanded for findings; the court did not hold these contracts unconscionable (at 450).
- Danaher J., dissenting: the appellant "seems to have known precisely where she stood"; prefers legislative
  remedy (at 450-451).

## Critical notes from the literature
- AUDIT_05 (`literature/audit/AUDIT_05.md:505`): the "education or lack of it" language is one factor in
  judging meaningful choice, not a freestanding test; the court remanded rather than holding the contracts
  unconscionable, and Danaher dissented. The earlier facet omitted both.
- Facet M3 (`literature/facets/M3_intermediaries_records.md:249, 313`): unconscionability is the nearest
  modern relative of the illiterate-signer protection, but it is a fairness test applied after the fact, not
  a duty to read and explain before signing; its application to platform terms was not checked.
