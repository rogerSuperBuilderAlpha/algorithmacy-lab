## Summary
The act's long title joins three aims: to amend the quarantine laws for sea ports, to stop the circulation of papers "calculated to excite disaffection among the coloured people of this state", and "to prevent said people from being taught to read or write". The preamble states the quarantine ground: ships arriving with free Black mariners or stewards must be kept from "communicating with the coloured people of this State". Sections 1-9 impose forty days' quarantine on such ships. They jail free Black seamen who come ashore, whip local Black people who communicate with them, and fine captains who do not carry them away. Section 10 makes it a capital offence for anyone to circulate or bring in printed or written papers "for the purposes of exciting to insurrection, conspiracy or resistance" among slaves and free people of colour. Section 11 is the teaching ban. Any slave, Black person, free person of colour or white person who teaches "any other slave, negro or free person of colour, to read or write either written or printed characters" is punished. Black offenders get fine and/or whipping. White offenders get a fine of up to five hundred dollars and imprisonment. Section 12 repeals the act of 9 December 1824. Governor George R. Gilmer assented on 22 December 1829.

## Key facts it relies on
- Long title: "to prevent the circulation of written or printed papers within this State calculated to excite disaffection among the coloured people of this state, and to prevent said people from being taught to read or write" (`ga1829.txt`:9619-9630; OCR-corrected).
- Preamble, quarantine ground: such persons are to be prevented "from communicating with the coloured people of this State" (p. 168).
- s. 1: forty days' quarantine for ships carrying "any free negro or free person of colour" as steward, mariner or passenger (p. 168).
- s. 10: circulating or bringing in "any printed or written pamphlet, paper or circular, for the purposes of exciting to insurrection, conspiracy or resistance" is "punished with death" (pp. 170-171).
- s. 11 (p. 171, page image): "if any slave, negro, or free person of colour or any white person shall teach any other slave, negro or free person of colour, to read or write either written or printed characters, the said free person of colour, or slave, shall be punished by fine and whipping, or fine or whipping at the discretion of the court; and if a white person so offending, he, she or they shall be punished with fine, not exceeding five hundred dollars, and imprisonment, in the common jail".
- s. 12 repeals the act of 9 December 1824 on the introduction of slaves.
- Assent: "Assented to, Dec. 22, 182[9]. GEORGE R. GILMER, Governor" (the final digit is OCR-damaged; the session is 1829).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet C (entry 3, l. 82-90) records the same reading and places s. 11 on p. 171 only by inference from the running heads 169 and 172. In this OCR the head directly above s. 10's continuation reads "SLAVES* *7l", which supports p. 171. No one had seen the page image when this card was written; the verifier has since confirmed p. 171 on it (see Verifier notes).
- Ledger P3-029 and FIX_CHECK_A (P3-071; P3-029, "fail-true") find that Georgia's ban is not narrower than its usual summary. It reaches every teacher, Black or white, and every pupil who is a slave or a free person of colour. The v3 sentence ("two of them are narrower") leaves Georgia out of the narrower pair, which is consistent with this.
- The act gives no ground for s. 11 itself. Its context is the incendiary-papers clause (s. 10, "insurrection"), so the "fearing insurrection" gloss the manuscript takes from Margo (2005) fits this act by context rather than by its own words (facet C, entry 3, "Stated ground").
- FIX_CHECK_C (l. 92) suggests adding the place, "Milledgeville", to the reference entry (v3 l. 337).
