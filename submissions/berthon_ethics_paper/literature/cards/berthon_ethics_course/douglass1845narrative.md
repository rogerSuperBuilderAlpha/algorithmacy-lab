## Summary
Douglass's autobiography of his enslavement in Maryland, published in Boston by the Anti-Slavery Office in 1845. The local copy is the DocSouth text of that edition, with its page marks ("Page 33" and so on). I read chapters VI and VII. In ch. VI, after he arrives at the Baltimore household of Hugh and Sophia Auld, Mrs. Auld begins teaching him the alphabet. Hugh Auld stops her and says that teaching a slave to read is "unlawful, as well as unsafe" and that reading "would forever unfit him to be a slave" (p. 33). Douglass treats the speech as a revelation: he now understood "the white man's power to enslave the black man" and "the pathway from slavery to freedom" (p. 33). In ch. VII he describes how Mrs. Auld turned against his reading and how he learned anyway, chiefly by making "teachers" of white boys in the street (pp. 38-39). He then reads *The Columbian Orator*. Reading lets him answer slavery's defenders, but it also brings the "discontentment" Auld predicted. "It had given me a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy" (p. 40). The two chapters pull both ways: reading reveals the path to freedom, and it also gives a knowledge of his condition that he has no power to act on.

## Key facts it relies on
- Title page: "Boston: Published at the Anti-Slavery Office, No. 25 Cornhill. 1845." (local text l. 180-192).
- Mrs. Auld taught him "the A, B, C" and some short words before Mr. Auld "found out what was going on" (p. 33, ch. VI).
- Auld "at once forbade Mrs. Auld to instruct me further, telling her, among other things, that it was unlawful, as well as unsafe, to teach a slave to read" (p. 33).
- Auld, "To use his own words": "if you teach that [boy] ... how to read, there would be no keeping him. It would forever unfit him to be a slave. He would at once become unmanageable, and of no value to his master" (p. 33). The subject of "would forever unfit" is "It", meaning teaching him to read.
- Douglass on the effect: "I now understood ... the white man's power to enslave the black man"; "From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom" (p. 33).
- Mrs. Auld came to see "that education and slavery were incompatible with each other" (pp. 37-38, ch. VII).
- How he learned: "making friends of all the little white boys whom I met in the street. As many of these as I could, I converted into teachers" (pp. 38-39).
- "It had given me a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy. It opened my eyes to the horrible pit, but to no ladder upon which to get out" (p. 40, ch. VII).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The Auld speech is Douglass's report, written years later, of a private conversation. Auld calls teaching "unlawful", but facet C found no Maryland statute in any source it opened, so the legal claim is unverified (`facets/C_literacy_withheld.md`:160, :278, :294).
- The passage "cuts both ways". Ch. VI makes reading "the pathway from slavery to freedom", and ch. VII makes it "a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy". Comprehension without leverage is therefore documented for the withheld reader, where the law denied the leverage, and not for every reader (`facets/X_counter_case.md`:123, :133).
- The Douglass sentence used as an early statement of "comprehension is not leverage" is the paper's one novel element on that point. Facet N5 found no prior work that uses Douglass on the limits of explanation (`facets/N5_prior_work_gap.md`:181).
- AUDIT_01 C11 left pp. 33-34 unchecked (`audit/AUDIT_01.md`:90). The local DocSouth text carries "Page 33" and "Page 40" marks, and I confirmed both pages this session.
- The source prints a racial slur in Auld's speech. Facet C substitutes bracketed words when quoting (`facets/C_literacy_withheld.md`:156). The manuscript's quotations avoid the slur.
