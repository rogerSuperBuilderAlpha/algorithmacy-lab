## What it argues
Nadella starts from an attribution gap: with traditional software "we had the tools and capability to trace behaviors to a specific code path" (P1), but for frontier models "we can't attribute model behaviors and outputs to specific inputs of training data or configurations of model weights", and organisations still give them "access to our most sensitive data" and "mission-critical actions on our behalf" (P2). Responsibility stays with the deployer: "A model provider's assurances do not relieve us of that responsibility" (P3). His remedy is containment, not acceptance or rejection of outputs (P4): "we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it" (P5). He sets alignment aside and proposes "strong, deterministic system design, human controls, and reliable operating procedures" around non-deterministic models (P6), and treats models as insider risks "not because they are necessarily malicious, but because any sufficiently capable actor ... can make mistakes or be compromised" (P7). Existing enterprise practice applies: identity, privilege limits, logs, containment boundaries (P8).

Chain-of-thought transparency is "a non-negotiable", and "Neuralese" cannot excuse opaque reasoning, but it "alone is not sufficient or dependable" (P9). Models checking models risks "nested black boxes" (P10). Controls over access and action "must sit outside the model", resting on an unnamed 1970s security principle that a program must not bypass the mechanisms enforcing its permissions (P11); the model is separated from its harness and action space (P12). Seven "principles of observability" follow (P13): model diversity (P14); "Observe everything", with "tamper-proof human readable evidence" for every meaningful action so an outcome can be reproduced without the model's own attestation (P15); continuous whole-system testing (P16); independent controls, where "Orgs" decide what a model may access and do (P17); independent auditability (P18); containment, with "an authorized person" able to pause or stop a model mid-task, "like an emergency brake" (P19); and incident disclosure "to those affected" (P20). The close: the best system "enables us to trust the model the least" (P21).

## Relation to the argument
Rulings D4 and D5, and premise P11. "We" is the deploying organisation throughout. The words "user", "customer", "employee" and "people" do not occur. "Human" occurs twice (P6, P15), "person" once (P19), "authority" once (P5), "affected" once (P20). The enterprise reading (D5) is the text's own. D4 needs care: the post does name "Neuralese" (P9), but disclosure is not its only move. P9 calls chain-of-thought transparency insufficient, and model diversity (P14), independent controls (P17) and containment (P19) disclose nothing to a reader, while P11–P12 put the controls outside the model. The post fits P11 of the argument map: authority sits with the deployer, load-bearing controls sit outside the model, and the residual human tasks are reading evidence (P15) and halting (P19). Who reads, at what rate, and how a person notices a fault in time to brake are unstated. P15's stated purpose is reconstruction ("reproduce how an outcome was achieved"), which reads as forensic, whereas P19 implies someone notices during the task. P20 is the only place a party outside the deployer appears, and it receives notice after a failure.

## Caution
