## Summary
Belland, an instructional-design researcher, writes an open-access monograph that grew out of his team's
meta-analysis of computer-based scaffolding in STEM education. The first two chapters set out what scaffolding is
and is not. He traces the metaphor to Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), who described tutors helping toddlers build
pyramids of blocks, and defines scaffolding as interactive support that builds on what learners know so that they
can take part in, and gain skill at, tasks beyond their unassisted abilities (p. 5). The definition excludes much:
a calculator, a job aid, instruction given before the problem, simply telling learners what to do, and support
that continues indefinitely (pp. 5-6, 18-19). Scaffolding is contingent, pairing dynamic assessment with "just the
right" support, and requires intersubjectivity, a shared sense of what success looks like (p. 18). Removal of
support, "fading", is Collins et al.'s later term, meant to transfer responsibility to the learner (p. 21). He
reports a mean effect of g = 0.46 for computer-based scaffolding, above typical educational-technology effects
(p. 27), and notes the term's long-lamented imprecision (p. 18).

## Key facts it relies on
- Definition: "interactive support that leverages what students already know to help them meaningfully participate
  in and gain skill at tasks that are beyond their unassisted abilities" (p. 5).
- "a calculator does not qualify as a scaffold because while it supports current performance, it cannot be
  reasonably expected to help users calculate independently ... in the future" (p. 5).
- "simply telling students what to do or how to do it does not qualify as scaffolding" (p. 6).
- Contingency: "dynamic assessment of the child's current performance characteristics and provision of just the
  right support"; intersubjectivity required (p. 18).
- "Support that continues indefinitely does not meet the scaffolding definition either" (p. 18).
- "A job aid does not meet the definition of scaffolding" (p. 19); "If the tool does all or most of the work ...
  the possibility of skill gain is compromised" (p. 19).
- Removing support "was later termed 'fading' by Collins et al. (1989)"; fading is "designed to gradually transfer
  the responsibility for the performance of the target skill from the scaffold provider to the scaffold receiver"
  (p. 21).
- Wood et al.'s six functions grouped as support for motivation (recruitment, frustration control, direction
  maintenance) and cognition (marking critical features, demonstration, reduction in degrees of freedom) (p. 22).
- "the average effect size of computer-based scaffolding was 0.46", against 0.13 and 0.16 for educational
  technology in mathematics and reading (p. 27).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Belland himself notes "the lack of precision that has emerged in the term scaffolding", citing Pea (2004),
  Puntambekar and Hübscher (2005) and Stone (1998) (p. 18); those critiques were not read here.
- The evidence is STEM problem-centred instruction, mostly K-12 and higher education; nothing concerns adults
  using commercial systems (`V5_scaffolding.md`, Q1).
- The meta-analysis was "In Press" at writing; the effect size is reported here, not re-checked.
- His definition is stricter than common usage: by it, permanent design aids (including the sister arm's
  re-representation conventions) are not scaffolds (`V5_scaffolding.md`, narrows 2).
