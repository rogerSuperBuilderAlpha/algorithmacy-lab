## Summary

Lovett's entry surveys civic (neo-)republicanism, whose central value is political liberty understood as non-domination, or independence from arbitrary power. He sets the republican view against Berlin's negative and positive liberty (§1.1) and argues through two stock cases, the kindly slave-master and the lightly governed colony, that freedom is a structural relation rather than a pattern of outcomes (§1.2). Pettit supplies the working definition: a person is free to the extent that no one has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (§1.2, citing Pettit 1999, 165). Republican liberty is negative in form but not equivalent to non-interference: the slave of a benevolent master remains unfree because the master could interfere (§1.3, §2.1). On what makes power arbitrary, Lovett reports two answers. The procedural view, his own, counts power as non-arbitrary when effective, resilient rules that are common knowledge control it. The democratic view counts it as non-arbitrary when those subject to it control it (§2.2). On either view arbitrariness means "uncontrolled," and neither defines it as unjust power. §4.1 notes that republicans who want to stop employers dominating workers advocate unions and workplace democracy.

## Key facts it relies on

- Pettit's definition, quoted with the source "(1999, 165; cf. Pettit 1997, 2001, 2012, 2014)"; the bibliography identifies 1999 as "Republican Freedom and Contestatory Democratization," in *Democracy's Value* (Shapiro and Hacker-Cordón, eds., CUP), 163–190 (§1.2; bibliography).
- "The slave lacks freedom because he is vulnerable to the arbitrary power of his master; whether his master happens to exercise that power is neither here nor there" (§1.3).
- "no matter how benevolent their particular master happens to be, no slave can be completely free until the institution of slavery itself is abolished" (§1.3).
- The republican programme aims "not to make our master a better person ... but to render him less of a master" (§2.1, citing Lovett and Ingham 2022).
- "Just because one is better able to cope with arbitrary power, it does not follow that one's domination is any less" (§2.2).
- Procedural view: power is not arbitrary "to the extent that it is reliably controlled by effective rules, procedures, or goals that are common knowledge to all persons or groups concerned (Lovett 2010, 2022)"; constraints "must be resilient over a wide range of possible changes or modifications in the relevant circumstances" (§2.2).
- Democratic view: power is not arbitrary "to the extent that it is directly or indirectly controlled by the concerned persons or groups themselves", illustrated from Pettit (2012, 57–58) (§2.2).
- Two caveats: "on either view, arbitrariness simply means uncontrolled and vice versa"; and "arbitrary or uncontrolled power should not be defined along substantive lines as power that is unjust or illegitimate" (§2.2).
- "in order to prevent the domination workers by their employers, many advocate stronger unions and workplace democracy (Gourevitch 2015)" (§4.1; the missing "of" is in the original).

## Critical notes from the literature

- The definition the manuscript quotes is Pettit 1999, p. 165, not Pettit 1997. Two secondary sources quote the 1997 book's different wording from p. 52 (O'Shea 2019; Gädeke 2020), per `literature/facets/N1_republican_domination.md` item 1a and ledger row P2-019.
- The procedural criterion ("rules that are common knowledge") is Lovett's own, and Lovett (2012, abstract) says it diverges from Pettit's view; the democratic view belongs to Pettit (2012) and others. Pettit's own institutional remedy is contestation: reasons, inclusive contestation and impartial forums, which Lovett reports from Pettit (1997, 186–7) in §4.2 (N1 items 1b, 1c; I did not read §4.2 this session).
- The liberal objection that non-domination reduces to secure non-interference (Goodin, Carter, Kramer, Moen) is listed by Lovett himself in §2.1, which answers it with the benevolent-master case. Simpson's charge that domination is inescapable is known in the arm only through Lovett and Pettit's reply (N1 §5).
- On work, N1 notes that Pettit and Lovett treat exit backed by basic income as the remedy for employer domination (Breen 2017 abstract), which the workplace republicans dispute.
- The entry is a living document: the copy read carries the 12 August 2026 revision. A later revision could move the quoted sentences.
