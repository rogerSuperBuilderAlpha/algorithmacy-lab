## Summary
Heyd, a linguist, argues that digital linguistics has framed online language as written text with spoken-like
features ("conceptual orality in a medium-based scripturality setting", p. 133) and has neglected genuinely
spoken digital communication: video, streaming, and talk with digital assistants (pp. 131, 133-134). She rereads
Ong's secondary orality, which "depends on writing and print for its existence" (Ong via p. 131), and finds it
broad enough to cover podcasts, typed CMC and algorithmic ordering (pp. 135-136). She proposes "tertiary
orality" for spoken CMC marked by two convergences Ong could not foresee: the blurring of spoken and written
modes, and a "destabilization of the human/nonhuman divide" in voices and linguistic agency (p. 139). Digital
assistants are her clearest case: "users speak to them, and the devices speak back" (p. 138). A case study of the
YouTube Reborn community shows oral routines "optimized and structured toward being read and recommended by a
machine" (p. 142). The article predates LLM chat and says nothing about ethics, governance or disclosure.

## Key facts it relies on
- "we are increasingly talking not just through, but with machines" (p. 131).
- Typed CMC research echoes "written text displaying certain spoken-like features", i.e. "conceptual orality in a medium-based scripturality setting" (p. 133).
- Medium-based digital orality "appears under-theorized and under-appreciated in digital linguistics" (p. 133).
- Ong's secondary orality is "both remarkably like and remarkably unlike primary orality" (Ong 1982, 133, quoted p. 136).
- "The term tertiary orality has been suggested before, but never fully theorized" (p. 136, n. 5; Lee 2013 as starting point).
- Digital assistants: "users speak to them, and the devices speak back" (p. 138).
- Tertiary orality: boundaries "between orality/scripturality and human/nonhuman agency become contested or irrelevant" (p. 139).
- YouTube routines are "oral discourse created by humans, but optimized and structured toward being read and recommended by a machine" (p. 142).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Builds on Ong, whose cognitive "great divide" is contested (Street, Finnegan, Scribner and Cole; see the Ong card's Caution in `submissions/algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/ong1982.md`). Heyd uses Ong's sociohistorical stages, not his cognitive claims.
- Facet V6 (Q2) found no refereed 2023-2026 work that extends this to LLM chat at length; the "return to orality" claim about chatbots lives in essays (Marz 2026).

## Key topics covered
secondary orality, tertiary orality, spoken CMC, conceptual vs medial orality (Koch and Oesterreicher), digital assistants, posthumanist linguistics, YouTube, algorithmic optimization of talk
