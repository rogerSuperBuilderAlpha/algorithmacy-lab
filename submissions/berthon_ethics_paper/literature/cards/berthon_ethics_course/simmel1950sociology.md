## Summary
In the chapter "The Triad", Simmel argues that adding a third element to a dyad creates three typical forms of
group that two elements cannot produce (p. 145). In the first, the third is non-partisan: a mediator who
brings the parties into contact and strips their claims of affect, or an arbitrator who weighs and decides
(pp. 146-148). Non-partisanship rests either on standing above the interests in conflict or on equal
involvement with both (pp. 149-150). In the second, the tertius gaudens, "the third who enjoys", the same
superior position is used for the third's own ends: the third gains while the two hold each other in check, or
when the parties compete for its favour and it can set conditions on that favour (pp. 154-156). In the third,
divide et impera, the third deliberately produces the conflict in order to dominate (p. 162). Simmel treats the
triad as a scheme to which "all cases of mediation can be reduced" (p. 148), and he notes that impartiality can
be mixed with many other relations between the third and the parties (p. 153).

## Key facts it relies on
- Chapter 4, "The Triad", runs pp. 145-169: §1 p. 145, §2 pp. 145-153, §3 pp. 154-161, §4 from p. 162 (contents,
  p. xiii; the next chapter begins p. 170).
- "The triad as such seems to me to result in three kinds of typical group formations" (p. 145).
- The mediator "must always keep out of any decision—whereas the arbitrator ends up by taking sides" (p. 148).
- The third is non-partisan "either if he stands above the contrasting interests ... or if he is equally
  concerned with both" (p. 149).
- "Both the mediator and the arbitrator wish to save the group unity from the danger of splitting up. But,
  evidently, the non-partisan may also use his relatively superior position for purely egoistic interests"
  (p. 154).
- The tertius may "make the interaction that takes place between the parties ... a means for his own purposes"
  (p. 154); translator's gloss: "the third who enjoys" (p. 154, n. 18).
- The tertius's advantage lies in "his possibility of making his decision depend on certain conditions"
  (p. 155).
- Divide et impera: "the third element intentionally produces the conflict in order to gain a dominating
  position" (p. 162).
