## Summary
Rahman studies one of the world's largest online labour markets, which he anonymises as "StarWorks". He uses
participant observation, 77 interviews (59 with freelancers, 18 with clients) and archival data (pp. v, 48,
50). His central claim is that the platform's algorithms build "modern day invisible cages, in which platforms
deliberately hide the norms and expectations for how people should behave" (p. v). The prevailing view is that
algorithms tighten Weber's iron cage. StarWorks first used a transparent five-star rating, which suffered heavy
inflation. It then replaced the rating with an opaque "Work Success Score" (WSS), introduced with no
explanation, and removed the public star rating from search results (pp. 42-43). Freelancers could not see how
the WSS criteria were weighted or predict when the score would change (p. 128). The result is "superstitious
reactivity": people "were never able to verify if and how their actions contributed to their success" (p. v). He also argues that
workers "unwittingly gave the platform full permission" through "digital boilerplate" terms of service, and he
proposes "algorithmic agnotology" as a framework for studying opaque algorithms (p. v).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. v: platforms create "invisible cages, in which platforms deliberately hide the norms and expectations for how people should behave."
- p. 42: "StarWorks previously used a transparent five-star rating algorithm to calculate freelancers' reputation on the platform."
- p. 43: over 90% of freelancers had at least 4.00/5.00 and 80% had 4.75. StarWorks "officially moved to a new algorithm … called the Work Success Score (WSS)", and "When the WSS was first introduced, no explanation for the score was given."
- p. 43: "a summarized, public 5-star rating was now completely removed from the main search results page."
- pp. 48, 50: 59 semi-structured interviews with freelancers; "in total, I conducted 77 interviews, 59 with freelancers and 18 with clients."
- p. 117: the platform "hid its algorithm to prevent gaming of the system", and freelancers "could not determine the how [sic] their actions impacted their success on the platform due to the concealed nature of the algorithmic evaluation." The algorithm "controlled their progression on the platform."
- p. 117: the components of invisible cages include habitually accepted digital boilerplate contracts, opaque inputs and processing, unpredictable impact, "no recourse", and changing conditions.
- p. 121: the cage's boundaries "can shift and change", and "The invisible cage … had a clear impact on freelancers' behavior."
- p. 128: "The processing of the WSS criteria was completely opaque for freelancers. None of the freelancers (or clients) had any insight about how the criteria StarWorks used to calculate the WSS were weighted."

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is an unrefereed dissertation. The refereed version is Rahman (2021, *ASQ*), which the arm has read only
  in its abstract (card `rahman2021invisible`; resolution C3, P4-068). The author must supply the ASQ PDF if the
  article's own pages are to carry these facts.
- Resolution C3 (P4-070): "discipline" appears in the literature review (p. 34) and in the account of
  terms-of-service enforcement (p. 116); it also appears in general discussion of evaluations (p. 123) and in the
  conclusion (p. 140), never as Rahman's word for what the opaque score did. Rahman's own words for the effect of the opaque score are "control" and
  "invisible cage".
- The evidence is one platform, anonymised, in one period. Rahman says he anonymised it "to highlight the broader
  phenomenon … not to single out any particular organization" (p. 42).

  rating with an opaque evaluation". The manuscript takes the replacement of a transparent rating by an opaque
  score. Source: pp. 42-43 (transparent five-star rating; WSS introduced with "no explanation"; public star
  rating "completely removed"). **holds**: the cited pages carry the claim.
- **L143**: "…and found that workers could not work out what the criteria were". The manuscript takes the claim
  that the criteria were unknowable. Source: pp. 117 and 128, not the cited pp. 42-43. **holds** in substance;
  the pin cite does not cover this clause. Add pp. 117, 128 if the dissertation is to carry it.
- **L143**: "…and that the opacity itself controlled them." The manuscript takes the idea that opacity worked as
  control. Source: p. v (platforms "deliberately hide the norms"), p. 117 (the hidden algorithm "controlled their
  progression"), p. 121 (the cage "had a clear impact on freelancers' behavior"). **narrower**: Rahman says the
  hidden algorithm controlled their progression and shaped behaviour through superstitious reactivity. He does
  not say that opacity, as such, controlled the workers. The v3 wording also differs from the closed ledger's
  agreed fix for P4-070 ("experienced the evaluation itself as a form of control"), which matches the 2021
  abstract more closely.
