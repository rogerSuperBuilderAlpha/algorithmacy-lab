## Summary
Margaret Douglass, a Southern white woman living in Norfolk, Virginia, gives a first-person account of her prosecution for teaching free Black children to read. In June 1852 she and her daughter Rosa opened a small school for free colored children (pp. 10-11). A grand jury presentment of 2 June 1853 charged both women with having assembled "with divers negroes, for the purpose of instructing them to read and to write" on 9 May 1853, "contrary to the Act of the General Assembly" (pp. 21-22). At trial in the Circuit Court she defended herself. She pleaded ignorance of the law and good intentions, calling herself "a Southern woman by birth, education, and feeling" and a former slaveholder. She closed by calling the statute "one of the most inhuman and unjust laws that ever disgraced the statute book of a civilized community" (p. 36). The jury found her guilty and fixed the fine at one dollar (p. 37). On 10 January 1854 Judge Baker added "an imprisonment of one month in the city jail" (p. 44), and she served that month. The book reprints the statute (Sec. 32: jail up to six months and a fine up to one hundred dollars for a white person who assembles with negroes to instruct them). It also reprints press accounts of the case.

## Key facts it relies on
- Title page: "imprisoned for one month in the common jail of Norfolk, under the laws of Virginia, for the crime of teaching free colored children to read" (local text l. 1-30).
- The school opened "on the first of next month, (which was June, 1852,) for free colored children" (pp. 10-11, l. 330-341).
- Summons: presentment of 2 June 1853 for acts on 9 May 1853, instructing "divers negroes ... to read and to write, contrary to the Act of the General Assembly" (pp. 21-22, l. 715-739).
- Her self-description in court: "I am a Southern woman by birth, education, and feeling. I have been a slaveholder myself" (l. 1144-1146, in the address that ends on p. 36).
- Closing words of her address: "I shall be only a single sufferer under the operation of one of the most inhuman and unjust laws that ever disgraced the statute book of a civilized community" (p. 36, l. 1260-1262).
- Her address was to the jury ("my address to the jury", p. 37, l. 1275). The verdict was guilty, "fixing the fine at one dollar, instead of one hundred" (p. 37, l. 1282-1288).
- Sentence of 10 January 1854 by Judge Baker: "imprisonment of one month in the city jail" (p. 44, l. 1531-1536). She "spent the month within the walls of a prison" (l. 1757-1762).
- Statute as reprinted: "If a white person assemble with negroes for the purpose of instructing them to read or write ... he shall be confined in jail not exceeding six months, and fined not exceeding one hundred dollars" (Sec. 32, appendix, l. 2171-2177).

- Her objection is limited. In the same address she says she would be a slaveholder again "if I felt so disposed" (l. 1144-1146, p. 33) and promises to teach "the colored race humility ... their duty to their superiors" (l. 1240-1244, p. 36). She objects to the anti-teaching law, not to slavery.
