## Summary
Hoeksema asks whether the standard, agent-relative republican account of domination, Pettit's capacity for arbitrary or uncontrolled interference, is enough to capture the power of digital platforms. He argues that it should be supplemented by radical republicanism, which gives priority to structures. That view draws attention to two things. The first is the economic rationales and socio-technical infrastructures behind platforms: asymmetries of knowledge and resources, and a tendency to monopolise and control infrastructure. The second is "constitutive domination" (Thompson), meaning influence on users' cognitive and evaluative dimensions through habituation, routinisation and manipulation. Privacy notices are too long to read, consent is made a condition of service, and dark patterns nudge acceptance. From this he draws four implications. Republicans "cannot rely on the idea of exit options or of rationally consenting citizens". Citizens must be helped to withstand habituation through strict regulation of manipulation, education, and access to platforms' behavioural research. Governments must not let reliance on private platforms undermine public tasks. Responses must restructure economic rationales and infrastructure, for example through a "digital commons" that "gives citizens a voice in the structures of the digital sphere" and through utility-style regulation.

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract: republicans "have reason to favour a more structural response to digital platforms over more direct and individualised forms of control that fit with a 'standard' republican approach, such as securing exit options and requiring user consent."
- "It makes little sense to curb the power of large tech corporations by securing exit options and consent, without giving priority to underlying (infra)structural issues such as the large asymmetries of knowledge and resources, and the tendency of platforms to monopolise and to control infrastructure" (reprint p. 63).
- "The first implication is that republicans cannot rely on the idea of exit options or of rationally consenting citizens" (reprint p. 67).
- His two reasons: (1) users "might quite easily be socialised, habituated or manipulated to simply accept the status quo, including the need to consent"; (2) platforms' power "flows largely from their control over resources, expertise and computational infrastructure", which reaches into public systems that are "inescapable" (reprint p. 67).
- "there is little place for reliance on 'notice-and-consent' mechanisms or other exit-strategy–based responses". Users "may, at best, assent, rather than consent" (Gourevitch) (reprint p. 67).
- Privacy notices "require too much time and cognitive load for most users to read". Services are made "dependent on consent". Dark patterns nudge acceptance (PMC, constitutive-domination section).
- A digital commons "gives citizens a voice in the structures of the digital sphere" (reprint p. 68).
- Conclusion: preventing online domination "requires more than securing exit options" (reprint p. 68).
- Footnote 1: he does not claim that standard republicanism is conceptually unable to absorb these insights.

## Where the manuscript uses it
- **L207**: "Hoeksema (2023) argues that exit and consent are the weakest answers to platform power". This takes his verdict on exit and consent. **narrower**: he says republicans "cannot rely on" exit or consent and that there is "little place" for them (reprint p. 67), and that more than exit is needed (p. 68). He does not rank them as the weakest answers.
- **L207**: "…for exactly this reason". The reason in context is that a firm may price a second track so that no one uses it, so the duty cannot rest on the operator's goodwill. **does not hold**: his reasons are habituation and manipulation into accepting the status quo, and structural dependency on platform infrastructure that extends into inescapable public systems (reprint p. 67). Neither concerns the cost or pricing of an alternative track.
