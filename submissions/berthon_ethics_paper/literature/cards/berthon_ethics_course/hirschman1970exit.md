## Summary
Hirschman asks how firms, organizations and states that lapse from efficient performance are brought back. He names two mechanisms. With exit, customers stop buying or members leave. With voice, they express dissatisfaction directly to management or to anyone who will listen (p. 4). He maps the split onto economics and politics. Exit is "neat", "impersonal" and "indirect"; voice is "messy", graduated and "political action par excellence" (pp. 15-16). The book's driving complaint is the economist's "bias in favor of exit and against voice". Milton Friedman's voucher proposal, which calls exit the "direct" way to express a view and voice "cumbrous political channels", is Hirschman's "near perfect example" (pp. 16-17). Hirschman concedes that exit "is widely held to be uniquely powerful" (p. 21). He then shows that it can fail. When quality declines across all firms at once, customers flit "from one deteriorating firm to another without any firm getting a signal". Competition can even suit manufacturers because "it keeps consumers from complaining" (pp. 26-27). Voice grows as exit declines (pp. 33-34). The ready availability of exit "can therefore tend to atrophy the development of the art of voice", which he calls "a central point of this book" (p. 43). In some conditions, a no-exit situation with effective voice beats limited competition (p. 55).

## Key facts it relies on
- Definitions. Exit means that "Some customers stop buying the firm's products or some members leave". Voice means that customers or members "express their dissatisfaction directly to management or to some other authority" (p. 4).
- The exit customer "uses the market to defend his welfare or to improve his position" (p. 15).
- "Exit belongs to the former realm, voice to the latter" (economics and politics, p. 15). "Voice is political action par excellence" (p. 16).
- "The economist tends naturally to think that his mechanism is far more efficient and is in fact the only one to be taken seriously" (p. 16). Friedman's passage is "a near perfect example of the economist's bias in favor of exit and against voice" (p. 17).
- In politics, by contrast, exit "has often been branded as criminal", as desertion, defection or treason (p. 17).
- "the exit option is widely held to be uniquely powerful" (p. 21). He presents this as the prevailing view, not his own.
- "Competition as Collusive Behavior": under a uniform quality decline, exit is "ineffective in alerting management to its failings". Competition "keeps consumers from complaining" (pp. 26-27).
- "Whoever does not exit is a candidate for voice". Voice "feeds on inelastic demand, or on the lack of opportunity for exit" (p. 34).
- "The presence of the exit alternative can therefore tend to atrophy the development of the art of voice. This is a central point of this book" (p. 43).
- Ch. 5: a no-exit situation can be "superior to a situation with some limited exit" (p. 55).
