## Summary

Margo, an economic historian, reviews Williams's *Self-Taught* (UNC Press), a history of how enslaved and freed African Americans acquired literacy. This card rests on the two-page review only. The text layer of the JSTOR file is clipped at the right margin, so I read both pages from the rendered page images (`pdfs/library/img/Margo-2.png`, `Margo-3.png`). Margo opens with his own census figures: illiteracy among southern-born African Americans fell from 84 percent in 1870 to 26 percent in 1920, mainly through the schooling of new generations. He credits Williams with a corrective: literate African Americans, not only Northern white teachers, "jump-started" the decline. He summarises her nine chapters. Chapter 1 covers the laws: "Fearing insurrection, southern state legislatures made it a crime" to teach slaves or free blacks to read and write. Later chapters cover literacy in the Union Army, freedpeople's conventions, Black teachers, and the post-war common-school laws. His one criticism is method. Williams makes little use of quantitative evidence, so she leaves untested whether the anti-teaching laws worked, though the 1850 and 1860 census samples record free Black literacy. He calls this a minor caveat and praises the book.

## Key facts it relies on

- Margo's own estimate: slave illiteracy "would have been 90 percent or higher (ages ten and older)" had the antebellum censuses recorded it (p. 590).
- Southern-born African-American illiteracy: 84 percent in 1870, 51 percent in 1900, 26 percent in 1920 (p. 590).
- Williams's book has a legal appendix "documenting various antebellum statutes pertaining to the instruction of slaves and free blacks" (p. 590).
- "Fearing insurrection, southern state legislatures made it a crime for persons to instruct slaves or free blacks to read and write, among other related prohibitions (for example, employing a slave to run a printing press, in the case of Georgia)" (p. 590).
- "most slave owners, it seems, believed that literate slaves were more likely to foment rebellion or run away" (p. 590).
- A federal report of 1868: Black teachers "accounted for slightly more than half of those teaching ex-slaves" (p. 590).
- "These laws, generally, also prohibited the teaching of free blacks, but were they effective in doing so? This question could have been addressed using the 1850 and 1860 integrated public use micro-data samples of the federal census" (p. 591).
- The ex-Confederate legislatures "passed laws establishing common schools, albeit racially segregated ones" (p. 591).
