## Summary
Section 1632 requires a business that negotiates "primarily in Spanish, Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, or Korean, orally or in writing" to deliver, before signature, a full translation of the contract in that language. It applies to listed consumer contracts: consumer credit sales, consumer loans, leases and certain others. The legislative findings say it "was enacted in 1976 to increase consumer information and protections for the state's sizeable and growing Spanish-speaking population". They ground the five languages in American Community Survey counts. The duty lapses if the consumer negotiates "through the party's own interpreter". That interpreter must be an adult fluent in both languages and must not be employed by or supplied through the business. On non-compliance the aggrieved person "may rescind the contract", and a sold contract is deemed rescinded against the assignee too. Waiver is "void and unenforceable".

## Key facts it relies on
- (a)(1): "This section was enacted in 1976 to increase consumer information and protections for the state's sizeable and growing Spanish-speaking population."
- (a)(3): approximately 3.8 million Californians "do not speak English well or do not speak English at all", and the five named languages are spoken by about 3.5 million of them (ACS 2009-2011).
- (b): the business "shall deliver to the other party ... and before the execution thereof, a translation of the contract or agreement in the language in which the contract or agreement was negotiated, that includes a translation of every term and condition".
- (b)(2): covers "a loan or extension of credit ... for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes".
- (h)(1)-(2): exception where the party negotiates "through the party's own interpreter", who must "read with full understanding both the English language" and the other language, and must not be "employed by, or whose service is not made available through, the person engaged in the trade or business."
- (k)(1): "Upon a failure to comply with the provisions of this section, the person aggrieved may rescind the contract or agreement".
- (l): "Any waiver of a provision of this section is contrary to public policy and is void and unenforceable."

## Critical notes from the literature
- The statute is triggered by the business's own choice of language in negotiation. A consumer who negotiates in English but reads poorly is not covered, and illiteracy is not addressed.
- The remedy is still a document: a translation that must be read. It moves the burden onto the business and adds rescission. It does not check understanding.
- Contrast `morales2008sun`: the Third Circuit bound a Spanish speaker to an English employment contract where the interpreter was a coworker the employer had asked to help. §1632(h) would not count such an interpreter as "the party's own".
