## Summary
Butterfield's short book attacks "the whig interpretation of history": "the tendency in many historians to write
on the side of Protestants and Whigs, to praise revolutions provided they have been successful, to emphasize
certain principles of progress in the past and to produce a story which is the ratification if not the
glorification of the present" (Preface). He treats it "not as a problem in the philosophy of history, but rather
as an aspect of the psychology of historians" (Preface). The Introduction rejects the historian as "avenger" who
divides the past into "good and evil, progressive and reactionary" (ch. 1, scan pp. 9-12). Chapter 2 names the root
fallacy: "The study of the past with one eye, so to speak, upon the present is the source of all sins and
sophistries in history, starting with the simplest of them, the anachronism" (scan pp. 29-30). The last chapter,
"Moral Judgements in History", charges the whig historian with regarding "himself as the judge when by his
methods and his equipment he is fitted only to be the detective", and calls retrospective moral verdicts "the
most useless and unproductive of all forms of reflection" (scan p. 79). He allows that such cautions "do not
eliminate the moral responsibility upon which Lord Acton set such store", but holds that the historian's office
is description, not verdict (scan p. 89).
