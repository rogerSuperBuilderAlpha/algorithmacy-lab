## Summary
Solove names the US regime of "rights to notice, access, and consent" regarding personal data "privacy self-management" and argues it "is being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities" (p. 1880). Two kinds of problem defeat it. Cognitive problems: people do not read notices, do not understand them, lack the background to choose, and choose with skewed judgment (p. 1888). Structural problems persist even for "fully informed and rational" people: too many entities hold data (scale), small disclosures combine into sensitive inferences (aggregation), and harms are cumulative and arrive later (pp. 1888-1893). The regime "cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals" (p. 1893). Opt-in will not fix it, because firms can condition service on consent (pp. 1898-1899). Yet he refuses to abandon self-management, which gives "some autonomy" (p. 1899), and names the "consent dilemma": the obvious alternative, paternalism, denies people choice (pp. 1881-1882, 1894). He proposes partial self-management, regulation of downstream uses when they are proposed (p. 1902) and more substantive privacy norms (pp. 1902-1903). The article is the introduction to an HLR symposium; it reviews others' evidence and reports no study of its own.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 1880: privacy self-management "is being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities."
- p. 1885: "making it simple and easy to understand conflicts with fully informing people about the consequences of giving up data".
- p. 1888: "(1) people do not read privacy policies; (2) if people read them, they do not understand them; (3) if people read and understand them, they often lack enough background knowledge to make an informed choice; and (4) ... their choice might be skewed by various decisionmaking difficulties."
- p. 1889: "the average person just does not have enough time or resources to manage all the entities that hold her data"; $781 billion figure cited to McDonald and Cranor at 564 (n. 41).
- p. 1893: "Privacy self-management cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals".
- p. 1898: the iTunes agreement "often extends to more than fifty screens"; opt-in leads "to more buttons to click and more forms to sign, but not to more meaningful privacy protection" (pp. 1898-1899).
- p. 1899: "For all its flaws, privacy self-management should not be abandoned."
- p. 1900: "perhaps the greatest practical impact of privacy self-management is not in informing individuals ... but in informing the companies"; it "is currently asked to bear far more weight than it can support."
- p. 1903: "Consent often becomes a convenient way to reach outcomes without confronting the central values at stake."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Calo (2012, p. 1033), cited by Solove at n. 20, concedes the textual case but defends experiential ("visceral") notice; Solove replies that privacy harms are harder to make visceral than smoking's (p. 1885). See `calo2012against`.
- Kaminski (2019, preprint p. 19) argues that critiques of individual transparency rights mischaracterise layered regimes such as the GDPR's; Solove's own EU discussion (pp. 1897-1898) is brief. See facet V4 §2.
- Many of the evidential claims (reading rates, survey misbeliefs) are cited from secondary sources in the footnotes (pp. 1884-1886, nn. 14-25).
