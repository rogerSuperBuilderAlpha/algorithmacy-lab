## Summary

Muldoon and Raekstad bring labour-republican theory to platform work. They define domination as subjection to uncontrolled power: "A is dominated by B if and only if B can interfere with A's choices in ways that A cannot control" (p. 590). Algorithmic domination is such power when an algorithm determines its operations without intermediate human judgment (p. 593). Drawing on Gourevitch, they say bosses dominate workers by (a) controlling information, (b) deciding tasks, and (c) assessing, rewarding and punishing performance (pp. 590–591). Ride-hail and food-delivery apps increase all three, they argue. The apps withhold information such as destinations and give workers one-way channels and template replies (pp. 595–596). Dynamic pricing and gamified offers widen bosses' interference (pp. 597–598). Customer ratings, which the company uses to reward or deactivate workers, put workers "at the mercy of random people" (pp. 598–599). The dominating agents are owners and managers acting as a group agent, and the power "inhere[s] in the socially produced ... corporate entity", not in the algorithm (pp. 593–594, 598). Self-regulation cannot remove uncontrolled power (pp. 594, 599). Their remedy is democratic ownership: platform co-operatives such as CoopCycle, with stated risks (pp. 600–601).

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract: "certain algorithms can facilitate new relationships of domination by sustaining a socio-technical system in which the owners and managers of a company dominate workers"; case study "ride hail and food delivery services" (p. 587).
- Definition of domination, "A is dominated by B if and only if B can interfere with A's choices in ways that A cannot control", including penalties that turn Z into "Z-minus" (Pettit 2012) (p. 590).
- Bosses' three powers: (a) information, (b) tasks, (c) assessment, reward and punishment (p. 591).
- "the owners and managers operate as a group agent of domination"; "we can clearly identify the owners and managers as the agents of domination" (pp. 593–594).
- "Each of these three information and computational asymmetries effectively decreases workers' ability both to understand and contest the decisions that are made over them" (p. 596).
- Workers "remain subject to the uncontrolled power of consumers and bosses"; "you are at the mercy of random people" (p. 598, quoting Lee et al.).
- "Workers have had their accounts deleted by an algorithm without warning and with no right to contest the decision" (p. 599).
- "Corporate promises to 'do better' through self-regulation are implausible solutions for uncontrolled powers" (p. 599).
- Remedy: "democratic ownership and control of productive assets and equal rights of participation in decision-making" (p. 601).

## Critical notes from the literature

- The sentence the manuscript quotes from p. 599 ("Workers have had their accounts deleted ...") carries no citation in the article. It states the authors' summary of the empirical literature, not a reported case.
- Facet N1 (`literature/facets/N1_republican_domination.md` §3, recommendation 4) notes that the authors already place the customer in the picture as a dominator whose power the platform builds (p. 598). That narrows the manuscript's "I add the second party".
- N1 contrasts their ownership remedy with Frye (2024), who relocates workplace domination to managerial discretion. The manuscript's operator duties sit closer to Frye.
- The authors concede that Pettit and Taylor deny that workers are dominated under suitable background conditions such as competitive markets or a basic income (p. 604, n. 4). Pettit (2006) is the arm's primary text for that view (N1 §2).
- Ledger P2-006: the article says only that Uber casts drivers as "partner-drivers" or "customers" of its software (pp. 591–592). It does not establish who contracts with the driver.
- Print year 2023 (vol. 22(4)); the article carries "© The Author(s) 2022" (p. 587). Crossref uses the online date (`AUDIT_05.md` M4-57).
