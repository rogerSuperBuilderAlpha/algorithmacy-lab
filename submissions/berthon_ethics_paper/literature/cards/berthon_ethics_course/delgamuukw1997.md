## Summary
The Gitksan and Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs claimed ownership of and jurisdiction over about 58,000 square
kilometres of British Columbia (headnote; para. 7). The trial judge, McEachern C.J., dismissed the claims in
reasons "nearly 400 pages long" (para. 6). The Supreme Court ordered a
new trial (headnote). Lamer C.J., for himself and Cory and Major JJ., with McLachlin J. concurring, held that the
trial judge erred in his treatment of oral history. The laws of evidence "must be adapted in order that this type
of evidence can be accommodated and placed on an equal footing with the types of historical evidence that courts
are familiar with, which largely consists of historical documents" (para. 87). The Gitksan adaawk and the
Wet'suwet'en kungax are sacred oral histories, "repeated, performed and authenticated at important feasts" (para.
93). The trial judge gave them no independent weight because they did not convey historical truth, were known
only within the communities and lacked detail, but these "are features ... of all oral histories" (para. 98).
His reasoning would mean the oral histories "would be consistently and systematically undervalued by the
Canadian legal system", contrary to *Van der Peet* (para. 98). The headnote also sets out the content of
aboriginal title, its proof and the test for justified infringement, and La Forest and L'Heureux-Dube JJ. concurred
in the result for separate reasons.

## Key facts it relies on
- Report, file and date: [1997] 3 S.C.R. 1010; no. 23799; 11 December 1997 (decision page header).
- Disposition: "A new trial is necessary" (headnote, Lamer C.J. and Cory, McLachlin and Major JJ.); La Forest
  and L'Heureux-Dube JJ. also order a new trial (headnote).
- Oral histories must be "placed on an equal footing with the types of historical evidence that courts are
  familiar with, which largely consists of historical documents" (para. 87).
- Adaawk and kungax are "repeated, performed and authenticated at important feasts", where dissenters may object
  (para. 93).
- The trial judge treated oral histories as useful "only ... as confirmatory evidence" (para. 98).
- "the oral histories of aboriginal peoples would be consistently and systematically undervalued by the Canadian
  legal system" (para. 98, Lamer C.J.).
- The trial judge also erred in discounting "recollections of aboriginal life" (para. 99).
- McLachlin J.: "I concur with the Chief Justice" (her reasons).

  para. 87 draws its rule by analogy from treaty-interpretation practice (*Sioui*).
