## Summary
A Barcelona commercial court asked whether Uber's service, which connects non-professional drivers using their own cars with people wanting urban journeys through a smartphone application, is a transport service or an information society service under EU law. The Grand Chamber held it a transport service. An intermediation service of that kind would in principle be an information society service (para. 35), but Uber's service is "more than an intermediation service" (para. 37): Uber selects the drivers, without its application neither drivers nor passengers would use the service, and it "exercises decisive influence over the conditions" of the service by fixing at least the maximum fare, collecting the fare and controlling vehicles and drivers, up to exclusion (para. 39). The service therefore forms "an integral part of an overall service whose main component is a transport service" (para. 40). It falls outside Directive 2000/31 (para. 42), Directive 2006/123 (para. 43) and Article 56 TFEU, coming under Article 58(1) TFEU (para. 44); with no common EU rules adopted, member states regulate it (paras 46–47). I read the CELEX text: the header, paras 21–50 and the ruling.

## Key facts it relies on
- Parties and court: "JUDGMENT OF THE COURT (Grand Chamber) 20 December 2017", Case C‑434/15, "Asociación Profesional Élite Taxi v Uber Systems Spain SL" (header). The text carries no ECLI.
- The service at issue connects "non-professional drivers using their own vehicle with persons who wish to make urban journeys" (para. 33).
- In principle such intermediation "meets ... the criteria for classification as an 'information society service'" (para. 35).
- Uber "exercises decisive influence over the conditions under which that service is provided by those drivers": maximum fare, collection of the fare, control "over the quality of the vehicles, the drivers and their conduct, which can, in some circumstances, result in their exclusion" (para. 39).
- Holding: the service "must be classified not as 'an information society service' within the meaning of Article 1(2) of Directive 98/34, to which Article 2(a) of Directive 2000/31 refers, but as 'a service in the field of transport'" (para. 40).
- "Consequently, Directive 2000/31 does not apply to an intermediation service such as that at issue" (para. 42).
- Member states regulate the conditions of such services "as EU law currently stands" (para. 47); ruling restates paras 48 and 40.

## Critical notes from the literature
- FIX_CHECK_B.md (P5-N03, pass): the step from *Elite Taxi* to the Digital Services Act is an inference, since the judgment predates the DSA and construes Directives 98/34 and 2000/31. The DSA applies to providers of information society services as defined in Directive 2015/1535 (DSA recital 5; art. 3(a)), and its recital 6 excludes cases "where the intermediary service constitutes an integral part of another service which is not an intermediary service as recognised in the case-law of the Court of Justice" (`32022R2065.txt`, recital 6). That recital supports the manuscript's inference.
- FIX_CHECK_B.md notes that the Court later treated a taxi-booking app as an information society service (*Star Taxi App*, C-62/19, 2020; not opened in this arm), which is why the manuscript's "may" must stay.
- The holding turns on Uber's control of non-professional drivers (UberPop, para. 39). Whether a given ride-hail service today meets the same test depends on its facts.
