## Summary

Mittelstadt notes that at least 84 AI-ethics initiatives have converged on high-level principles resembling the four principles of medical ethics. He argues that the convergence gives little ground for confidence. Medicine makes principlism work through four things AI development lacks: (1) common aims and fiduciary duties, (2) professional history and norms, (3) proven methods to translate principles into practice, and (4) robust legal and professional accountability mechanisms (abstract; §2). Developers owe their primary duties to shareholders, and no patient-like party holds primacy, so users "cannot trust that developers will act in their best interests" (§2.1). Abstract principles such as fairness and dignity are essentially contested and hide disagreement (§2.2). Value-sensitive methods have mostly been tested in academic settings (§2.3). Codes of ethics change behaviour only when organisations embed and enforce them, and computing has no licensing or sanction comparable to medicine's (§2.4). Without punitive mechanisms, "a principled approach runs the risk of merely providing false assurances of ethical or trustworthy AI" (§2.4). He recommends sustainable pathways to impact with binding accountability, bottom-up case work, licensing of developers of high-risk AI, a shift from professional to organisational ethics, and ethics as a process rather than technological solutionism (§3). ## Key facts it relies on

- "At least 84 public-private initiatives have produced statements describing high-level principles" (abstract; §1).
- AI development lacks "(1) common aims and fiduciary duties, (2) professional history and norms, (3) proven methods to translate principles into practice, and (4) robust legal and professional accountability mechanisms" (abstract; §2).
- Industry-sponsored initiatives have been "characterised as mere virtue signalling intended to delay regulation"; corporate commitments "provide policy-makers with a reason not to pursue new regulation" (§1).
- "The absence of a fiduciary relationship in AI means that users cannot trust that developers will act in their best interests when implementing ethical principles in practice" (§2.1).
- A meta-analysis "found that the mere existence of a code has no discernible effect on unethical behaviour"; an effect appears only when codes are "embedded in organisational culture and actively enforced" (§2.4).
- "Without complementary punitive mechanisms and governance bodies to 'step in' when self-governance fails, a principled approach runs the risk of merely providing false assurances of ethical or trustworthy AI" (§2.4).
- "Signing up to self-regulatory codes lacking clearly defined and enforceable obligations costs developers nothing" (§3).
- Recommendation 3, "License developers of high-risk AI", and recommendation 4, "Shift from professional ethics to organisational ethics" (§3).
