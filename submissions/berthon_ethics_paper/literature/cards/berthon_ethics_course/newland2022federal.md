## Summary
The first report of the Department of the Interior's investigation of the Federal Indian boarding school
system, submitted by Assistant Secretary Bryan Newland to Secretary Haaland under a transmittal letter dated to
the 1 April 2022 deadline; the cover reads "May 2022". The report builds the first official list of Federal
Indian boarding schools: 408 schools on 431 sites in 37 states or then-territories, 1819–1969 (pp. 6, 91). It
sets the schools inside a "twin Federal policy of Indian territorial dispossession and Indian assimilation
through Indian education" (p. 91) and documents how children were brought in: laws after 1871 to compel
attendance, the 1893 Act authorizing the withholding of rations, and transport without parents' consent
(pp. 35–36). It names the schools' "militarized and identity-alteration methodologies" (renaming, hair-cutting,
suppression of languages, drill; p. 7), manual labor as curriculum and as a substitute for funding (pp. 8, 59,
92), corporal punishment (p. 8), "rampant" abuse (p. 56), burial sites at about 53 schools and over 500 recorded
child deaths at 19 schools (pp. 8–9). It closes with findings and eight recommendations and says further
investigation is needed (pp. 9, 91–92). The PDF itself carries no volume number; Vol. II followed in July 2024
(`pdfs/E/fibsi2.txt`, cover "Vol. II July 2024").

## Key facts it relies on
- System size: 408 Federal Indian boarding schools on 431 sites across 37 states or then-territories,
  1819–1969 (p. 6; finding 1, p. 91).
- Compulsion: "After 1871, Congress enacted laws to compel Indian parents to send their children to school";
  the Act of 3 March 1893 let the Secretary withhold rations, "including those guaranteed by treaties" (p. 35).
- "There is ample evidence in Federal records demonstrating that the United States coerced, induced, or
  compelled Indian children to enter the Federal Indian boarding school system" (p. 36); Navajo children
  "transported to boarding schools ... without their parents' consent" (p. 36).
- Finding 2: children "were induced or compelled by the Federal Government to experience the Federal Indian
  boarding school system" (p. 91).
- Language: methods included "discouraging or preventing the use of American Indian, Alaska Native, and
  Native Hawaiian languages" (p. 7); finding 7: languages suppressed "through punishment, including corporal
  punishment" (p. 92).
- Labor: the system "predominately included manual labor ... as part of school curricula" (p. 8); section 9.1,
  "Use of Child Labor as Curricula", opens with the Meriam Report (1928): the labor "would ... constitute a
  violation of child labor laws in most states" (p. 59); finding 6, labor used "to compensate for the poor
  conditions of school facilities and lack of financial support" (p. 92).
- Abuse: "Rampant physical, sexual, and emotional abuse; disease; malnourishment; overcrowding; and lack of
  health care in Indian boarding schools are well-documented" (p. 56).
- Deaths: about 19 schools "accounted for over 500 ... child deaths" with the count expected to rise (p. 9).
- Keyword search of the whole text: "literacy" 0 hits; "read and write" occurs twice, both in narrative of
  Russian schooling in Alaska (p. 65) and missionary schooling in Hawai'i (p. 71), not as a cause of harm.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet E (`literature/facets/E_literacy_imposed.md:85-106`) reads the report as attributing the harm to
  "removal, militarised discipline and assimilation"; "Literacy" does not occur, and "English instruction is
  treated as the vehicle of assimilation". Its pp. 51 and 54 quotations (Kennedy Report: "force the complete
  abandonment of his native language"; ARCIA 1904: teaching the child "to speak English is essentially the
  first step") concern spoken English, not reading.
- The report is preliminary by its own account: findings "remain under investigation", COVID-19 and continuing
  resolutions limited access to records, and the burial and death counts are expected to rise (pp. 6, 9).
- Many quotations in it are the Department's own reproductions of ARCIA, the Meriam Report and the Kennedy
  Report; facet E did not open those at source, and neither did I.
- Ledger P4-042 recorded "no local copy in `pdfs/E/`" and could not confirm labor and abuse. A local copy now
  sits at `pdfs/E/fibsi1.pdf` (29.6 MB, PDF metadata "U.S. Department of the Interior", created 7 May 2022);
  this card confirms labor (pp. 8, 59, 92) and abuse (p. 56) in it.
- The report never uses the phrase "forced labor"; its terms are "manual labor" and "child labor".

## Where the manuscript uses it
- **Line 123**, "the United States Department of the Interior (Newland, 2022) document the removal of
  Indigenous children, forced labor, abuse, and the suppression of languages." Takes: that the report records
  these four harms. Source: removal pp. 35–36 (section 6.3 "Indian Child Removal"); labor pp. 8, 59, 92; abuse
  p. 56; languages pp. 7, 92. **narrower**: removal, abuse and language suppression hold; the report says
  "manual labor" and "child labor", not "forced labor", though the children's attendance was compelled.
- **Line 123**, "Neither attributes the harm to the teaching of reading." Takes: the report does not blame
  reading instruction. Source: whole-text search ("literacy" 0; "read and write" only in Alaska and Hawai'i
  narrative, pp. 65, 71). **holds**: the report attributes harm to removal, assimilation methods, labor and
  punishment. It does name "education" as the means of assimilation (pp. 7, 91), which a reader could take as
  close to the line.
- **Line 123**, "participation was compulsory". Source: pp. 35–36 ("laws to compel"; ration withholding;
  "coerced, induced, or compelled"), p. 91 ("induced or compelled"). **narrower**: the report says "induced or
  compelled", not that attendance was compulsory for every child.
- **Line 123**, "one language of record was enforced". Source: pp. 7, 40, 92 (Native languages discouraged or
  prevented through punishment; English as "common medium"). **narrower**: the report concerns the language of
  speech and instruction; "language of record" is the manuscript's gloss.
- **Line 203**, "Section 3.6 recorded what compulsory participation meant at its worst". Internal cross-reference
  resting on line 123. **narrower**, for the same reason as "compulsory" above.
- **Line 355, reference entry** "Newland, B. (2022). *Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative investigative
  report* (Vol. 1). U.S. Department of the Interior." **holds**: cover gives the title, May 2022 and Assistant
  Secretary Bryan Newland. The PDF carries no "Vol. 1" label; the label distinguishes it from Vol. II (2024).
