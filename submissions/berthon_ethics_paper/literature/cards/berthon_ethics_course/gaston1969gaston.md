## Summary
The Voting Rights Act of 1965 automatically suspended literacy tests in jurisdictions that had used a test or device and had low registration or turnout in 1964. Gaston County, North Carolina, was one. A suspended jurisdiction could reinstate its test only by proving in the District Court for the District of Columbia that no test had been used in the preceding five years "for the purpose or with the effect of denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race or color" (§4(a)). Gaston County sued to reinstate North Carolina's test, which required an applicant to "read and write any section of the Constitution in the English language". The Government answered that the test burdened Black citizens "for whom the County had maintained separate and inferior schools". The Supreme Court, per Harlan J., affirmed the three-judge court's denial of relief. A court may consider whether a literacy requirement has a discriminatory effect "because the State or subdivision which seeks to impose the requirement has maintained separate and inferior schools" (p. 293). The County had not carried its burden. Recent equalization "does nothing for their parents", so "'Impartial' administration of the literacy test today would serve only to perpetuate these inequities in a different form" (pp. 296-297). Black J. dissented.

## Key facts it relies on
- Syllabus (p. 285): the Government contended the test "placed an onerous burden on the Negroes for whom the County had maintained separate and inferior schools". The District Court held the County had not met its burden.
- p. 287: Use of the State's literacy test was suspended on 29 March 1966. The County sued on 18 August 1966 "seeking to reinstate the literacy test". §4(a) puts the burden of proof on the jurisdiction.
- p. 293: "it is appropriate for a court to consider whether a literacy or educational requirement has the 'effect of denying ... the right to vote on account of race or color' because the State or subdivision which seeks to impose the requirement has maintained separate and inferior schools for its Negro residents who are now of voting age."
- pp. 294-295: of persons over 25 in 1960, "the proportion of Negroes with no schooling whatever was twice that of whites in Gaston County".
- p. 295: N.C. Const. Art. VI, §4: every applicant "shall be able to read and write any section of the Constitution in the English language". A Black minister testified it placed "an especially heavy burden on the County's older Negro citizens".
- pp. 296-297: the County's fair 1962 re-registration and recent school integration "fall wide of the mark". "Gaston County systematically deprived its black citizens of the educational opportunities it granted to its white citizens." The judgment was "Affirmed".
- p. 297: "Mr. Justice Black dissents" for the reasons in his *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* opinion.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet D reads *Gaston* as one of four sources that separate literacy from competence: low literacy followed from "segregated and inferior" schools (`facets/D_literacy_as_gate.md`:531). It also notes that *Gaston* and Black J. in *Oregon v. Mitchell* rest the ban on unequal schooling, which *Lassiter* (1959) had not considered (`facets/D_literacy_as_gate.md`:543).
- The holding is statutory and procedural. It interprets §4(a) of the Voting Rights Act and allocates the burden of proof. It does not hold literacy tests unconstitutional. Facet D records that the 1970 Amendments then barred tests nationwide until 1975 (`facets/D_literacy_as_gate.md`:464).
- AUDIT_01 D6 confirmed the facet's *Gaston* quotations against the U.S. Reports text (`audit/AUDIT_01.md`:114).
- Ledger P5-040: in *Gaston* it was the county that ran the schools and sought to reinstate the test. The State's constitution supplied the test, but the actor before the Court was the County.
