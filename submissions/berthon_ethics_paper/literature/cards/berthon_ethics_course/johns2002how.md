## Summary
In an AHR Forum, Johns replies to Elizabeth Eisenstein's critique of his *The Nature of the Book* (1998). Eisenstein posits a single "print culture" that flows from the press itself, marked by fixity, standardization and output, and she judges historians by whether they "acknowledge" that revolution. Johns proposes instead "a cultural history of print": an account of how printers, booksellers, readers and regulators made print what it was, studied locally (pp. 109-110, 116-117). He reads Eisenstein's own readings of his book as commonplacing, lifting remarks out of context (pp. 111-115). He shows that her showcase example, Gassendi's supposed printed call to observe the 1631 transit of Mercury, rests on a chain of secondary readings and never happened as she describes (pp. 115-116). On regulation he denies that censorship split Europe cleanly. The Index was "effectively ignored" in France, and he can think of no Protestant state before 1695 without a press regime (p. 119). Standardization, too, was a social achievement, such as the bishop of Freising checking each printed missal (p. 121). The idea of a "printing revolution" itself dates from about 1800 (pp. 122-123). Contemporaries' fear of disorder kept the book trade intact "with the end of licensing in 1695" and produced the copyright law of 1710 (p. 124).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 109: Johns concedes that *The Nature of the Book*'s "geographical focus is far too restricted" to replace Eisenstein's scope.
- p. 112: quoting his 1998 book, "Supporters of the patents regime … retorted that the security associated with privileges enabled the production of worthwhile texts that would never appear at all in other circumstances."
- p. 119: "in France, for example, the Index was effectively ignored."
- p. 119: "I can think of no Protestant state before 1695 that had no regulatory regime governing its press, so the contrastive appeal to censorship involves an appeal to a null set." n. 33: "The Dutch in particular did have such a regime."
- p. 119: "licensing was part and parcel of the regimes of privilege and registration, which were designed to protect good works as well as to hinder bad."
- p. 122: the "specific concept of a printing revolution came about late in the day, arriving around 1800".
- p. 124: "why did the world of the book not collapse with the end of licensing in 1695? … it did not collapse precisely because contemporaries feared that it would". Their efforts "led to the development of the first copyright law in 1710" (citing Feather).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet B (l. 113, 119, 183, 217): Innis (1951, p. 26) dates "the lapse of the Licensing Act in 1694", against Johns's 1695 (p. 124). Facet B left the conflict unresolved and did not open the statutes.
- Facet B (l. 106, 203): Johns gives no figures on who was excluded from print. He treats licensing as part of privilege and registration rather than as "censorship", which complicates a reading of the Stationers' regime as plain exclusion.
