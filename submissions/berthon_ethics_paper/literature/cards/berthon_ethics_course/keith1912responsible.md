## Summary
This card covers one page of a three-volume survey of the dominions. Page 1082 falls in Part V ("Administration and Legislation"), in Keith's account of how the colonies restricted the immigration of coloured people. The page opens at the end of a speech by Mr. Chamberlain to a Conference with the colonies. Chamberlain argued that exclusion should rest on a ground "which can be defined in an Act of Parliament", reiterated his approval of "the Natal principle", and asked for a settlement that would spare the feelings of the Queen's Indian subjects. Keith then describes the Natal Act No. 1 of 1897. It "embodied the principle of a test of writing in a European language an application for admission in a prescribed form", and it excluded paupers, idiots, diseased persons, criminals and prostitutes. Chamberlain commended the same principle to the Australian colonies in a dispatch of 20 October 1897. In that dispatch the Japanese Minister was reported satisfied with exclusion by a language test. Keith lists the colonies that followed: Western Australia (1897, No. 13), New South Wales (1898, No. 3, restricted to the writing test by the Legislative Council), Tasmania (1898, No. 69) and New Zealand (No. 33, the next year). Victoria's two Houses disagreed, and the page breaks off at the Commonwealth.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 1082: Chamberlain's position that exclusion rest on a ground "which can be defined in an Act of Parliament, and by which the exclusion can be managed with regard to all those whom you really desire to exclude".
- p. 1082: "He then reiterated his approval of the Natal principle".
- p. 1082: "The Natal Act No. 1 of 1897 referred to in the speech by Mr. Chamberlain embodied the principle of a test of writing in a [E]uropean language an application for admission in a prescribed form, as well as excluding paupers, idiots, diseased persons, criminals, and prostitutes".
- p. 1082: the principle was "held up to approval also as regards the question of Japanese susceptibilities in a dispatch of October 20, 1897, from Mr. Chamberlain to the Australian Colonies".
- p. 1082: Western Australia 1897 (No. 13); New South Wales 1898 (No. 3), "the Bill being restricted to the writing test by the Legislative Council"; Tasmania 1898 (No. 69); New Zealand "a similar Act (No. 33) in the next year".
- p. 1082: "In Victoria the two Houses disagreed, and nothing was done."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Keith is a secondary source for the Natal Act. Resolution C1 (P3-071, "blocked" (a)): the Act's own text (Natal *Statutes* 1897, or the enclosure in the Colonial Office correspondence) was not opened. The author must supply its s. 3 to say "European language" on primary authority.
- Resolution C1 (P3-013 note, l. 117): Lake (2006, p. 221) quotes the Natal Premier describing the Bill's test as reading and writing "in the English language". Keith's "a European language" may describe the Act as passed. The two sources differ on the language, and no one has seen the Act to settle it.
- Keith's sentence is about writing out an application in a prescribed form. It is a writing test, not a reading test. The Australian marginal note "See Natal Act 1897, No. 1, s.3" (card `ira1901immigration`) agrees that Natal was the model.
- Facet D (l. 481) found no source for when the Natal test ended.
