## Summary
From the parts read: Kaminski and Urban argue that the United States should adopt an individual right to contest AI decisions. They model it on due process "but adapted for the digital age". They present it as a complement to the ex ante, systemic governance that US proposals favour (abstract, p. 1957). The GDPR (Article 22) already recognizes one. The authors claim to fill a gap with "four contestation archetypes" that vary from rules to standards and from procedure to substance. They illustrate these with the GDPR, the DMCA's notice-and-takedown process, the right to be forgotten and the Fair Credit Billing Act's chargebacks, among others (pp. 1957-1958, contents). Part III.F names a design variable: who decides. A scheme may use "a neutral arbiter, have a mediating platform adjudicate, or have one of the parties themselves adjudicate" (p. 2030). Part IV.B.2 holds that a neutral arbiter is "core to due process". A right to contest private-sector AI, however, "is unlikely to include a neutral arbiter, at least at first bite". Neither their archetypes nor Article 22 has one: "interested actors make the decisions" (pp. 2038-2039). They suggest an arbiter with "an independent position within a company" or hybrid private-public appeals. The Conclusion calls the right "both normatively desirable and practically feasible" (p. 2047).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet N2 (l. 210, 289, 310): Kaminski and Urban already make the manuscript's independence point for private-sector AI generally. What the manuscript adds is the triad (the "mediating platform" of p. 2030 is the party it analyses) and the historical precedent. Facet N2 also notes that the Platform Work Directive, Art. 10(2), has since enacted a version of their "independent position within a company".
- The authors do not treat the missing neutral arbiter purely as a failing. They argue that "setting up the right incentives for a nonneutral decision-maker can improve both outcomes and legitimacy" (p. 2039). They see "merit in a hybrid system in which initial arbiters are not necessarily neutral but those who review their decisions are", citing Van Loo (pp. 2038-2039).
- Facet M2 (l. 88, 148) called this paper the field's move from informing the subject to contestability, and a citation a referee would expect.
