## Summary
Leonard's 1949 study, reissued by UC Press in 1992, argues that romances of chivalry shaped the conquistadors'
imagination and that fiction reached Spanish America in quantity throughout the colonial period. I read
chapter VII, "Light Literature and the Law" (pp. 78-88). Leonard attacks a "legend", repeated by
nineteenth-century Spanish American historians, that the Crown sealed the colonies off from fiction (pp.
78-80). The royal orders were real. The Queen, "acting as sovereign in the absence of her imperial lord",
instructed the House of Trade at Seville on 4 April 1531 to stop "books of fiction in the vernacular" from
going to the Indies, "since this is bad practice for the Indians" (p. 81). The order was repeated to Viceroy
Mendoza on 14 July 1536 and by Prince Philip to the House of Trade on 13 September 1543 (pp. 81-83). Leonard
reads the repetition as proof that the bans were "largely ineffective from the outset" (p. 83). He also reads
their aim as protecting Indian converts, not Spaniards, from confusing fiction with Scripture (pp. 83-84).
Merchants kept shipping fiction, and officials looked away (pp. 84-85). Meanwhile the Crown and friars taught
Indians to read Spanish and Latin (pp. 85-87). The 1992 introduction (pp. x-xii) places the book before the
ethnohistorical turn.

## Key facts it relies on
- Order of the Queen to the House of Trade, Ocaña, 4 April 1531: "many books of fiction in the vernacular ...
  go to the Indies; since this is bad practice for the Indians and something with which it is not well for
  them to be concerned or read" (p. 81).
- The same order allows only books "relating to the Christian religion and morality upon which the
  above-mentioned Indians ... may practice the art of reading" (p. 81).
- Instruction to Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza, 14 July 1536, repeats the ban; "there has not been due care in
  the enforcement of this matter" (pp. 81-82).
- Order signed by the Prince, later Philip II, to the House of Trade, 13 September 1543 (pp. 82-83).
- "The need to repeat these commands at short intervals is in itself an indication that the prohibition of
  fictional books in the overseas realms was largely ineffective from the outset" (p. 83).
- The decrees' "primary concern" was the Indians' reading, not that of Spaniards and Creoles (pp. 83-84).
- Toledo reported in Peru (c. 1569) that the royal order "had not been observed or executed up to now"
  (p. 88).
- Leonard's work first appeared in 1949 (introduction, p. xi).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Adorno's introduction (pp. xi-xii) says Leonard's book "reveals an unwillingness to reconcile a valedictory
  interpretation of Spanish history with the known history of the conquests", and places it before Gibson's
  and Murra's ethnohistory. His reading of royal intent as benevolent should be weighed with that in mind.
- Facet M5 (`literature/facets/M5_wider_cases.md:160, 256`): the Spanish case bans what may be read by people
  who can read, not learning to read, and the bans did not work. The paper should name the case as withholding
  of reading matter, not of the competence.
- Facet M5 (`:300`): Leonard's archival sources for the 1531 and 1543 orders sit in his notes, which were not
  opened. The *Recopilación de Indias* title was not verified.
