## Summary
The Digital Services Act regulates intermediary services in the internal market. I read only the provisions the
manuscript relies on. An "online platform" is a hosting service that stores and disseminates information to the
public at a recipient's request (Art 3). Section 3, "Additional provisions applicable to providers of online platforms", which holds Arts 20–21, does not apply to
micro or small enterprises unless designated very large (Art 19). Art 20 requires online platforms to give
recipients, for at least six months, a free electronic internal complaint-handling system against decisions
taken because information is illegal or incompatible with the terms and conditions: removal or restriction of
content, suspension or termination of service or of the account, and restriction of monetisation (Art 20(1)).
Complaint decisions must be reasoned and "taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not
solely on the basis of automated means" (Art 20(5)–(6)). Art 21 lets recipients choose a certified out-of-court
dispute settlement body, impartial and independent of both sides (Art 21(3)(a)), without prejudice to going to
court "at any stage" (Art 21(1)); the body "shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement" (Art 21(2)).
If the body finds for the recipient, the platform bears all fees and reasonable expenses (Art 21(5)). The
Regulation applies from 17 February 2024 (Art 93(2)).

## Key facts it relies on
- Art 20(1): complaints lie against decisions taken "on the grounds that the information provided by the
  recipients constitutes illegal content or is incompatible with its terms and conditions", including (c)
  "decisions whether or not to suspend or terminate the recipients' account".
- Art 20(4): providers "shall reverse" unfounded decisions "without undue delay".
- Art 20(6): decisions are "taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the
  basis of automated means."
- Art 21(1), first subpara: recipients "shall be entitled to select any out-of-court dispute settlement body that
  has been certified"; third subpara: without prejudice to the right "to initiate, at any stage, proceedings to
  contest those decisions ... before a court".
- Art 21(2), third subpara: "The certified out-of-court dispute settlement body shall not have the power to impose
  a binding settlement of the dispute on the parties."
- Art 21(3)(a): certification requires the body to be "impartial and independent, including financially
  independent", of providers and recipients.
- Art 21(4): decisions within 90 days, extendable to 180 for highly complex disputes.
- Art 21(5): if the body finds for the recipient, "the provider of the online platform shall bear all the fees";
  for recipients the procedure is "free of charge or at a nominal fee".
- Art 19(1): Section 3 does not apply to micro or small enterprises (except Art 24(3)).
- Art 93(2): applies from 17 February 2024.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet N2 (`literature/facets/N2_platform_law.md:46-53, 278`) finds the manuscript's line 173 "the cost of
  reaching it falls on the weaker party" wrong for the DSA route, because Art 21(5) shifts fees to the platform
  when the user wins; it holds only for the court route.
- Facet N2 (`:53`, `:111-117`, `:281`) raises a scope doubt: CJEU *Elite Taxi* (C-434/15, para. 40) classified an
  UberPop-type service as "a service in the field of transport", not an information society service, so the DSA's
  Arts 20–21 may not reach the paper's ride-hail drivers.
- Ledger P5-017: Art 20 complaints cover content and account decisions on illegality or terms-incompatibility
  grounds, not every platform decision (pay, for example).
- Facet N2 (`:249-255`, `:318`) cites Appeals Centre Europe, a certified Art 21 body (Transparency Report, May 2026,
  p. 11): 72% default decisions because platforms withheld the content in suspension disputes, and implementation
  largely unknown; I did not open it.
