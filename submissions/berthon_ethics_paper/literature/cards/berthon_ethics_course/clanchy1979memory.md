## Summary
Clanchy traces England's shift, between the Norman Conquest and Edward I, from proving rights by memory, witnesses and symbolic objects to proving them by documents. Royal records multiplied, and with them the need for "at least a nodding acquaintance with letters" down to peasant smallholders, who by 1300 made grants by charter (Hunnisett p. 67). Documents "did not immediately inspire trust" (p. 230, via Ong); Ong (p. 94) adds his own gloss that witnesses could be questioned and texts could not. Reading usually meant hearing and writing meant dictating (p. 219, via Hunnisett). Under Edward I the Crown's attorneys argued in the quo warranto prosecutions that only a written charter warranted a franchise, a rule that "threatened to disfranchise nearly all the magnates"; the Crown compromised in 1290 and conceded tenure "from time out of mind". The Earl Warenne's "ancient and rusty sword", produced as his warrant, is told by Walter of Guisborough (p. 21) and Clanchy calls the story "certainly inaccurate in details" but telling. The shift made forgery central: monks, the writing experts, "were also the greatest forgers" (snippet; page unknown). The statute of Exeter of 1285 required even bondsmen to hold seals.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Ong (1982, p. 96) overstates the forgery count as "44 are certainly forged, only 64 certainly authentic"; Clanchy's words are "likely to be spurious" with doubts about 56 more.
- Hunnisett (1981, p. 67): the extent and depth of lay literacy in 1300 "must remain conjectural, and Dr. Clanchy is not completely consistent about this"; Robertson (1980, p. 613): Clanchy "may be overly optimistic about the literacy of manorial reeves".
- Facet A: Harrison (2012) finds no general shift to written evidence in two manor courts and shows lords seizing serfs' charters; the book's subject is mainly the elite and royal administration.
