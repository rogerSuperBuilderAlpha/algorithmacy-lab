## Summary

Moser introduces a special issue marking 150 years since the Valuev Directive. On 30 July (Old Style 18 July) 1863 the Russian Minister of Internal Affairs, Petr Valuev, issued a secret directive. It stopped the approval of Ukrainian-language books with religious content, of a pedagogical nature, or "intended for mass consumption", and it allowed only belles-lettres (p. 3). In 1876 the Ems Ukaz imposed "an even more rigorous ban" that stayed in force, with minor amendments, until 1905 (p. 3). Moser quotes the directive's stated ground, in his own translation from Miller: that a distinct "Little Russian" language "did not, does not and cannot exist" (p. 3, n. 1). Ukrainian, he argues, was left a "peasant language". Russian served as the language of schools, media and administration while other European nationalities built theirs (p. 3). He then summarises the issue's contributions (pp. 4–6). Johannes Remy's article shows that censors often banned works merely for being in Ukrainian. Remy attributes breaches in the 1870s to corrupt censors and finds that the restrictions "gradually eroded before they were repealed" (p. 6). Maxim Tarnawsky argues that Ukrainian literature grew but stayed invisible, and that the bans strengthened Ukrainian identity across imperial borders (p. 6).

## Key facts it relies on

- The Valuev Directive, 30 July (OS 18 July) 1863: "the approval of books in the Little Russian language that have religious content as well as those of a pedagogical nature or that are intended for mass consumption" was to cease; "only those works which are in the category of belles-lettres" were permitted (p. 3).
- The Ems Ukaz, 30 (OS 18) May 1876, signed by Aleksandr II: "an even more rigorous ban of the Ukrainian language" that "(with minor amendments) stayed in force until the Russian Revolution of 1905" (p. 3).
- The directive's ground: "as the Valuev Directive put it, 'a [distinct] Little Russian [i.e., Ukrainian] language did not, does not and cannot exist' (Miller 240-41)" (p. 3).
- Note 1: all quotations from the directive come from Russian originals found in Miller, in Moser's translation (p. 3). The Works Cited list Miller's book as Aleteiia, 2000.
- "The language of instruction at 'Little Russian' schools was thus Russian"; Ukrainian "was doomed to largely remain a 'peasant language'" (p. 3).
- Remy: censors "frequently prohibited the publication of manuscripts merely because of their Ukrainian language"; 1870s breaches were "a result of the corruption of Russian censorship organs"; the restrictions "gradually eroded before they were repealed, although their impact continued right up to the revolution" (pp. 5–6).

## Critical notes from the literature

- Moser reports the directive's ground in his own translation from Miller, so the manuscript quotes the circular at second hand (ledger P3-042). Ledger P3-042 gives Miller as "2003"; Moser's Works Cited gives 2000 (Aleteiia), and facet M5 cites a "Miller 2003" through Danylenko. The edition question is open.
