## What it argues

The abstract (p. 1): the two standard face benchmarks "are overwhelmingly composed of lighter-skinned subjects (79.6% for IJB-A and 86.2% for Adience)"; the authors "introduce a new facial analysis dataset which is balanced by gender and skin type" (the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark, labelled with the dermatologist-approved Fitzpatrick scale), "evaluate 3 commercial gender classification systems using our dataset and show that darker-skinned females are the most misclassified group (with error rates of up to 34.7%). The maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males is 0.8%. The substantial disparities in the accuracy of classifying darker females, lighter females, darker males, and lighter males in gender classification systems require urgent attention if commercial companies are to build genuinely fair, transparent and accountable facial analysis algorithms." Key findings (§4.1, p. 8): "All classifiers perform better on male faces than female faces (8.1% − 20.6% difference in error rate)"; "better on lighter faces than darker faces (11.8% − 19.2% difference)"; "worst on darker female faces (20.8% − 34.7% error rate)"; Microsoft and IBM "perform best on lighter male faces (error rates of 0.0% and 0.3% respectively)"; "The maximum difference in error rate between the best and worst classified groups is 34.4%." The vendors are Microsoft, IBM and Face++ (§4.2, p. 8); "None of the commercial gender classifiers chosen for this analysis reported performance metrics on existing gender estimation benchmarks in their provided documentation. The Face++ terms of use explicitly disclaim any warranties of accuracy" (p. 8).

The surveillance framing is the paper's own, argued by citation. "Someone could be wrongfully accused of a crime based on erroneous but confident misidentification of the perpetrator from security video footage analysis" (p. 1). "At least 117 million Americans are included in law enforcement face recognition networks. A yearlong research investigation across 100 police departments revealed that African-American individuals are more likely to be stopped by law enforcement and be subjected to face recognition searches than individuals of other ethnicities (Garvie et al., 2016). False positives and unwarranted searches pose a threat to civil liberties" (p. 2). "Face detection and classification algorithms are also used by US-based law enforcement for surveillance and crime prevention purposes" (p. 3). The conclusion asks for "increased demographic and phenotypic transparency and accountability in artificial intelligence" (§5, p. 12).

## Relation to the argument

Repairs the draft clause in "Moral Hazards," bullet 4: "technologies that expose marginalized populations to automated surveillance and exclusion." The planner's audit first guessed this paper as the "Stanford" referent; that was wrong (MIT Media Lab and Microsoft Research), and wangkosinski2018.md now carries "Stanford" and "inevitable." What Gender Shades carries is the surveillance-and-exclusion clause, and it carries it better than Kosinski: the classifiers audited are products sold as APIs, the error falls on darker-skinned women, and the paper ties the vendors' technology to law-enforcement face-recognition networks that already search African-American faces disproportionately (pp. 2–3). Proposed wording: "Commercial face classifiers misclassify darker-skinned women at error rates up to 34.7 percent against at most 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men, and the same vendors' technology sits in law-enforcement face-recognition networks that already search African-American faces disproportionately (Buolamwini & Gebru 2018)."

A second point serves the paper's thesis directly. The remedy the authors ask for is vendor transparency and accountability — performance reporting broken down by subgroup (p. 12) — and Gebru is a coauthor of the Model Cards proposal that mitchell2019.md records. The audit's remedy, like the card's, is disclosure to an adopter or regulator, never standing for the person searched; the paper can cite the two together as the field's disclosure remedy in its strongest form.

## Caution

The task audited is binary gender classification, not identification or verification; the error rates are for gender labels and do not transfer to face-matching accuracy. Three vendors, API versions as of 2017. The 117-million figure and the police-department findings are Garvie et al.'s (2016), cited rather than measured; the paper demonstrates disparity in a laboratory benchmark and argues the surveillance harm by inference. The Pilot Parliaments Benchmark is built from official parliamentary portraits, so image conditions are more uniform than field surveillance footage. Nothing in the paper concerns a system that binds two people.
