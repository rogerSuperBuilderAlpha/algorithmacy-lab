## What it argues

The technical claim is that diffusion models, assumed "impervious to traditional data poisoning attacks, which typically require poison samples approaching 20% of the training set," are in fact vulnerable, because "the number of training samples associated with a specific concept or prompt is generally on the order of thousands" — "concept sparsity" — and because poison samples "can be carefully crafted to maximize poison potency" (Abstract, §1). Nightshade "can completely control the output of a prompt in Stable Diffusion's newest model (SDXL) with less than 100 poisoned training samples," its images "look visually identical to their benign counterparts," its effects "'bleed through' to related concepts," and "a moderate number of Nightshade attacks on independent prompts can destabilize a model and disable its ability to generate images for any and all prompts" (Abstract). The policy claim is in §8. "It is increasingly evident that there is significant power asymmetry in the tension between AI companies that build/train models, and content owners trying to protect their intellectual property. As legal cases and regulatory efforts move slowly forward, the only measures available to content owners are 'voluntary' measures such as opt-out lists [101] and do-not-scrape/train directives [102] in robots.txt. Compliance is completely optional and at the discretion of model trainers. ... Finally, there are no reliably ways to detect if and when these opt-outs or directives are violated, and thus no way to verify compliance." Glaze and similar tools "are insufficient for this purpose," being "optimized to disrupt local fine-tuning operations." Nightshade, by contrast, "can provide a powerful disincentive for model trainers to respect opt-outs and do not crawl directives": IP owners "can all apply prompt-specific poisoning to their images, and (possibly) coordinate with other content owners on shared terms" — Disney poisoning "Cinderella" while coordinating with others on "Mermaid." It works because high potency means "uploading Nightshade samples widely can have the desired outcome, even if only a small portion of poison samples are actually crawled," because "machine unlearning ... is limited in scalability," and because even if detected it "would act as proactive 'do-not-train' filter that prevents models from training on these samples" (§8). The authors report 250K downloads in the first five days of release and that "Google, Meta, Stability.ai and OpenAI have all been made aware of this work prior to this publication" (§8).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9, and this is the source that says why a *signal* of refusal is not a refusal. The paper's §8 is a compact statement of the condition Principle II responds to: the opt-out exists, the mediator decides whether to honour it, and the refuser cannot verify compliance. Nightshade's answer is to make non-compliance costly rather than to ask for it to be respected — to give the do-not-train directive teeth — which is the difference between a hermeneutic remedy (declare your preference and hope it is read) and a structural one (change what the other party can do). Two moves in §8 are the paper's own argument stated by security engineers. First, the coordination move: owners who "coordinate with other content owners on shared terms" are Simmel's two parties combining, which is what dissolves the *tertius*'s advantage, and it is Zong and Matias's (2024) cost-redistribution facet (each poisoner lowers the cost of the next refusal, as with Tor). Second, the enforceability move: the authors are explicit that a voluntary opt-out with no verification is "completely optional," which is the empirical premise for saying that GDPR Article 21(5)'s "automated means" and robots.txt are necessary but not sufficient. What CUTS AGAINST, and the paper must say so, is that Nightshade is an attack. The poisoned model is degraded for every downstream user, including parties who never scraped anything, so the cost of this refusal is redistributed onto bystanders, and the paper's Principle II — refusal "without losing basic utility" — has to say whose utility. The relation is also dyadic in the paper's framing (owner against trainer), with the co-ordinating owners on the same side; the counterpart whose coordination the refusal severs is nowhere modelled. Finally, the escalation logic is DiSalvo's adversarial design (already carded) at its most literal, and the paper should decide whether Principle II licenses that or only the legal and interface-level forms.

## Caution

The attack sections were skimmed; the "less than 100" and "250 attacks" figures are the authors' summary. §8 is advocacy by the tool's builders, released as a product with a large uptake they themselves report, and the claim that Glaze is "insufficient" for this purpose is a comparison of their own two tools. Efficacy against models with data-curation defences is treated in §7, which was not read closely. The arXiv v3 text may differ from the IEEE version, and no IEEE page numbers are attached to quotations. Nothing in the paper concerns coordination through a platform; the "power asymmetry" is between content owners and model trainers.
