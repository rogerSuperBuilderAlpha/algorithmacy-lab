## What it argues

"'interoperability' means the ability to exchange information and mutually use the information which has been exchanged through interfaces or other solutions, so that all elements of hardware or software work with other hardware and software and with users in all the ways in which they are intended to function" (Art. 2(29)). Article 6(7): "The gatekeeper shall allow providers of services and providers of hardware, free of charge, effective interoperability with, and access for the purposes of interoperability to, the same hardware and software features accessed or controlled via the operating system or virtual assistant... as are available to services or hardware provided by the gatekeeper. Furthermore, the gatekeeper shall allow business users and alternative providers of services provided together with, or in support of, core platform services, free of charge, effective interoperability with... the same operating system, hardware or software features... as are available to, or used by, that gatekeeper when providing such services," subject to "strictly necessary and proportionate measures to ensure that interoperability does not compromise the integrity" of the system. Article 6(9): "The gatekeeper shall provide end users and third parties authorised by an end user, at their request and free of charge, with effective portability of data provided by the end user or generated through the activity of the end user... including by providing, free of charge, tools to facilitate the effective exercise of such data portability, and including by the provision of continuous and real-time access to such data." Article 6(10) gives business users and their authorised third parties "effective, high-quality, continuous and real-time access to, and use of, aggregated and non-aggregated data" generated by their end users, with personal data only "when the end users opt in." Article 7 requires gatekeepers' number-independent messaging services to "make the basic functionalities... interoperable with" other providers' services "by providing the necessary technical interfaces or similar solutions... upon request, and free of charge" (Art. 7(1)), with a published "reference offer laying down the technical details and general terms and conditions" (Art. 7(4)), end-to-end encryption "preserved across the interoperable services" (Art. 7(3)), and end users who "shall remain free to decide whether to make use of the interoperable basic functionalities" (Art. 7(7)). Recital 57 states the aim: "to allow competing third parties to interconnect through interfaces or similar solutions to the respective features as effectively as the gatekeeper's own services or hardware." Recital 70 forbids circumvention "by using unjustified technical protection measures, discriminatory terms of service, unlawfully claiming a copyright on application programming interfaces or providing misleading information."

## Relation to the argument

RQ8: the legal precondition for Principle I. Sorted: **precondition for counter-delegation proper, not itself a counter-agent**. Three findings. First, the statute already has the actor the paper needs and a name for it: "third parties authorised by an end user" (Art. 6(9)) is a client-side agent with a right of "continuous and real-time access" to the user's own activity data. That is the narrowest possible counter-agent — it reads out, it does not act in — but it is on the books, free of charge, and it is the seam through which Keller's (keller2021.md) "Magic APIs" would have to pass. Second, the interoperability duties (6(7), 7) run to *competing providers* and *messaging services*, not to the ranking, matching or pricing functions that make a platform a mediator in the construct's sense; nothing obliges a gatekeeper to accept a user's agent as a counter-party to its determinations. Jennings et al. (jennings2001.md) presume a protocol both sides accept; the DMA supplies one only for messages and OS features. Third, the messaging article shows what a mandated seam looks like when it exists — a reference offer, a request, a three-month clock, preserved encryption — and the paper can use it as the template for what a counter-agent right would require. CUTS AGAINST the paper's Principle I as currently drafted: the one interoperability the law mandates is between *intermediaries* (messaging services, app stores), not between a party's agent and the intermediary that binds her; the paper should say that the legal ground for parity is partial and that 6(9) is a data right, not a standing right. The sovereignty chapter's reading of the DMA as opening exit against a contingent gate, not conferring standing over a necessary function, is confirmed by the interoperability articles.

## Caution

Only the interoperability and portability provisions were read; Articles 5 and 6(1)–(6), the designation rules and the Commission's specification decisions were not re-read here, and the 2025 non-compliance decisions are outside this card. The DMA applies to designated gatekeepers only, so most of the platforms in the construct's cases (labour, delivery, marketplaces below the thresholds) are not bound. Whether any "third party authorised by an end user" has in fact built a service on Art. 6(9) access is an empirical question this card does not answer.
