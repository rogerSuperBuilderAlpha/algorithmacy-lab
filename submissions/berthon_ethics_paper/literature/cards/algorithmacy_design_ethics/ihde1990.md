## What it argues

Ihde's "phenomenology of technics" (the book's Program One) analyses human–technology–world relations into four types, which Verbeek renders with Ihde's notations: embodiment, (human – technology) → world, where the technology withdraws into the body (eyeglasses, the phone through which "we speak with other people"); hermeneutic, human → (technology – world), where "human beings read how technologies represent the world" (MRI, metal detector, the wristwatch in Hickman's summary); alterity, human → technology (world), where the technology is a quasi-other; and background, human (technology/world), where it is the context (heating, the refrigerator's hum) (verbeek2015, p. 29). On alterity, Corti et al. quote Ihde: "Technological otherness is a quasi-otherness, stronger than mere objectness but weaker than the otherness found within the animal kingdom or the human one." Program Two, cultural hermeneutics, carries multistability: the same artifact stabilizes differently in different contexts, Hickman's hammer that "can drive nails, serve as a paperweight, or function as a weapon," which Verbeek uses to say a technology's "mediating power is hardly predictable" (verbeek2014, p. 86). I can give no page numbers for any of this from the book itself.

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, structural axis: the hermeneutic relation is the nearest existing relation to an interpreting intermediary, because the instrument reads the world for the human and the human reads the instrument, but the reading is one-way and the world does not read back. The construct's bilateral hermeneutic relation would put a second human where Ihde puts the world and let the instrument read in both directions; nothing in the four relations, as reported, provides that. The alterity relation is the wrong fit for a platform, since a quasi-other is faced, not read through, and the construct's intermediary is precisely not the party one is coordinating with. Ihde's framework has no threshold either; every relation is a mediation. RQ2: multistability is the theoretical ground for the prediction that one seam stabilizes differently for the worker and the customer, but it is a claim about artifacts across cultures and uses, and its application to two simultaneous parties in one interaction is the paper's extension.

Against the tightened definition (adaptivity pass, 2026-09-18). Multistability is the field's only dynamic concept and it is dynamic on the human side: the same hammer is nail-driver, paperweight or weapon depending on who takes it up (Rosenberger and Verbeek 2015, pp. 26–29, on Ihde's Necker-cube origins and "relational strategy"). The artifact holds still while readings vary. Wellner (2024, p. 10) marks the difference precisely: a bench "come[s] in various designs," an AI system "show[s] different responses ... even to the same user at the same time and place." The four relations are therefore an inventory of fixed mediations read variously, and the hermeneutic relation, the nearest analogue to an interpreting intermediary, has the instrument represent a world that does not read back. No remedy, no second human; and the Ihde text itself is still unread, so this classification rests on the field's own summaries of it.
