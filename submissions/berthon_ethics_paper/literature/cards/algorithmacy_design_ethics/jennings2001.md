## What it argues

An agent is "an encapsulated computer system that is situated in some environment and that is capable of flexible, autonomous action in that environment in order to meet its design objectives" (p. 2, after Wooldridge). Most problems "require or involve multiple agents: to represent the decentralised nature of the problem, the multiple loci of control, the multiple perspectives and/or the competing interests," and "perhaps the most fundamental and powerful mechanism for managing inter-agent dependencies at run-time is negotiation—the process by which a group of agents come to a mutually acceptable agreement on some matter" (pp. 2–3). It "is required both when the agents are self interested and when they are cooperative" (p. 3). Research divides into "Negotiation Protocols" (rules, "the permissible types of participants (e.g. the negotiators and any relevant third parties)"), "Negotiation Objects" (single-issue price to "hundreds of issues"), and "Agents' Decision Making Models" (pp. 3–4). Negotiation is "a distributed search through a space of potential agreements" (p. 6); the minimum capability is "the ability to make and respond to proposals" (p. 7), enriched by critiques and counter-proposals (pp. 8–9), and by arguments — "threats," "rewards," "appeals" — that "modify the recipient's region of acceptability or its rating function" (p. 11). Three method families: game-theoretic (mechanism design with properties such as Pareto efficiency, individual rationality, stability, simplicity, and "Distribution: A protocol should ideally be designed to ensure that there is no single point of failure (such as a single arbitrator)," pp. 13–15), whose limits are that "human preferences cannot be characterised even by a simple ordering over outcomes" and that perfect rationality "cannot actually be attained" (pp. 16–17); heuristic (the authors' tactics, trade-off and issue-manipulation mechanisms, pp. 18–20); and argumentation-based, in which "agents may not be truthful in the arguments that they generate," so recipients must weigh "credibility" (p. 22). The challenges section names three gaps: a best-practice repository; "knowledge elicitation and acquisition for negotiation behaviour... At present, there is virtually no work on how a user can instruct an agent to negotiate on their behalf. Such instruction needs to convey the broad negotiation attitude that the agent should adopt, the extent to which the agent can negotiate autonomously, and the degrees of freedom that the agent can explore"; and "predictable negotiation behaviour... to ensure that users are comfortable to delegate negotiation decisions to an autonomous piece of software and that when they do so they are sure that the agent will act within its negotiation mandate" (pp. 24–25; the repository point is on p. 24, the two quoted gaps on p. 25).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. This is the card that shows a counter-agent negotiating with a platform's agent is not science fiction: by 2001 an established field had protocols, decision models and argumentation for exactly that exchange. Sorted: **counter-delegation proper (the technical substrate)** — agents that act for a principal, against other agents, in real time — but with the human side empty; the authors say so in the one sentence the paper should quote, that there is "virtually no work on how a user can instruct an agent to negotiate on their behalf." Algorithmacy is what that sentence names: the competence to set the mandate, the autonomy, and the degrees of freedom of an agent that contests for you, and to know when it has exceeded them. Two further points. Their "Distribution" desideratum — no "single arbitrator" as a point of failure — is a negotiation-theory statement of the construct's structural axis; a platform that both matches and prices is the single arbitrator this literature designs against. And the argumentation strand (agents may lie; credibility must be assessed) is the interpretive component of the construct in agent form. CUTS AGAINST the construct in one way: the whole field presumes the counterpart is *willing* to negotiate under a protocol, whereas the construct's intermediary "commits determinations" without a protocol for counter-proposals; Principle I therefore needs an interoperability precondition (see eu2022dma.md) before any of this machinery applies.

## Caution

A keynote surveying the authors' own work, from 2000; it predates learning-based and LLM negotiators entirely, and its examples are service contracts and auctions. The preprint's pagination is not the journal's. The paper contains no empirical evaluation and no deployed system, and its human user is mentioned only in the challenges section. The "third parties" it admits are protocol participants such as auctioneers, not an intermediary with its own objective.
