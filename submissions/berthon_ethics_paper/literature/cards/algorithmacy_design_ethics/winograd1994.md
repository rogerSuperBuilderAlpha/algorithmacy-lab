# Winograd, T. (1994). Categories, disciplines, and social coordination. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, 2(3), 191–197.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/BF00749016 · **Read depth:** full_text (PDF at lri.fr/~mbl/ENS/CSCW/2015/papers/Winograd-JCSCW94.pdf, Kluwer page images with journal pagination) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed reply in the field's journal; a position piece · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator (the Coordinator as a shared "language game" structuring workflow between people), with the designer as the party who supplies the structure · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Winograd's reply grants Suchman's premise and rejects her inference. Speech act theory "starts out with the goal of finding categories that can be applied to recurrent patterns of social action through language," and "no systematic account can fully capture 'the richness of mental life or social interaction'" (p. 192); the theory becomes a "doctrine" only "when it is taken to be a full account, rather than a basis for building objects for people to use" (p. 192). He rejects Suchman's "is" and stands by "can be theorized as," and frames the design question as "How do you design to augment people's capacity to act?" (p. 192). The core analogy is bookkeeping: his grandfather ran a small business out of his pocket, but "you cannot run even a moderately small company without regularized (disciplined) accounting procedures, which enable people to follow what is happening in situations far removed in space and time from their personal setting" (pp. 193–194). Hence the abstract's claim that "explicit speech act theory, like explicit accounting procedures, enforces a kind of uniformity that is necessary in any communication situation where ambiguity and vagueness cannot be routinely resolved through direct personal contact and knowledge" (p. 191). "The language game does not determine a person's actions, it provides the space of actions in which one can move" (p. 194); "Structure is not an imposition of control for authoritarian motives, but a necessity of continued operation. The question is not whether to impose standardized regimes, but how to do so appropriately" (p. 195). He then concedes three points from Suchman: explicit representation of commitments suits some settings and not others (engineering change orders in manufacturing, yes; a telecommunications research lab, no; email-served communities experience it "as an imposition," p. 195); representations must be built "with the participation of the people who live the situations being represented" (p. 195), which Action Technologies' graphical workflow tool attempts; and "it is a dangerous form of blindness to believe that any representation captures what is meaningful to people in a situation" (p. 196). He ends: "People will adapt and reinterpret whatever they find in their environment, and they will do so in ways that simultaneously reproduce the existing social structure and create a clearing for social innovation" (p. 196).

## Relation to the argument

Winograd supplies, from the designer's side, the exact condition under which a mediator between two humans becomes necessary rather than optional: when the parties are no longer "physically coupled," when "direct personal contact and knowledge" cannot resolve ambiguity, a shared structure that both must obey becomes "a precondition for effective cooperation" (p. 191). That is a statement about the *loss of the direct channel* producing the *need for a binding third* — the structural axis in the vocabulary of 1994. It is also Winner's claim (b) in miniature: distributed coordination "requires" a regularized discipline the way a railway requires a timetable. The paper therefore helps RQ6 and RQ9 by showing that CSCW's own founder already argued that scale and distance make the intermediary's authority a practical necessity. It CUTS AGAINST the construct's objective axis, however, and does so directly: for Winograd the structure has no objective of its own — it is a "language game," a notation like Laban's or Mozart's tonal conventions, and "the salient word ... is 'appropriately'" (p. 195). A shared bookkeeping convention does not pursue anything; it is jointly owned. The construct's distinction between a channel (literacy) and a party (algorithmacy) is precisely the distinction Winograd refuses to draw for the Coordinator, and he is probably right about the Coordinator: a fixed, inspectable, participatorily designed protocol is closer to a convention than to an interested party. This gives the paper a clean way to say what changed between 1994 and the platform: the mediator acquired an objective and an inference engine, and the parties lost the ability to inspect the notation. Suchman's 1995 counter — that "the definition of objectives is itself a political process" and "there is no single ... measure of effective coordination within an organization" (1995, p. 93) — is where the objective axis first enters the debate, as a dispute over whose "effectiveness" the structure serves.

## Caution

A reply, not a study; the evidence for the Coordinator's success is Winograd's own report ("much greater success ... in the management of engineering change orders," p. 195) and Medina-Mora et al. (1992), which is an Action Technologies paper. He was closely associated with Action Technologies, the company that sold the product (Flores founded it; Winograd's exact role there is not verified here), and the paper does not state that association — it names Action Technologies only as the maker of a workflow tool (p. 195). His bookkeeping analogy assumes the ledger is mutually inspectable and jointly owned; the paper has nothing to say about a mediator whose ledger is hidden from the parties or whose "appropriateness" is set by an outsider. Do not cite this as evidence that structured coordination is benign; cite it for the argument that distance makes a binding structure necessary and for the explicit denial that such a structure has interests.
