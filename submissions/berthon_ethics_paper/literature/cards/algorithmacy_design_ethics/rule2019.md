## What it argues

From the abstract. Contextual integrity "promises to distinguish between forms of disclosure that are ethically justified and others, based on the social relationships and purposes of the settings in which they occur," but "this article holds that Nissenbaum's claims are not fulfilled. Notions that norms underlying any given domain of human conduct are unambiguous or uncontested simply do not withstand close examination. Indeed, most social norms, particularly in rapidly changing domains of human conduct like privacy practices, are volatile and highly contested. Many different interpretations of the 'correct' norms are possible for any given context, and thinkers are always inclined to embrace those interpretations that most closely fit their pre-existing mind-sets and value positions." The doctrine's resulting "flexibility has helped make the doctrine enormously attractive to policymakers seeking to defuse conflicts ... But it has hardly yielded unambiguous policy prescriptions that all parties could agree on."

## Relation to the argument

Rule is the published critic the plan expected, and his objection is the one the plan predicted: contextual integrity cannot say which norms are the legitimate ones, because the norms of any context are contested and interpreters read their own commitments into them. That is an objection to the *input* of Nissenbaum's heuristic — the "entrenched informational norms" the evaluator is told to explicate — and it lives entirely inside her unit of analysis. Rule does not dispute that privacy is about flows of information about a person, or that the remedy is a norm; he disputes whether the norms can be identified with enough determinacy to prescribe anything.

The paper's critique is orthogonal, and the draft should say so in a clause so a reviewer does not take it for Rule's. This paper grants Nissenbaum her norms — grant that employment's informational norms are settled and legible — and objects that the benchmark built on them has no term for the mediator's determination (unit of analysis), no agent inside the flow who can run the heuristic, and a presumption whose remedy is protective by construction. If Rule is fully answered, the paper's critique stands untouched; if the paper's critique is answered, Rule's remains. The two together bracket the account: indeterminate at the input, silent at the output. Where they meet is Rule's "rapidly changing domains" — the volatility he says defeats norm-identification is the tempo problem the paper locates in the adaptive mediator and that Nissenbaum concedes in `nissenbaum2019.md` (p. 248). Cite Rule for the existing objection in one sentence; do not lean on him for anything the abstract does not contain.

## Caution

Abstract only. The essay's actual arguments, examples and any constructive proposal are unread, and the characterisation of it as purely diagnostic rests on the abstract's silence about alternatives. Rule's own position, from his earlier books, is a data-protection sociologist's — his likely alternative is stronger institutional limits on data collection, which would again be norms/institution — but that is inference from his other work, not from this article. Nothing in the abstract addresses mediators, determinations, matching or platforms.
