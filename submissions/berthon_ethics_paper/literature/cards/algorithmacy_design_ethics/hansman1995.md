# Hansman, R. J., Pritchett, A., & Midkiff, A. (1995). "Party line" information use studies and implications for ATC datalink communications. MIT International Center for Air Transportation / Aeronautical Systems Laboratory. 9 pp.

**Identifier:** NASA NTRS 20020032693 (STI type "preprint", publication date 1995, grant NAG2-716; PDF at ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20020032693/downloads/20020032693.pdf) · a paper of the same title, authors ordered Pritchett, Midkiff & Hansman, appears in *Proc. 14th Digital Avionics Systems Conference* (1995), pp. 26–31, doi:10.1109/DASC.1995.482801 (Crossref-verified, not opened; presumably the same text) · **Read depth:** full_text (NTRS preprint) · **Source-tier:** conference preprint on a NASA/FAA grant; summarizes two surveys and one full-mission simulation · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator — pilot, controller, and the overhearing pilots on a shared frequency, with the channel (broadcast voice vs discretely addressed datalink) as the object of study · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The party line is important, unevenly used, and hard to act on alone. The paper condenses Midkiff & Hansman (1993) and Pritchett & Hansman (1994, 1995). Across all surveyed elements, pilots gave a Critical (5) rating 42% of the time and Important (4) a further 28%; Terminal Area and Final Approach drew over 40% Critical, Cruise under 30%, each successive phase differing from the last at p < 0.01 except the last two (§2). Table 1 lists what a majority rated Critical: aircraft on the landing runway, traffic at uncontrolled and controlled airports, windshear, missed approaches, visibility, thunderstorms, surface winds, braking action, icing, aircraft crossing the active runway, approach clearances, terminal routing, and — telling for the essay — "Error of Controller." Operational type, not flight hours, drove the differences: general-aviation pilots rated PLI important in every phase, airline pilots mainly near the airport. Asked what "the big picture" means, pilots named traffic first and weather second. The simulation scripted nine PLI events into background ATC chatter for seven crews in NASA Ames' MVSRF during the voice-control phase of a datalink experiment. Crews acted on windshear (6 of 6), holding-EFC validity (6 of 7) and turbulence (5 of 7) — low-workload, within their own authority — but on the traffic events they mostly noticed and did nothing: 7 of 7 were aware that the aircraft ahead had not cleared the landing runway and none acted; 5 of 7 never registered a VFR aircraft told it would violate their holding airspace; 6 of 6 missed an aircraft crossing the active runway while they were lined up for takeoff (Table 3). The explanation is social as much as cognitive: "the professional relationship dynamics between flight crewmembers and air traffic controllers may be a reason pilots were observed to be reticent to insinuate that a controller error had taken place based upon PLI alone," and a wrong reading of traffic PLI "has a high perceived penalty" (§3). Loss of PLI, they note, "would affect not only the aircraft equipped with datalink, but also the non-equipped aircraft" (§1). Conclusion: PLI "is an important but not always compelling source of Global Situation Awareness," and datalink is "an opportunity to present the information currently available by Party Line communications in a more reliable, available and intuitive manner" (§4).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9, and the single best empirical record for the essay's structural claim. The overhearing pilot is a third party whose awareness depends on a channel two others are using, and the paper measures what that party does with it: often nothing, because acting on overheard coordination means contesting a controller. That behavioural finding is directly the "contest what it commits" limb of algorithmacy, observed in a human triad before any algorithm arrived — the third party can infer an error and still not challenge the party that committed it. The paper's design remedy (deliver PLI as displays, on request, stored until the pilot has time) is what a mediator does when it re-supplies, on its own terms, the awareness the closed channel took away; the essay can name that as the moment coordination becomes mediated rather than merely observed. Objective axis: absent, as with all datalink work of this era; the mediator has no interest. The paper also supplies the multi-equipage point — that closing the channel for some degrades it for all — which is the lab's "mixed environment" case in the field.

## Caution

Seven crews, one simulator, events scripted to force a testable response, no TCAS in the cockpit; the authors say the crews may have lacked the corroborating picture that would have licensed action. The simulation was a control phase borrowed from another study, so there is no within-study datalink condition. Survey percentages are over all elements pooled and the respondent counts are given in the underlying reports, not here. Author order differs between the NTRS preprint and the DASC proceedings; cite the DASC version for the published record and check its text against the preprint before quoting.

## S2 adversarial verification (2026-09-18)

**Verdict:** confirmed

**What I checked:** Downloaded the NTRS preprint (20020032693, 9 pp.), checked every quotation and survey percentage against the text, and read Tables 1 and 3 from page renders because their layout does not extract cleanly.

**Findings:** Survey figures match: Critical ratings 42% and Important 28% over all elements; over 40% Critical in Terminal Area and Final Approach, under 30% in Cruise; successive phases differ at p < 0.01 except Final Approach versus Terminal Area. Table 1 lists "Error of Controller" under Critical (Ground Ops, Terminal Area and Final Approach) as the card says, along with the other elements named. Table 3 (p. 7) gives exactly the counts the card reports: Windshear 0/0/6, Holding/EFC 0/1/6, Turbulence 1/1/5, Aircraft on Runway of Intended Landing 0/7/0, Traffic Watch While Holding 5/2/0, Aircraft Crossing Active Runway 6/0/0 (not aware/aware no action/action taken). "Seven flight crews flew the experiment" and "Nine specific PLI elements" are on p. 6. All quotations are verbatim, including the "professional relationship dynamics" sentence, "high perceived penalty," the non-equipped-aircraft point (§1), "important but not always compelling source of Global Situation Awareness" and the datalink "opportunity" sentence (§4). One nuance the card omits: the same sentence says the crews "instead indicated awareness and increased their vigilance," which slightly softens "did nothing." The relation-to-argument reading of the overhearing pilot as a third party who infers and does not contest is supported by Table 3 and the p. 7 discussion.
