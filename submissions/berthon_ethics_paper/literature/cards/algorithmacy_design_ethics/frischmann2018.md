# Frischmann, B., & Selinger, E. (2018). *Re-Engineering Humanity*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Identifier:** ISBN 978-1-107-14709-6 · **Read depth:** partial — a publisher-typeset excerpt PDF (front matter, Introduction, chapters 1–11, pp. 1–208 of ~300) hosted on the NYC Rules site; I read the Introduction, chapter 3, and the passages on complacency and friction in chapters 5, 6, 8, 9 and 11, located by search; chapters 12–14 and the appendices were not in the file · **Source-tier:** university-press monograph (law and philosophy of technology) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read (of the excerpt) · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world and designer–stakeholders (platform, user, "techno-social engineers") · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The book's thesis is that "techno-social engineering" — the shaping of people by technologies and the institutions that deploy them — is conditioning us to behave like simple machines: "We are being conditioned to obey. More precisely, we're being conditioned to want to obey" (p. 6). The path is a downward slope toward "programmed lives of convenience and cheap bliss," with cheap bliss defined by analogy to engineered snack food: "Cheap bliss is addictive. If it weren't, you could stop eating after you had exactly one potato chip" (p. 3); chapter 2 ends "To what end? The answer: cheap bliss" (p. 34). Engineered complacency is named on p. 8 as one of four pathways down the slope: "If we're engineered to avoid critically questioning innovation, it's hard for us to pay attention to whether change accords with values we deem important or to deliberate about strategies for avoiding change that threatens our values" (p. 8); the Introduction adds that conventional progress narratives "obscure the truth and engineer complacency" (p. 6). Chapter 3 makes friction the explicit variable. On the slope metaphor, "frictional resistance" is "cultural resistance to the dominant logics driving progress, alternative logics ... or even governance institutions"; slipperiness "relates to countervailing forces that could reduce or even eliminate the friction," and "When techno-social engineering creates complacency and habituation, the effectiveness of such resistance is reduced" (p. 42). The clickwrap chapter shows the mechanism at scale: as boilerplate creeps across contexts "we become more comfortable, complacent, and easier to nudge" (p. 67), and "When such creep leads us to be complacent, to follow the herd and passively accept matters that should require deliberation, our humanity itself is at risk" (p. 80). In the smart-transport chapter they draw the normative line: "Tolerating some congestion, some friction, some inefficiency, even some transaction costs may be necessary to sustain an underdetermined environment conducive to human flourishing" (p. 141). Chapter 9 turns the point on relationships: "so-called frictionless communication doesn't simply make it easier to reach out to others, but also can burden our lives" (p. 158), and attentiveness to others is "one way we go about showing others they're worth the metaphorical trouble — that they aren't valued only in circumstances when they're easy to get along with and don't impose friction on our lives" (p. 162).

## Relation to the argument

Frischmann and Selinger give RQ3 a criterion that sits between Vallor's and the cooptation reading and pulls both ways. Their p. 141 sentence is a formation criterion of Vallor's kind — friction is worth its cost when it keeps the environment "underdetermined," that is, when it leaves the person something to decide — and their p. 42 account makes friction the very thing that lets deliberation resist the slope. Engineered complacency is, on this reading, what a frictionless intermediary does to the competence algorithmacy names: it removes the occasions on which a person would notice what the intermediary is doing. On the objective axis they are explicit that the slope is driven by "powerful companies [that] use smart technologies to gain control over us by framing our choices and nudging us" (p. 3), which is the cooptation reading. But the Manjoo case cuts against "whose objective" as the criterion: the user "appears to say that if technology companies can deliver cheap bliss by optimizing his life, he's all for it. He's even willing to pay for their services with agency and self-determination" (p. 3). The corrupting objective is one the user has adopted as his own, so a test that asks whose objective the friction serves would clear it. Only a formation criterion catches it. Their p. 162 point about relationships is the closest any source in this cluster comes to the mediated triad: frictionless mediation between two people changes what the two owe each other, not just what each gets.

## Caution

I read about two-thirds of the book from an excerpt file and located the friction and complacency passages by search; the final chapters, where the authors say they "revisit this issue" (p. 141), were not available, so the fullest statement of their criterion is unread. The argument is conceptual and polemical, built on thought experiments (Nozick's experience machine, the Manjoo/Alexa column) and secondary citation of behavioural research; no studies of their own. "Techno-social engineering" is deliberately broad — contracts, GPS, fitness trackers, smart cities — and the book does not distinguish channel from party intermediaries. The slippery-slope form of the argument is one the authors themselves acknowledge is speculative (p. 41).
