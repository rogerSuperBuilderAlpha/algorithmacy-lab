## What it argues

A scoping review of 84 documents "containing ethical principles or guidelines for AI" (p. 3), 88% released after 2016, issued mostly by private companies (n=19) and governmental agencies (n=18), then academic and research institutions (n=9), inter-governmental bodies (n=8), non-profits and professional associations (n=7 each) (p. 3); the USA (n=20) and UK (n=14) supply more than a third, the EU's institutions and member states 19 together, and "African and South-American countries are not represented independently from international or supra-national organisations" (p. 5). Two coders, working in Nvivo, tagged 3,457 codes of which 1,180 pertained to ethical principles, mapped them to thirteen categories and merged two, with the literature monitored until 23 April 2019 (pp. 18–20).

"No single ethical principle appeared to be common to the entire corpus of documents, although there is an emerging convergence around the following principles: transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility, and privacy. These principles are referenced in more than half of all the sources" (p. 7). Beneficence, at 41, is not among them.

The non-maleficence section opens: "References to non-maleficence outweigh those to beneficence by a factor of 1.5 and encompass general calls for safety and security or state that AI should never cause foreseeable or unintentional harm" (p. 9). Harm "is primarily interpreted as discrimination, violation of privacy, or bodily harm," with "negative impacts on long-term social well-being" among the "less frequent characterizations" (p. 9), and "Harm-prevention guidelines focus primarily on technical measures and governance strategies" — privacy by design, standards, legislation, "tests, monitoring, audits and assessments" (pp. 9–10). The beneficence section opens: "While promoting good (beneficence in ethical terms) is often mentioned, it is rarely defined, though notable exceptions mention the augmentation of human senses, the promotion of human well-being and flourishing, peace and happiness, the creation of socio-economic opportunities, and economic prosperity" (p. 11); the two sources cited for "well-being and flourishing" are Smart Dubai's *AI Principles & Ethics* (ref. 34) and the IEEE's *Ethically Aligned Design*, first edition (ref. 90). Who should benefit is equally unsettled: "private sector issuers tend to highlight the benefit of AI for customers, though many sources require AI to be shared and to benefit 'everyone', 'humanity', both of the above, 'society', 'as many people as possible', 'all sentient creatures', the 'planet' and the environment" (p. 11).

The autonomy section draws the split the draft needs in the guidelines' own vocabulary: "Some documents refer to autonomy as a positive freedom, specifically the freedom to flourish, to self-determination through democratic means, the right to establish and develop relationships with other human beings, the freedom to withdraw consent, or the freedom to use a preferred platform or technology. Other documents focus on negative freedom, for example freedom from technological experimentation, manipulation or surveillance" (pp. 11–12). "Freedom to flourish" is the Internet Society's policy paper (ref. 36); "technological experimentation" is the Rathenau Institute (ref. 82), "manipulation" the EU HLEG (ref. 33), "surveillance" the EGE (ref. 38).

The Discussion states the tilt as a finding and explains it cognitively: "As codes related to non-maleficence outnumber those related to beneficence, it appears that, for the current AI community, the moral obligation to preventing harm takes precedence over the promotion of good. This fact can be partly interpreted as an instance of the so-called negativity bias, i.e. a general cognitive bias to give greater weight to negative entities. This negative characterization of ethical values is further emphasized by the fact that existing guidelines focus primarily on how to preserve privacy, dignity, autonomy and individual freedom in spite of advances in AI, while largely neglecting whether these principles could be promoted through responsible innovation in AI" (p. 15). The one source cited for the promotive alternative (ref. 110) is Taddeo & Floridi, "How AI can be a force for good," *Science* 2018. Two further Discussion sentences matter: "existing guidelines fail to establish a full correspondence between principles and actionable requirements, with several principles remaining uncharacterized or disconnected from the requirements necessary for their realization" (p. 14); and "risk-benefit evaluations will lead to different results depending on whose well-being it will be optimized for by which actors" (p. 16).

## Relation to the argument

This is evidence about a **corpus of guidelines** — corporate, governmental, professional, NGO — and about nothing else. It anchors the draft's opening sentence that the guidance engineers receive reduces design ethics to a negative mandate, and it does so with a count (60 documents name non-maleficence, 41 beneficence), a ratio the authors themselves state (1.5), and a sentence in which the authors draw the protect/promote line without prompting ("preserve … while largely neglecting whether these principles could be promoted"). It cannot be cited as evidence about Winner, Vallor, Verbeek, Value Sensitive Design or Nissenbaum; none of the five is in the corpus, which is gray literature, and the fairness matrix in pass F3 has to establish each account's orientation on its own text.

Three narrower uses. First, the autonomy split gives the draft the protect/promote distinction in the guidelines' own terms — positive freedom "to flourish" against negative freedom "from … manipulation or surveillance" — and shows which side the corpus takes: the mechanisms the review lists for promoting autonomy are transparency, "predictable AI," "increasing people's knowledge about AI," notice and consent (p. 12), every one a literacy or disclosure instrument. Second, the review's own map of where the corpus locates remedies is a census finding at the field level: harm is prevented by "technical measures and governance strategies," i.e. by the designer and the institution; nothing in the eleven principles' catalogued strategies places an instrument in the hands of the person the system determines, and the appeal/redress items sit under justice as after-the-fact recourse. Third, "whose well-being it will be optimized for by which actors" (p. 16) is the objective-setter question the mediated triad turns on, asked once and not pursued. The review also identifies the promotive pole of the corpus for us: its citation for guidelines that ask whether principles "could be promoted" is the Floridi school (ref. 110), which is the reading `floridi2018.md`'s re-read confirms — that school has beneficence first and delivers it through incentives and adoption, not through design.

## Caution

The unit is *mentions across documents*: a document counts once for a principle however lightly it names it, so 60:41 measures how many guidelines say "harm" versus how many say "benefit," not how much weight either receives inside a document, and 60/41 is 1.46, which the authors round to 1.5 (the Discussion restates the ratio as "codes," so whether 1.5 is a document ratio or a code ratio is not settled by the text). The coding scheme puts well-being on both sides of the ledger — under beneficence's codes and, as "negative impacts on long-term social well-being," under harm — so the tilt is partly a property of how the codes were drawn. The authors' own explanation is "negativity bias," a cognitive account; the fiduciary explanation belongs to Mittelstadt and to this paper's inference, not to them. The corpus closed in April 2019 and predates large language models; it is English-heavy, MEDC-heavy and gray, and the authors say so (pp. 17–18). Two small inconsistencies in the source: the Results text lists the principles "by frequency" with dignity before sustainability while the table has sustainability 14 and dignity 13 (p. 6 vs. p. 7); and the closing paragraph names four convergent principles, omitting justice (p. 17). Quotations are preprint wording; the VoR (pp. 389–399) may differ in phrasing, and only the table has been checked against it.
