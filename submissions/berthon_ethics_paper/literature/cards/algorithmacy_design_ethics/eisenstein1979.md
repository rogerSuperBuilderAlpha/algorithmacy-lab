## What it argues

As Johns summarizes it (2002, p. 107), the 794-page work "advanced a small set of assertions describing the core elements of a 'culture' brought about by the press — elements principally consisting of increased production rates, textual standardization, and preservative power — and then a long list of suggestions as to the effects of those elements on various aspects of early modern life," setting the stage for the Reformation and the Scientific Revolution; "the two volumes cited very few books made during that period, and told the history of none," relying instead on modern historians as "her primary sources" (p. 107). Verbatim lines Johns quotes with pages: the printing revolution was "sui generis," something "to which conventional models of historical change cannot be applied" (p. 168); "ancient and medieval scientific traditions were transformed by the capacity of printing to transmit records of observations without any loss of precision and in full detail" (p. 470); the press was "of no more consequence than any other inanimate tool" — a line "immediately dismissed as 'counter-factual speculation,'" since "the transforming powers of print" did "take effect" (p. 703); and the Gassendi transit-of-Mercury story (p. 631). Ong's précis (1982, p. 115): Eisenstein "spells out in detail how print made the Italian Renaissance a permanent European Renaissance, how it implemented the Protestant Reformation and reoriented Catholic religious practice, how it affected the development of modern capitalism, implemented western European exploration of the globe, changed family life and politics, diffused knowledge as never before, made universal literacy a serious objective, made possible the rise of modern sciences." Ong also cites her p. 64 for how hard it is "to imagine earlier cultures where relatively few persons had ever seen a physically accurate picture of anything" (Ong p. 125). Johns's 1998 précis of her fixity claim (snippet, *Nature of the Book* p. 10): "According to Eisenstein, printing meant the mass reproduction of precisely the same text, repeatable on subsequent occasions and in different locations. No longer need any work suffer the increasing corruption that Eisenstein assumes to be endemic to any 'script culture.'"

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Eisenstein is an institutional-register source — her effects are Reformation, science, capitalism, "universal literacy a serious objective" — and she is the strongest statement that print *itself* supplied the new order's ground of trust (fixity, preservation). She is therefore the source to cite for the affordance side and the one Johns's "achievement" reading corrects. Note that even Ong, who admires the book, keeps his own print claims to consciousness and defers the social claims to her; the paper should not do the reverse and hang institutional claims on Ong.

The scaffolding essay's use. Eisenstein is the closest thing the essay's conclusion has to a source, and Ong's summary phrase "made universal literacy a serious objective" is the nearest verified support for "converted literacy ... into an existential requirement." It is a much weaker claim than the essay's: an objective is not a requirement, and nothing quoted here says the illiterate were "stripped of agency." Johns 2002 (pp. 108–109, 120–121) and the 1983 Clanchy chapter he cites (n. 34) hold that her account of manuscript culture, and so of the before/after, is overdrawn.
