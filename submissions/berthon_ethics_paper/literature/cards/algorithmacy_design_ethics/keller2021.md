# Keller, D. (2021). The future of platform power: Making middleware work. *Journal of Democracy*, 32(3), 168–172.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1353/jod.2021.0043 (Project MUSE DOI, not independently resolved; the journal's own page gives volume 32, issue 3, July 2021, pages 168–72) · **Read depth:** full_text (the Journal of Democracy website carries the whole essay, with MUSE "[End Page n]" markers, which are the page locators used below) · **Source-tier:** peer-edited policy journal (Johns Hopkins University Press / National Endowment for Democracy); a short response essay in an exchange, not a research article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world — a user, a platform, and a competing curation layer; the user's friends appear as data subjects whose content the middleware would need to see · **Remedy locus:** relation (a user-chosen intermediary inserted between the user and the platform's ranking), assessed sceptically · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Keller is "very much a fan" of Fukuyama's middleware — "content-curation services that could give users more control over the material they see on internet platforms such as Facebook or Twitter," under which "competing middleware services could offer feeds curated according to alternate ranking, labeling, or content-moderation rules" (p. 168). She places it in a family: Masnick's "protocols not platforms," Doctorow's "adversarial interoperability," her own "Magic APIs," Dorsey's "algorithmic choice," and reads it as "broadly analogous to unbundling requirements for telecommunications providers," where "the resource in question is user data or content" (pp. 168–169). Its American selling point is constitutional: "Unlike many other proposals to curtail platform power, it does not violate the First Amendment. That makes middleware a path forward in a neighborhood full of dead ends" (p. 169).

The essay's work is "at least four problems to be solved" before "we can execute on the middleware vision" (p. 169). Two she flags for others: technical feasibility — "how technologically feasible is it for competitors to remotely process massive quantities of platform data?" — on which she marks herself "a cautious optimist," and money — "Without a profit motive for middleware providers, the magic will not happen, or it will not happen at large enough scale" (p. 169). Two she argues. *Curation costs*: "Facebook deploys tens of thousands of people to moderate user content in dozens of languages"; "We cannot expect comparable investment from a diverse ecosystem of middleware providers" (pp. 169–170). A shared database of basic facts about content could cut redundant work, but "No one has yet built this database," "this celestial reference book could never approach completion," and the sharing model risks "spreading not just information, but major platforms' assessments of content," which "could reimpose the very speech monoculture from which middleware was supposed to save us" (p. 170). The cautionary precedent is the Internet Content Rating Association, which planned "an ecosystem of trusted third-party curators": "ICRA's third-party curators never showed up. Assessing all that content was hard and thankless, even when the internet was much smaller" (pp. 170–171). *Privacy*: the user can license her own data to a vendor, but "The harder question concerns my friends' data" (p. 171). Fukuyama's answer — middleware "will not see privately shared content from a user's friends" — "hobbles middleware as a tool" and leaves vendors able to "miss out on content from over 80 percent of users" on Facebook (p. 171). Power Ventures, which aggregated feeds, was sued "out of existence" under hacking statutes; NYU's 2020 ad-observer extension drew a Facebook threat "because the tool could also 'see' posts from those users' friends" (p. 171). Every fix is "clunky": consent barrages, ex post lawsuits, incumbent policing of competitors, or technical solutions she has "not seen convincing" versions of (p. 172). Her closing figure is a Venn diagram of privacy, speech and competition: "Interoperability, including for content-moderation purposes, is always smack in the middle. It touches every circle. This is what makes it hard" (p. 172).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8, and this is the essay the paper must answer rather than cite past. Middleware is Principle I in policy vocabulary: a user-selected agent that sits inside the coordination and re-ranks on the user's behalf. Sorted: **counter-delegation proper** — the object of the essay is exactly a client-side agent acting for one party in real time, the only card in this cluster besides Fukuyama et al. and the obfuscation tools where that is so. Keller's four problems are the paper's own obligations, and each one has a triadic reading the essay does not give. The privacy problem is the sharpest: the counter-agent cannot act for the user without reading the other party's signals, and "my friends' data" is precisely the second human's contribution to the coordination. Her account treats those friends as data subjects with a privacy claim; the construct treats them as the co-determined party whose own interests the intermediary also serves, which makes the objection worse — a counter-agent that reads both sides on one side's behalf reproduces, at small scale, the asymmetry it was meant to correct. The curation-cost problem is the objective axis: a counter-agent must supply its own determinations, and the shared database she proposes would let the platform's interpretations flow back in through the side door. The ICRA precedent is empirical evidence that user-chosen curators do not spontaneously appear. CUTS AGAINST the paper's optimism about Principle I on all four counts; the paper should concede the first three and argue that the friends'-data problem is not a privacy problem but the triad's defining feature, which the counter-agent must be designed around rather than legislated past.

## Caution

Five pages, no method, no data beyond two cited statistics (Bailey et al.'s 53.9 public-status friends of 328, a Pew private-account share). Keller is a former Google associate general counsel, which she discloses; the framing is American constitutional law and the examples are Facebook and Twitter circa 2021. The essay is a reply to Fukuyama and presupposes his proposal; read it with fukuyama2021.md. It says nothing about matching, labour platforms or any setting where the "content" is a determination binding two people; the transfer to the construct's cases is the paper's inference.
