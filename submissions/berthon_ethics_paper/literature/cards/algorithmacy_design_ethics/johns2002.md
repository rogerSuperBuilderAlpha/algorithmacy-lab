## What it argues

Johns frames the disagreement with Eisenstein as two linked questions: "the degree of autonomy that historians should or should not ascribe to readers of printed works" and "the character, potency, and even existence of something called 'print culture'" (p. 106). His summary of her position: *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* "advanced a small set of assertions describing the core elements of a 'culture' brought about by the press — elements principally consisting of increased production rates, textual standardization, and preservative power — and then a long list of suggestions as to the effects of those elements" (p. 107); "her central hypothesis has never, in fact, been subjected to test" (p. 109, in a parenthesis). The methodological contrast is put in three sentences that the paper can quote directly: "Where Eisenstein asks what print culture itself is, I ask how printing's historic role came to be shaped. Where she ascribes power to a culture, I assign it to communities of people. Most generally, where she is interested in qualities, I want to know about processes" (p. 110).

The middle section reads Eisenstein's own critique as a specimen of reader autonomy — her Streater "methodological individualism" charge ignores the next sentence, "he was not alone" (p. 111; *Nature of the Book*, p. 323); her "piracy" reading mistakes "practices labeled piratical" for practices that were piratical (p. 112); her Gassendi transit-of-Mercury story, in which a printed letter supposedly organized simultaneous observation across Europe, "proves on examination to be entirely fictitious," the actual letter being handwritten and printed only later by Schickard (pp. 114–115). Johns is careful to limit the lesson: "My point is not that readers are anarchic. They are not. There are consistent conventions in effect in such situations, and they impose effective limits on those interpretations that last" (p. 113).

The constructive section is what this paper needs. "A cultural history of print should, I submit, be broadly constructivist about its subject ... It amounts to holding that one should account for the very character of print historically, by telling how it was shaped by communities of printers, booksellers, readers, and (for want of a better term) censors ... The development of print should not be ascribed primarily to individual actions but to collective practices. (To adopt Eisenstein's idiom for a moment, an apt slogan for the approach might be something like 'guns don't kill people, society kills people.')" (p. 116). On the press's affordances he concedes and then qualifies: "It is intuitively compelling to attribute textual standardization, a massive increase in output, and the quality of fixity to 'technological effects' independent of 'personal actions' ... There is in principle no major problem with positing textual standardization or uniformity as a consequence of printing. On first pass, we can agree that they were. But once we get beyond introductory generalizations, we need to become a little more discriminating. We soon find that the claim to uniformity is easily exaggerated ... Moreover, we will further want to know how standardization, multiplication, and distribution came about — not just in the abstract but to the extent and in the way that they were in fact achieved. The trouble with resorting to print culture as an explanans is that it has nothing to say on these topics" (p. 120). The reader's burden follows: "in a world in which both 'ignorant craftsmen' and masters were at work, a reader might need to work, too, in order to discern the differences between their respective products ... attributing credit to a printed book might involve knowing about its conditions of production — which is exactly what *The Nature of the Book* spent so long arguing" (p. 120). His decisive example is Eisenstein's own fifteenth-century Freising missal: the bishop "would order every single copy of the impression checked individually against a single, manuscript original. Far from being an effect of printing, uniformity was a result of this unusual practice — a practice directly modeled on measures taken in manuscript workshops ... He knew well that if he relied on the attributes of the press and did not exercise some 'personal processes,' then standardization — and its consequences — could not be relied on at all" (p. 121); "the uniformity exhibited by printed materials was as much a product of social actions as of the inherent properties of the press" (p. 121). On the 1695 lapse of licensing: the world of the book "did not collapse precisely because contemporaries feared that it would. They therefore worked hard to avoid the possibility ... the creation of copyright [in 1710] was a result of a polity contemplating almost exactly the possibility that we are invited today to dismiss as self-evidently ridiculous" (p. 124). And: "Contemporaries knew better than to rely on any intrinsic virtues printing might have in order to make such far-reaching decisions. Conventional practices and accepted representations provided a firmer basis for action" (pp. 123–124). The idea of a "printing revolution" itself, he argues, "came about late in the day, arriving around 1800 and achieving the status of an orthodoxy only in the mid-nineteenth century" (p. 122), with Gutenberg installed beside Watt and Newton as a secular icon (p. 123). The close: "Is history conditioned by print, or print by history? I have argued that the latter is the case" (p. 124), and the aim is "to appreciate the achievement of printing as just that: an achievement" (p. 125). A footnote adds the manuscript-continuity point: "since scribal copying remained a vital part of intellectual life until at least the eighteenth century, print needs to be seen less in terms of a radical break than in those of an environment combining speech, manuscript, and print in mutual interaction" (p. 120 n. 34, citing Love, McKenzie, Fox and Clanchy 1983).

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. The exchange supports the competence/institution register directly and says nothing cognitive. On the working hypothesis — that the competence to live with print was built by institutions rather than read off the technology — Johns is an ally, with one precision the paper must keep. He does not say the press supplied nothing: standardization, multiplication and fixity are granted "on first pass" as consequences of printing (p. 120). What he denies is that they could be relied upon without practices: the missal's uniformity was "a result of this unusual practice" (p. 121); credit "might involve knowing about its conditions of production" (p. 120); the book trade survived 1695 because people "worked hard to avoid" its collapse (p. 124). His exact formulation is "as much a product of social actions as of the inherent properties of the press" (p. 121) — a both/and, not a substitution. For the paper's design principles this is the right shape: the reader's competence was a competence to assess conditions of production, and it was made tolerable by conventions (registration, privilege, licensing, then copyright) that bounded what a printed object could claim. That maps onto agentic parity, structural refusal and bounded outputs as institutional, not pedagogical, remedies. It weakens the analogy only if the paper wants print's fixity to have been given by the press alone; Johns's position is that this is the very thing that was achieved, and he calls the achievement an achievement.

The scaffolding essay's use. Johns contradicts the essay's conclusion at three points. He rejects "the particular concept of a revolutionary shift to a unitary print culture" (p. 109) and dates the very idea of a "printing revolution" to c. 1800 (p. 122), so "Print shattered that monopoly" is a nineteenth-century historiographic frame, not a fifteenth-century event. He holds that manuscript, speech and print coexisted "in mutual interaction" into the eighteenth century (p. 120 n. 34), which cuts against a clean before/after. And his whole argument is that print did not by itself make texts authoritative — readers had to do work to credit them — so the essay's picture of an individual "condemned to total dependence upon institutional interpreters" because they could not read inverts his point: dependence on institutions (the Stationers, licensers, learned societies) is what made print credible for the literate too.

## Caution

This is a polemical reply in a forum, focused on England and on the history of science; Johns concedes that *The Nature of the Book* "is not designed to replace *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* in terms of scope" (p. 109). The piece contains no data on literacy rates, on who could read, or on the illiterate; it is about the credibility of printed objects among people who could read them. Eisenstein's original article and her reply (pp. 126–128) were not read (paywalled), so her side is known here only through Johns's quotations of it (see eisenstein2002). The source copy is a course-site PDF; its text was checked against page headers but not against the OUP site.
