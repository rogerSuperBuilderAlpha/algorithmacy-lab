## What it argues

The book's thesis, in Ong's words, is "how much orality could linger in the presence of writing, even in an administrative milieu" (Ong p. 94), and how England moved across 1066–1307 from trusting memory and witness to trusting documents. The verified content (via Ong, first-edition pages): "'documents did not immediately inspire trust' (Clanchy 1979, p. 230). People had to be persuaded that writing improved the old oral methods sufficiently to warrant all the expense and troublesome techniques it involved" (Ong p. 94). Before documents, collective oral testimony established facts: the 1127 dispute over Sandwich customs dues was settled by twelve men of Dover and twelve of Sandwich, "'mature, wise seniors of many years, having good testimony,'" each swearing to what "'I have received from my ancestors, and I have seen and heard from my youth'" (pp. 232–3; Ong p. 94). "Witnesses were prima facie more credible than texts because they could be challenged and made to defend their statements, whereas texts could not"; notarial authentication "developed late in literate cultures, and much later in England than in Italy" (pp. 235–6; Ong pp. 94–95). Documents were authenticated by objects — a knife on a parchment thong (p. 24), a sword laid on an altar to convey Hetherslaw to Durham c. 1130 (p. 25) — and the Earl Warenne story of the "ancient and rusty sword" produced under Edward I's quo warranto, which Clanchy treats as doubtful but telling (pp. 21–2; Ong p. 95). Early charters were undated, Clanchy suggesting that "'dating required the scribe to express an opinion about his place in time'" (pp. 231, 236–41, 238; Ong p. 95). Forgers "were not 'occasional deviants on the peripheries of legal practice' but 'experts entrenched at the centre of literary and intellectual culture in the twelfth century'"; of 164 extant charters of Edward the Confessor, 44 are certainly forged and only 64 certainly authentic (p. 249; Ong p. 96). "'Remembered truth was ... flexible and up to date'" (p. 233; Ong p. 96). Accounts were "audited," i.e. heard, into the twelfth century (pp. 183, 215; Ong p. 117); manuscripts rarely indexed (pp. 28–9, 85, 144; Ong p. 122); a chapter on "The technology of writing" (pp. 88–115; Ong pp. 80, 92); Eadmer composing in writing "felt he was dictating to himself" (p. 218; Ong p. 93). Johns adds that Clanchy (1983) found "Eisenstein's descriptions of manuscript culture wanting" (Johns 2002, p. 120 n. 34). The 3rd edition, per the sibling card's publisher description, adds a rewritten opening chapter on the Norman Conquest and a postscript on the book's reception.

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. This is the case the paper's history section should lead with, because it is the one where trust visibly migrates from persons to documents and the migration is institutional through and through: juries of memory, symbolic objects, undated charters, forgers at the centre, notarial methods arriving late. None of it requires a claim about cognition, and Clanchy's own register is modest ("did not immediately inspire trust"). It also carries a lesson for the design argument: documents became trustworthy through authentication practices (seals, dating, notaries, registries) built after the medium, which is Johns's point made two centuries earlier — the competence to live with a record was a competence in its authentication conventions.

The scaffolding essay's use. Clanchy is the strongest evidence *against* the essay's "jealously guarded ... clerical and scribal guild" framing. His whole book is about lay and administrative use of documents between 1066 and 1307, long before print, and (per the sibling arm's E10 audit) the third edition's material on lay literacy from the twelfth century is the standard citation for treating pre-modern literacy as a graded practical competence rather than a clerical monopoly. The essay's "Prior to Gutenberg, literacy was the jealously guarded possession of an elite clerical and scribal guild" is contradicted by the one source in this set that studied the question directly.
