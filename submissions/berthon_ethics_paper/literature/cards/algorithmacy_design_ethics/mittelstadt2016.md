# Mittelstadt, B. D., Allo, P., Taddeo, M., Wachter, S., & Floridi, L. (2016). The ethics of algorithms: Mapping the debate. *Big Data & Society*, 3(2), 1–21.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/2053951716679679 · **Read depth:** full_text (SAGE open-access HTML version of record, body and footnotes read in full; section headings cited because the HTML carries no page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, systematic review with a prescriptive map; the standard citation for the six-concern taxonomy · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (data subject vs algorithm/data controller), with a distributed-responsibility network flagged as future work · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Algorithms of interest "turn data into evidence for a given outcome," use that outcome "to trigger and motivate an action," and this "complicates apportionment of responsibility." From this the paper derives six concerns: three epistemic (inconclusive, inscrutable and misguided evidence), two normative (unfair outcomes, transformative effects) and one overarching (traceability) ("Map of the ethics of algorithms"). A systematic review then populates the map with seven themes. On inscrutability the paper adopts Turilli and Floridi's (2009) definition of transparency as "the availability of information, the conditions of accessibility and how the information ... may pragmatically or epistemically support the user's decision-making process," notes that "transparency is often naïvely treated as a panacea," and reports the trade-off literature: disclosure "would facilitate ill-intentioned manipulations of search results (or 'gaming the system'), while not bringing any advantage to the average non-tech-savvy user (Granka, 2010; Zarsky, 2016)"; "Releasing information about the algorithm's decision-making logic in a simplified format can help ... However, complex decision-making structures can quickly exceed the human and organisational resources available for oversight"; "Transparency disclosures may prove more impactful if tailored towards trained third parties or regulators." It reports Hildebrandt and Koops's call for "'smart transparency' by designing the socio-technical infrastructures responsible for profiling in a way that allows individuals to anticipate and respond to how they are profiled." On traceability it records that machine learning defeats the linear designer-responsibility model — "nobody has enough control over the machine's actions to be able to assume the responsibility for them" (Matthias 2004: 177) — producing "an accountability gap ... wherein blame can potentially be assigned to several moral agents simultaneously," and that "Assigning moral agency to artificial agents can allow human stakeholders to shift blame to algorithms (Crnkovic and Çürüklü, 2011)." The "Points of further research" section names two traceability gaps: "insufficient attention has been given to distributed responsibility, or responsibility as shared across a network of human and algorithmic actors simultaneously," and "a de-responsibilisation of human actors, or a tendency to 'hide behind the computer'" (Zarsky 2016: 121), with Arendt on bureaucracy as the analogue. The conclusion invokes Floridi's LoA: discussion of algorithms "inevitably encounters problems of abstraction or 'talking past each other' due to a failure to specify a level of abstraction (LoA) for discussion, and thus limit the relevant set of observables."

## Relation to the argument

RQ7 on three fronts. (1) On the transparency critique, this is the review that states the pre-2025 consensus in one place: exposure is not a panacea, simplified disclosure "can help" but overwhelms, and it is more useful to auditors than to subjects. It does not predict Bo et al.'s (2025) ordinal result; it does supply the use-relative definition of transparency (information that "may pragmatically or epistemically support the user's decision-making process") under which raw confidence exposure fails by definition when it does not support a decision — which is the principle behind Seamful Boundary Disclosure. Hildebrandt and Koops's "smart transparency" (anticipate and respond to how one is profiled) is the closest precedent in this school for the anticipatory component of algorithmacy and should be cited as such. (2) On deflection, the paper records inside the Floridi school the risk that Floridi and Sanders's own accountability-for-artificial-agents move "can allow human stakeholders to shift blame," and names de-responsibilisation and distributed responsibility as open problems — which Floridi (2016) then answers. The paper's two gaps are the "distributed" and "denied" of the companion draft's triad. (3) On the dyad: the parties throughout are "data subjects" and "data processors/controllers"; even the mediated examples in the introduction (recommendation, filtering, dispute-resolution algorithms "that replace human decision-makers in dispute mediation") are catalogued without modelling the two mediated humans. The paper's own closing appeal to LoA is a small gift: it says the field talks past itself for want of a stated level of abstraction, which is what the lab's Φ instrument forces its user to state.

## Caution

A literature review with a prescriptive map; its authority is organisational, not evidential, and the map is offered "in beta." Coverage is limited to literature explicitly about "algorithms," and the authors say much adjacent work (machine ethics, surveillance studies) is only touched. The transparency findings are reported from other sources (Granka, Zarsky, Kitchin) rather than established, and the "gaming" trade-off is asserted from a search-engine context. The GDPR discussion predates the regulation's application and is superseded by Wachter et al. (2017, 2018). Nothing on LLMs, confidence displays or interface design.
