## What it argues

The abstract makes two claims. First, a "virtue-based normative framework" belongs in IT ethics because it can reconcile "a number of significant methodological conflicts and debates in the existing literature" and "correct for a strong utilitarian bias in the research methodologies of existing empirical studies on the social and ethical impact of IT"; second, social networking technologies bear on "three virtues typically honed in communicative practices: patience, honesty and empathy." The 2015 paper describes the 2010 argument as showing that new media practices "have the ambiguous potential to either weaken the moral skills and virtues engendered by communicative practices, or to encourage and strengthen their cultivation" (Vallor 2015, pp. 116–117). Vallor's SEP entry (§3.3) reports a related, broader move, which the entry attributes to Spence (2011), not to the 2010 paper: a call to "expand the scope of philosophical inquiry beyond its present concern with narrowly interpersonal ethics to the more universal ethical question of prudential wisdom," asking "Do SNS and related technologies help us to cultivate the broader intellectual virtue of knowing what it is to live well, and how to best pursue it? Or do they tend to impede its development?" The 2010 paper is thus the first statement of the practice-based formation criterion that the 2015 paper generalises: a communicative medium is judged by whether the practices it affords cultivate or erode the virtues those practices exist to train.

## Relation to the argument

For RQ3 the paper's value is that it applies the formation criterion to a case that is structurally closer to the construct than anything in Vallor 2015: two people coordinating through a platform. Patience, honesty and empathy are virtues of the relation between the two parties, and the question whether the medium erodes them is a question about what the intermediary does to both sides at once. That is the construct's structural axis, approached from the virtue side. Whether Vallor treats the SNS as a channel or a party — whether the platform's own objective (engagement, in 2010 terms) enters her analysis — could not be determined from the abstract and summaries, and this is the one thing the essay would most want from the full text. The paper does not discuss friction.

## Caution

A card written from an abstract and two author summaries can state the thesis but not the argument, the examples, or any page-referenced claim; do not quote this paper in a draft until the full text has been read. The 2010 SNS landscape (Facebook before the News Feed algorithm dominated, Twitter at 140 characters) is not the algorithmically curated one the essay addresses, and the paper predates the cooptation concerns the essay imports. The SEP entry is a later text by the same author and may restate the 2010 view in stronger terms than the original.
