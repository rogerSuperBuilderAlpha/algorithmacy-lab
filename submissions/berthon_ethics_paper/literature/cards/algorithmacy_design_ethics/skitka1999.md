# Skitka, L. J., Mosier, K. L., & Burdick, M. (1999). Does automation bias decision-making? *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 51(5), 991–1006.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted PDF of the published article from Skitka's UIC site, recovered via a Wayback snapshot; journal pagination intact) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed HCI journal; controlled laboratory experiment · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one participant, one automated monitoring aid) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The first study to compare the same task with and without an aid. Eighty undergraduates flew eight 8-minute-20-second trials of NASA's W/PANES simulation — a joystick tracking task, waypoint call-outs and a four-gauge monitoring task — half with an Automated Monitoring Aid that announced events and recommended actions and was described as "not completely reliable," while "all their other instruments were always 100% accurate" (pp. 996–997). Across 100 events the aid failed to prompt on six (omission opportunities) and gave a wrong directive on six (commission opportunities). On the six unprompted events the automated group responded with 59% accuracy against 97% for the unaided group, F(1,62) = 44.32 (p. 999); on the six contradicted directives the automated group averaged 3.92 commission errors, a 35% accuracy rate, with only one participant error-free and 23.1% erring on all six (p. 999). When the aid worked, the aided group scored better on the 88 normal events (83.03 vs 71.85 correct); tracking performance did not differ, and neither did perceived difficulty or effort (p. 1000). Within the aided group, belief that the aid improved accuracy correlated with omission and commission errors at .44 and .59, and self-reported diffusion of responsibility to the aid at .44 and .66 (Table 1, p. 1001). The theoretical framing runs through three social-psychological mechanisms: cognitive miserliness, social loafing — "people treat computers who share task responsibilities as a 'team member'" (p. 992, citing Nass) — and obedience to authority, illustrated by the 21 of 22 nurses who dosed patients on an unfamiliar physician's phone order (p. 993).

## Relation to the argument

Objective axis, and the paper that gives the diffusion-of-responsibility mechanism its numbers. On this pass's question: a pure dyad in the design, but with a conceptual twist worth quoting. The authors get their hypotheses by treating the computer *as if* it were a second human — a team member to loaf beside, an authority to obey — and the correlations bear that out. The second human is a metaphor for the automation, never a party on its far side. The discussion reports that "adding a second 'crewmember'" had no effect on error rates in students or pilots (p. 1004), and that crewmember is again same-side. The 100%-reliable gauges are the closest thing to a direct channel in the design: the participant could always reach the world without the aid, and still deferred to it 65% of the time. That is the literacy-side baseline the construct's structural axis contrasts with — the coordination factors, the channel survives, and reliance still miscalibrates. It says nothing about what happens when the channel is gone.

## Caution

Students, a low-fidelity part-task, and a 94%-reliable aid whose failures are dense (12 of 100 events); participants underestimated its reliability at ~81% (p. 999), so "trust" here was already below the aid's true reliability and bias still appeared. Commission errors have no unaided comparison by construction (p. 998). The exploratory correlations are within a 40-person cell. The aid's objective is the participant's own; nothing tests an aid whose recommendation serves another party.
