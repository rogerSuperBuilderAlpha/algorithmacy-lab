# Lee, J. D., & See, K. A. (2004). Trust in automation: Designing for appropriate reliance. *Human Factors*, 46(1), 50–80.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted PDF of the published article, University of Iowa CSL site, recovered from a 2005 Wayback snapshot; journal pagination intact) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, flagship HF journal; integrative review with a conceptual model, no new data · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one trustor, one automation; a same-side multiperson case is named once as unexplored) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Trust is "the attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual's goals in a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability" (p. 51, restated p. 54), and the paper's whole apparatus hangs on that goal clause: "Trust is not a consideration in situations where the trustor does not depend on the trustee to perform some function related to the trustor's goals" (p. 54). Appropriate trust is then defined by three mismatches between trust and capability. Calibration is "the correspondence between a person's trust in the automation and the automation's capabilities," with overtrust above the diagonal leading to misuse and distrust below it leading to disuse (p. 55, Figure 2); resolution is how finely trust differentiates levels of capability; specificity is whether trust attaches to a subfunction or mode (functional) and tracks moment-to-moment capability (temporal) (pp. 55–56). The information that supports trust comes in three bases borrowed from Lee and Moray (1992) and mapped onto Mayer et al.'s ability/integrity/benevolence: performance (what the automation does), process (its algorithms — "trust is in the agent and not in the specific actions of the agent"), and purpose, "the degree to which the automation is being used within the realm of the designer's intent," which "corresponds to faith and benevolence and reflects the perception that the trustee has a positive orientation toward the trustor" (p. 59). Trust and reliance form a closed loop (Figure 4, p. 68): a first-order differential equation fit to Lee and Moray's (1994) pasteurization-plant data accounts for 60–86% of the variance in reliance (p. 70), trust is conditioned by the automation's worst behaviours, and the reliability below which trust collapses is estimated variously at 90%, 70% and 60% across studies (p. 72). The design list is explicit: "Design for appropriate trust, not greater trust," show past performance, reveal process, and "show the purpose of the automation, design basis, and range of applications in a way that relates to the users' goals" (p. 74).

## Relation to the argument

This is the source the objective axis has to answer, and reading it in full sharpens the tooth rather than dulling it. Every term — trust, calibration, purpose, benevolence — is indexed to *the trustor's* goals; the automation "embodies the intentionality of the designers" (p. 66) but the model only ever asks whether that embodied intent serves the operator. Benevolence is "the extent to which the intents and motivations of the trustee are aligned with those of the trustor" (p. 59); an intermediary pursuing an objective neither party set fails the definition rather than scoring low on it, so calibration has no diagonal to sit on. On this pass's core question the answer is a documented absence. The model is a dyad throughout: the authors note that interpersonal trust has a "symmetry" in which trustor and trustee are each aware of the other's trust, and that "there is no such symmetry in the trust between people and machines" (p. 66) — the machine does not trust back, and no third party appears on the far side of it. The nearest approach is the closing agenda: "Research has not yet considered the evolution of trust in multiperson groups that share responsibility for managing automation," and "meta trust," a person's trust that another person's trust in the automation is appropriate, is flagged as "an unexplored area" (pp. 75–76). Those multiple persons are co-operators on the same side of the same automation, not a counterpart whose interests the automation also serves. Lewandowsky et al.'s (2000) finding that people delegate differently to a human than to automation (p. 66) is the only place a second human stands in the automation's position, and there the human is the alternative trustee, not a co-principal.

## Caution

An integrative review, not a test; the model is conceptual and the empirical anchors (Lee & Moray 1992, 1994; Muir & Moray 1996) are microworld process-control studies with small samples. The paper predates recommender systems, platforms and language models; its examples are autopilots, navigation aids and pasteurization plants where the designer's intent and the operator's goal coincide by construction. The "purpose" basis of trust is the hook for the objective axis, but the paper does not consider a purpose that diverges from the user's; that reading is ours, not theirs. The 60–86% variance figure is a single lab study, and the reliability drop-off thresholds are called "highly system and context dependent" by the authors themselves.

## Relation to Principle III — bounded outputs, not mechanistic interpretability (cluster F pass, 2026-09-18)

**Remedy locus:** subject (calibrate the operator's trust) served by artifact design (display performance, process, purpose).

Lee and See's three bases of trust are the three candidate remedies for RQ10 laid side by side, and the paper can use their own text to choose among them. *Performance* is "what the automation does"; *process* is its algorithms, and trust at that level "is in the agent and not in the specific actions of the agent"; *purpose* is "the degree to which the automation is being used within the realm of the designer's intent" and "corresponds to faith and benevolence" (p. 59). Mechanistic interpretability is a process-basis remedy — trust the agent because you can see its algorithm. Principle III is a performance-basis remedy — rely on what the automation does, within a declared envelope, because that is the level at which the coordinated party can observe and verify. Their developmental claim, taken from Muir (1994) and Rempel et al., is that interpersonal trust runs performance → process → purpose but that trust in automation "can also follow an opposite pattern, in which faith is important early in the interaction, followed by dependability, and then by predictability" (p. 66). A lay party on a platform starts from faith in the stated purpose and has no route to the process level at all; Principle III builds the performance basis she would otherwise reach last. Their design list confirms the choice: "Design for appropriate trust, not greater trust" and "show past performance" come before "reveal process," and the paper can say why the first two suffice where the third does not — process-level trust presupposes "the designer's intent" is the user's, which the objective axis denies (the original relation section's point that benevolence "fails the definition rather than scoring low on it"). One warning they supply against Principle III's own excess: trust "is conditioned by the automation's worst behaviours" and collapses below reliability thresholds that are "highly system and context dependent" (p. 72), so a declared envelope that is breached even rarely does more damage than none; bounded outputs must be bounds that hold. The dyad caution stands: no counterpart party, and "purpose" is never a purpose that diverges from the trustor's.
