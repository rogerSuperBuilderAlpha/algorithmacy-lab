## What it argues

The book opens on the cell phone, which "directly help[s] to generate new ways of communicating and interacting. They create new ways of dealing with appointments; long-term planning" (p. 4), and on Latour's script: "Like the script of a movie or a theater play, artifacts prescribe how their users are to act when they use them. A speed bump, for instance, has the script 'Slow down when you approach me'" (p. 10), with a table at p. 11 that runs "Script / Translation of action / Invitation and inhibition / Delegation: deliberate inscription / Multistability: context" and the gloss "Technological mediation is context-dependent" (p. 11). Chapter 3 meets Floridi and Sanders directly: agents that "learn how to 'behave' in different environments could qualify as an agent. They use the ability to cause good or evil as the criterion for morality: 'An action is said to be morally qualifiable if and only if it can cause moral good or evil'" (p. 49), which Verbeek reports on the way to his own hybrid account. Chapter 4 distinguishes force from persuasion, "a speed bump that makes it impossible to drive at high speed without damaging your car. But mediation can also take the form of persuasion: by giving feedback on people's actions" (p. 83), and asks for "techniques of technology use" that "require experimentation" so that something — the subject of the sentence is cut by the snippet boundary, see Caution — "acquires a desirable shape" (p. 84); through "active appropriation of technological mediations," "a moral subject actually takes on a double moral responsibility" (p. 87). Chapter 5, "Morality in Design," carries the Achterhuis moralization proposal (p. 95, the water-saving showerhead), Jelsma's "script logic" ("if these do not fit with user practices and interpretations, unintended outcomes will result," p. 114) and Friedman's cookie-consent browser as a value-sensitive design case (pp. 114–115).

Chapter 6, "Moral Environments," is the application to "Ambient Intelligence and Persuasive Technology" (p. 120), with Ambient Intelligence defined as "a combination of 'ubiquitous computing' with intelligent user interfaces (Brey 2005)" (p. 121). Its cases: refrigerators that "give feedback on people's eating patterns and offer menu suggestions" and "cameras ... that automatically detect deviant behavior and report it to the police" (p. 122); the Persuasive Mirror, which "produces a manipulated image of somebody's face, extrapolating the effects of one's lifestyle to the future" (p. 123), the FoodPhone and the HygieneGuard (p. 123). Such technologies "make it possible to tailor ways of influencing people's behavior that can be exercised" (p. 124; snippet cut). Three "behavior-steering forms of mediation" are distinguished: force ("a speed bump leaves car drivers hardly any choice"), persuasion (feedback on driving style) and seduction (road curves and markings) (p. 126); Berdichevsky and Neuenschwander's framework is expanded (p. 128) into three sites of assessment, "the intended persuasion, the form of mediation (including the method of persuasion), and the" [third site, snippet cut] (p. 130). The FoodPhone "invites its users to be 'observers' of their eating behavior" and may produce "an unhealthy obsession with food" (p. 131). The ethical stance asks people to approach these technologies "as powers that help to shape subjectivity and that require active appropriation and experimentation" (p. 134); "some forms of Ambient Intelligence force people to behave in a certain way, as when an Intelligent Speed Adaptation system matches the speed of people's cars to the speed limit with the help of a GPS system," and "Persuasive Technologies can be seen as externalizations of human conscience. Norms and principles of behavior are implicitly or explicitly materialized in a technological artifact, which exposes human beings permanently to its" influence (p. 135). Chapter 7 adds that "Persuasive Technology can also be seen as a form of composite intentionality. Here the character of technological intentionality is not hermeneutic-perceptual but pragmatic. Persuasive technologies have a built-in 'intention'" (p. 148).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, adaptivity axis, and this is the load-bearing negative result. The in-volume search returns zero occurrences of "adaptive," "algorithm" and "profile" in the whole book, and "learn" only three times, once for Floridi and Sanders' criterion (p. 49), once for bodies that "learn to operate weapons" (Foucault, p. 69), once for us who must "learn to live with" technology (p. 155). The book therefore knows the learning criterion, reports it, and does not build on it: Verbeek's own chapter on smart environments describes detection, feedback, extrapolation and GPS matching, every one a fixed rule applied to a reading, and its categories (force, persuade, seduce; intended persuasion, form of mediation) sort mediations by strength and visibility, never by whether the rule itself changes with the person. The p. 124 "tailor" is the closest word and it means designers tailoring an influence, not a system tailoring itself. Verbeek is fixed on the primary text; the persuasive cases are active but designed once.

Structural and objective axes. Persuasive technology with "a built-in 'intention'" (p. 148) and technologies as "externalizations of human conscience" (p. 135) are where the objective axis appears in Verbeek: the artifact carries a norm the user did not set. But whose conscience is externalized is always the designer's or the policymaker's, with one user in front of it; there is no second user whose interest the norm trades against. Remedy locus splits between the artifact (designing mediations, "Morality in Design") and the subject ("active appropriation and experimentation," p. 134; "techniques of technology use," p. 84). The p. 108 passage reported through Redaelli, that technologies need not be "held morally accountable," still cuts against any reading that treats the platform as the accountable party.

## Caution

Redaelli's page numbers (VII, 4, 7, 15, 17, 42, 55, 57, 58, 108, 127, 145, 146) were not re-checked. The "tailor" sentence at p. 124 and the third site at p. 130 need their full sentences before use. The number of "externalizations of human conscience" (p. 135) is unsettled: an in-volume search for the plural phrase on 2026-09-18 returned no hit and Google's spell-corrector offered the singular "externalization of human conscience," so check the noun before quoting. The claim that "adaptive" and "algorithm" occur nowhere rests on Google's index of the scan, which can miss hyphenated or OCR-damaged tokens; treat it as strong evidence, not proof.

**Corrected, 2026-09-19 (F3 pass):** the "What it argues" paragraph above originally paraphrased the p. 84 "acquires a desirable shape" sentence as "one's relation to other people acquires a desirable shape." That paraphrase is unsupported and probably wrong — the F3 re-check (below) found the snippet cuts the subject of "acquires" before it names it, and the parallel sentence in Verbeek's own 2009 restatement of this chapter gives "the technological mediation of their subjectivity" (2009, p. 260) as what takes on the desirable shape, not a relation to other people. Do not cite this card for a claim that Verbeek designs for the relation between two people; the corrected reading is the opposite — chapter 4 is about a single subject's own relation to technology.

## Relation to the protect/promote question (2026-09-19, F3 pass)

The route that worked was indirect: DuckDuckGo located a full PDF of Verbeek (2009), "Cultivating humanity," the published form of chapters 2 and 4, now carded as verbeek2009.md at full text; the verdict below rests on it.

Two things the new snippets change on this card. The p. 84 sentence reads, in the window Google returned, "... acquires a de-sirable shape. As I will elaborate in the next chapter, 'techniques of technol-ogy use' require experimentation. The distance needed to gain a free relation to technology and to modify and shape its impact on our ..." — the subject of "acquires" is cut, and this card's earlier paraphrase, "one's relation to other people," was supported by neither the snippet nor the 2009 text, where the thing given "a desirable shape" is "the technological mediation of their subjectivity" (2009, p. 260). That paraphrase has been corrected above (Caution); what the sentence shapes is the subject's own relation to the technology ("a free relation to technology"), not a relation to other people. The p. 80 snippet confirms the chapter's Foucauldian frame — "the practice of the self, that will allow us to play these games of power with as little domination as possible" (Foucault 1997b, 298, as quoted). The second p. 84 snippet — "desirable ways. For answering the question of what kind of mediated subjects we want to be, to be sure, the ethical frameworks from classical virtue ethics and modern deontological and utilitarian systems can continue to play ..." — is the 2009 p. 260 sentence nearly verbatim, which is what licenses reading chapter 4 through the 2009 chapter.

Verdict for the matrix. Telos: promotive — the book is framed against a humanist ethics that displaced "the good life" (2009, p. 241), and chapter 5 ("Morality in Design," the Achterhuis showerhead at p. 95) and chapter 6's persuasive technologies (pp. 122–135 above) are instruments for steering behaviour toward ends the designer judges good. Instrument: promotive on both sides, artifact (moralizing design) and subject ("active appropriation and experimentation," p. 134). Who delivers the promotive part: the designer–user–artefact alliance, for one user's good life; the beneficiary is the person in front of the artefact, and the one two-person case, the expecting parents (2009, p. 253), is a single decision-maker facing one machine. No sentence in the snippets or in the 2009 chapter assigns promotive work to the relation between two people mediated by a third. The reformulated claim ("none of the five assigns the promotive work to the design of the relation") holds for Verbeek; the unreformulated "all five are protective" does not, and the paper should not say it.
