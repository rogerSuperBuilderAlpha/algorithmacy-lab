# Ananny, M., & Crawford, K. (2018). Seeing without knowing: Limitations of the transparency ideal and its application to algorithmic accountability. *New Media & Society*, 20(3), 973–989.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/1461444816676645 · **Read depth:** abstract_only + secondary (SAGE page reached in the browser but restricted access for this account; no author-deposited copy found — the USC Annenberg "download" link serves Ananny's CV, Crawford's publications page links only the DOI, the H2O casebook entry is a title-only stub, and Semantic Scholar lists no open-access PDF; the secondary characterisations below come from Tsamados et al. 2022 §4 and §7 and Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell 2018 n. 6, both read in full) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, very heavily cited (SAGE reports 1,099 Web of Science citations) · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** unverified · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

From the abstract (verbatim from the SAGE page): "Models for understanding and holding systems accountable have long rested upon ideals and logics of transparency. Being able to see a system is sometimes equated with being able to know how it works and govern it — a pattern that recurs in recent work about transparency and computational systems. But can 'black boxes' ever be opened, and if so, would that ever be sufficient? In this article, we critically interrogate the ideal of transparency, trace some of its roots in scientific and sociotechnical epistemological cultures, and present 10 limitations to its application. We specifically focus on the inadequacy of transparency for understanding and governing algorithmic systems and sketch an alternative typology of algorithmic accountability grounded in constructive engagements with the limitations of transparency ideals." Keywords: accountability, algorithms, critical infrastructure studies, platform governance, transparency.

What citing sources I did read attribute to it: Tsamados et al. (2022, §4) cite it for the claims that "complete transparency can itself cause distinct ethical problems," that transparency "can also overwhelm users with information and thus, render the algorithm more opaque" (paired with Kizilcec 2016), that algorithmic malleability "may also be abused to blur the history of its evolution and leave end-users in a state of confusion," and that constant revision "requires those affected by the algorithm to monitor it constantly and update their understanding accordingly — an impossible task for most." Tsamados et al. (§7, §8) also cite it for the point that the process by which decisions are delegated to algorithms "does not account for all stakeholders and is not void of structural inequalities," and for the proposal that providers "ought to facilitate public discourse about their technology." Wachter et al. (2018, n. 6, citing p. 8) list it among sources for the claim that disclosure can allow data subjects to "game or manipulate the decision-making system."

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, transparency-critique question. On the secondary evidence available, Ananny and Crawford **anticipate a failure mode adjacent to Bo et al.'s (2025) but do not predict it**: the limitation Tsamados et al. attribute to them is *overload* — more disclosure producing less understanding — and the paired empirical citation is Kizilcec (2016), which is the closer precedent for a non-monotonic effect of transparency on trust. Whether the paper contains a prediction specific to confidence or probability exposure I could not verify, and the paper should not say it does until someone reads the ten limitations in the version of record. What the secondary evidence does support is a weaker but useful sentence: by 2016 the critical literature already held that seeing inside a system is not knowing it, that disclosure can overwhelm, and that a system's constant revision defeats a one-time explanation — the last of which is the "anticipatory" and "adapt as it changes" component of algorithmacy stated as a limit of transparency. The "accountability grounded in constructive engagements with the limitations of transparency" typology (abstract) is the natural citation for framing the paper's affordances as accountability mechanisms that do not run through exposure.
