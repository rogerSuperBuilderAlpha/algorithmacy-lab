## What it argues

Text-to-image diffusion models "can learn to mimic the artistic style of specific artists after 'fine-tuning' on samples of their art" (Abstract), and the artist's position, as the authors state it, is a forced choice: "most artists are faced a choice to 1) do nothing, or 2) stop sharing samples of their art online to avoid training models, and in doing so cripple their main way to advertise and promote their work to customers" (§1). Glaze is "a technical alternative": artists "apply 'style cloaks' to their art before sharing online. These cloaks apply barely perceptible perturbations to images, and when used as training data, mislead generative models that try to mimic a specific artist" (Abstract). The cloak shifts "the artwork's representation in the generator model's feature space towards a chosen target art style," so that a mimic trained on cloaked images learns a shifted style (§1). Evaluated with "1,156 participants from the artist community" (§1), the tool is reported as "highly successful at disrupting mimicry under normal conditions (>92%) and against adaptive countermeasures (>85%)" at low perturbation levels, with "92% of surveyed artists" finding the perturbations "small enough not to disrupt the value of their art" (Abstract, §1). The limitations section is candid. Protection "relies on artists cloaking a portion of their art in the mimic model's training dataset," which established artists whose work "has already been downloaded" cannot do, so they "need to rely on opt-out and removal options at art repositories" (§8). And "a system like Glaze that protects artists faces an inherent challenge of being future-proof. Any technique we use to cloak artworks today might be overcome by a future countermeasure, possibly rendering previously protected art vulnerable"; the authors "are under no illusion that Glaze will remain future-proof in the long run," offer it as "an important and necessary first step," and hope it gives "some protection to artists while longer term (legal, regulatory) efforts take hold" (§8). Released free in March 2023, with the caveat "clearly state[d]" to users that "Glaze is not a permanent solution against AI mimicry" (§8).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9. Glaze is "withhold training data" as a working form of structural refusal, and it has exactly the shape Principle II specifies: the artist severs the loop (the model cannot learn her style from what she posts) without losing basic utility (she keeps posting, and her audience keeps seeing the work). The forced choice the authors describe in §1 — participate or exit — is Hirschman's pair, and Glaze is the third option, exit-within. It is also Zong and Matias's (2024) "refusal by obfuscation," and it descends directly from Brunton and Nissenbaum's (2015) obfuscation already carded in this library: the refuser accepts that collection will occur and corrupts what the collector can make of it. Two features make it the paper's best concrete case. The refusal is *technical*, not hermeneutic: the artist need not understand the diffusion model, only run the tool, which is the point of Principle II's contrast with interpretation. And it needs no cooperation from the mediator, unlike Article 21(5) of the GDPR or Article 38 of the DSA, which presuppose a controller who honours the signal; the authors say "legal and regulatory action can take years, and are difficult to enforce internationally" (§1). What CUTS AGAINST is what the authors themselves say. A cloak is a de Certeau tactic in the strict sense — "whatever it wins, it does not keep" — and §8 says so in engineering terms: the protection is not future-proof, already-scraped work is unprotected, and the tool is a stopgap "while longer term (legal, regulatory) efforts take hold." Principle II cannot rest on an arms race, so the paper should cite Glaze as proof that structural refusal is buildable, and Nightshade (Shan et al. 2024) and the legal instruments as what turns a tactic into a standing option. The structure is also dyadic: artist against mimic. The counterpart the artist coordinates with — the customer who finds her through the same platforms the scrapers use — is present only as the reason exit is too costly.

## Caution

The design and evaluation chapters were skimmed, and the headline percentages are the authors' own summary of CLIP-based scores and artist ratings; they were not checked against the tables. The user studies sample artists recruited "in coordination with the professional artist community," a population with a stake in the result. The arXiv v6 text postdates the USENIX version by two years and may differ from it; no USENIX page numbers are attached to the quotations. The paper is about style mimicry by fine-tuned diffusion models, not about platforms, coordination or allocation, and its success criterion is the failure of a mimic, not any change in the relation between artist and platform.
