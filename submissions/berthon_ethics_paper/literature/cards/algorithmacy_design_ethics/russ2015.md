# Russ, A. L., Chen, S., Melton, B. L., Saleem, J. J., Weiner, M., Spina, J. R., Daggy, J. K., & Zillich, A. J. (2015). Design and evaluation of an electronic override mechanism for medication alerts to facilitate communication between prescribers and pharmacists. *Annals of Pharmacotherapy*, 49(7), 761–769.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/1060028015581678 · PMID 25855701 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (PubMed record; the SAGE full text returned 403 and no author-deposited copy was found) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed pharmacy journal; simulated crossover usability study · **Evidence basis:** secondary (abstract) · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator — prescriber, pharmacist, and the CPOE/e-prescribing alert system whose override field carries the prescriber's reasoning to the pharmacist · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

From the abstract: computerized medication alerts "can often be bypassed by entering an override rationale, but prescribers' override reasons" are frequently unclear to the pharmacists who review them. The team redesigned the override mechanism for adverse-reaction and drug–drug interaction alerts, replacing free text with "a predefined set of override reasons" and altering timing, navigation and text entry, and tested it in a counterbalanced crossover with 20 prescribers in a simulated prescribing environment. Prescribers found the redesign more efficient, and "appropriate override reasons significantly increased"; the authors call a menu-based override "an important first step toward a more standardized menu" for communication between prescribers and pharmacists through e-prescribing systems. The abstract reports the efficiency rating (Wilcoxon signed-rank, P = 0.032), the gain in appropriate override reasons (median change 3.0, interquartile range 3.0, P < 0.0001), and two usability counts (5 prescribers had difficulty finding the new mechanism; 3 read the navigation as meaning the alert could not be overridden); it gives no override percentages.

## Relation to the argument

RQ9. This is a real, citable case in which software sits between two clinicians and both parties act through it: the system fires a determination (an interaction flag) that neither clinician set — the rule base comes from a vendor knowledge base — the prescriber must answer it to proceed, and the pharmacist downstream must interpret the prescriber's answer as relayed by the system. The title says the design goal outright: to "facilitate communication between prescribers and pharmacists," which means the alert override field is being treated as the channel between two humans. Structurally that is the essay's triad in a hospital. The gap between this case and the essay's construct is the direct channel: a pharmacist can still phone the prescriber, and the paper's motivation (override reasons "frequently ambiguous to pharmacists who review orders") is precisely that the mediated channel is degrading a coordination that used to be direct. On the objective axis the case is weak in the same way as datalink — the alert system has no interest of its own — though the vendor knowledge base does set the thresholds, and a companion open-access study (Van De Sijpe et al. 2022, BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 22, doi:10.1186/s12911-022-01783-z, abstract read via Europe PMC) reports 38,409 very severe DDI alerts in one teaching hospital, 88.2% overridden by prescribers, pharmacist review of overrides yielding recommendations in 3.2% of reviewed cases with 79.2% accepted — a loop in which the pharmacist contests the prescriber's override of a determination neither of them wrote.

## Caution

Abstract only; I have not seen the methods, the override-reason taxonomy, or the pharmacist-side evaluation, and the 20-prescriber simulation measured usability and appropriateness of reasons, not any downstream pharmacist outcome. The pharmacist does not appear as a participant, so the study is dyadic in execution even though triadic in design intent. Do not cite numbers from the Van De Sijpe abstract beyond those given above without opening the full article (Springer returned a cookie redirect; PMC served a CAPTCHA).
