## What it argues

"In order to clarify the intended use cases of machine learning models and minimize their usage in contexts for which they are not well suited, we recommend that released models be accompanied by documentation detailing their performance characteristics... Model cards are short documents accompanying trained machine learning models that provide benchmarked evaluation in a variety of conditions, such as across different cultural, demographic, or phenotypic groups... and intersectional groups... Model cards also disclose the context in which models are intended to be used, details of the performance evaluation procedures, and other relevant information" (abstract). The nine sections (Figure 1, §4): Model Details, Intended Use ("Primary intended uses," "Primary intended users," "Out-of-scope use cases"), Factors, Metrics, Evaluation Data, Training Data, Quantitative Analyses ("Unitary results," "Intersectional results"), Ethical Considerations, Caveats and Recommendations. The analogy is the datasheet: "the electronic hardware industry provides datasheets with detailed characterizations of components' performances under different test conditions" (§2). The stakeholder list (§3) includes "Impacted individuals who may experience effects from a model" who "can better understand how it works or use information in the card to pursue remedies." The cards are self-authored and bounded by the author's discretion: "the amount of detail corporations choose to disclose might be different from academic research groups. This section should not be seen as a requirement to compromise private information or reveal proprietary training techniques" (§4.1); "Training Data. May not be possible to provide in practice" (Figure 1). Metrics are context-dependent and the paper's example is two-sided: "in a surveillance scenario, surveillors may value a low false negative rate... On the other hand, those being surveilled may value a low false positive rate" (§4.4.1). The two examples are a CelebA smile detector (false discovery rate "much higher" on older men) and Perspective API's TOXICITY classifier, where version 1 "has low performance for several terms, especially 'lesbian', 'gay', and 'homosexual'" (§5). The limits are stated: "the usefulness and accuracy of a model card relies on the integrity of the creator(s) of the card itself. It seems unlikely, at least in the near term, that model cards could be standardized or formalized to a degree needed to prevent misleading representations of model results," so cards are "one transparency tool among many," alongside "algorithmic auditing by third-parties," "'adversarial testing'," and "more inclusive user feedback mechanisms" (§6).

## Relation to the argument

RQ11, the establishment's own text for the "model cards" remedy the paper names as misapplied. What the card discloses is what the *model* does across groups, written by its *maker*, for a reader who is choosing whether to *adopt* it — the paper's three misplaced loci in one document. Two passages matter. The surveillor/surveilled example (§4.4.1) is the one place the authors write two parties with opposed interests in the same metric, and they resolve it by recommending "listing all values and providing context about which were prioritized" — disclosure of the trade-off, not standing over it. And "Impacted individuals" appear in the stakeholder list as readers who might "pursue remedies," with no account of what remedy a card enables; the paper can quote this as the field's own admission that the second party is on the list but not given an instrument (compare green2019.md). The authors' §6 concession — cards depend on the creator's integrity and cannot prevent "misleading representations" — is the same self-attestation limit the C2PA card records, and the two should be cited together: a model card is the intermediary's press release about its model. CUTS AGAINST the paper if it treats model cards as a literacy remedy aimed at end-users; six of the seven readers on the authors' own list are practitioners, developers, policymakers and organisations, and the seventh, "Impacted individuals," is given a card to read but no account of how a lay reader would use it beyond "pursue remedies" (§3). The paper's critique should be that the card discloses to the wrong party and at the wrong time (release, not determination), which the authors' own reader list supports.

## Caution

A proposal with no evaluation of whether anyone reads or acts on cards; the authors call for "studying how model information is interpreted and used by different stakeholders" as future work (§6). Both examples are the authors' own models or their employer's. The arXiv version was read, not the ACM pages; section numbers match the ACM reference format but page-level citation must be checked against the 220–229 pagination. Nothing in the paper concerns matching, ranking or any deployed system that binds two people.
