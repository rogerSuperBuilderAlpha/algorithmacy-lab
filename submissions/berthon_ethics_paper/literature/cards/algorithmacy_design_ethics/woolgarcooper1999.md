## What it argues

As reported by the secondary sources: Woolgar and Cooper accept that the bridges story is contestable on the facts — they recount a student (named as Jane Douglas in the reading-group post) who told a class she had regularly ridden buses over the Long Island bridges — but argue that Joerges's "debunking" repeats the error it attacks, granting "ontological priority to things over words" (p. 435 per (ii)) by holding that the bridges-actual should overrule the bridges-articulated. Their alternative is that artifacts have an "essential ambivalent quality" (p. 438 per (ii)): the "properties of the artifact are the outcome, not the cause, of their reception" (p. 439 per (ii)); the questions to ask of Winner's story are "what does the text do?" and "how does it gain the status of truth?" (quoted in (ii) without a page number); and the story functions as an "urban legend for academics," so that "if Winner's bridges didn't exist you would have to invent them" (p. 441 per (ii)). Joerges (1999), writing before their reply, characterizes Woolgar's 1991 position as the view that Winner "claims as a definitive effect ... 'what might elsewhere be treated ... as essentially contingent and contestable versions of the capacity of various technologies'," and complains that this camp "somehow avoid[s] referring to a world outside the multilingual murmurs" — "how to do things with words, exactly not ... how to do things with things."

## Relation to the argument

The RQ6 brief says not to lean on the bridges, and this paper is the reason on the constructivist side, as Joerges is on the empirical side. Its use for the design-ethics paper is limited but real: the "ambivalence" thesis — that an artifact's political properties are settled in reception rather than in form — is the strongest available statement of the position the Φ-based construct must answer, because Φ claims to read a structural property (irreducibility) off the coordination form itself, independent of how the parties narrate it. A reviewer from STS will raise this. The honest reply is that Φ is computed on a stated model of who can reach whom, and that model is itself a representation open to contest (the RQ7 levels-of-abstraction point), so the construct concedes model-relativity while denying that the verdict is *only* reception. CUTS AGAINST the construct if taken whole: a fully ambivalent artifact cannot "pursue an objective neither party set," because on this view objectives are ascribed in reading. The paper should engage this by locating the objective in an *operator* (the platform firm) rather than in the artifact, which is where Suchman (1995) also locates it.
