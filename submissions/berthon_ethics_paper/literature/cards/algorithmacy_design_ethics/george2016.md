## What it argues

The abstract (verbatim, via Consensus): "To advance meaning in life (MIL) research, it is crucial to integrate it with the broader meaning literature, which includes important additional concepts (e.g., meaning frameworks) and principles (e.g., terror management). A tripartite view, which conceptualizes MIL as consisting of 3 subconstructs—comprehension, purpose, and mattering—may facilitate such integration. Here, we outline how a tripartite view may relate to key concepts from within MIL research (e.g., MIL judgments and feelings) and within the broader meaning research (e.g., meaning frameworks, meaning making). On the basis of this framework, we review the broader meaning literature to derive a theoretical context within which to understand and conduct further research on comprehension, purpose, and mattering. We highlight how future research may examine the interrelationships among the 3 MIL subconstructs, MIL judgments and feelings, and meaning frameworks."

The paper is the parallel tripartite account to Martela and Steger, published the same year, with "comprehension" where they say "coherence" and "mattering" where they say "significance." Secondary sources give the comprehension definition. The authors' own MEMS chapter (Park et al. 2020) glosses the three as "comprehension (a sense that one's life and the world are understandable and coherent), purpose (a sense that one has valuable goals that one is working towards), and mattering (a sense that one's existence is of significance in the world)." Rudd et al. (2019, *J. Consumer Psychology*), citing George & Park 2017 (p. 206) and Heintzelman & King, gloss comprehension "at the most basic level" as "the perception that stimuli are predictable and conform to recognizable patterns," and "at a more elaborate level" as "a feeling that life and the world has structure and order (vs. being uncertain, unpredictable, and fragmented), an understanding of one's place in the world, and the general feeling that life makes sense." Costin and Vignoles (2021, *J. Personality*) write that "Coherence (also called 'comprehension'; George & Park, 2016) is about perceptions of order, but specifically applied to self-related experiences." A later paper citing George & Park 2016 (the *J. Clinical Psychology* meaning-making study in the Scholar Gateway results) quotes them at p. 217 calling for research on "how violations of meaning frameworks (e.g., occurrence of [an] event that is inconsistent with belief) and subsequent meaning maintenance efforts … may differentially relate to the subconstructs." Martela and Steger (2016, p. 534) credit George and Park with the first longitudinal evidence that purpose and general meaning are distinct (r = 0.61 but different predictors).

## Relation to the argument

This is the comparandum RQ4 asked for: does George and Park's "comprehension" demand more than Martela and Steger's "coherence"? On the evidence available it does not. Every gloss traceable to the authors or their close readers runs on "understandable and coherent," "structure and order," "predictable," and "make sense," with the opposite pole "uncertain, unpredictable, and fragmented." The word "comprehension" invites a stronger, explanatory reading, but the construct beneath it is the same pattern-and-order sense that Heintzelman and King supply to both papers. The convergence of two independent tripartite accounts on a predictability-shaped first facet strengthens the RQ4 finding: Principle III's "operational predictability" is not a weakened substitute for what the meaning literature calls comprehension; it is what that literature means by it. One extra thing this paper offers, if its body is read: the "meaning frameworks" and "violations of meaning frameworks" machinery (p. 217), which would let the draft say what happens to comprehension when a mediator behaves inconsistently with the model a person has built of it.

## Caution

The body has not been read; the p. 217 quotation is second-hand and the comprehension definition is reconstructed from the authors' 2020 chapter and two citing papers, one of which cites the 2017 rather than the 2016 George & Park paper. Do not put page-cited claims from this paper into the draft until a copy is in hand. The three secondary glosses agree, but agreement among citing authors is not the same as the source's own wording. George and Park's later empirical work (MEMS, 2017) finds the three subconstructs separable, but Costin and Vignoles (2020, 2021) find coherence the weakest predictor of global meaning judgments; the arm should not lean on comprehension as the dominant facet without saying so.
