# Dietvorst, B. J., Simmons, J. P., & Massey, C. (2015). Algorithm aversion: People erroneously avoid algorithms after seeing them err. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 144(1), 114–126.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1037/xge0000033 · **Read depth:** full_text (APA online-first copy hosted by Wharton Marketing, headed "2014, Vol. 143, No. 6, 000", pp. 1–13; final 2015 pagination not seen, so page cites below refer to the online-first copy) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top psychology journal; five incentivized experiments · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one forecaster chooses between own/another person's forecasts and a statistical model; no second human is served by the model) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

People abandon a statistical model faster than they abandon a human for the same kind of mistake, and they do so even after watching the model win. Five studies (N = 361, 206, 410, 1,036, 354) had participants forecast real outcomes — MBA students' success percentiles from admissions data, or the rank of U.S. states by departing airline passengers — and then tie an incentivized bonus either to a human's forecasts (their own in Studies 1–3, a yoked stranger's in Study 4) or to a model built from the same data. The model was better in every study: correlation with outcomes r = .53 vs .16 on the MBA task and r = .92 vs .69 on the airline task, with humans producing 15–29% and 90–97% more absolute error respectively (Table 3). Yet participants who had seen the model perform chose it significantly less often than those who had not, in all four studies that manipulated exposure, and the effect held among the 83% (610 of 741) in the model-and-human conditions who had literally watched the model beat the human. Seeing the model perform lowered confidence in it in every study; seeing the human perform lowered confidence in the human in only one (Study 4). Mediation runs through confidence in the model, not the human, and confidence does not fully account for the choice: at equal confidence most people still chose the human (Fig. 4). The authors put it plainly: "People are more likely to abandon an algorithm than a human judge for making the same mistake" (p. 11), and they hedge the Nate Silver 2012 episode as a non-generalizable victory for perfect-looking algorithms. Limitations they list themselves: two tasks, no variation in algorithm transparency or user involvement in building it, and a residue of aversion not explained by seeing errors.

## Relation to the argument

RQ4 (structural axis). This is the founding dyad of the reliance literature: one human, one model, one outcome, and the human's only question is whose forecast to bet on. No second human's interests pass through the model. Where a second person appears (Study 4's yoked participant) they are a rival forecaster, an alternative source the model competes with, not a party the model also serves. The one near-triadic aside is in the General Discussion: people will use algorithms more "when errors are unseen, the algorithm is unseen (as it often is for patients in doctors' offices), or when predictions are nearly perfect" (p. 11). The authors notice, in passing, that a professional often runs a model on a client who never sees it, and then use the observation only to explain adoption, not to ask what the unseeing client should be able to do. That is the confirmed absence this card records: the triad is visible from inside the paper and not modeled. On the objective axis the paper has nothing to say; the model's objective (accurate prediction) is the participant's own, so cooptation cannot arise by design.

## Caution

Lab and MTurk samples of students and crowdworkers, 20-minute tasks, stakes of $0–$10. The "algorithm" is a linear model whose outputs the participant sees but cannot query or adjust (the 2018 follow-up changes that). The version read is the online-first copy, so quoted page numbers will not match the 144(1) pagination. Nothing here bears on interpreting an intermediary's dealings with another party; use it for the baseline asymmetry in error tolerance, and for the doctors'-office aside as evidence that the field saw the triad and looked away.
