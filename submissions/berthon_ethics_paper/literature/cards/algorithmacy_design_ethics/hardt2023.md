## What it argues

"We initiate a principled study of algorithmic collective action on digital platforms that deploy machine learning algorithms. We propose a simple theoretical model of a collective interacting with a firm's learning algorithm. The collective pools the data of participating individuals and executes an algorithmic strategy by instructing participants how to modify their own data to achieve a collective goal" (abstract, p. 1). The firm observes a mixture P = αP* + (1 − α)P₀ where α is the collective's fraction and P* the distribution its strategy induces, and "runs a learning algorithm A on P" (p. 2). The question is "how the success S(α) grows as a function of collective size α" and the "critical mass" needed for a target (Definition 2.1, p. 4). Why collective: "In large-population settings such as online platforms, an individual contributes an infinitesimal fraction of the data used by the learning algorithm. Thus, under reasonable manipulation constraints, individual behavior is largely powerless in systematically changing the deployed model" (p. 4). Two goals are analysed for classification, "planting a signal" (make the classifier map a hidden trigger g(x) to a chosen label y*) and "erasing a signal" (make it invariant to a feature), with critical mass bounded by the signal's uniqueness ξ (Theorems 3.3, 3.5, 3.7). For risk minimisation and gradient descent, gradient-neutralising and gradient-control strategies let "collectives... force the firm to deploy any model with small suboptimality on P₀ of the collective's choosing" (p. 2). Experiments: "almost thirty thousand resumes from a gig platform for freelancers," a DistilBERT skill tagger, "more than two thousand model training runs"; feature-plus-label manipulation controls the prediction "well below 0.5% of the dataset, i.e., fewer than 125 manipulated training data points," text-only manipulation "at a threshold of around 10% of the instances of the target class" (pp. 2, 8). "The more well-trained the model is, the lower the threshold for the collective's success" (p. 2). The stance is stated: "Our approach to algorithmic collective action is decidedly not adversarial. Instead, the strategic manipulations arise through a misalignment of the firm's and the individuals' objectives. Individuals legitimately optimize their utility through data sharing and coordination" (p. 3). It is "a conceptual reversal of strategic classification": "we consider individuals that strategically anticipate the optimizing behavior of the firm" (p. 3). The erasure strategy "requires knowledge of statistics about P₀," which "highlights an important benefit of collective action: information sharing" (p. 6). The discussion adds an Olson-style incentive model: joining is rational once α exceeds the critical mass for S* = c/(1 − γ), after which "the collective is thus self-sustaining" (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing** — the paper's own title says so, §2 (p. 4) says individuals alone are "largely powerless," and its instrument is a pooled strategy run over training data, not a client-side agent acting for one person inside a live coordination. Its value to the paper is that it gives the objective axis a formal home: the firm's objective is explicit, the individuals' is different, and the construct's "pursues an objective neither set" becomes a misalignment the collective can exploit. Two findings the paper should carry. First, the learned-rule case is *more* tractable for the coordinated party than the fixed-rule case (compare sigg2025.md): because the intermediary learns from them, "collectives of vanishing fractional size can exert significant control." That is an argument for Principle I that runs through the intermediary's own dependence on the parties, and it is the closest the literature comes to showing that parity can be engineered rather than granted. Second, the erasure result — a collective can make the model ignore a feature — is a mechanism for the contestation the construct names, without any appeal or explanation channel. CUTS AGAINST the paper's Principle I framing in two ways: the paper's phrase "Algorithmic collectives" is careful never to name an agent that acts for a member, so citing it as a counter-delegation source would misread it; and the model has no second human at all — the far side of the classifier is a label, not a person whose interests the firm also serves. Read it as the theory of why standing must be collective on learning platforms, not as a description of an instrument.

## Caution

Toy settings by design: Bayes-optimal or risk-minimising firms, strategies that assume the collective knows the firm's learning algorithm, and one experiment on one dataset with a small transformer. The paper makes no empirical claim about any real collective; the gig-economy framing is motivation, and the resume dataset is used for a skill-tagging task the platform may not run. The "not adversarial" stance is a normative choice the authors state, not a result; the same mathematics describes data poisoning, and the paper says so.
