## What it argues

Non-AI-experts explore prompts opportunistically, not systematically, and their failures trace to two sources: over-generalisation from single observations and a social lens carried over from human-to-human instruction. The authors built BotDesigner, a no-code chatbot design tool with a Conversation view for local testing and an Error Browser for regression-testing a prompt change against every labelled conversation, and used it as a design probe with ten participants (professionals and graduate students, none with prompt-design experience) asked to make a recipe chatbot behave like a TV chef. Every participant could iterate on prompts locally, but none used the systematic testing interface, most declared success after a single improved utterance, and error labelling died out once participants saw it did not feed the bot. The social lens showed up as insistence on politeness, a strong bias toward direct instruction over in-line examples even after examples visibly worked, expectations that the bot would "remember" prior conversations, and the assumption that "Do not say X" would be understood as a human would understand it. The paper maps these struggles onto the six learning barriers of end-user programming (design, selection, coordination, use, understanding, information) and onto known novice-versus-expert differences in software engineering and ML (Table 2: implicit vs. explicit specification, overconfident vs. cautious testing, opportunistic vs. systematic debugging). Its design section asks for tools that show cause and effect, make effective examples salient at the moment of need, and, drawing on Chalmers and on Inman and Ribes, favour "seamful" interfaces that make the app act in non-human ways rather than pass as a social actor.

## Relation to the argument

RQ11: the establishment's own evidence on the "prompt engineering" remedy the paper names as misapplied. What the study shows is that prompting is a *dyadic* skill — one person, one model, one intended behaviour — and that even in that dyad the skill is opportunistic, socially miscalibrated and resistant to the systematic tooling offered. Two consequences for the paper. First, the remedy-in-the-subject fails on its own ground: participants who could see cause and effect still did not test, so more literacy did not produce more competence; the paper can cite this as the field's own finding that the skill does not scale by instruction. Second, the setting contains no second human and no intermediary objective; the model does what the prompt says, badly. Nothing here reaches the construct's case, where the "prompt" one party writes is interpreted by a system that also reads the other party and pursues a third objective. The transfer the paper wants — that prompt engineering is the wrong competence for a mediated coordination — is supported negatively: the study's failures are failures to specify one's own intent to a compliant system, a strictly easier problem than the construct's. The "seamful" recommendation is, as the scaffolding card notes, a bridge to the frictional-transparency affordance; for this arm it is also the one place the paper's Principle III (predictability over visibility) and the prompting literature meet — the authors want interfaces that make the system behave non-humanly *so that users predict it correctly*. CUTS AGAINST nothing; it grounds the critique of prompt engineering from inside HCI.

## Caution

Ten participants, one task, one model (text-davinci-002), sixty minutes each, with interviewers intervening when participants stalled; the authors "cannot (and will not)" make prevalence claims and say the sample skews toward STEM early adopters. There is no expert comparison group: Table 2's "expert" column comes from prior work on programming and ML. Do not cite it as showing that experts prompt differently, only how novices prompt. The "seamful" suggestion is a design proposal, not a tested intervention. This arm did not re-open the PDF; the reading is the scaffolding pass's.
