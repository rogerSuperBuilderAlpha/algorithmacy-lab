## What it argues

Refusal "is a practice of saying 'no' to how data are collected or used, and rejecting the processes, goals, or authority of data collectors" (§1), or in Simpson's phrase, which the authors adopt, a practice of "say[ing] no without being given a right to say no" (§2). The paper's move is to treat that practice as design: "Because refusers work to reshape socio-technical systems, we argue that refusal is an act of design" (Abstract), since "everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones" (§3, quoting Simon). Written "from the standpoint of people who refuse, rather than the institutions that seek their compliance" (Abstract), it offers four facets that every refusal has, whatever strategy it uses: **autonomy** (individual or collective), **time** (reactive or proactive), **power** ("the extent to which refusal makes change possible") and **cost** ("whether or not refusal can reduce or redistribute penalties experienced by refusers") (Abstract, §5). The cases are IBM's Diversity in Faces dataset and the Illinois class action, the movement to ban police facial recognition, the First Nations OCAP principles, Janet Vertesi's "digital homesteading," CBP airport face scanning, Tor as "refusal by obfuscation," and the Stop LAPD Spying Coalition.

The power facet is the part this arm needs, and its first heading states the problem outright: "Some Offers to Refuse Are Designed to Placate Instead of Create Change" (§5.3.1). Data collectors "often use these pathways of dissent to limit refusers' agency by directing them away from other unspoken possibilities," and "if data collectors frame choices such that all possible options benefit them at the chooser's expense, then people must find ways to refuse on different terms." The CCPA opt-out from facial-recognition training data "sets up a lose-lose situation" — opt out and bias the model against people like you, opt in and train the tool sold to immigration enforcement — and "these opt-out systems do not provide the option of challenging or constraining companies' power to pursue data collection for facial recognition." The contrast case is OCAP, where "exercising power is not merely engaging with what has been offered, but 'attempting to negotiate the terms of one's engagement'" (§5.3.2, quoting Benjamin); "Refusal also involves creating new options and changing the landscape of engagement going forward." On cost: "introducing even small costs can make people less likely to opt out of data collection" (§5.4); "it is coercive to say one has a choice, when one of those choices is automatically penalized" (§5.4.2, quoting Benjamin); and some strategies, Tor among them, "reduce or redistribute the costs of future refusal" through network effects (§5.4.3). The ideal refusal is "collective, proactive, powerful, and low-cost" (§6.1). For designers the lesson is a warning: computer scientists tend to see non-users "as either passive resources to be mined for design ideas, or problems to be solved through design," whereas "refusal from below requires active exertion of agency that exceeds what software is designed to offer," and sometimes "improving the software means acting against the refusers' interests" (§6.2). The close calls, with Benjamin, for *institutionalized* refusal: "cultivate norms and develop mechanisms that allow those who are targeted by a particular initiative to voice dissent on their own terms," and put "greater onus [...] on institutions to incorporate the concerns and insights of prospective research subjects ... upstream" (§6.3).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9, and this is the design vocabulary Principle II was missing. "Structural refusal, not hermeneutics" says that the remedy is not to read the mediator better but to withdraw from, or re-set, the relation; Zong and Matias supply the distinction in their own terms. Their §5.3.1 is a general account of what Lyons et al. (2021) found in one case: an offered channel of dissent that leaves the objective in place is a refusal "designed to placate," and its function is to direct the refuser "away from other unspoken possibilities." Interface-level contest is such an offer. The power facet then gives the criterion Principle II needs: a refusal counts as structural when it changes "the terms of one's engagement" rather than selecting among the options the operator framed. Three further pieces are usable directly. The cost facet is the distribution problem (Cotter & Reisdorf; de Certeau's "elbow-room") restated as a design variable, and the Tor case shows that a refusal affordance can be built so that each additional refuser lowers the next one's cost — which is the design answer to Simmel's observation that the *tertius* profits only while the parties stay uncombined. The time facet separates reactive contest (Lyons's robo-debt appeals) from proactive refusal (the facial-recognition bans), and Principle II belongs on the proactive side. And §6.3's "institutionalized refusal" is how a design principle written from above can avoid the §6.2 trap: the designer's job is to build the mechanism by which those targeted "voice dissent on their own terms," not to decide for them.

Two things CUT AGAINST the paper. First, the authors are explicit that refusal from below "exceeds what software is designed to offer"; a Principle that *designs in* refusal is, on their account, a designer's move, and they warn that designers' improvements can act "against the refusers' interests." The paper must own that Principle II is institutionalized refusal in their sense and say what stops it from becoming a placating offer — the answer has to be that the refusal option changes the operator's objective function, not the subject's menu. Second, their structure is dyadic: refuser against collector. The only place a second affected party appears is "privacy dependencies" (§5.1.2, a photograph depicts several people), and there the second party is a co-victim, not a counterpart whose coordination the refusal also severs. In the triad, one party's refusal binds the other, and nothing in the framework prices that.

## Caution

A framework paper illustrated by curated North American cases, chosen iteratively "to illustrate contrasting positions" (§4); the authors say the framework's generative power "falls short of the ideal" and that the cases "are not meant to be an exhaustive representation." No user study, no evaluation of any refusal, and the standpoint is deliberately partisan ("writing about and for those who are typically excluded"). The subject matter is data collection, not coordination through a mediator; the platform cases the paper needs (a worker refusing an allocation, a customer refusing a ranking) are not among their examples. Quotations are from the ACM HTML, which has no page numbers; Crossref carries two dates, 20 March 2024 (online) and 31 March 2024 (print issue); the ACM page shows the online date, so there is no disagreement to resolve, only a choice of which date to cite.
