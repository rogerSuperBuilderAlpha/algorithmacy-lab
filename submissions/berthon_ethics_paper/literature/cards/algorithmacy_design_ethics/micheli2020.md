## What it argues

Four ideal-type models "emerging from the practices of" actors other than big platforms: "data sharing pools, data cooperatives, public data trusts and personal data sovereignty" (abstract). Data governance is defined as "the power relations between all the actors affected by, or having an effect on, the way data is accessed, controlled, shared and used, the various socio-technical arrangements set in place to generate value from data, and how such value is redistributed between actors" (p. 5). The analytical dimension that matters here is reciprocity, defined in Table 1 as "The power relation between stakeholders for data access and use" (p. 8) and glossed in the text as "the difference between unilateral approaches, such as those in which big data corporations hold most of the decision-making power, to mutual data governance models in which more stakeholders take part in the governance of data" (pp. 9–10). Data sharing pools are partnerships among data holders (the Waze Connected Citizens Program) in which "data subjects tend to be excluded from the relation and are at best depicted as passively benefiting from it" (p. 12). Data cooperatives let subjects "voluntarily pool their data together" (p. 12, quoting Ho and Chuang) and "preserve democratic control over their data" (p. 13), with "high reciprocity since 'all parties are stakeholders and equally affected and bound by the governing rules'" (Ho and Chuang, quoted p. 13); "Enabling mechanisms for DCs are 'bottom-up data trusts' (Delacroix and Lawrence, 2019)" (p. 13); examples are MIDATA and Salus Coop, and "this model struggles to compete and scale up against tech giants" (p. 13). Public data trusts have a public body as trustee, with "trusted intermediaries" and a needed "legal framework mandating private companies to grant access to data of public interest" (pp. 14–15). Personal data sovereignty rests on "personal data spaces, like Digi.me, Citizen-me or Meeco, which consist of 'intermediary services' allowing users to store their personal data... and control their sharing with third parties" (p. 16), strengthened by GDPR Art. 20; its limit is that these "are currently adopted by only a niche of users and often fail to scale beyond pilots," and "Envisioning citizens as 'market agents'... might not fully address the asymmetries of power" (p. 16). The authors "did not find a single model to be 'recommended'" (p. 20) and note that "the same 'buzzwords' can be associated to different rationalities... Data trusts might be powerful means to reduce the power unbalances of the current data economy if adopted within DCs, while they may foster very different aims in DSPs" (p. 20).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing — a typology, with one model that is individual and client-side**. Micheli et al. are useful to the paper for a distinction the sovereignty card did not draw: three of their four models are collective (pools, cooperatives, public trusts) and one, personal data sovereignty, is an *individual* intermediary chosen by the user — the closest thing in the data-governance literature to a client-side agent, and the authors' verdict on it is the verdict the paper must expect for Principle I in isolation: niche adoption, pilot failure, and a "market agent" framing that "might not fully address the asymmetries of power." Their reciprocity dimension is a governance-studies name for the construct's moderator/mediator gradient — from "unilateral" decision by the platform to "mutual" arrangements in which those bound take part — and the paper can borrow it to say that Φ > 0 is the unilateral end. The pools model, where data subjects are "excluded from the relation," is a documented case of the second party vanishing from a coordination that runs on her data. CUTS AGAINST a purely client-side Principle I: on this evidence the individual intermediary exists, has existed since the early 2000s, and has not scaled; the paper should cite the finding rather than propose the tool as new.

## Caution

The preprint, not the version of record, was read; page numbers will not match the Sage article and should be re-pinned. The review is European, non-systematic and, as the authors say, assesses value "more at the level of the imaginary, than from evaluations of tangible outcomes" (p. 19). Its data subjects are citizens and patients; no labour or matching platform is analysed, and "reciprocity" is a property of governance arrangements, not of a live coordination between two people.
