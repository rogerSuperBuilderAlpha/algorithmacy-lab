## What it argues

As restated by its author. The book "give[s] an account of privacy in terms of expected flows of personal information, modeled with the construct of context-relative informational norms," whose "key parameters" are "actors (subject, sender, recipient), attributes (types of information), and transmission principles (constraints under which information flows)" (2011, p. 33). The 2019 essay presents "the book's narrative" (2019, p. 233) as five theses: privacy is appropriate flow, not stoppage; appropriate flows conform to contextual informational norms; norms are specified by five parameters; norms' ethical legitimacy is evaluated by interests of affected parties, ethical and political values, and contextual purposes and values; and all five parameters must be specified (2019, pp. 225–231). For contexts and norms it cites the book at pp. 129–158 (2019, nn. 6, 10) and, for the relation to neighbouring theories of social spheres, at pp. 132–137 (n. 7) — which places chapter 7 there. On the conservatism objection the book raised in 2004, the author reports: "The approach outlined in Privacy in Context remains conservative, but instead of flatly rejecting novel flows, it presumptively favors entrenched norms, simultaneously offering a process for adjudicating between the two. To begin, evaluators follow the approach laid out in CI for pinpointing changes, followed by a three-layered comparative analysis to locate respective strengths and weaknesses. Based on these findings, the evaluator recommends in favor of either the status quo or the challenger on grounds of moral quality" (2019, p. 232); and "According to the book's narrative, when established, normative practices confront competing, novel, disruptive sociotechnical practices, the analysis that ensues compares the two in terms of the three-layer criteria" (2019, p. 233). Chapter 8's title, "Breaking rules for good," is consistent with that being where the process lives; the card cannot confirm it. The co-authored survey restates the same structure: "though CI is conservative, in the sense that it presumes in favor of entrenched norms, it nevertheless has built into it a set way for systematically evaluating and updating norms. This is done by examining balance of interests, general ethical and political values, and contextual values and purposes. It follows that norms adapt to their environment" (Benthall et al., 2017, p. 11), and calls the teleological factors — "Contextual ends, purposes, and values" — "the 'essence' of a context" (p. 10). The same survey's verdict from the engineering side: "To the extent that CI has a conservative impulse, it is to warn against the agitation caused by disruptive technologies that change the environment too quickly for social evolution to adapt. This grand theory of privacy is not actionable for computer scientists" (p. 47).

## Relation to the argument

The plan asked whether Part III already answers the "no agent" critique, and whether the Principle III bridge survives the book. On the carriers available: the book names an agent, and the agent is the one the critique predicts. The 2019 restatement of the book's process has "evaluators" who pinpoint changes and "the evaluator" who "recommends" (p. 232) — a third party performing a comparative analysis, not the person inside the flow. Benthall et al. (2017, p. 47) say from the implementer's side that the process is "not actionable for computer scientists," which is the same point about the design tempo: the theory's adaptation runs at the speed of "social evolution," and a mediator that changes its own flows runs faster. So the book, as its author describes it, confirms rather than answers the second critique. The first critique — no term for a determination binding a second party — is likewise confirmed by every carrier's inventory of parameters (five, all about a flow of information about a subject); none of the carriers reports a chapter on decisions, allocations or matches. The third, the Walzer ally reading, is carried by the 2004 article and does not need the book. The Principle III bridge (a declared operating envelope as a norm of flow the mediator commits to) rests on the transmission-principle parameter, which the book made explicit (2019, pp. 228–230, citing the book at 96): the concept is there, and the enforcing instrument in every carrier is law, policy or the evaluator's recommendation, never something the mediated person holds. **Remedy locus: norms/institution** is entered on that basis and is marked as secondary-sourced in the census.
