# Dietvorst, B. J., Simmons, J. P., & Massey, C. (2018). Overcoming algorithm aversion: People will use imperfect algorithms if they can (even slightly) modify them. *Management Science*, 64(3), 1155–1170.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher PDF hosted on the Wharton faculty site, version-of-record pagination) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top management journal; three incentivized experiments plus five supplementary studies (S1–S5) on OSF · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (forecaster and a model told to be off by 17.5 percentiles on average; Study S5 adds another person's forecasts as an alternative advisor, not as a co-served party) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

A small, bounded right to override converts refusal into use. In Study 1 (Wharton lab, N = 288), participants forecasting high-school students' math percentiles chose an admittedly imperfect model 32% of the time when the choice was all-or-nothing, but 73% when they could change 10 of 20 forecasts and 76% when they could adjust every forecast by up to 10 percentiles (χ² = 24.19 and 28.40, both p < 0.001), and they earned more as a result ($1.53 and $1.46 vs $1.14). Study 2 (MTurk, N = 816) shrank the allowance: 47% chose the unmodifiable model against 71%, 71% and 68% for adjust-by-10, -5 and -2, so "willingness to use the model was not detectably altered by imposing an 80% reduction of the amount by which they could adjust" (p. 1161). Study 3 (N = 818) showed that people who had adjusted the model for a round were more satisfied, rated the model as better relative to themselves, and chose to keep using it after feedback; constraining the adjustment kept forecasts closer to the model and improved accuracy. The General Discussion reports Study S5, a 2 × 2 in which the entity whose forecasts could be adopted was a person or an algorithm: the ability to modify raised choice of the human's forecasts from 21% to 41% and of the algorithm's from 46% to 69%, the same gain (p. 1168). The prescription is managerial: never frame adoption as all-or-nothing, and accept that "participants in our studies did often worsen the algorithm's forecasts when given the ability to adjust them. However, we may have to accept this error so that, overall, people make less error" (p. 1168).

## Relation to the argument

RQ4, structural axis, with a direct bearing on the contestation clause of algorithmacy. The paper is the cleanest evidence that the *right to modify* — not its magnitude — is what makes a person accept a determination they know to be imperfect; 2 percentiles buys as much as 10. That is a design finding about contestability as a felt affordance, which the essay can use for the "contest what it commits" limb without stretching. But the setting is one human and one model, and the model commits nothing that binds anyone else; the forecaster's adjustment costs no third party. The only other human (Study S5) is a substitute advisor. So the paper models how a person tolerates an intermediary's error when the person alone bears it; it does not reach the case where an adjustment to the mediator's output shifts an outcome onto someone on the far side of the mediator. Confirmed absence of a second served party. On the objective axis, the model's objective is again the user's own, so the "objective neither party set" condition cannot be exercised.

## Caution

Students and crowdworkers; stakes up to $5; a single forecasting task in the main studies (a second task in S2/S3); adjustment ranges only 2–10 percentiles, so the authors cannot say what one percentile or twenty would do. The satisfaction and long-run-use results come from a single second round. Professional forecasters were not studied, and the authors flag settings where forecasters hold information the model lacks as ones where tight constraints might hurt. Do not read the S5 human-advisor result as a triad; it is a two-advisor comparison with one decision-maker.

## S2 adversarial verification (2026-09-18)

**Verdict:** confirmed (main results; two details unverifiable in the copy I could open)

**What I checked:** The Wharton-hosted publisher PDF the card cites did not resolve for me; I retrieved the accepted manuscript from Penn's ScholarlyCommons (handle 20.500.14332/39569, 38 pp., "Running Head: OVERCOMING ALGORITHM AVERSION") and checked every statistic and quotation against it.

**Findings:** All headline numbers match the manuscript: Study 1 N = 288 with 32% choosing the unmodifiable model against 73% (change-10, χ² = 24.19) and 76% (adjust-by-10, χ² = 28.40), both p < .001; Study 2 N = 816 with 47% against 71%, 71% and 68% for adjust-by-10, -5 and -2; Study 3 N = 818; the model "off by 17.5 percentiles on average"; Study S5's 21% to 41% (human) and 46% to 69% (algorithm) with the z-test reported as non-significant. Both quotations ("not detectably altered by imposing an 80% reduction" and "we may have to accept this error so that, overall, people make less error") are verbatim. Two things I could not check: the version-of-record page numbers (1161, 1168) and the average bonuses ($1.53, $1.46, $1.14), which do not appear in the manuscript's text and may sit in a table or only in the published version. The relation-to-argument section's claim that the right to modify, not its magnitude, drives use is exactly the authors' own conclusion from Study 2.
