## What it argues

The article's claim is that ordinary users can resist algorithmic power, and that the resistance they can mount is tactical, complicit and corrective. The abstract opens the gap — "little has been said about the possibilities of users to resist algorithmic power" (p. 523) — and closes it with the thesis: "user agency in relation to algorithms can emerge from alternative uses of platforms, in the aftermath of algorithmic logics, and give birth to complicit forms of resistance that work through 'repair' politics oriented towards correcting the work of algorithms." The introduction fixes the shape of that agency: "media practices of repair are tactics to correct existing shortcomings within algorithmic culture rather than by producing alternative pathways. In that sense, they establish reactive user agency in an algorithmic aftermath"; the tactics "can be based on users complying with algorithmic logics but resisting their output, 'tricking' algorithms to work toward unintended ends through what we call 'repair politics,' or the politics of partial and improvisational correction of biased results of algorithmic work" ("The drama of algorithmic culture"). The theoretical frame is Raymond Williams on media as practice, Feenberg's "technical politics," and, for the form of the tactic, de Certeau and Scott. The single case is Johanna Burai's *World White Web* (2015): a Swedish design student, finding only white hands in Google Image results for "hands," photographed six non-white hands, bought a .net domain, wrote a press release, and had friends share it; once Dazed, Buzzfeed, the BBC's Fader and Swedish public broadcasters uploaded the images, "several of her images climbed to the top results in Google's image search, where some of them still remain" ("The World White Web project"). The authors are exact about what this was: "Burai's intervention was not revolutionary – it was an example of an incidental and, to a large extent, spontaneously organized media campaign with no dramatic consequences"; it "was tactical, incidental, and sporadic"; "Burai did not repair or alter the code of the algorithm in any way. Thus, we understand repair politics as a corrective work that works through improvisations, patches and ingenuity, together with and within algorithmic systems, to make them generate unintended, alternative outputs" ("User tactics and repair politics"). Scott's "calculated conformity" and de Certeau's tactic "determined by the absence of a proper locus" carry the analysis; the resistance "imply[s], in their intention or meaning, an accommodation with the system of domination" (Scott, as quoted). The conclusion names the family: "It is a complicit form of resistance, one that does not deny the power of algorithms but operates within their framework, using them for different ends"; "in the age of algorithmic governance resistance might be increasingly dependent on collaboration with traditional media"; and "We have traced a very specific form of algorithmic resistance that emerges from within the system, a tactic that is reformist rather than revolutionary (de Certeau, 1984). Many other ways of resisting algorithmic power … are thinkable (e.g., more structural approaches advocating for regulation at the policy level or individual tactics of obfuscation)" ("Resisting algorithmic power"). One sentence disclaims the burden the framework might seem to place: "We are not suggesting, of course, that it is the responsibility of users alone to try to change and act on power asymmetries produced by algorithmic systems."

## Relation to the argument

The draft's closing line asks for "concrete mechanisms of **counter-agency, structural parity, and automated refusal**," and the audit offered "algorithmic resistance" as a possible anchor for the first term. Read in full, the article does not anchor it; it is the best-articulated statement of the thing Principle I is defined against. Four features settle that. The resistance is human, not delegated: Burai acts by campaigning, and nothing acts for her. It is one-off and reactive — "incidental," "sporadic," "in an aftermath" — where counter-delegation is a standing agent inside a live coordination. It succeeds by *complying* with the algorithm's logic (feeding PageRank the high-authority uploads it rewards), which is subject-side accommodation, the "better reader" the draft says cannot carry the remedy. And its parties are one user and a search engine: no counterpart whose coordination the same determination binds, so nothing to contest on another's behalf. The authors themselves file obfuscation (`bruntonnissenbaum2015.md`) and regulation as *other* families, which confirms that repair politics is not the client-side instrument. CUTS AGAINST the draft's use of "algorithmic resistance" as a synonym for counter-agency, and confirms the plan's hypothesis (§1.7, §5). What the paper can take: (i) a citable, sympathetic account of why individual tactical repair does not scale — the authors concede the result is "quite insignificant" in measurable terms and rests on media attention; (ii) their own sentence that it is not "the responsibility of users alone," which the draft can quote when it argues that literacy-based remedies individualize a structural problem; (iii) the article's explicit taxonomy, which places counter-delegation outside "repair" and beside obfuscation and regulation. Proposed replacement for the closing line: retire "counter-agency" and name the arm's own Principle I term — "…and instead build mechanisms of counter-delegation — a party's own agent acting inside the coordination, the micro-instrument of collectively won standing — structural refusal, and bounded outputs." If the draft wants a contrast sentence: "What users can do alone is repair — tactical, complicit correction after the fact (Velkova & Kaun, 2021); what the design principles ask for is an agent that contests before the determination lands."

## Caution

One case, one query, one activist, observed by the authors from interviews and public coverage; no measurement of the search results beyond their report that "some of them are usually among the top ten." The register is media theory (Williams, Feenberg, de Certeau, Scott), not HCI or platform economics, and the object is a search engine, not a two-sided market; every transfer to the mediated triad is the paper's inference. The article is from 2019 (online first) and predates the LLM turn; its "algorithm" is PageRank.
