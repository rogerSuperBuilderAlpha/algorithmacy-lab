# Friedman, B., & Nissenbaum, H. (1996). Bias in computer systems. *ACM Transactions on Information Systems*, 14(3), 330–347.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/230538.230561 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher scan with original pagination, from the author's site at nissenbaum.tech.cornell.edu) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal, ACM TOIS; conceptual framework built from 17 published cases · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator (NRMP, Sabre/Apollo) alongside human–technology (MLSA, BNAP) · **Remedy locus:** designer and professional community — anticipate contexts of use, state constraints on an initial screen (p. 344), refuse a client's wanted bias with professional backing (p. 344); the affected user has no instrument, and "biased systems offer no equivalent means for appeal" (p. 331) is left as diagnosis · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-19

## What it argues

Bias in a computer system is systematic and unfair discrimination — a system that "denies an opportunity or a good or ... assigns an undesirable outcome to an individual or group of individuals on grounds that are unreasonable or inappropriate" (p. 332) — and it enters through three doors: preexisting bias imported from institutions or from "the client commissioning the design or the system designer" (Table I, p. 334); technical bias from tool limits, decontextualised algorithms, random-number defects, and the formalisation of human constructs; and emergent bias, which "arises only in a context of use," typically "some time after a design is completed, as a result of changing societal knowledge, population, or cultural values" (p. 336). The authors derived the framework from 17 systems in banking, commerce, education, medicine and law (p. 333) and test it on three cases. The National Resident Match Program is the one that matters here: hospitals and students each submit ranked lists, "are not permitted to make other arrangements with one another or to attempt to directly influence each others' rankings" (p. 336), and the Admissions Algorithm, in every case of conflict, favours the hospital's ranking over the student's. The NRMP's own operators defended this as replicating "what happens in an actual admissions process without a computerized matching program," since changing it "would imply changing the NRMP's role from one of passive facilitator to one in which the NRMP would be intervening" (Graettinger and Peranson, quoted p. 337). Friedman and Nissenbaum call this preexisting bias and add that "centralized computing systems with widespread use can hold users hostage to whatever biases are embedded within the system" (p. 339), because opting out of the NRMP means forgoing most placements. The couples case is their emergent-bias exhibit: the algorithm handled married pairs badly only once enough women entered medicine. The Multilevel Scheduling Algorithm case records that users with long jobs "uncovered the MLSA's strategy and developed a counterstrategy" — stopping and restarting jobs so each chunk re-entered the top-priority queue (p. 340). The remedies section asks designers to anticipate contexts of use, to "articulate constraints on the appropriate contexts of a system's use" on an initial screen (p. 344), and to seek professional-community backing when "a client actually wants a bias to be present" (p. 344).

## Relation to the argument

This is the earliest text in the library that describes the construct's structure without its vocabulary. The NRMP has both axes: two parties who cannot reach each other (no direct channel, by rule), an intermediary that interprets both rank lists and commits a determination that binds both, and an objective — preserve the pre-computer hospital advantage — set by one side and its operator rather than by the matched pair. The sentence on p. 331 that "unlike in our dealings with biased individuals with whom a potential victim can negotiate, biased systems offer no equivalent means for appeal" is the contest deficit the construct names, stated thirty years early. The MLSA counterstrategy is algorithmacy avant la lettre — users inferred the scheduler's objective and adapted tactically — though the authors report it as evidence of bias, not as a competence to cultivate, and their remedies run through designers and the professional community, never through users. Emergent bias bears on "adapt as it changes" but with the arrow reversed: in Friedman and Nissenbaum the system stays fixed and the population or knowledge drifts; the construct's intermediary is what drifts. Both readings need stating. For RQ5 the paper shows that VSD's founding text already treated the commissioning client as a source of bias, not as a stakeholder whose value has standing — the seed of the cooptation reading that later VSD stakeholder theory does not develop.

## Caution

A 1996 conceptual paper: 17 systems known "by and large" from the published literature, no user study, no measurement, pre-machine-learning, and the three worked cases are a matching algorithm, a 1960s scheduler and a Prolog encoding of a statute. The bias definition requires unfairness, so a system that pursues an operator objective fairly across both parties falls outside its scope — the paper is not a general theory of cooptation. The NRMP reading above is mine; the authors frame it as bias, and Roth's later redesign of the match is not covered.

## Relation to the protect/promote question (2026-09-19, F3 pass)

Protective in telos and instrument, without a promotive term. The construct the paper defends is "freedom from bias," defined by what a system "denies" or "assigns" unfairly (p. 332); the remedies ask designers to anticipate contexts of use and to "articulate constraints on the appropriate contexts of a system's use" (p. 344), and the professional community to back a designer against "a client [who] actually wants a bias to be present" (p. 344). Nothing in the paper says what a fair match or a fair schedule should *deliver* to the matched parties; fairness is the absence of unreasonable discrimination. For the matrix this is the cleanest protective row of the five, and it is also the one text that has the construct's structure on the page: the NRMP binds two parties through an algorithm neither can reach, and the remedy locus is the designer, never the relation.
