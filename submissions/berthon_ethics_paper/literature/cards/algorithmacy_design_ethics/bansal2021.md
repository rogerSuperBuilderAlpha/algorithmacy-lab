# Bansal, G., Wu, T., Zhou, J., Fok, R., Nushi, B., Kamar, E., Ribeiro, M. T., & Weld, D. S. (2021). Does the whole exceed its parts? The effect of AI explanations on complementary team performance. *Proceedings of the 2021 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '21)*, 16 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3411764.3445717 · arXiv:2006.14779v3 (12 Jan 2021) · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v3, the camera-ready with the ACM header; DOI confirmed from the ACM reference block in the PDF; ACM article number not verified, so omitted) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top HCI venue; large crowdsourced experiments · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one crowdworker plus a classifier "Marvin"; the decision subjects invoked in the framing — defendants, patients — never enter the design) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Explanations did not buy complementary performance; they bought compliance. The authors first show that every prior study claiming explanations help (Table 1: deceptive reviews, income, loan default, hypoxemia, nutrition, Quiz Bowl, house prices) had an AI that beat the best human–AI team, so "one would achieve an even better result by stripping humans from the loop." They then fix the AI's accuracy near the human's — RoBERTa at 84% on beer and Amazon book reviews against 82–87% unassisted humans, 65% vs 67% on LSAT items — and run 1,626 crowdworkers (about 100 per condition) through confidence-only, Explain-Top-1, Explain-Top-2, an Adaptive strategy that explains the runner-up only when confidence is low, and expert-written explanations. Every team condition beat both human and AI alone (beer: 0.89 vs 0.84 and 0.82), but no explanation condition beat showing confidence (beer z = −1.18, p = .24; book z = 1.23, p = .22; LSAT z = 0.427, p = .64). Split by AI correctness, explaining the top class raised accuracy when the AI was right and lowered it when the AI was wrong, so "explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness." Adaptive explanations shifted agreement in the intended direction without moving final accuracy. Self-reports: 47% used the AI as a prior guide, 25% as a post-check, 22% mostly ignored it, 6% mostly followed it; 27% of those using confidence named an explicit threshold (about 83–90%). The discussion worries that in medicine the "presence of explanations simply soothes the experts (e.g., doctors), making them more compliant so they blindly (or become more likely to) agree with the computer" (§6.2).

## Relation to the argument

RQ4, structural axis, with a caution for the essay's transparency affordances. The paper is the standard citation for "explanations increase reliance, not appropriate reliance," and the essay should use it as such. Structurally it is a dyad throughout: the introduction says the AI "augments one or more humans" and names judges deciding bail and doctors deciding care, so the second human — the defendant, the patient — is present in the motivation and absent from the experiment, where the task is labelling a review. Confirmed absence of a second served party. The paper also cannot speak to the objective axis: Marvin's objective is the same label the worker is paid for. What it does give the essay is a mechanism for why a coopted intermediary would be dangerous — an explanation that reads as sensible raises acceptance independently of correctness — and a design hint (Adaptive) that a mediator which discloses its doubts changes the reliance pattern even when accuracy does not move.

## Caution

MTurk workers on sentiment and LSAT items, 13–16 minutes, a fixed 84% or 65% AI, LIME saliency highlights or short expert text, one accuracy metric. The authors themselves list expertise, high-stakes domains and richer explanation formats as unexplored. The Adaptive result is suggestive on agreement and null on accuracy. Nothing about how a decision subject on the far side of Marvin would fare, and the "complementary performance" framing counts only the worker's accuracy.

## S2 adversarial verification (2026-09-18)

**Verdict:** corrected

**What I checked:** Downloaded arXiv 2006.14779v3 (16 pp., ACM camera-ready header) and checked every quotation, every statistic and the self-report percentages against the text.

**Findings:** Two small slips fixed in the body. (1) The §6.2 quotation read "simply soothe the experts"; the paper says "the presence of explanations simply soothes the experts (e.g., doctors), making them more compliant so they blindly (or become more likely to) agree with the computer." (2) The LSAT comparison of Explain-Top-1 against confidence is reported as z = 0.427, p = .64, not z = 0.43; the book z = 1.23 carries p = .22, now added. Everything else holds: 1,626 users (Table/§4.3), about 100 per condition, RoBERTa 84% on beer and book reviews against 87% and 85% unassisted humans before example selection and 82% ± 0.09 in-study on beer, 65% vs 67% on LSAT, team accuracy 0.89 vs AI 0.84 and human 0.82, beer z = −1.18 p = .24, self-report split 6/47/25/22%, 27% naming a confidence threshold with means 83 ± 8 (beer) and 89 ± 7 (book), 13 minutes (beer/book) and 16 minutes (LSAT) mean completion, the "stripping humans from the loop" and "augments one or more humans" sentences, and "explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness." The relation-to-argument section claims nothing the paper does not support; the "standard citation for explanations increase reliance" reading is the authors' own framing in the abstract and §6.
