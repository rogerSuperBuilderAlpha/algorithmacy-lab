## What it argues

The paper takes "meaning" from positive and existential psychology and makes it a quality of interaction alongside "consistency, transparency, usability, and positive emotions" (p. 1). Its framework has five components, "connectedness, purpose, coherence, resonance, and significance," which "are oriented toward the self/the world, motivation, understanding, feeling/intuition, and evaluation" (p. 1). Three assumptions are stated up front (p. 3): the framework addresses "meaning as a moment-to-moment experience," treats meaning "as a chiefly subjective experience" that "must be subjectively generated," and holds that the experience "is not ineffable" but decomposable and "empirically assessed in some form."

Table 1 (p. 4) is the piece the draft can use, because it names each component's *absence*: connectedness ("always connected to the self and the world") is absent as "Self-alienation"; purpose ("sense of core goals, aims, and directions") as "Aimlessness"; coherence ("comprehensibility and making sense of one's experiences") as "Absurdity and uncertainty"; resonance ("clicking with something or feeling it is right") as "Feeling of 'wrongness' and anxiety"; significance ("enduring value and importance") as "Triviality."

Coherence is defined as "the extent to which one's experiences make sense" (p. 5); "An event or experience makes sense to the extent that a person can assess how their experience fits their personal beliefs, goals and previous experiences in an expected way," and "Objects and images that are comprehensible or presented in an expected manner may also inspire a sense of coherence" (p. 5), with Heintzelman et al.'s seasonally ordered tree pictures as the example. When coherence fails, an experience "may devolve into 'a string of events that fails to coalesce into a unified, coherent whole'" (p. 5, quoting Steger). The authors then separate coherence from purpose: "it is about understanding 'what' one is doing and experiencing rather than 'why' that is so" (p. 5). On the relations among components: "Coherence arguably constitutes a necessary condition for purpose, resonance and significance: If we cannot make sense of our experiences, they feel wrong, and it is difficult to evaluate whether they matter to us and set future goals. But a sense of coherence alone will not automatically render our experiences purposeful or significant" (p. 6).

The literature review settles the vocabulary question. The verb the field uses is *afford*: "Hassenzahl et al. [41] called for design to focus on affording moments of meaning, and argued that meaning stems from the extent a product satisfies various psychological needs. However, this call is not backed up by any empirical data" (p. 2). Hassenzahl's group "concluded that it is difficult to imagine situations in which interactive technologies afford experiences of meaning. In contrast, Mekler and Hornbæk found that this depends on users' motivation [84]. Eudaimonically motivated (i.e., striving to pursue personal ideals) and social experiences were considered more meaningful than hedonic experiences, which pertained to relaxation and short-term pleasure. Relatedly, Lukoff et al. found that users consider habitual smartphone interactions to be among the most meaningless [72]" (p. 2). "Some work has looked into the design qualities that afford the experience of meaning" (p. 2), and the introduction records that "Some researchers even argue that computers struggle to support and might easily undermine meaning [69, 70]" (p. 1). On design the authors are sceptical of easy claims: "Both quotes suggest that the experience of meaning comes easy, provided it is targeted accordingly through design. In contrast, Ambe et al. stressed that 'the added meanings, contributed by the users themselves, are personal, encouraged but not provided by design'" (p. 8). Their open questions include designs that "help finding patterns in experiences, help foster predictability" (p. 10), and the link to SDT: "Martela et al. recently found psychological need satisfaction predictive of meaning in life [77], lending credence to the claim that need satisfaction contributes to meaningful user experiences [41]. It is yet to be empirically examined to what extent the five components relate to the satisfaction of individual needs" (p. 11).

## Relation to the argument

The draft sentence this card repairs (DRAFT.md, Introduction): "Evaluating a design through the lens of human flourishing requires examining whether an artifact fosters 'meaning-affording design' or installs an extractive, 'meaning-hostile' environment that fractures daily life, hijacks attention, and replaces authentic agency with algorithmic control."

"Meaning-affording" is nobody's term as a hyphenated adjective, and "meaning-hostile" is nobody's at all; the draft should drop the quotation marks on both. But the verb underneath the first is real and traceable: Hassenzahl et al. (2013) "called for design to focus on affording moments of meaning," and Mekler and Hornbæk write of "design qualities that afford the experience of meaning" (p. 2). So the draft's coinage compresses a genuine CHI usage, and the honest fix is to cite the usage and use its words. The second half of the pairing has a real anchor too, in Table 1's absence column: what a "meaning-hostile" environment would produce is, in this framework's terms, self-alienation, aimlessness, absurdity and uncertainty, and triviality (p. 4). Proposed replacement wording, to run after the SDT sentence proposed in peters2018.md:

> The same question can be asked of meaning. Mekler and Hornbæk (2019) treat meaning as a quality of interaction with five components—connectedness, purpose, coherence, resonance and significance—and name what its absence looks like: self-alienation, aimlessness, "absurdity and uncertainty," and triviality (Table 1, p. 4). Whether "interactive technologies afford experiences of meaning" (p. 2) is, on their reading, an open empirical question, and habitual smartphone use is the case users themselves rank as most meaningless (p. 2, citing Lukoff et al.).

Two things cut against the draft's use. First, the framework is about one person's experience, generated subjectively (p. 3); it offers no purchase on an *environment* that is hostile, only on experiences that lack the components, so the draft's "extractive ... environment that fractures daily life" is not something this paper can be cited for. Second, its coherence is the same predictability-shaped construct that martela2016.md records: comprehensibility "in an expected way" (p. 5), ordered trees, patterns and predictability (p. 10). That confirms the audit's Bucket 2 finding rather than the draft's current Martela and Steger bullet: a bounded-output design principle is what this coherence construct calls for, and the paper says so in its own open question about designs that "help foster predictability" (p. 10). The draft's "unpredictable, black-box algorithmic environments destroy baseline cognitive coherence" is a hypothesis consistent with both papers, not a finding of either.

For the plan's vocabulary question: the eudaimonic/hedonic split is here as the authors' own prior result (p. 2), and it links meaning to SDT need satisfaction (p. 11), so the draft can move from "meaning" to "need support" in one step with these two cards.

## Caution

A conceptual synthesis with no new data; the illustrative analysis is of twenty CHI papers chosen by counting the string "meaning*" (p. 7). The authors flag their own limits: the framework is silent on the *sources* and content of meaning (p. 9), says little about "the meanings of interaction" in the semiotic sense (p. 10), and assumes subjective meaning while acknowledging the objective/intersubjective debate (p. 9). No operationalisation of all five components existed at publication (p. 10). The 2016 CHI paper by the same authors on eudaimonic versus hedonic experiences ("Momentary pleasure or lasting meaning?", their ref. [84]) is summarised here on p. 2 and was not carded separately; cite it directly if the draft leans on the eudaimonic finding rather than on the framework.
