## What it argues

"Rather than providing a definition of agency as a binary property, we identify 4 key characteristics which, particularly in combination, tend to increase the agency of a given algorithmic system: underspecification, directness of impact, goal-directedness, and long-term planning. We also discuss important harms which arise from increasing agency – notably, these include systemic and/or long-range impacts, often on marginalized or unconsidered stakeholders" (abstract). The four: "Underspecification: the degree to which the algorithmic system can accomplish a goal provided by operators or designers, without a concrete specification of how"; "Directness of impact: the degree to which the algorithmic system's actions affect the world without mediation or intervention by a human"; "Goal-directedness"; "Long-term planning" (§2.1). The authors use "agency" "to counter the somewhat prevalent view that the developers of an algorithmic system have full control over its behaviour" while insisting that "attribution of agency to an autonomous system in no way shifts moral responsibility from humans onto that system" (§2.2). Principal–agent theory is the intuition: "The agent acts... on behalf of the principal to achieve the principal's goals... Crucially, the agent and principal have different incentives and information" (§2.2). Harms (§4): "systemic, delayed harms" (a single rent-setting algorithm; reinforcement-learning recommenders that "will have incentives to change or manipulate users' internal states"); "collective disempowerment," either "diffusion of power away from humans" or "exacerbating the extreme concentration of power amongst the 'coding elite'" — with Lazar's "publicity requirement" invoked: "Collective self-governance requires not just having decisions be made, but understanding why those decisions are made" (§4.2); and "harms yet to be identified" (reward hacking, convergent instrumental goals). Paths forward (§5) are audits, simulation, scenario planning and regulation. Deployment is expected to follow "industry interests, and not the interest of those most likely to be harmed" (§3.3.1).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8, checked as the nearest candidate for a peer-reviewed treatment of personal agents acting for users against platform agents. Verdict: **adjacent, not relevant to Principle I** — the paper's agent always belongs to the operator; its principal is the firm; and the individuals who could be served by an agent of their own appear only as "stakeholders" who suffer harms. There is no sentence in the text contemplating an agent on the user's side. Sorted: **grounds the why, not an instrument** — and the "why" it grounds is Principle III's, not Principle I's: its four characteristics are a vocabulary for saying how far an intermediary has moved from carrying signals to pursuing "a goal provided by operators" without a specified "how," which is the objective axis of the construct in FAccT terms. "Directness of impact" (no human in the loop) is the structural axis: a determination that binds without a human's intervention. The paper is therefore useful as a definitional bridge — an intermediary with high underspecification, direct impact and goal-directedness is a mediator in the construct's sense — and the paper's Φ > 0 could be glossed for that audience as a triadic special case of their "increasing agency." CUTS AGAINST the paper's Principle I in one indirect way: everything the authors say about agency's harms (underspecified goals, drift, reward hacking) applies to a counter-agent acting for a user as much as to the platform's, so Principle I must answer why a second agentic system reduces rather than doubles the problem. The search for a peer-reviewed personal-agent-versus-platform-agent source found only preprints (see counter_agent_search_notes.md); this card records that the best-known candidate is about the operator's agent.

## Caution

A position paper: no cases studied, no data, its examples drawn from press and prior work; 22 authors with randomised order and a safety-community framing (convergent instrumental goals, Cicero, AutoGPT) that a design-ethics reader may find speculative. The arXiv version was read; page-level citation requires the ACM 651–666 pagination. The paper's treatment of "principal" is a one-paragraph analogy, not a theory of delegation, and should not be cited as such.
