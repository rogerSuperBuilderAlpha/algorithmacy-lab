## What it argues

The 2021 revision "was undertaken in close cooperation between the SAE On-Road Automated Driving (ORAD) Committee and ISO TC204/WG14 through a Joint Working Group formed in 2018," adding terms and "multiple corrections and clarifications ... to address frequently misunderstood concepts" (Rationale). Its self-description is explicit about what it is not: "This document does not provide specifications, or otherwise impose requirements on, driving automation systems (for further elaboration, see 8.1)"; its guiding principles are that it should "Be descriptive and informative rather than normative," "Provide functional definitions," "Be consistent with current industry practice" and "Be useful across disciplines, including engineering, law, media, and public discourse" (Rationale). Standardising the levels serves, among other purposes, "Clarifying the role of the (human) driver, if any, during driving automation system engagement" and "Answering questions of scope when it comes to developing laws, policies, regulations, and standards" (Rationale).

The term the paper's driver analogy needs is real and is clause 3.21 of the ISO/SAE text. "OPERATIONAL DESIGN DOMAIN (ODD): Operating conditions under which a given driving automation system or feature thereof is specifically designed to function, including, but not limited to, environmental, geographical, and time-of-day restrictions, and/or the requisite presence or absence of certain traffic or roadway characteristics." Note 1: "While Level 3 and 4 ADS features/vehicles are designed to operate exclusively within their respective ODDs, some ODD conditions are subject to rapid change during on-road operation (e.g., inclement weather, obscured lane lines). Such transient changes in the operating environment do not necessarily represent an 'ODD exit,' as the ADS determines when such a change in conditions requires fallback performance (whether by the fallback-ready user or ADS)." Note 2 refers to "Section 6" for "the significance of ODDs in the context of the levels of driving automation." Neighbouring defined terms in clause 3 include the dynamic driving task (3.10), DDT fallback (3.12), minimal risk condition (3.16), monitor (3.18) and object and event detection and response (3.19).

## Relation to the argument

RQ10. The driver analogy is already in the engineering vocabulary, and "operational design domain" is its term: a declared envelope of conditions within which a system is "specifically designed to function," outside of which responsibility passes, by a defined fallback, to a human or to a minimal-risk manoeuvre. That is Principle III stated as a taxonomy. What the driver of a Level 3 car needs to know is the ODD and the fallback regime — is the feature engaged, are we inside its domain, who takes over when we leave it — and not the perception stack; which is Sarter and Woods's mode awareness (know the regime, not the computation) and Muir's predictability basis (trust grounded in consistent behaviour within stated conditions) given a legal-engineering form. The paper can use the ODD to say what a "bounded output" is for a mediator: a stated domain of inputs and situations for which its determinations are guaranteed to fall within an envelope, plus a stated fallback when they cannot. The joint ISO/SAE issue also shows the concept has crossed from engineering into "laws, policies, regulations," which is where the paper wants Principle III to land. Two features CUT AGAINST using it as more than vocabulary, and the paper should say so. First, J3016 is explicitly non-normative — it "does not ... impose requirements" — so the ODD is a definition, not a guarantee; the guarantee, if there is one, comes from the manufacturer's own declaration and whatever regulator enforces it. Second, Note 1 hands the boundary judgement to the system: "the ADS determines when such a change in conditions requires fallback performance." The system decides whether it is still inside its own envelope. For a mediator in the triad that is the problem, not the solution — the party that commits determinations also adjudicates whether the conditions for its determinations hold — and Principle III must specify who declares and who verifies the domain, or it inherits the self-certifying structure the standard leaves open. The structure is a dyad (user and system) over a world; there is no second human whose coordination the ODD also governs, though the "fallback-ready user" is the closest engineering analogue to a party who must stand ready to take over a coordination the mediator drops.

## Caution

The standard's body — the level definitions, clause 6 on ODD and the levels, clause 8's disclaimers — is behind SAE's and ISO's paywalls and was not read; clause 3.21 and the Rationale are quoted verbatim from the free previews, and the clause number is the PAS's, assumed but not confirmed to match J3016_202104's own numbering. Whether the ODD concept has been given normative force elsewhere (UNECE regulations, national type-approval rules, BSI PAS 1883's ODD taxonomy) is not covered here. Nothing in the document concerns platforms, coordination or objectives; it is a vocabulary for one class of automation, and the analogy is the paper's.
