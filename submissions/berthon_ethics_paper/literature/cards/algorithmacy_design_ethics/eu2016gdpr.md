# European Parliament & Council (2016). Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (General Data Protection Regulation), Articles 21–22. *Official Journal of the European Union*, L 119, 4.5.2016, pp. 1–88.

## What it argues

Section 4 of Chapter III is headed "Right to object and automated individual decision-making." Article 21(1): "The data subject shall have the right to object, on grounds relating to his or her particular situation, at any time to processing of personal data concerning him or her which is based on point (e) or (f) of Article 6(1), including profiling based on those provisions. The controller shall no longer process the personal data unless the controller demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights and freedoms of the data subject or for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims." For direct marketing the right is absolute: the subject "shall have the right to object at any time ... which includes profiling to the extent that it is related to such direct marketing" (21(2)), and on objection "the personal data shall no longer be processed for such purposes" (21(3)). The right must be "explicitly brought to the attention of the data subject and shall be presented clearly and separately from any other information" (21(4)), and — the clause that matters for technical refusal — "In the context of the use of information society services, and notwithstanding Directive 2002/58/EC, the data subject may exercise his or her right to object by automated means using technical specifications" (21(5)).

Article 22(1): "The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her." Paragraph 2 removes that right where the decision "(a) is necessary for entering into, or performance of, a contract between the data subject and a data controller; (b) is authorised by Union or Member State law ...; or (c) is based on the data subject's explicit consent." In cases (a) and (c) the controller "shall implement suitable measures to safeguard the data subject's rights and freedoms and legitimate interests, at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision" (22(3)). Paragraph 4 bars such decisions on special-category data unless Article 9(2)(a) or (g) applies. (Article 23 lets Union or Member State law restrict Articles 12–22 for listed public interests.)

## Relation to the argument

RQ9, and the two articles fall on opposite sides of Principle II's line. Article 22(3) is the legal form of hermeneutic contest: intervention, a point of view, a challenge to the decision — the "last line of defence" Lyons et al. describe, exercised after the determination and leaving the processing in place. Article 21 is the legal form of structural refusal: an objection that stops the processing itself, "at any time," "including profiling," and, under 21(5), by "automated means using technical specifications." That last clause is the closest thing in European law to a mandate for a machine-readable refusal signal, and it is the hook on which Glaze- and Nightshade-style refusal (Shan et al. 2023, 2024) and robots.txt-type opt-outs hang. So the paper can say that "sever the feedback loop" is not only a design wish but an existing right, with two qualifications that are themselves findings. First, Article 21(1) is defeasible: the controller keeps processing if it "demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds ... which override" the subject's interests, so the refusal is contestable by the mediator, and nothing in the Article preserves the subject's continued use of the service. Second, and more damaging, Article 22's strong right evaporates exactly where the construct locates the mediator. The 22(2)(a) carve-out for decisions "necessary for entering into, or performance of, a contract" is the platform case — the allocation, the ranking, the price are the performance of the contract — and in that case the subject is left with 22(3)'s hermeneutic safeguards only. The Regulation thus institutionalises the very substitution Principle II objects to: where the mediator is contractual, refusal gives way to contest. Rudin (2019) reads the same clause from the modelling side and concludes that "only an explanation is required, not an interpretable model"; the construct's reading is that only *contest* is required, not refusal. Both cut the same way. CUTS AGAINST the triad: the Regulation knows one data subject and one controller. The counterpart party whose coordination a 21(1) objection also severs (the customer who no longer sees the worker's profile, the seller whose listing the buyer's objection de-personalises) has no standing in Section 4 at all, and the sovereignty arm notes that the business user's rights sit in a different instrument (Regulation 2019/1150).

## Caution

Three articles of an 88-page regulation, read as text; no case law was consulted this pass, and the sovereignty arm's card records that the CJEU's SCHUFA (C-634/21) and Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22) judgments have widened Article 22's reach in ways this card does not capture. Whether a platform's allocation or ranking is "necessary for ... performance of a contract" is contested in the literature and not settled here. Article 21(5)'s "technical specifications" have no agreed implementation, and the Article does not say what happens to the service once the objection is upheld.
