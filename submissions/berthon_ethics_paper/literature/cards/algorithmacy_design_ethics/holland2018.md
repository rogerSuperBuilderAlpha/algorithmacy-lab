# Holland, S., Hosny, A., Newman, J., Joseph, J., & Chmielinski, K. (2018). The Dataset Nutrition Label: A framework to drive higher data quality standards. arXiv:1805.03677.

## What it argues

The abstract (p. 1): "The Dataset Nutrition Label (the Label) is a diagnostic framework that lowers the barrier to standardized data analysis by providing a distilled yet comprehensive overview of dataset 'ingredients' before AI model development... the Label is comprised of diverse qualitative and quantitative modules generated through multiple statistical and probabilistic modelling backends, but displayed in a standardized format." The prototype has seven modules on ProPublica's Dollars for Docs dataset. The benefits are stated by audience: "For data specialists, the Label will drive more robust data analysis practices, provide an efficient way to select the best dataset for their purposes, and increase the overall quality of AI models... For those building and publishing datasets, the Label creates an expectation of explanation, which will drive better data collection practices" (p. 1). The premise: "Like the fruit of a poisoned tree, an AI model trained on problematic or missing data will likely produce problematic outcomes" (p. 2).

The reader is named without ambiguity. "Three goals drive this work. First, to inform and improve data specialists' selection and interrogation of datasets and to prompt critical analysis. Consequently, data specialists are the primary intended audience" (p. 5), where "data specialist" is defined as "all professionals utilizing data in automated decision making systems: data scientists, analysts, machine learning engineers, model developers, artificial intelligence researchers" (p. 2, n. 3). The public appears once: "While targeted mainly at a professional audience, many of the modules do not require expert training for interpretation and can thus be utilized in a public-facing Dataset Nutrition Label" (p. 6). The paper positions itself beside Gebru et al.'s "Datasheets for Datasets," noting that under that proposal "dataset, API, and model creators would be responsible for creating the datasheets, not end users" (p. 5). The limitations (§4.1, p. 14): generalization across dataset types; "problematic ground truth data may lead to futile or even harmful comparisons"; for proprietary data "This would likely require that the dataset creator or controller create the Label"; and "the Label might not prompt the right questions or reactions for the data specialist, leaving certain biased data undetected."

**Successor.** Chmielinski, K. S., Newman, S., Taylor, M., Joseph, J., Thomas, K., Yurkofsky, J., & Qiu, Y. C. (2022). "The Dataset Nutrition Label (2nd Gen): Leveraging context to mitigate harms in artificial intelligence." arXiv:2201.03954 (v1 10 January 2022, v2 10 March 2022). Its abstract describes "an updated Label in late 2020" with "context-specific Use Cases & Alerts presented through an updated design and user interface targeted towards the data scientist profile." The arXiv record carries no journal reference, so no peer-reviewed version was confirmed; read at abstract depth only and not carded. The peer-reviewed cousin remains Gebru et al.'s datasheets (CACM 2021), unverified on this pass.

## Relation to the argument

Grounds the phrase "data nutrition labels" in the draft's opening (line 65): "Mainstream AI ethics promotes prompt engineering, mechanistic interpretability, model cards, data nutrition labels, and digital provenance standards... on the assumption that if users are simply taught how to 'read' the black box, human agency will be preserved." Cite as "dataset nutrition labels (Holland et al. 2018)" — the proper name is "Dataset Nutrition Label," and the citation is to a preprint, which the reference list should show.

The card says what mitchell2019.md says. The Label discloses to a downstream reader — the "data specialist" at "the point of data analysis," before a model exists — and never to a coordinated second party. The person the eventual model decides about is not in the paper; the "public-facing" variant is an afterthought at p. 6 with no reader described. One correction to the draft's framing follows. Its sentence says these instruments teach *users* to read the black box; the Label's reader is the builder, not the end user, so the sharper critique is that the document is addressed to the wrong party for the end user's purposes, upstream of the model and to its maker. Proposed wording for the draft, if the author wants the point in the sentence: "each of these documents is addressed to the builder or adopter of a model; none is addressed to the person the model is used on." The paper's own limitation — that a Label for proprietary data "would likely require that the dataset creator or controller create the Label" (p. 14) — is the same self-attestation limit the C2PA and Model Cards cards record, and the three should be cited together.

## Caution

A draft preprint and a prototype: tabular data only, datasets under 10k rows, one demonstration dataset (§2, p. 6); the supporting survey is the authors' own, of unreported size, which they call limited in reach and "disproportionately indexed to American and European respondents working in the private sector" (§5). No evaluation of whether anyone reads or acts on a Label. Nothing in the paper concerns a deployed system or a decision about a person.
