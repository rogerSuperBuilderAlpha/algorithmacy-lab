# Armer, J. M. (1992). Social psychological effects of literacy by language combinations in an African society. *Sociological Inquiry*, 62(3), 308–326.

## What it argues

Abstract, verbatim: "According to the 'autonomous' model, literacy in any language produces uniform consequences for individuals such as interest in current affairs, personal efficacy, status aspiration, and logical thinking. This view is countered by the 'ideological' model which holds that consequences vary across languages depending upon the processes through which literacy is acquired and the purposes for which it is intended. These competing views are tested with survey data from 576 young men in Kano, Nigeria. Sixteen autonomous model dependent variables are regressed on dummy variables of illiteracy (the comparison category) and major literacy combinations in three languages — Arabic, Hausa, and English. The results show many insignificant effects and wide variations in coefficients for different literacy combinations, even when adjustments are made for Arabic literacy measurement limitations and for social selectivity in literacy acquisition. The evidence provides support for the ideological model of literacy consequences and has theoretical implications for the control and use of literacy for personal and social development."

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. A new card, not in the sibling library. It gives the paper a quantitative test, not just ethnography, on the cognitive/attitudinal register: literacy "in any language" does not produce uniform individual effects; effects depend on "the processes through which literacy is acquired and the purposes for which it is intended." That is Street's ideological model confirmed by survey, and it is one more reason to keep the paper's claims at the level of what an order requires rather than what a medium does to a person. It also states the two models in one clean sentence each, which the paper can quote in place of Street's own definitions until Street is read.

The scaffolding essay's use. The Kano result — three literacies with different consequences, and illiterates as the comparison group who are not uniformly worse off on the sixteen measures — is a small-scale version of Graff's objection to "stripped of agency." Not about print.

## Caution

Abstract only; sample is 576 young men in one city; the dependent variables are attitudes and self-reports, not institutional outcomes; the study is a test of individual-level effects and says nothing about whether institutions in Kano required literacy. Access flag: Wiley paywall for the body.
