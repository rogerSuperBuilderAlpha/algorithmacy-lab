# Parasuraman, R., & Riley, V. (1997). Humans and automation: Use, misuse, disuse, abuse. *Human Factors*, 39(2), 230–253.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1518/001872097778543886 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (verbatim abstract via Consensus/Semantic Scholar index; the article is paywalled at SAGE and no author deposit, CORE, OpenAlex or Wayback copy was found) plus secondary characterisation from Lee & See (2004, pp. 50–51) and Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000, p. 294), both read in full · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, flagship HF journal; conceptual review · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology, with designers and managers named as a separate source of "abuse" · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The abstract sets out the four-part taxonomy that the later literature treats as canonical. Use is "the voluntary activation or disengagement of automation by human operators," and trust, workload and risk influence it, though "large individual differences make prediction of automation use difficult." Misuse is "over reliance on automation, which can result in failures of monitoring or decision biases," and the factors named for monitoring are workload, reliability and consistency, and "the saliency of automation state indicators." Disuse, "the neglect or underutilization of automation, is commonly caused by alarms that activate falsely," typically because the base rate of the target condition is ignored in setting the false-alarm/miss trade-off. Abuse is the designer's and manager's category: "the automation of functions by designers and implementation by managers without due regard for the consequences for human performance, tends to define the operator's roles as by-products of the automation," and "can also promote misuse and disuse of automation by human operators." Lee and See (2004, p. 50) gloss misuse as inadvertently violating the automation's critical assumptions and disuse as rejecting its capabilities; Parasuraman et al. (2000, p. 294) restate the abuse point as designers automating "every subsystem that leads to an economic benefit for that subsystem" and leaving "the operator to manage the rest," so that "the human operator's roles and responsibilities [are defined] in terms of the automation."

## Relation to the argument

The pass was told to check whether "abuse" ever gestures at a third party's interest, and at the level of the abstract it does gesture — but the third party is the designer or manager who *deploys* the automation, and the harm is to the operator's role, not to a counterpart on the other side of the automation. That is a two-tier structure (blunt end sets the automation, sharp end lives with it), which is exactly the objective axis's premise that the objective belongs to someone other than the parties coordinating; it is not the triad. The abstract's four categories all take one operator and one automation over a shared task, and none names a second human whose interests the automation also manages. So the answer to the pass's question is: no second party; the designer appears only as the origin of the automation's shape. The paper is still the right cite for the claim that the classic taxonomy already locates the automation's objective outside the operator, which is the seed the objective axis grows from.
