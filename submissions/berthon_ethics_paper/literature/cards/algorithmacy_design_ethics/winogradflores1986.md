## What it argues

As quoted by Suchman (1994, pp. 180, 183–184), the book presents "the network of speech acts that constitute straightforward conversations for action — those in which an interplay of requests and commissives are directed towards explicit cooperative action," pictured as a state diagram (Figure 5.1, pp. 64–65) "in which each circle represents a possible state of the conversation and the lines represent speech acts" — Request, Promise, Counter, Accept, Reject, Withdraw, Renege, Declare complete. The design proposal (p. 159, quoted at Suchman p. 180): "We are not proposing that a computer can 'understand' speech acts by analyzing natural language utterances ... What we propose is to make the user aware of this structure and to provide tools for working with it explicitly. This is being done experimentally in a computer program that we are developing called a 'coordinator', designed for constructing and controlling conversation networks in large-scale distributed electronic communication systems ... An individual performs a speech act using THE COORDINATOR by: selecting the illocutionary force from a small set of alternatives ...; indicating the propositional content in text; and explicitly entering temporal relationships to other (past and anticipated) acts." The motivation (pp. 161–162, quoted at Suchman pp. 180–181): "In their day-to-day being, people are generally not aware of what they are doing ... more or less blind to the pervasiveness of the essential dimensions of commitment. Consequently, there exists a domain for education in communicative competence"; implementations "used to manage large software engineering enterprises" reported that "by providing a computer tool to maintain the structure of the requests and commitments, they were able to greatly improve productivity" (p. 161). Kiem (2013, n. 4) cites p. 163 of the 1987 printing for the book's claim that design is ontological — "the design of practices and possibilities to be realized through artifacts," in Flores et al.'s 1988 restatement quoted by Suchman (p. 186).

## Relation to the argument

The Coordinator is the earliest CSCW instance of a system that formats a commitment between two people and holds the record against both. On the construct's axes it is a *partial* case (see suchman1994.md for the full argument): the interpretation is delegated to the users, who tag their own illocutionary force — the machine does not infer; the commitment is inscribed and enforced by the machine — it warns of breakdowns and keeps the ledger; the direct channel survives — the book's own goal is "to make the user aware of this structure," and users could and did talk outside it. The system therefore binds both parties without interpreting either, which is the opposite profile from a matching platform that interprets both parties and binds them through a determination they never see. That contrast is worth stating in the paper, because it answers the question this pass was asked: does the Coordinator satisfy the construct? Structurally, only half — it is a channel with a memory. On the objective axis, its authors state one openly: "education in communicative competence," i.e. a normative improvement of the parties, which Suchman reads as managerial. That is an objective neither coworker set, but it is an *announced* one, and the construct's cooptation reading depends on the objective being neither announced nor inspectable. A further point for RQ8 and RQ9: the book's phrase "education in communicative competence" is the closest thing in this literature to a *competence* the mediator demands of its users — and it is a competence the *designer* prescribes (learn our ontology of requests and promises), not one the user develops to read the mediator. Algorithmacy inverts the direction of that competence.

## Caution

Not read. Quotations are reproduced from Suchman (1994), whose page references are to the 1986 Ablex edition; Kiem's p. 163 is the 1987 Addison-Wesley printing and may not align. The productivity claim is the authors' own report of an unnamed deployment. The book contains nothing about optimization, inference, or a party with hidden interests; do not modernize it.
