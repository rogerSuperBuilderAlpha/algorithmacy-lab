# Suchman, L. (1995). Speech acts and voices: Response to Winograd et al. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, 3(1), 85–95.

**Identifier:** doi not confirmed (Kluwer 1995; page image carries journal pagination) · **Read depth:** full_text (PDF at lri.fr/~mbl/ENS/CSCW/2016/papers/Suchman-JCSCW95.pdf) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed response closing a journal debate (Winograd's reply plus twelve commentaries) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator, with explicit attention to a fourth position — the designer's "location" and "voice" · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Suchman restates the 1994 critique around two premises: "designers of CSCW systems are designers of organizational life," and CSCW technologies "require the construction of a relation between computational formalisms and the structuring of the organizational activities that are to be transformed" (p. 85). She narrows the target: "my concern with respect to the Coordinator is less an argument with the use of speech act theory as such, than with the proposal that any system of categories might provide the grounds for a universally applicable, normative system of organizational behavior," and "even if it were the case that such a normative system somehow turned out to be practically adequate ... I would still want to question whose interests were represented in the resulting order. There are no more universal criteria of organizational effectiveness than there are universal categories of human communicative intent" (p. 87). She admits she did no study of the Coordinator in use and defends reading designers' texts as "fair game for critique" (pp. 88–89), listing four aspects of technology analysis: articulated design premises, how they are inscribed in the artifact, design practices, and how the artifact is taken up "including appropriations that result effectively in its redesign" (p. 89). On Randall's challenge she locates politics not in categories as such but in "who produces artifacts for, or on behalf of, whom, on what premises and with what stated objectives. It is there that the politics lie" (p. 90), and she concedes being "deeply undecided on the question of the extent to which how artifacts can be taken up, while clearly not determined by how they are configured, is nevertheless structured in various ways by specific aspects of their design" (p. 91). Footnote 11 recaps Bowers's use of Winner's bridges. Her strongest new move answers Curtis's claim that coordination technologies will be judged "on performance": "Performance evaluations presuppose some definition of what the objectives of a particular social/technical arrangement are and the definition of objectives is itself a political process," and against Winograd's "structure of interactions that is effective for coordination within an organization" she replies that "there is no single structure of interactions or measure of effective coordination within an organization ... Interests vary, sometimes even conflict, and some voices speak louder than others" (p. 93). Footnote 12 disclaims that "speech act theory, the Coordinator, or any other technology is inherently bound to surveillance and control" (p. 92).

## Relation to the argument

This is where the **objective axis** is first stated in CSCW terms: the mediator's "effectiveness" is defined by someone, and that someone is neither of the coordinated parties. Suchman does not say the Coordinator has an objective of its own; she says its objective is the designer's/manager's, stated in what she calls "the neutral voice from which this statement is made" (p. 93). That is the cooptation reading one step short of the platform — the third objective is external and human, not internal to the mechanism. For RQ6 the paper is more useful than the 1994 original because it explicitly refuses Winner's object-centred reading: politics is "the deeply reflexive relation between artifacts and the circumstances of their production and use" (p. 90), a relation, not a property of the thing. That supports the pass's contention that Winner's claim (b) must be re-read as a claim about a *form* to fit the construct, and it shows one CSCW author already making that move. For RQ9 the four-aspect list (p. 89) is a usable audit frame. CUTS AGAINST: her stated remedy is voice — "acknowledge discordant voices within the CSCW community, and let them speak" (p. 90), new working relations with unions and worker associations (p. 94) — not a competence in the coordinated parties. The construct's move from *voice in design* to *skill in use* is not in her, and a reader who knows this literature will ask why the paper prefers a user competence to participatory design; the answer has to be the structural one (no direct channel, no seat at the design table) rather than anything Suchman supplies.

## Caution

A closing response in a debate; it cites twelve commentaries this pass did not read (Button, Lynch, Randall, Bogen, Curtis, Harper, de Michelis, Malone, King, Orlikowski, Agre, Grudin and Grinter), so some of what she concedes cannot be checked against what was said to her. Same limits as the 1994 paper: no field data on the Coordinator, and a system with no inference or optimization.
