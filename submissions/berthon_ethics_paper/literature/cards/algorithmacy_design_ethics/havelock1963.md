## What it argues

Havelock's claim is that Greek culture "at the turn of the century [c. 400 BC] was still essentially oral" (p. 39), and he meets the obvious objection — inscriptions, decrees, poets who wrote — with a distinction the paper can use: "the habit of public inscription does not necessarily imply popular literacy: it might imply its opposite ... In each case we may be dealing with a situation best described as craft literacy, in which the public inscription is composed as a source of referral for officials and as a check upon arbitrary interpretations. As for the poet, he can write for his own benefit and thereby can acquire increased compositional skill, but he composes for a public who he knows will not read what he is composing but will listen to it" (p. 39). The decisive variable is not the technology but the readership: "The clue to the whole problem lies not in the use of written characters and writing materials, on which scholarly attention has been concentrated, but upon the supply of readers, and this depended on a universalisation of letters. The reading trauma, to use a modern term, had to be imposed at the primary level of schooling, and not the secondary" (p. 39). "One cannot build up a habit of popular literacy on a fund of inscriptions" (p. 40). He posits "a stage, characteristic of the first two-thirds of the fifth century, which we may call semi-literacy, in which writing skills were gradually but rather painfully being spread through the population without any corresponding increase in fluent reading" (p. 40), notes that "the educational apparatus, as so often since, lagged behind technological advance, and preferred to adhere to traditional methods of oral instruction when other possibilities were becoming available" (pp. 40–41), and dates the outcome: "by the middle of the fourth century the silent revolution had been accomplished, and ... the cultivated Greek public had become a community of readers" (p. 41). The cognitive register follows at once: "Once it is accepted that the oral situation had persisted through the fifth century, one faces the conclusion that there would also persist what one may call an oral state of mind as well; a mode of consciousness so to speak" (p. 41).

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Havelock is the origin of both registers, and pp. 38–41 show the seam between them. The competence/institution claim — that the effect of a writing technology depends on "the supply of readers," which depends on schooling, which lags the technology — is his and is modest, and it is the claim this paper's history section should make. The cognitive claim ("an oral state of mind") is what Ong amplifies and what the critics contest. Havelock's "craft literacy," in which inscriptions serve "as a source of referral for officials and as a check upon arbitrary interpretations" (p. 39), is also the earliest statement in this set of writing as an institutional instrument of accountability held by a few, which is close to the paper's picture of an incumbent order's competence.

The scaffolding essay's use. Havelock supports the essay only by analogy and only for Greece: a stage of craft literacy did exist there, and it did end with a "universalisation of letters." He says nothing about Gutenberg, the medieval church, or the vernacular Bible, and his "silent revolution" took the schooling system, not a machine, to accomplish. The essay's clerical-monopoly framing cannot be footnoted to him.

## Caution

A classicist's argument about Athens, resting on Plato's testimony and on inferences from Old Comedy; the dating of Athenian literacy is contested and Havelock's own later work (1976, 1982) restated it. Only four pages were read here. The scan's pagination was not checked against a printed copy; Ong cites the same passages as pp. 38–41 region ("craft literacy," Havelock 1963) without page numbers, so the pages given should be treated as probable.
