## What it argues

As quoted in Hertz's notes: "A large part of the work of innovators is that of 'inscribing' this vision of (or prediction about) the world in the technical content of the new object" and "I will call the end product of this work a 'script' or a 'scenario'" (p. 208); the method cannot rest on "the designer's or user's point of view alone. Instead we have to go back and forth continually between the designer and the user, between the designer's projected user and the real user" (pp. 208–209), reading both "the world inscribed in the object" and "the world described by its displacement"; and "technical objects thus simultaneously embody and measure a set of relations between heterogeneous elements" (pp. 205–206). Latour's companion chapter fixes the vocabulary Akrich introduced: scripts, scenes or scenarios "played by human or nonhuman actants" (n. 3); de-scription as "the retrieval of the script from the situation" (n. 4); prescription as "whatever a scene presupposes from its transcribed actors and authors" (n. 8); de-inscription and subscription for actors who escape or accept their lot (n. 15); pre-inscription for "all the work that has to be done upstream of the scene" (n. 16). The widely repeated sentence that "like a film script, technical objects define a framework of action together with the actors and the space in which they are supposed to act" does not appear in Hertz's notes; I have not verified it and the paper should not quote it from this card. The chapter's cases, by every account I read, are lighting kits and generators sent from France to West Africa, where the script written for one user population meets another.

## Relation to the argument

RQ1: script is the design-time inscription of a user and the construct's "determinations that bind" are its run-time descendant, so the paper owes Akrich the term and should not attribute it to Verbeek, who says as much ("with Madeleine Akrich, [Latour] coined the term 'script'," verbeek2008b, p. 94). The "go back and forth between the designer's projected user and the real user" method (pp. 208–209) is dyadic in the designer–user sense; there is no second user whose script differs from the first's, and the de-scription is of one object by one analyst. RQ2: de-scription, the retrieval of the script from the situation, is what a seam does for a user without an analyst, and Akrich's insistence that the script is legible only by moving between designer and user is a warning that a seam authored from the designer's side alone discloses the projected user, not the real one.

## Caution

The chapter itself is unread. The quotations are taken from a third party's notes and could be mis-transcribed or mis-paginated; S2 must open the chapter. I have not seen the African case material and describe it only as others do. The term "script" is here, but the four-fold vocabulary (prescription, pre-inscription, circumscription) is Latour's elaboration in the neighbouring chapter and the joint Akrich–Latour glossary, not necessarily Akrich's own wording.
