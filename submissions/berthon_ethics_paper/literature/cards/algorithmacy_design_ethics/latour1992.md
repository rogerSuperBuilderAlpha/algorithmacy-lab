## What it argues

The seat belt that will not let the car start, the door-closer "on strike" at La Villette, the Berliner key that cannot be withdrawn until the door is relocked: Latour uses these to argue that sociology's "missing mass" of morality sits in nonhumans, "the hidden and despised social masses who make up our morality" (p. 153). Delegation is the core operation, defined by a ledger: list the work people would do without the door in one column and the "gentle pushing" with it in the other, and the difference is what has been "delegated (or translated or displaced or shifted down) to the hinge" (p. 154). He then gives the method that matters here: "every time you want to know what a nonhuman does, simply imagine what other humans or other nonhumans would have to do were this character not present. This imaginary substitution exactly sizes up the role, or function, of this little character" (pp. 154–155; the sentence begins at the foot of 154 and runs onto 155). Delegation to a human groom fails on cost and reliability; delegation to a spring succeeds but "an unskilled nonhuman groom thus presupposes a skilled human user," and this behaviour "imposed back onto the human by nonhuman delegates" is, after Akrich, "prescription. Prescription is the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms" (p. 157). Prescriptions discriminate (the hydraulic closer against "my little nephews nor my grandmother," p. 158) and can be resisted by a foot or a wedge (p. 159). Two further ideas: the gap between "the prescribed user and the user-in-the-flesh," which "nothing in a given scene can prevent" (p. 161); and circumscription, the "gradient of concentric circles" a machine draws around itself, illustrated by photocopier labels from "ADD PAPER" through "ADD TONER. SEE MANUAL, PAGE 30," the homemade "DON'T ADD THE TONER YOURSELF, CALL THE SECRETARY," to "DO NOT OPEN — DANGER, HIGH VOLTAGE": "Each of these messages addresses a different audience, from the widest ... to the narrowest" (pp. 161–162). Morality and force lie on one gradient, from "classic moral human beings endowed with self-respect" through signs to "stubborn and efficient machines," and "it is the complete chain that makes up the missing masses, not either of its extremities" (p. 166). The seat belt shows re-inscription of contradictory demands (firm and lax, fast to buckle and to unbuckle), and "the program of action is in practice the answer to an antiprogram against which the mechanism braces itself" (p. 168). The notes define the vocabulary: script and de-scription (nn. 3–4), prescription (n. 8), de-inscription and subscription (n. 15), pre-inscription (n. 16), circumscription (n. 17).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, structural axis: pp. 154–155 already contain a removal test for whether a mediator is load-bearing. Imagine the nonhuman absent and ask what the humans would then have to do; if the answer is "nothing much," the thing was a channel, and if the answer is "rebuild the wall," it was a party. Φ formalizes this counterfactual, and Latour himself flags the risk in the method (n. 5, citing Collins). The chain from morality to force (p. 166) is a structural triad by construction: an enunciator who is absent, a delegate that acts in its place, and an enunciatee who is prescribed to. That is closer to the construct than anything in postphenomenology, with one difference that matters for the objective axis: the delegate serves the maker's programme, not an objective "neither party set." Two findings cut against the construct. First, "binds both" is not given by structure; Latour shows binding is achieved only through escalating delegation against antiprograms (the sensor unlinked, the belt that straps you at the door), so it is a degree and a contest, not a property. Second, the photocopier gradient (pp. 161–162) shows that designers already author asymmetric disclosures by audience, and that a disclosure can restrict agency ("CALL THE SECRETARY") as readily as enable it. For RQ2 this is asymmetric seamfulness described in 1992 without the name, and it warns that a seam is itself a prescription about who may act.

## Caution

Essayistic and deliberately provocative; no empirical study, a handful of doors, keys and belts, and the author admits he "built in my article a made-up author" (p. 160). The pagination I cite is the reprint's, not the MIT Press original, and any citation in the paper must either use the reprint or be re-paginated against the 1992 volume. The "prescribed user" is a class, never two persons with opposed interests, and the enunciator is always a designer or engineer, never an operator with an ongoing objective, so the triad here is design-time, not run-time.
