# Floridi, L. (2016). Faultless responsibility: On the nature and allocation of moral responsibility for distributed moral actions. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A*, 374(2083), 20160112.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1098/rsta.2016.0112 · **Read depth:** full_text (Royal Society HTML version of record; section numbers cited because the HTML carries no page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, themed issue "The ethical impact of data science" · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** network of human, artificial and hybrid agents producing a distributed action; patient = the affected system · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Distributed moral actions (DMAs) are "morally good or evil (i.e. morally loaded) actions caused by local interactions that are in themselves neither good nor evil (morally neutral)," produced by "a network of agents, some human, some artificial (e.g. a program) and some hybrid (e.g. a group of people working as a team thanks to a software platform)" (abstract). Standard ethics cannot allocate responsibility for them because it makes intentionality a necessary condition and "intentionality is not closed under causal implication": if Alice means to cause a and Bob means to cause b, and a and b cause C, it does not follow that Alice and Bob mean to cause C (§2, steps (1)–(7)). Floridi's remedy is a patient-oriented ethics that asks whether "the patient affected is morally better or worse off" and then treats the multi-agent system "as being equivalent to a multi-layered neural network": forward propagation outputs the DMA, and back propagation allocates distributed moral responsibility (DMR) "to all the agents in the network that bring about the DMA" (§1, §4). The five steps are: identify the DMA; identify the causally accountable network; back-propagate responsibility "to make each agent in N prima facie equally and maximally responsible"; correct the action; repeat until the outcome is axiologically satisfactory (§4). Step three borrows from strict liability — "responsible by default or poena sine culpa" — and step five from epistemic-logic common knowledge: if all agents know they will all be held responsible, the evil action is less likely to be output at all.

Two Dutch Supreme Court cases calibrate the mechanism. In "the three cyclists" (HR 9 March 1948) each of three riding abreast is fully responsible "because it is very easy for each of them to rectify the situation"; in "the four boats" (HR 19 January 1931) only the fourth ship moored is responsible "because it was much more difficult for the other three to rectify the situation" (§4). Responsibility is therefore back-propagated in proportion to the ability to avoid the outcome, with an overridability clause for nodes that "show no involvement." The close of the objections section names the failure this is meant to stop: "Too often 'distributed' turns into 'diffused': everybody's problem becomes nobody's responsibility" (§5, final paragraph before the Conclusion). Footnote 22 concedes that "this appeal to common knowledge does not address different agents' relative costs of defection from the network" — an executive can quit in protest, a junior employee cannot.

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, second half. This is the ethics-side account of what the companion draft, citing Stark and Vanden Broeck, calls accountability "distributed, deflected, and denied": Floridi's "distributed turns into diffused" is the same diagnosis, and Tsamados et al. (2022) pair this paper with Rubel et al.'s "agency laundering" — blaming the algorithm to distance oneself from an outcome — as the mechanism of denial. The paper also gives the triad's irreducibility an ethical consequence it did not have before. The four-boats rule says responsibility falls on whoever could most easily rectify the situation; the three-cyclists rule says it falls on everyone when anyone could. In the triad, Φ > 0 means no direct channel survives — neither human can rectify the coordination by leaving it — while the intermediary can change its own objective at will. On Floridi's own calibration, then, irreducibility is the condition under which the humans' default responsibility is overridden and the intermediary (or its operator) carries it. That is an argument the paper can make with a source from inside the Floridi school, and it links the Φ verdict to an allocation rule rather than leaving it as a description.

The paper CUTS AGAINST the construct in one respect. Its network nodes interact through "morally neutral" local actions; nothing in it models a node that pursues its own objective and whose objective *is* the source of the loaded outcome. A platform is not a neutral node, so the mechanism as written would back-propagate full default responsibility to both humans for outcomes the intermediary's objective produced. Only the overridability clause and footnote 22 (unequal costs of defection) rescue the humans, and Floridi leaves both undeveloped.

## Caution

Conceptual, with no empirical component and, by design, no axiology: the mechanism "says nothing about the axiology implemented" (§5) and works equally to invert a good outcome. The two legal cases are traffic and mooring rules, not platform cases, and the bot example (Wikipedia bots, footnote 20) is explicitly deferred to "another article." The patient is "the affected system," a single receiver; the paper never considers agents who are simultaneously nodes in the network and the patients of its output, which is exactly the position of both humans in the triad. The risk-aversion and innovation-stifling challenges (§5) are acknowledged, not resolved. The last ~3,000 characters of the HTML (references) were truncated in retrieval; the body was complete.
