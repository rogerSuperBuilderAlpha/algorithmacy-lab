# Logg, J. M., Minson, J. A., & Moore, D. A. (2019). Algorithm appreciation: People prefer algorithmic to human judgment. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 151, 90–103.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher PDF hosted on the first author's site, version-of-record pagination; supplement on OSF not read) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, leading JDM journal; six pre-registered experiments (1A not pre-registered) plus a benchmark study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (a judge receives identical numeric advice labelled as from "an algorithm" or from other people; the human advisor is an alternative source, never a co-served party) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Before any performance feedback, lay people weight advice more when it is labelled algorithmic. The design holds the advice constant and varies only its label, and it uses Weight on Advice (WOA), the fraction of the gap between initial estimate and advice that the judge closes. Weight-from-a-photograph (Exp 1A, N = 202): WOA .45 for "an algorithm" vs .30 for "other participants" (d = .42). Billboard rank forecasts (1B, N = 215): .37 vs .21. Predicting a stranger's romantic attraction (1C, N = 286): .38 vs .26. Asked to predict 1C, 119 JDM researchers forecast aversion instead: they predicted people would favour human advice by .14, where participants actually favoured the algorithm by .11 (d = 1.25). Joint presentation did not reverse it: 75% chose the algorithm (Exp 2, N = 154). Using Dietvorst et al.'s materials without feedback (Exp 3, N = 403), 88% tied their bonus to the algorithm over another participant and 66% over themselves; the self is the moderator. National-security professionals (Exp 4, 70 experts vs 301 MTurk) discounted every advisor and were less accurate than the lay sample when the advisor was an algorithm. A benchmark study (N = 671) shows the appreciation is still under-weighting: advice averaged from 314 people deserved WOA 1.0 and got .66. The authors propose a "theory of machine" — lay beliefs about how algorithmic and human judgment differ in input, process and output — and close on a warning: "In an age when we routinely allow technological systems to direct our attention and our spending... we all ought to be concerned about our vulnerability to manipulation" (p. 100).

## Relation to the argument

RQ4, structural axis, and a small foothold on the objective axis. Structurally this is a Judge–Advisor System with one judge; the "other people" whose estimates are averaged into the advice are inputs to the algorithm, not parties it serves, and no one on the far side of the advice bears the judge's decision. Confirmed absence of a second human party. Two things are usable. First, the finding that appreciation survives a "black box" with no process description (§2.2.2) means people extend reliance to an intermediary whose objective they cannot inspect, which is the precondition the essay needs for cooptation to be a live risk rather than a hypothetical. Second, the closing paragraph on manipulation and unread terms and conditions is the paper's own gesture toward the objective axis — the intermediary may be pursuing something other than the judge's accuracy — but it is a gesture, not a design or a measurement. The theory-of-machine proposal is the nearest thing in this cluster to the "interpretive" limb of algorithmacy: it asks what people infer about an algorithm's inputs and process, though only for a dyad.

## Caution

The advice was an average of many past participants, truthfully labelled either way, so "algorithm" here means a trivial aggregator; nothing generalizes to models that optimize a hidden objective. No feedback in any experiment, by design, so the result is about first contact. Experts were 67 of 70 male government employees recruited through one contact and rewarded with a raffle; the lay–expert comparison is confounded by everything that differs between the samples, which the authors say. Effect sizes are small to medium. The manipulation remark is an aside in the conclusion, not a finding.

## S2 adversarial verification (2026-09-18)

**Verdict:** corrected

**What I checked:** The version-of-record PDF on the first author's site is no longer reachable (domain does not resolve; no Wayback capture). I checked every number against the HBS working-paper version (WP 17-086, 56 pp.), which is the same set of experiments.

**Findings:** All sample sizes, WOA means, effect sizes and percentages in the card match the working paper: 1A N=202, .45 vs .30, d=.42; 1B N=215, .37 vs .21; 1C N=286, .38 vs .26; 119 researchers, d=1.25; Exp 2 N=154, 75%; Exp 3 N=403, 88% and 66%; Exp 4 70 experts vs 301 MTurk; benchmark N=671, 314 people, WOA .66 against a normative 1.0; 1A not pre-registered. One clarity fix in the body: "mean predicted gap +.14, actual −.11" used an unexplained sign convention; it now reads that researchers predicted a .14 preference for human advice while participants favoured the algorithm by .11. Two things could not be verified against the version of record: the closing quotation (the working paper's wording is "In an age when we routinely allow our attention and our spending to be directed by technological systems ... we all ought to be concerned about our vulnerability to manipulation," which differs from the card's; the p. 100 wording should be re-checked) and the "67 of 70 male" expert-sample detail, which I could not find in the working paper.
