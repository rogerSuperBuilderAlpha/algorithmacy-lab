# Gran, A.-B., Booth, P., & Bucher, T. (2021). To be or not to be algorithm aware: A question of a new digital divide? *Information, Communication & Society*, 24(12), 1779–1796.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/1369118X.2020.1736124 (open access, CC BY-NC-ND; published online 9 March 2020) · **Read depth:** full_text (the Taylor & Francis version of record, obtained from the University of Copenhagen repository, curis.ku.dk; page numbers are the journal's) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed communication journal; a representative national web survey (N = 1,624, Norway, November 2018) with cluster analysis · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (Internet users and "algorithm-driven recommendations, advertisements and content") · **Remedy locus:** subject (awareness as a "meta-skill"), with the authors' own doubt about devolving responsibility to users · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18 (upgrades gran2021or.md in the coordinative_sovereignty library, which had not opened the article)

## What it argues

"We, therefore, ask whether having awareness of algorithms or not corresponds to a new reinforced digital divide" (p. 1779). Awareness is self-reported on one five-point item — "What kind of awareness do you have of algorithms being used to present recommendations, advertisements, and other content on the internet?" — chosen over platform-specific probes and limited to one question "due to the risk of a suggestibility effect" (p. 1783); the Norwegian term "'kjennskap'... occupies a space between 'knowledge' and 'awareness'" (p. 1783). Results: "41% of the Norwegian population perceive that they have no awareness of algorithms. Twenty one per cent report low awareness, 26% some awareness, 10% high awareness and only 3% indicate that they have very high awareness" (p. 1784); open comments validate the measure (χ² = 256.1, Cramer's V = 0.515, p. 1785). Age is a "generational divide" ("No awareness" 74% at 70+, 15% at 30–39; V = 0.212), education is "strongly associated" (V = 0.145), and men report more awareness than women (V = 0.181) (pp. 1785–1786). Awareness is associated with holding "more clearly positive or negative attitudes" (p. 1788). Six types: "the unaware (40.6%), the uncertain, the affirmative, the neutral, the sceptic and the critical," the last "characterized by a perception of high algorithm awareness" and "predominately very negative attitudes," younger, male, urban and highly educated (pp. 1789–1791). Discussion: "61% of the Norwegian population report having no or low awareness of algorithms" (p. 1791); awareness "is better understood as a meta-skill, a knowledge or understanding that may improve other digital skills" (p. 1791); "the higher the awareness of algorithms, which correlates with the higher levels of education, the more negative are the attitudes towards them" (p. 1792). The users are "'complicit' and actively involved in shaping the information environment," which raises the question "To what extent will the user also be held responsible for it – a 'you-get-the-infrastructure-you-deserve-logic'?" (p. 1792). New divides "are created based on the uneven distribution of data and knowledge, between those who have the means to question the processes of datafication and those who lack the necessary resources" (p. 1792).

## Relation to the argument

RQ11 and the rival-construct family. The paper's construct is *awareness*, the lowest tier of the algorithmic-literacy ladder, and its instrument is a single self-report item; it is useful to the paper as the population-scale evidence that the literacy paradigm's own precondition is absent for a majority (61% none or low) and unevenly distributed by age, education and gender. Two passages do more work than the sovereignty card noticed. First, the "you-get-the-infrastructure-you-deserve" question is the authors' own statement of the paper's objection to remedy-in-the-subject: if the infrastructure learns from users, then a literacy remedy makes users responsible for a structure they did not set — Livingstone's Foucauldian warning (livingstone2004.md) restated for algorithms. Second, the finding that awareness correlates with *negative* attitudes rather than with any capacity to act is evidence that knowing the intermediary is there is not the same as having any purchase on it, which is the gap Principle I addresses. CUTS AGAINST the paper's supersession claim only if "the means to question the processes of datafication" (p. 1792) is read as already naming contestation; the authors mean resources and knowledge, not an instrument, and their study measures neither inference about a determination nor any co-determined party — recommendations, ads and feeds are the objects throughout.

## Caution

One self-report item in one country in one wave; the authors list the limits themselves — self-confidence may inflate reports, and "one's perception of awareness need not rise with actual awareness" (p. 1784). The sample is a weighted web panel; the cluster solution has a silhouette of 0.2, which the authors accept as valid but which is weak. Attitude items were asked only of those reporting at least "low" awareness (N = 1,048). Nothing here concerns platforms that match or price, and nothing measures what users do.
