## What it argues

Sludge is Thaler's coinage, and Sunstein's article defines it as "a kind of friction, large or small, that people face when they want to go in one or another direction" (p. 4); the later *Sludge Audits* abstract narrows it to "excessive or unjustified frictions, such as paperwork burdens, that cost time or money." The anchor number is that in 2015 the US government imposed 9.78 billion hours of paperwork, worth $195.6 billion at $20 an hour, "more than double the budget of the Department of State" (p. 4); the human cost is access — FAFSA, free school meals, the Earned Income Tax Credit, voter registration — and the fix is "automatic enrollment, greatly simplified forms, and reminders" (abstract), illustrated by USDA direct certification reaching "more than 11 million children ... (about 91 percent of the eligible population)" in 2014–15 (p. 5). The definitional footnote matters for RQ3: "It should also be clear that sludge can be for good or for bad. It is reasonable to see sludge as a kind of nudge; friction can nudge people in a helpful or unhelpful way" (n. 23, pp. 4–5). Part III, "Justifying Sludge," lists four justifications: "(1) program integrity; (2) self-control problems; (3) privacy; and (4) targeting" (p. 10). Targeting is the ordeal mechanism: "willingness to pay in terms of time and an effort (WTPT, for short)" can screen for need where money cannot, but "much of the time, sludge is a singularly crude method of targeting ... In some cases, ordeals work in concert with the limitations faced by poor people, so as specifically to select out those with the highest need" (p. 14). The verdict on all four is that "these defenses of sludge turn out to be more attractive in principle than in practice" (abstract), the test is cost–benefit, and institutions should run routine "Sludge Audits." Howard's review confirms the book keeps this structure — a tour of sludge, "justifications for certain kinds of sludge," and the audit proposal — and criticises it for stopping at auditing.

## Relation to the argument

The RQ's framing — Sunstein says friction harms, the cognitive-forcing literature says friction helps — is too coarse, and the correction is useful. Sludge is by definition *unjustified* friction, and Sunstein's own second justification, "self-control problems" (p. 10), is the slot into which a cognitive forcing function fits. The disagreement between the two literatures is therefore not about whether friction can help but about the criterion of justification. Sunstein's is welfarist cost–benefit anchored in Thaler's "as judged by themselves," and that is exactly what Buçinca et al. (2021) show cannot settle the case: judged by themselves, users reject the friction that helps them. On the objective axis Sunstein is closer to the cooptation reading than the RQ suggests: the private-sector sludge he names — cancellation ordeals where "9 in 10 consumers were initially refused" (n. 24, p. 5) — is friction that serves the firm's objective, and his ordeal analysis is a theory of friction as a screening device serving the institution's allocation goal. What he lacks is any formation criterion: nobody in Sunstein's account becomes better or worse at anything by encountering friction; they only get or fail to get the benefit. That is the gap Vallor fills, and it is why the two accounts cannot be reconciled by adding a term to the cost–benefit ledger.

## Caution

This card rests on a preliminary draft the author labels "subject to significant revision and expansion," not on the 2021 book, and the book may have changed definitions and examples; any quotation attributed to *Sludge* itself must be checked against the book before it enters a draft. The setting is administrative burden — forms, waiting, reporting — imposed by governments and firms on citizens and consumers; nothing here concerns interfaces that interpret or bind two parties, and "friction" is used in the everyday sense, not the interaction-design sense of Cox et al. (2016). The numbers are US federal figures from the OMB Information Collection Budget, monetised at a stipulated wage.
