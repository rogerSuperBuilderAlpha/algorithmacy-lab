# Hoff, K. A., & Bashir, M. (2015). Trust in automation: Integrating empirical evidence on factors that influence trust. *Human Factors*, 57(3), 407–434.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/0018720814547570 · PMID 25875432 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (verbatim abstract from the University of Illinois Experts record; article paywalled at SAGE, no deposit found in IDEALS, CORE, OpenAlex or Wayback) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, flagship HF journal; systematic review · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (by the review's own inclusion rule) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

A systematic review of "empirical research on trust in automation from January 2002 to June 2013," which is to say of the decade after Lee and See. The eligibility rule is stated in the abstract: "Papers were deemed eligible only if they reported the results of a human-subjects experiment in which humans interacted with an automated system in order to achieve a goal. Additionally, a relationship between trust (or a trust-related behavior) and another variable had to be measured." That yielded "101 total papers, containing 127 eligible studies." The synthesis is a three-layer model — "dispositional trust, situational trust, and learned trust" — with design recommendations "for creating trustworthy automation" and "environmental conditions that can affect the strength of the relationship between trust and reliance." The stated aim is a "new lens for conceptualizing the variability of trust in automation" to guide "training interventions and design procedures that encourage appropriate trust."

## Relation to the argument

The cite for the shape of the field, and the inclusion criterion is itself the finding this pass wants: the 127 studies are, by definition, ones in which "humans interacted with an automated system in order to achieve a goal" — the human's goal. A study in which the automation served a second party's goal, or its own, would not have been excluded by that wording, but nothing in the abstract suggests any such study was found, and the three layers (disposition, situation, learning) are all properties of the one trustor. Whether the body of the review contains any triadic study cannot be determined from the abstract; the honest statement is that the field's own 2015 map is drawn with a dyadic pen and reports no exception in its summary. The "appropriate trust" it seeks to encourage inherits Lee and See's goal-indexed definition without, at abstract level, restating it.

## Caution

Abstract-only. The 101/127 counts, the date window and the three-layer names are verbatim; everything else about the body — which factors sit in which layer, what the design recommendations are, whether any included study involved a second human principal — is unread and must not be asserted. The review stops in mid-2013, before large-model assistants and most platform-intermediary work.
