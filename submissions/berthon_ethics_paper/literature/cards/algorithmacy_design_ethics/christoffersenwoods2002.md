## What it argues

Thirty years of "natural experiments" in flight-deck and control-room automation show that "many accidents represent a breakdown in coordination between people and technology," so "automation and people have to coordinate as a joint system, a single team" (p. 2, citing Hutchins 1995 and Billings 1996). The obstacle is the Substitution Myth: designers assume "automation activities simply can be substituted for human activities without otherwise affecting the operation of the system," when the work is really "a network of interdependent and mutually adapted activities and artifacts" (p. 3). Adding a partially autonomous agent "is, in many respects, like adding a new team member," with new coordination demands that "receive relatively little attention in development projects," leaving operators with Wiener's questions — "what is it doing? why is it doing that? what is it going to do next?" (p. 3). "The issue is not the level of autonomy or authority, but rather the degree of coordination," and "cooperating automation is both observable and directable" (p. 4). Observability needs a shared representation of the problem state and of other agents' activities — how hard the agent is working, which tactics it has tried, "how close is the automation to the limits of its competence?" (pp. 4–6) — and "data availability does not equal informativeness" (p. 5); the new representations must be event-based, future-oriented and pattern-based (p. 6). Directability opens with "Who owns the problem?": following Billings, "as long as some humans remain responsible for the outcomes, they must also be granted effective authority and therefore ultimate control," which means designers must "view the automation as a resource which exists to assist human agents" (p. 7). All-or-nothing takeover forces people "to buy control of the problem at the price of the considerable computational power" of the automation; what is needed are "intermediate, cooperative modes" in which "automated agents need to be flexible and they need to be good at taking direction" (p. 7). Pilots who cannot reconcile the automation's activity with their own picture "simply switch off some automated systems in critical situations" (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

Structural axis, and the source that names the two affordances the construct's competence would need from the other side: observability (for "infer what the intermediary is doing") and directability (for "contest what it commits" and "adapt as it changes"). Both are argued from open human work settings — controllers who "can often infer what other controllers are working on just by observing which displays or control panels they are attending to," surgical teams with "direct, common access to information about the problem (patient) state" (p. 5) — so multiple humans are in the model, but as co-workers with a shared problem. On this pass's question: no counterpart. The automation is "a resource which exists to assist human agents," ownership of the problem is settled by responsibility for the outcome, and the design question is how to keep the humans in charge of a machine that shares their goal. The one line that reaches toward the objective axis is the observation that technology-driven designs "give de facto control over how problems are solved to the machine agent" (p. 8); the chapter treats that as a design error to reverse, not as a structure a second party might be living inside.

## Caution

A synthesis chapter in an edited volume; no new data, and the evidence is the authors' prior field studies of cockpit automation plus a handful of expert-system evaluations. Preprint pagination differs from the published chapter (pp. 1–12); quotations should be re-located before citation. The prescriptions (observability, directability) are design principles asserted from cases, not validated interventions. Written for supervisory control of process and vehicle automation, where the human is legally responsible for the outcome; that premise does not carry to a platform user.
