## What it argues

An explanation of an automated decision "does not necessarily hinge on the general public understanding of how algorithmic systems function ... explanations can, in principle, be offered without opening the 'black box'" (p. 4). Read "as a means to help a data subject act rather than merely understand," an explanation has three aims: "(1) to inform and help the subject understand why a particular decision was reached, (2) to provide grounds to contest adverse decisions, and (3) to understand what could be changed to receive a desired result in the future, based on the current decision-making model" — and "none hinge on explaining the internal logic of automated decision-making systems" (p. 4). The instrument is the counterfactual: "You were denied a loan because your annual income was £30,000. If your income had been £45,000, you would have been offered a loan" (p. 5), a "statement of how the world would have to be different for a desirable outcome to occur," computed as the "closest possible world" — though "providing several explanations covering a range of diverse counterfactuals ... may be more helpful" (p. 6). The distinction from the XAI literature is stated flatly: existing explanation "refers to an attempt to convey the internal state or logic of an algorithm," whereas "counterfactuals describe a dependency on the external facts that led to that decision" (p. 6). Part III shows they are cheap to compute: hold the weights fixed, minimise distance to the original point subject to hitting the target output (Eq. 2), using an L1 norm weighted by inverse median absolute deviation to force sparse, few-variable changes. On a stripped LSAT dataset a 941-weight, 40-neuron network yields "Person 3: If your LSAT was 33.5, and you were 'white', you would have an average predicted score (0)" — the counterfactual surfaces a race dependence without any model internals (pp. 18–20); the Pima diabetes example gives "If your 2-Hour serum insulin level was 154.3, you would have a score of 0.51" (p. 21). Part IV argues the internal-logic route is hopeless in principle: state-of-the-art models compose "more than a thousand" functions with "more than ten million parameters," while "the working memory of humans can contain around seven distinct items," so "it remains unclear whether 'human-comprehensible meaningful information' about the logic involved in a particular decision can ever exist" (p. 22). The conclusion concedes limits: counterfactuals are "a minimal form of explanation," insufficient "where it is important to understand system functionality," and they "do not provide the statistical evidence needed to assess algorithms for fairness or racial bias" (pp. 46–47); models change, so controllers must keep "audit logs" of the model state at decision time (n. 213); and the authors recommend "auditing APIs" through which individuals or third parties request counterfactuals (p. 45).

## Relation to the argument

RQ7 and the counterfactual-exploration affordance. This is the legal-ethics ground the brief asked for, and it is stronger than a ground: the three aims are the three components of algorithmacy in a different order — understand what the intermediary did (interpretive), contest it (tactical), learn what to change for next time (anticipatory) — and the paper's central claim, that all three can be served "without conveying the internal logic of the algorithmic black box (beyond a limited set of dependencies)" (p. 46), is the same claim the Seamful Boundary Disclosure affordance makes against raw exposure. Together with Burrell's opacity argument (which the paper cites at nn. 5, 10, 12, 70, 73), it gives the design principle a theoretical reason: the alternative to raw exposure is not less information but information of a different kind, indexed to what the subject can do. The paper also names the tension the construct has to face. Counterfactuals are recommended partly because, with "reasonable limitations ... on the number of counterfactuals," they are "less likely to provide information that ... allows gaming of decision-making systems" (p. 46) — so the same authors who ground the affordance would cap it to prevent exactly the tactical adaptation algorithmacy names as a competence.

The paper is dyadic throughout — data subject and data controller — and this CUTS AGAINST a naïve transfer. In the triad the counterfactual for party A ("had your rating been X, you would have been matched") discloses a dependency of the determination on B's data or on the intermediary's objective; the "closest possible world" for A is computed over facts about B. Wachter et al. never consider a counterfactual whose recipient is one of two parties bound by the same decision, and their "rights and freedoms of others" caveat (p. 46) is precisely where the second human enters. The paper's "audit logs" and "auditing APIs" are nevertheless the concrete mechanisms a triadic counterfactual affordance would need, since the intermediary "changes as it changes."

## Caution

A legal-doctrinal paper with two small demonstrations on toy datasets (LSAT, Pima) and no user study; nothing here shows that people understand, trust, or act better on counterfactuals — that evidence, where it exists, is later and mixed. The counterfactuals assume feature independence (III.C), which the authors defend as adequate for data audits but which can produce unactionable or misleading advice. The GDPR analysis is as of 2017–18 and I did not read it closely. The arXiv copy's pagination differs from the journal's; every page number above needs converting before citation.
