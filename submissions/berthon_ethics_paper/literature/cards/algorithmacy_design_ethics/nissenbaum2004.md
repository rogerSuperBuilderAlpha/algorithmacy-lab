## What it argues

The article "posits a new construct, 'contextual integrity,' as an alternative benchmark for privacy" (p. 119), built to handle public surveillance — shopper cards, CCTV, RFID, public records online — which the three-principle framework of American privacy policy (limit government, protect sensitive information, protect the private sphere) cannot reach because it treats information in public as "up for grabs" (pp. 136, 143). Its "central tenet" is that "there are no arenas of life not governed by norms of information flow" (p. 137). Contexts are social spheres in the sense of Walzer, Bourdieu and Friedland & Alford — "as sweepingly defined as, say, spheres of life such as education, politics, and the marketplace or as finely drawn as the conventional routines of visiting the dentist, attending a family wedding, or interviewing for a job" (p. 137; the sources at n. 67) — and Nissenbaum declines to theorise them further: "There is no need here to construct a theory of these contexts" (p. 137). The definition: "I posit two types of informational norms: norms of appropriateness, and norms of flow or distribution. Contextual integrity is maintained when both types of norms are upheld, and it is violated when either of the norms is violated. The central thesis of this Article is that the benchmark of privacy is contextual integrity" (p. 138). Appropriateness governs what information fits a context (pp. 138–140); norms of flow govern its movement between parties and are "profoundly influenced by Michael Walzer's pluralist theory of justice" (p. 140), whose spheres each distribute their own good by their own criterion — "in the sphere of employment, jobs (goods) are allocated to those with appropriate talents and qualifications" (p. 141). Flow principles include free choice, discretion, confidentiality, need, entitlement and obligation (p. 142).

Part III.C raises two objections against itself. First, "contextual integrity is conservative in possibly detrimental ways," since entrenched flows may be "deleterious even in the face of technological means to make things better" (p. 143). Second, tied to convention it "loses prescriptive value or moral authority," in an era when "changes are thrust upon people and societies frequently without the possibility of careful deliberation" (p. 144). Her answer: "I propose that the requirement of contextual integrity sets up a presumption in favor of the status quo; common practices are understood to reflect norms of appropriateness and flow, and breaches of these norms are held to be violations of privacy" (p. 145). Then the Walzer passage, verbatim: "Further, Walzer considers it a form of tyranny when goods of one sphere intrude into, or become dominant in, not only one sphere but many; local norms that embody the settled rationale of the tyrannized sphere are overturned as those who possess vast amounts of dominant goods are able to exert tyrannical power over those who do not" (p. 145, citing Walzer at 17–20). And the stance: "A presumption in favor of the status quo for informational norms means we initially resist breaches, suspicious that they occasion injustice or even tyranny. We take the stance that the entrenched normative framework represents a settled rationale for a certain context that we ought to protect unless powerful reasons support change" (p. 145). The presumption "does not, however, rule out the possibility of a successful challenge where adequate reasons exist"; entrenched norms are "compared with novel practices that breach or threaten them, and judged worth preserving, or not, in terms of how well they promote not only values and goods internal to a given context, but also fundamental social, political, and moral values" (p. 146). The values list — informational harm, informational inequality, autonomy and freedom, preservation of relationships, democracy — follows (pp. 146–151); under inequality she writes that institutions which "wield significant power over the fates of individual citizens and clients," given "free reign in collecting and using information," further tip "the balance of power in their favor" (p. 147), and, via van den Hoven, that in mediated commercial transactions "the parties in the transaction are far from equal" and the individual has "no power to retract or redraw the arrangement" (p. 148). The evaluative questions are protective throughout: "whether the information practice under consideration harms subjects; interferes with their self-determination; or amplifies undesirable inequalities in status, power, and wealth" (p. 155). The model "is prescriptive in that it is intended to serve as a justificatory framework for prescribing specific restrictions on collection, use, and dissemination of information about people" (p. 155). The conclusion returns to Walzer: "certain types of exchanges be blocked in order to preserve complex equality. Distribution principles of one sphere should not be permitted to intrude into others, so that those who are wealthy in one sphere are not allowed to spread tyranny to others" (p. 156). Law is one remedy among several, and the political one is triggered "when violations of norms are widespread and systematic as in public surveillance, when strong incentives of self-interest are behind these violations, when the parties involved are of radically unequal power and wealth" (p. 157).

## Relation to the argument

The plan's three-part critique holds against this text, with one correction and one refinement.

*First, unit of analysis — confirmed.* Every parameter of the benchmark is a property of information about a person: what type, who reveals it, to whom, under what principle. The one place a binding determination appears is the school example on p. 155, where teachers "collect and aggregate information about students in order to assign grades," and the article judges the practice acceptable as an information practice; the grade itself, the thing that decides the student's fate, is never the object of evaluation. The NRMP case in `friedman1996.md` — a student's rank list flowing to the program, a hospital's list flowing the other way, an algorithm committing a match that binds both — decomposes under this benchmark into two flows, each appropriate to the employment context (p. 141 puts jobs in Walzer's employment sphere), and the tie-break that favours hospitals is invisible to norms of appropriateness and flow. The nearest the account comes is layer (a) of the later three-layer test, "interests of affected parties," which could register the student's loss as a harm — but as a consequence of a flow to the subject, never as a relation between the two people the same determination binds. That is the census finding: **Remedy locus: norms/institution.** The remedy is a norm restored or revised by a court, a legislature or a professional association (p. 157); the person inside the flow appears only as the subject whose expectations were breached.

*Second, the heuristic has no agent — confirmed, and the text supplies the reason.* The evaluation on p. 146 is a comparison between "entrenched norms" and "novel practices," conducted by whoever is in a position to weigh "fundamental social, political, and moral values." The article never says who. The examples of adjudication are courts (Florida v. Riley, Kyllo, pp. 144), legislatures (HIPAA, the DPPA, pp. 142, 147) and the "painstaking analysis of details" a scholar performs (p. 154). And the objection she herself raises on p. 144 — that "changes are thrust upon people and societies frequently without the possibility of careful deliberation" — is the adaptive-mediator problem stated in 2004: the method assumes a novel practice that holds still long enough to be compared with an entrenched one. `nissenbaum2019.md` records her own later concession on exactly this point.

*Third, the Walzer passage is an ally — confirmed, with a precision the draft should keep.* The passage on p. 145 is the paper's cross-sphere mechanism in Nissenbaum's words, and pp. 156–157 make it a remedy: blocked exchanges, and a political response when the parties are "of radically unequal power and wealth." What the draft must not say is that the platform is Walzer's "dominant good." The dominant good in her account is the thing convertible across spheres — for a platform, the information it holds, which `nissenbaum2011.md` (p. 44) names as "the raw materials of their industry" — and the platform is its possessor. A platform that mediates employment, credit, housing and speech at once is the possessor of vast amounts of a dominant good, exerting the "tyrannical power" the passage describes. Read that way the passage is an exact ally; read as "the platform is a dominant good" it is a category slip a Walzer-literate reviewer will catch. The remedy that follows — "we initially resist breaches" — is protective by construction, which is the protect/promote finding arriving from inside the account.

*The promotive term.* The draft should not say Nissenbaum has no promotive term. She has one, and it sits at the level of the context: norms are judged by "how well they promote not only values and goods internal to a given context, but also fundamental social, political, and moral values" (p. 146), and the "settled rationale of any given context" serves "important cultural, social, and personal ends" (p. 145). That is a teleology of institutions — the ends of medicine, of elections — not of a person's flourishing; the person's side of the ledger is the six protective values on pp. 146–151.

*The correction to the naive collapse argument* is already in the plan and this text confirms it from the other end: contexts are spheres of social life with roots in Walzer and Bourdieu (p. 137, n. 67), not venues or platforms, so "the mediator defines the context" is a move the theory does not permit; the dispatch algorithm operates inside employment and answers to employment's norms. The paper's critique must therefore be that the benchmark, correctly applied, has nothing to say about the determination — not that the benchmark collapses.

*The two Nissenbaums.* The library also holds `bruntonnissenbaum2015.md` (Obfuscation, *Remedy locus: relation*), cited for Principle I. These are two answers by one author to the same asymmetry — the "far from equal" parties of p. 148, with "no power to retract or redraw the arrangement." Contextual integrity answers it with a norm someone else must enforce; obfuscation answers it with an instrument the weak party operates alone. The second exists because the first has no user-side instrument, and the draft should say so in one sentence.

*Principle III bridge (plan §4).* Partly holds. A transmission principle is a constraint the flow is committed to, and Nissenbaum wants commitments from operators (see `nissenbaum2011.md`, pp. 43–44). What binds the operator to the commitment in her account is law or "reasonable expectations," never the relation — so contextual integrity supplies the concept of a declared envelope and lacks the instrument that holds the mediator to it. The bridge can be stated in that form and no stronger.

## Caution

A 2004 law-review article on public surveillance — RFID, CCTV, public records online, consumer profiling — written before social platforms, before machine-learning inference, and before Nissenbaum's own 2010 book replaced the two-norm formulation with five parameters (see `nissenbaum2019.md`, p. 228). It contains no empirical study and, by its own account (p. 137), no theory of contexts. The NRMP reading in the Relation section is this card's, not the article's; the article never mentions matching, ranking or any determination binding two parties. The "no agent" finding is an argument from silence about who runs the evaluation, and the 2010 book's Part III, which was not reached (see `nissenbaum2010.md`), may name one. The end page 157 is the last text page of the deposit; a p. 158 leaf carries only a running head.
