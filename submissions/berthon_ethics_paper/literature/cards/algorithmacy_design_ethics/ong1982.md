## What it argues

Ong's thesis is stated in the cognitive register and he does not soften it: "In an oral culture, restriction of words to sound determines not only modes of expression but also thought processes" (p. 33); "More than any other single invention, writing has transformed human consciousness" (p. 77); "Without writing, the literate mind would not and could not think as it does, not only when engaged in writing but normally even when it is composing its thoughts in oral form" (p. 77). Chapter 3 lists nine "characteristics of orally based thought and expression": additive rather than subordinative, aggregative rather than analytic, redundant or "copious," conservative or traditionalist, close to the human lifeworld, agonistically toned, empathetic and participatory, homeostatic, situational rather than abstract (pp. 36–57). He flags the list's status himself: "This inventory of characteristics is not presented as exclusive or conclusive but as suggestive, for much more work and reflection are needed" (p. 36). The cognitive evidence he leans on is Luria's 1931–32 Uzbek fieldwork on syllogisms, categorization and self-description (pp. 49–56), and he already concedes the objection later critics would press: "One could argue that responses were not optimal because the respondents were not used to being asked these kinds of questions ... But lack of familiarity is precisely the point: an oral culture simply does not deal in such items as geometrical figures, abstract categorization, formally logical reasoning processes, definitions" (p. 54). He also says, against the crude reading, "Nor must we imagine that orally based thought is 'prelogical' or 'illogical' in any simplistic sense" (p. 56) and that "oral cultures can produce amazingly complex and intelligent and beautiful organizations of thought and experience" (p. 56).

The institutional material that this paper needs sits mostly in chapter 4. On law: "The law itself in oral cultures is enshrined in formulaic sayings, proverbs, which are not mere juris-prudential decorations, but themselves constitute the law. A judge in an oral culture is often called on to articulate sets of relevant proverbs out of which he can produce equitable decisions" (p. 35). On the homeostasis of oral record, following Goody and Watt: the Tiv genealogies "actually used orally in settling court disputes have been found to diverge considerably from the genealogies carefully recorded in writing by the British forty years earlier," and the Gonja state myth lost two of Ndewura Jakpa's seven sons when two territorial divisions disappeared — "The integrity of the past was subordinate to the integrity of the present" (pp. 47–48). On the onset of literacy: writing arrives "necessarily at first in restricted sectors"; "Literacy can be restricted to special groups such as the clergy (Tambiah 1968, pp. 113–14)"; Havelock's "craft literacy" stage is one in which "writing is a trade practiced by craftsmen, whom others hire to write a letter or document as they might hire a stone-mason to build a house ... At such a craft-literacy stage, there is no need for an individual to know reading and writing any more than any other trade" (pp. 91–92). The section "From memory to written records" (pp. 94–96) is a précis of Clanchy's first edition: "'documents did not immediately inspire trust' (Clanchy 1979, p. 230). People had to be persuaded that writing improved the old oral methods sufficiently to warrant all the expense and troublesome techniques it involved"; the 1127 Sandwich tolls dispute settled by twenty-four jurors swearing to what "I have received from my ancestors, and I have seen and heard from my youth"; "Witnesses were prima facie more credible than texts because they could be challenged and made to defend their statements, whereas texts could not"; charters authenticated by knives and swords rather than by writing; forgers "'experts entrenched at the centre of literary and intellectual culture in the twelfth century'"; and Clanchy's line that "'Remembered truth was ... flexible and up to date'" (pp. 94–96). On texts and authority: "There is no way directly to refute a text. After absolutely total and devastating refutation, it says exactly the same thing as before. This is one reason why 'the book says' is popularly tantamount to 'it is true'" (p. 78). On the "grapholect": the written national language "has resources of a totally different order of magnitude," and "It is bad pedagogy to insist that because there is nothing 'wrong' with other dialects, it makes no difference whether or not speakers of another dialect learn the grapholect" (pp. 105–106).

Chapter 5 is Ong's short treatment of print. He defers the social effects to Eisenstein ("Even a cursory glance at Elizabeth Eisenstein's two volumes ... makes abundantly evident how diversified and vast the particular effects of print have been," p. 115) and pursues "subtler effects of print on consciousness" (p. 116). His two institutional observations are (i) the commodification of the word: "In the very early days of print ... a royal decree or privilegium was often secured forbidding the reprinting of a printed book by others than the original publisher. Richard Pynson secured such a privilegium in 1518 from Henry VIII. In 1557 the Stationers' Company was incorporated in London to oversee authors' and printers' or printer-publishers' rights ... Typography had made the word into a commodity. The old communal oral world had split up into privately claimed freeholdings" (pp. 128–129); and (ii) closure: "print is comfortable only with finality" (p. 129), and with the fixed point of view "the 'reading public' came into existence — a sizable clientele of readers unknown personally to the author" (p. 132). He notes the early reception was mixed: Squarciafico in 1477 argued that "'abundance of books makes men less studious,'" while "others saw print as a welcome leveler: everyone becomes a wise man or woman" (pp. 78–79; the Squarciafico sentence begins on p. 78). He also records that "the transition from orality to literacy was slow" (p. 112): medieval universities "never tested knowledge or intellectual prowess by writing, but always by oral dispute" (p. 112), "At least as late as the twelfth century in England, checking even written financial accounts was still done aurally, by having them read aloud" — whence "'auditing', that is, 'hearing' account books" (p. 117), and "Manuscript culture in the West remained always marginally oral" (p. 117).

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Ong supports the cognitive register far more strongly than the competence/institution register, and he supports it in exactly the form the critics attack ("restructures consciousness," Luria's syllogisms). Citing him for the paper's actual claim — that a literate order required competences of its members that an oral order did not, and that documentary record replaced memory as the ground of trust — means citing his chapter 4 institutional passages (pp. 91–96, 105–106, 128–129), where he is in fact reporting Havelock, Tambiah, Goody and Clanchy, not his own psychodynamics. The analogy is strengthened in one specific way: Ong's own account of writing's "vatic" authority ("there is no way directly to refute a text," p. 78) and of the grapholect as an unavoidable requirement (p. 106) is a description of an institutional demand placed on a member, not of a rewired mind. It is complicated in another: the restricted-literacy and craft-literacy material (pp. 91–92) shows that for most of the history of writing the illiterate were not disenfranchised by illiteracy, because reading was a hired trade like masonry. The category-error analogy therefore holds for the moment when literacy became a general requirement, not for writing as such, and Ong dates that moment in Greece to "around Plato's time ... more than three centuries after the introduction of the Greek alphabet" (p. 92). Any use of Ong must carry the concession that his cognitive thesis is contested (see street1984, halverson1992, finnegan1988, scribner1981, armer1992).

The scaffolding essay's use. The essay's conclusion says that before Gutenberg literacy was "the jealously guarded possession of an elite clerical and scribal guild" and that print "converted literacy from a specialized guild privilege into an existential requirement," leaving the illiterate "stripped of agency." Ong supports the first half only in the weak form: literacy "can be restricted to special groups such as the clergy" (p. 91) and a craft-literacy stage exists in many cultures (p. 92); he does not describe medieval Europe as a clerical monopoly, and the Clanchy material he relays (pp. 94–96) is precisely about lay use of documents in twelfth- and thirteenth-century administration, before print. He does not support the second half at all: his print chapter says nothing about print making literacy an "existential requirement" or stripping anyone of agency; it says print commodified the word (pp. 128–129), fixed texts (p. 129), created a reading public (p. 132), and that its reception ran from "less studious" to "welcome leveler" (p. 79). The essay's sentence should not be attributed to Ong.

## Caution

Ong's primary evidence for the cognitive claims is secondary (Parry, Lord, Havelock, Goody, Luria, Clanchy); the book is a synthesis, not a field study. Its "great divide" between oral and literate consciousness is the target of Street (1984), Finnegan (1988), Halverson (1992) and of Scribner and Cole's Vai findings, which Ong cites only for the syllabary (pp. 84, 87), not for their negative result on general cognitive effects; his one other mention of Cole and Scribner is second-hand, via Fernandez (1980) on syllogisms in the Luria section (p. 52), and again not of the Vai result. Ong never uses the word "oracy" (it is Wilkinson's, 1965; see wilkinson1965); his terms are "primary orality," "oral culture," and "verbomotor." The print chapter is explicitly brief and defers to Eisenstein for social effects; it cannot carry a claim about print's institutional consequences on its own. The copy read is a later printing with light editorial updates; a quotation that turns on wording about the Soviet Union or Yugoslavia should be checked against a 1982 Methuen copy.
