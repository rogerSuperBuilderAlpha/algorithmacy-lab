## What it argues

Hirschman studies decline rather than growth: an organization's performance "is assumed to be subject to deterioration for unspecified, random causes which are neither so compelling nor so durable as to prevent a return to previous performance levels, provided managers direct their attention and energy to that task" (pp. 3–4, as quoted by Levy & Young 2020). Members have two responses. *Exit* is when "customers stop buying the firm's products or some members leave the organization"; *voice* is when they "express their dissatisfaction directly to management or to some other authority to which management is subordinate or through general protest addressed to anyone who cares to listen" (p. 4, as quoted by Larratt-Smith & Leon 2022). Exit is the economist's mechanism, impersonal and binary; voice is political and graded, running from "faint grumbling to violent protest" (p. 16, per Levy & Young). The book's question is "how a typical market mechanism and a typical nonmarket, political mechanism work side by side, possibly in harmony and mutual support, possibly also in such a fashion that one gets into the other's way and undercuts its effectiveness" (p. 18, per Wilson 2009). The central finding is that exit tends to undercut voice: the members who care most about quality, who would be the most effective voices, are the first to leave, so the organization loses its critics and can go on declining (chs. 4–5, per Dowding & John 2007 and the HUP description: "exit often undercuts voice while being unable to counteract decline"). Loyalty is the factor that "can neutralize within certain limits the tendency of the most quality-conscious customers or members to be the first to exit" (p. 79, reported identically by Wilson 2009 and Larratt-Smith & Leon 2022); it "holds exit at bay and activates voice" (p. 78, per Levy & Young). The chapter the sovereignty arm's audit points to for the comforted-monopolist argument is ch. 5, "How monopoly can be comforted by competition": an available alternative siphons off the critics and leaves the monopolist undisturbed. The sovereignty arm's card confirms the chapter titles from its audit, not from a table of contents.

## Relation to the argument

RQ9. Principle II says refusal, not hermeneutics, and Hirschman is the source that says what refusal *is* when the relation cannot simply be left. His scheme gives the paper a three-way distinction the contestability literature collapses. Interface-level contest is *voice* addressed to the mediator — and Lyons et al.'s robo-debt case is Hirschman's prediction in miniature: voice that succeeds case by case while the decline continues. Leaving the platform is *exit*, and the comforted-monopolist argument explains why exit is not the remedy either: where a rival exists, the most quality-sensitive parties leave, the mediator loses precisely the users who would have pressed it, and the objective stays as it was. Principle II's refusal is therefore neither. It is what the sovereignty arm calls exit-within — severing the feedback loop (the profiling, the ranking, the allocation rule) while remaining a member — and Hirschman's own apparatus supplies its justification: the refusal has to be built so that it does not siphon off voice. That is the same requirement Zong and Matias (2024) state from the other side when they say an offer to refuse must change "the terms of one's engagement" rather than placate. The sovereignty arm uses the exit/voice pair as the spine of its necessary/contingent mediator diagnostic; for this paper the payoff is narrower and sharper. A mediator whose coordination the parties can factor (Φ = 0) leaves them exit and direct dealing; a mediator that is a party (Φ > 0) leaves voice as the only channel, and Hirschman shows voice alone is the weakest position. Principle II's refusal affordance is the attempt to give Φ > 0 parties something with the force of exit without its cost. CUTS AGAINST: Hirschman's decline is exogenous and "repairable"; management wants to recover, so voice can work. A mediator pursuing an objective neither party set is not declining from its own point of view; it is succeeding. Voice then has no receptive management to reach, which is stronger than anything in the book, and the paper should not cite Hirschman as if he had considered a firm for which the members' dissatisfaction is the product working as designed. His scheme is also dyadic: the two parties in the triad are each in an exit/voice relation with the mediator, and one party's exit is the other's loss of a counterpart, which the book never has to price.

The table of contents in that scan gives the chapters as 1 Introduction and doctrinal background (p. 1), 2 Exit (p. 21), 3 Voice (p. 30), 4 A special difficulty in combining exit and voice (p. 44), 5 How monopoly can be comforted by competition (p. 55), 6 On spatial duopoly and the dynamics of two-party systems (p. 62), 7 A theory of loyalty (p. 76), 8 Exit and voice in American ideology and practice (p. 106), 9 The elusive optimal mix of exit and voice; "exit undercutting voice" is therefore chapter 4's subject, with "Voice as a residual of exit" a section of chapter 3. The book's examples are Nigerian railways, schools, political parties and firms; nothing in it concerns an automated intermediary, and Hirschman's 1993 revision of the scheme for the East German case (exit and voice reinforcing each other) is not reflected here.
