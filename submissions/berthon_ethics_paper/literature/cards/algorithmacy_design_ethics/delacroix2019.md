# Delacroix, S., & Lawrence, N. D. (2019). Bottom-up data trusts: Disturbing the 'one size fits all' approach to data governance. *International Data Privacy Law*, 9(4), 236–252.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1093/idpl/ipz014 (open access, CC BY-NC-ND) · **Read depth:** full_text of the OUP advance-access PDF (17 pp., "2019, Vol. 0, No. 0," from the University of Cambridge repository, handle 1810/301307; this is the publisher's typeset OA version before issue pagination, so page numbers below are the advance-article pages 1–17, not 236–252) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed law journal; doctrinal-institutional proposal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world with a fourth actor: data subjects, data controllers, and an interposed trustee "bound by a fiduciary obligation of undivided loyalty" who "negotiate[s] data use" for the subjects (p. 1) · **Remedy locus:** relation (a new intermediary inserted between subject and collector, holding the subjects' rights collectively) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18 (upgrades delacroix2019bottom.md in the coordinative_sovereignty library from introduction-only to full text)

## What it argues

Key points: "The current lack of legal mechanisms that may plausibly empower us, data subjects to 'take the reins' of our personal data leaves us vulnerable. Recent regulatory endeavours to curb contractual freedom acknowledge this vulnerability but cannot, by themselves, remedy it—nor can data ownership"; "We argue that the power that stems from aggregated data should be returned to individuals through the legal mechanism of Trusts"; "Bound by a fiduciary obligation of undivided loyalty, the data trustees would exercise the data rights conferred by the GDPR (or other top-down regulation) on behalf of the Trust's beneficiaries. The data trustees would hence be placed in a position where they can negotiate data use in conformity with the Trust's terms, thus introducing an independent intermediary between data subjects and data collectors"; "Unlike the current 'one size fits all' approach to data governance, there should be a plurality of Trusts, allowing data subjects to choose a Trust that reflects their aspirations, and to switch Trusts when needed" (p. 1). The vulnerability is cumulative: "the data we leak daily has become something by reference to which we may be continuously judged"; "seemingly inconsequential decisions have downstream effects outside our control"; "How do we control for this death by a thousand cuts?" (pp. 2–3, 5). Ownership is "both unlikely and inadequate" (p. 1); the GDPR "divides us primarily into data subjects and data controllers," and "the subject... has neither the expertise nor the time to unpick each data controller's motivations and methods" (p. 4). The Trust: "data subjects choose to pool the rights they have over their personal data within the legal framework of the Trust... the data subjects tend to be both the settlors and the beneficiaries," and "It is important that there be a variety of data Trusts, arising out of a mix of publicly and privately funded initiatives. Each Trust will encapsulate a particular set of aspirations, reflected in the terms of the Trust" (p. 5); the phrase "ecosystem of Trusts" is theirs (pp. 2, 5, 8), and "constitutional terms" appears at pp. 8 and 17. Fiduciary duty is "considerably more onerous than a 'duty of care'"; "the burden of proof is reversed," trustees must show they promoted the beneficiaries' interest "with appropriate degrees of impartiality, prudence, transparency and undivided loyalty," and "cannot allow their own interests to conflict with those of the beneficiaries" (pp. 5–6). That condition sinks Balkin's information fiduciaries: "a fiduciary obligation towards data subjects is incompatible with the data controllers' responsibility towards shareholders" (p. 6). The Trust is a voice mechanism — "The collective setting of terms by the Trust is a way for data subjects to pool their rights to acquire a 'voice'" — with the 19th-century Land Societies as precedent (p. 7). Four conditions for an ecosystem: low barriers to entry, secure data, portability and erasure rights (p. 8); "The successful development of an ecosystem of Trusts is contingent on their ability to make use of the currently limited rights around data portability and data erasure" (p. 8).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing — an instrument, but not a client-side one**. This is the one card in the collective family that actually specifies an actor who *negotiates* on the coordinated party's behalf, and the specification is instructive by what it lacks. The trustee acts for a pool, at the level of terms ("negotiate data use in conformity with the Trust's terms"), before the fact, and its loyalty is enforced by equity, not by design. Nothing in the proposal puts anything inside the live coordination: the trustee sets the conditions under which a platform obtains its input and then "leaves the mediator in place" (the sovereignty chapter's gloss, faithful to the text). Three transfers. The "death by a thousand cuts" diagnosis is the construct's reason that per-determination contestation is not enough — each binding decision is trivial and the harm is cumulative — and it argues for standing over the *rule*, not the *instance*. The undivided-loyalty argument against information fiduciaries is the paper's cleanest way to say why the intermediary itself cannot be the user's agent: a mediator "pursues an objective neither set," and Delacroix and Lawrence show, in trust-law terms, that an actor with a business interest in the data cannot owe the subject undivided loyalty. That is the ground for Principle I's insistence on a *separate* agent. And the portability/erasure conditions are the same legal precondition the paper needs for any counter-agent to plug in (see eu2022dma.md, Art. 6(9)). CUTS AGAINST a purely client-side reading of Principle I: on their argument, one person's data rights, "leveraged individually," are "unlikely to be as impactful" (p. 2); the counter-agent needs a pool behind it.

## Caution

Doctrinal and prospective; no trust of this kind existed when the paper was written, and the "Case Studies" and "Implementation challenges" sections (pp. 9–17) were read only in outline. English trust law is the frame, and the authors themselves flag "cross-jurisdictional aspects" as unresolved. Page numbers are the advance-access pages; map them to 236–252 before citing. The data subject's counterpart is a "data collector" or "data user," not a second coordinating human; the model is subject–controller with a trustee interposed.
