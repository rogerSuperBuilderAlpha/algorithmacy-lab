## Summary

Read: pp. 23-34 and 127-131 of the printed book (publisher's version, text on disk from an earlier run); the drafter read pp. 24-25 and 127-130. Hoeksema's dissertation applies republican freedom to the digital sphere. On pp. 24-25 he takes uncontrolled interference as the mark of arbitrariness, notes that platforms can exclude users, change their algorithms and share data at will, and says that this discretion may itself be a loss of freedom whether or not users notice. He sets Pettit's agent-centric conception against structural accounts and adopts the term "social republicanism" for the structural strand. On whether isolated or minor power counts as domination he reports four positions: Gädeke's exclusion of episodic power, on which he stays agnostic (pp. 26, 28); Pettit's, that domination has no lower threshold (p. 29); McCammon's impositional-power test, under which a store clerk's power to refuse a sale is not domination (pp. 29-30); and micro-domination in aggregate (pp. 31-34), which he judges the better answer to the charge that digital domination is slight and easy to escape. In ch. 5 he treats users as customers and consumers of platforms in a market and records the republican split on markets: Pettit and Taylor hold that competition and exit can keep consumers from arbitrary power, while others hold that capitalist markets enable domination. Most relations discussed are standing ones with platforms; a one-off sale appears once, as the candy-store example (p. 30).

## Key facts it relies on

- "Pettit's reformulation of arbitrary interference as uncontrolled interference is helpful here". [quote] p. 24
- "A digital platform can, in many cases, arbitrarily exclude users, change their algorithms, and share their data with third parties." [quote] p. 24
- "where sufficient safeguards, democratic control, and opportunity for contestation exist, digital platforms can interfere with users in desirable ways". [quote] p. 24
- Gädeke "problematises episodic domination with the example of an armed mugger in a park"; on her account it is social structure that assigns agents their positions in a dominating relation. [quote] p. 26
- On whether only structures constitute domination: "In this work, however, I remain agnostic towards this question." and "I agree with Gädeke that excluding episodic power from the concept of domination would bring more conceptual clarity". [quote] p. 28
- Reporting Pettit: "there is no conceptual threshold that must be crossed before arbitrary power can be called domination." [quote] p. 29
- Illustrating McCammon's test with a purchase of candy: "The store clerk can also arbitrarily refuse service." Because alternatives exist and going without is no hardship, "There is no impositional power in play." [quote] p. 30
- "Conceptually, micro-domination supports the idea that, at least at first, any arbitrary power of any capacity must be taken into consideration—no matter how insignificant or isolated." [quote] p. 32
- Of X and its users: "Barring some more extreme cases of social media addiction, calling this impositional power pushes the example too far." [quote] p. 33
- "In all of these one is a customer to a digital platform and a consumer of their services, with all associated data practices." [quote] p. 128
- On the account he calls digital consumerism, and admits is "somewhat exaggerated", the power asymmetry between consumers and platforms "is to be resolved through consumer empowerment, for example through transparency and disclosure, improved conditions of consent, or individually enforceable rights." [quote] p. 130
- For Pettit and Taylor, markets "offer important means of organising society in a non-dominating way" and "If all goes well, they will never be subject to arbitrary power by virtue of competition and exit mechanisms." [quote] p. 130
- Others hold that capitalist markets act as regulators that enable domination (Vrousalis 2020). [paraphrase] p. 130

## Critical notes

- This is the closest source so far to applying republican domination to customers: it treats consumers as dominable by platforms where choice is thin (LinkedIn, Windows, X), and it sets out the exit objection that denies it. He answers the objection in two places: opting out is not open to everyone and domination is "never an all-or-nothing matter" (p. 131 n. 70); and small subjections add up to micro-domination (pp. 31-34). He does not claim more than that: of X's users he says they "are not left at the mercy of the whims of the platform or its owner" (p. 33).
- Tests he reports, none of them his own: Pettit's uncontrolled power, with no lower threshold but varying intensity and extent (pp. 24, 29); Gädeke's structural account, which excludes episodic power (pp. 26, 28); McCammon's impositional power, which he finds "mostly unsuccessful" at separating serious from unserious cases (p. 34); and micro-domination in aggregate (O'Shea, Lazar; pp. 31-34). For a single transaction the only passage is the candy-store example, where the answer on McCammon's test is no (p. 30).
- Chapter 1 is the dissertation's introduction; chapter 5 is a paper marked "Submitted to Techné in June 2025" (chapter title page), so pp. 127-131 had not passed journal review when the book was printed.
- Not read: the rest of the dissertation (pp. 1-22, 35-126, 132-205), including its articles on digital cooperatives and the commons. Gädeke, McCammon, O'Shea and Lazar are known here only through Hoeksema's report.
