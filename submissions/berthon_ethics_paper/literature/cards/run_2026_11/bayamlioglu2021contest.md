## Summary

Read: Abstract, section 1 and sections 2.1-2.4, with section 5.1 and sections 6-7 at the Fable read (a KU Leuven repository copy of the publisher's Early View PDF, paginated 1-21, not the issue's 1058-1078). Bayamlıoğlu reads GDPR Article 22 as a due-process provision whose core remedy is the right to contest solely automated decisions, and treats the "right to explanation" debate as secondary. He argues that Article 22(3) obliges the controller to make the decision contestable against a human arbiter, or to stop automating. He distinguishes first-layer transparency (information and access rights, Arts. 13-15) from a second layer of design, procedural and institutional measures. Part VI outlines four regulatory options, from a ban on solely automated decisions to permission subject only to first-layer access and notification (pp. 16-17), and the Conclusion says a practical way of exercising rights under Article 22 is still far off (p. 17). The duty falls on the data controller. He also records that Article 22 can be avoided by nominal human involvement.

## Key facts it relies on

- The right to contest automated decisions "as provided by Article 22" is described as "a due process provision with concrete transparency implications"; §1 adds that the paper's perspective "enables an interpretation of Article 22 as a due process provision". [quote] Abstract (p. 1); §1 (p. 3)
- "the right to contest is the backbone of the safeguards provided under the GDPR". [quote] §2.3.1 (p. 5)
- Art. 22(3) "obliges the data controller either to render automated decisions contestable or to cease ADM at all." [quote] §2.3.2 (p. 6)
- "What is required by Article 22(3) is not about informing or disclosing but rendering the decision contestable at least against a human arbiter." [quote] §2.3.2 (p. 6)
- The right to contest is likened to an "obligation of result", against information duties as "obligations of conduct". [paraphrase] §2.3.2 (p. 6)
- Significant data-driven practices "may be kept out of the reach of EU data protection regime simply by the nominal involvement of a human in the decision-making process." [quote] §2.1 (p. 4)
- "contesting ML-based decisions is not about reading off the computer code"; contestation needs four things: what the system learns about persons, the decision rules that turn findings into results, the decision's impact in its context, and the accountable actors behind it. [quote] §2.4 (p. 7; the four requirements run pp. 7-8)
- The Art. 22(2)(b) derogation can leave ADM under public law with weaker contestability standards than ADM based on consent or contract. [paraphrase] §2.2 (p. 5)
- Because the GDPR makes data protection by design a legal obligation, liability for the design choices and operation of the system "lies with the data controller as the addressee of the norm and thus, may not be shifted to the contractors or third-parties." [quote] §5.1 (p. 13)

## Critical notes

- Contests Wachter et al. (2017) on two points: that the right to contest may be set aside where the likelihood of success is low (§2.3.1, p. 5), and that the GDPR holds no ex-post right to explanation, a debate he calls "mostly irrelevant" on his reading (§2.3.2, p. 6). He reports their conclusion that each Article 22 safeguard is "individually enforceable" without saying whether he shares it; what he calls right is their point that the reading chosen determines how a decision can be contested (§2.3.1, p. 5).
- Limits: applies to "solely" automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; says data protection law is not where standards of lawfulness of outcomes are found (§2.3.1). Pre-dates the AI Act; the parts read have no provider/deployer discussion beyond the §5.1 sentence on contractors and third parties.
- Bearing for Q3: the controller owes contestability as something like an obligation of result, at least against a human arbiter (§2.3.2); "Contestability by Design" is named once, in §5.1 (p. 13), as a possible extension of Article 25 credited to Almada 2019. Person acted on gets: record partial (information and access rights); brake partial (human intervention); envelope no; standing yes, as a right to contest (§2.3.2); loss cover no. Not read: §§3-4 and §§5.2-5.4 (pdf pp. 8-12, 13-16).
