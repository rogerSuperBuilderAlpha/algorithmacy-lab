## Summary

Read: abstract, contents and the opening of the introduction (pp. 83-86), and pp. 120-121, 124-125, 133-141 and 146 (about eighteen of 64 pages); Parts I and II and the rest of Part III are not read.
Scout B logged this lead as MISMATCH (SSRN record 2020); the published version is George Washington Law Review 90(1),
2022, so the lead's year and venue were right and the card follows the journal. Arbel and Becher argue that language
models can act as "smart readers" of contracts, disclosures and privacy policies, with four capabilities: simplifying,
personalising, construing and benchmarking terms. Smart readers could relieve the no-reading problem and give
consumers on-demand know-your-rights help that subsidised lawyering cannot scale to. They also carry risks: errors
correlated across users, adversarial attacks by "interested firms" that tune contract text to mislead the model, bias,
and the temptation for courts to expand the duty to read before the technology and its distribution justify it. They
suggest that if smart readers succeed, regulation might shift from mandatory rules toward raising adoption, and add that
smart readers "do not necessarily portend the end of pro-consumer legal measures" (pp. 135-136). On reader error they
find that existing doctrine leaves the whole risk with the consumer (pp. 138-139).

## Key facts it relies on

- p. 83 (abstract): smart readers "can: simplify complex legal language; personalize the contractual presentation to the user’s specific sociocultural identity; interpret the meaning of contractual terms; and benchmark and rank contracts based on their quality." [quote]
- p. 83 (abstract): "Although smart readers can overcome traditional information barriers and empower parties, they rely on black-box models that sophisticated parties can exploit." [quote]
- p. 121: "Even more pernicious than correlated errors are adversarial attacks by interested firms." [quote] By slightly modifying the contract "one can mislead smart readers into parsing the agreement in ways that are desirable to the attacker." [quote]
- p. 121: it will take time before consumers use smart readers in place of lawyers, "but a much shorter time before they start using them as a substitute for not reading at all—especially if platforms like Amazon and Yelp start integrating a contract score into their listings." [quote]
- p. 125: subsidised lawyering "fails to scale"; "Smart readers can relieve some of this pressure by providing on-demand know-your-rights services." [quote]
- p. 134: "Rather than an ethical issue that justifies legal intervention, lack of reading may be a technological challenge that is increasingly solved." [quote]
- p. 135: "if smart readers reach this stage, it may be more effective to focus regulatory efforts on increasing adoption rates than to set mandatory rules and enforcement mechanisms." [quote]
- pp. 135-136: "even if smart readers can solve the reading problem, they will not necessarily address other market and reputational failures, so they do not necessarily portend the end of pro-consumer legal measures." [quote]
- p. 137: against the maker of the reader, "recourse against the producer is likely to be very limited" [quote]; pp. 138-139: "standard doctrines would allocate the entirety of the risk of smart reader error to the consumer." [quote]
- p. 140: proposes requiring sellers "to disclose key terms in a “smart reader-friendly” manner as a condition for their enforcement", with a stronger contra proferentem rule or a revived duty to warn under Restatement (Second) § 211. [quote; paraphrase]
- p. 141: "if access to smart readers is unequally distributed so that only certain classes of consumers benefit from them, such a judicial shift can lead to regressive cross-subsidies that exacerbate inequalities." [quote] They "urge courts and policymakers to resist attempts to jump the gun and prematurely expand the duty to read." [quote]

## Critical notes

- Contest and limits: written before current LLM agents (the examples use GPT-3, 2020-21); the authors' later chapter (`arbel2025how`, abstract only in the library) revisits the question with LLMs and was not read here. Scout B's note that DOI 10.1017/9781009279079.019 belongs to Elvy and Kim is wrong: Crossref lists Arbel and Becher as its authors (checked 18 Nov 2026).
- Strongest use against the paper (the card's inference, not the authors' words): if the person acted on can run her own reader, the burden of reading (STRUCTURE 3.4, 4.4 "who carries the loss") moves from her to a machine, and the paper's pairing with the presumption that the signer had read loses force. The authors' own claim is that lack of reading "may be a technological challenge that is increasingly solved" (p. 134); they qualify it at pp. 135-136 and do not say the cost is near zero ("If smart readers are cheap and accessible", p. 140).
- What it leaves standing: the authors' own risks match the paper's question of who runs the reader: firms can attack readers (p. 121), platforms may supply the score (p. 121), and unequal access makes a stronger duty to read regressive (p. 141). Their remedy (p. 140) puts conditions on the seller, the drafter, not the reader.
- Person acted on gets: record — no; brake — no; envelope — partial (terms disclosed in reader-friendly form, a proposal, p. 140); standing — no; loss cover — partial (contra proferentem and § 211 proposals, p. 140; under existing doctrine the consumer bears the whole risk of reader error, pp. 138-139).
