## Summary

Read in full (main text; footnotes not read) in the publisher's web version, reached through a public Internet Archive capture (23 Oct 2026) because
law-ai.org returned a Cloudflare challenge and SSRN is blocked to scripted clients. The page shows "datePublished"
29 Oct 2023 and "dateModified" 21 Feb 2026; the PDF version linked from the page was not opened, so the web text may
differ from the SSRN record. Maas reviews technology-law scholarship on how metaphors and analogies shape policy at
five points (innovation, impact study, agenda, framing, legislative and judicial response), gives cases from internet
and AI law, and lists 55 analogies for AI with their regulatory implications. Part IV sets out the risks of
"unreflexive" analogies: one analogy hides the others, it can miss a key trait or allege one that does not exist, it
limits understanding of the technology "on its own terms", and once entrenched it can lead to "ineffective or even
dangerous laws"; legal definitions fixed unreflexively risk "governance misspecification". Maas does not argue against analogy; he asks for a process for choosing and
testing analogies, with a list of questions (what the metaphor foregrounds, what it occludes, its regulatory
implications).

## Critical notes

- Scope limit: Maas reviews analogies between AI and other technologies or institutions; he does not discuss analogies between the competence a medium demands (literacy) and AI, and does not test any historical analogy against a rule like Bartha's. His caution is procedural, not a refutation of any one analogy.
- Strongest use against the paper (the card's inference from Part IV's questions; Maas names no historical analogy to literacy or print): the history of literacy is one frame among many; choosing it foregrounds custody, record and reading, and may occlude features the agent case has and print did not (speed, adaptation, multi-agent interaction). The paper must show what its frame occludes, not only what it reveals.
- What it leaves standing: Maas says an analogy is "more or less suitable" for a purpose, which is consistent with STRUCTURE 2.3's claim of prima facie plausibility (Bartha) rather than proof.
