## Summary

Abstract only; paraphrase. Gingerich tests two rival accounts of the official, uniform, secret ballot. One holds that it frees dependent voters from local elites and so raises competition. The other holds that where illiteracy is widespread, adopting it may amount to a suffrage restriction. He uses municipal voting returns from Brazil's Second Republic (1945-1964), where the official ballot was introduced in stages; the abstract says this staggered rollout lets the ballot's effect be assessed, and does not name the method. The abstract reports that the main consequence of the reform was a restriction of the suffrage: poor and dependent voters found it very hard to vote, and parliamentary debates indicate that the effect was foreseen and intended.

## Key facts it relies on

- The article contrasts a liberation account of the secret ballot with a suffrage-restriction account for settings of widespread illiteracy (abstract) [paraphrase].
- The data are municipal-level voting returns for Brazil's Second Republic, 1945-1964, with a staggered rollout of the official ballot (abstract) [paraphrase].
- The abstract states that the primary consequence of the reform was suffrage restriction (abstract) [paraphrase].
- The abstract states that parliamentary debates show the restriction was an anticipated and intended effect (abstract) [paraphrase].

## Critical notes

- Abstract only; the size of the effect, the identification and the debates cited are in the article. No page locator applies beyond "abstract".
- The abstract mentions illiteracy as the setting but does not report who the debates' speakers were, so it gives no names of contemporary objectors. Its stated finding names poor and dependent voters; that illiteracy was the channel is the premise of the account the finding favours, and the abstract does not report it as a result in terms.
- Kousser (card kousser1974shaping) reaches a parallel result for the US South from before-and-after turnout; the two works differ in method and place, and this card does not test whether they agree on mechanism.
- A 2013 working paper by the same author, "Can Institutions Cure Clientelism? Assessing the Impact of the Australian Ballot in Brazil" (IDB-WP-428), found in discovery and apparently an earlier version, sits behind a bot challenge and was not opened.
- Bearing: supports (STRUCTURE 3.2, candidate 1 of PLAN 4.5): a measured case outside the US where a ballot design acted as the gate, and where the abstract says the exclusion was intended. This rests on the abstract alone.
