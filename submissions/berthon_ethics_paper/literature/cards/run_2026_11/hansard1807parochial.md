## Summary

Read in full, in the UK Parliament's Historic Hansard web edition, which gives the printed column numbers 798-806.
Hansard of 1807 reports speeches in the third person and in summary, so the words are the reporter's, not a
verbatim transcript. Samuel Whitbread moved the second reading of his bill for parish schools for the children of
the poor. Davies Giddy moved to put it off three months, arguing that schooling the labouring poor would make them
despise their lot, read seditious pamphlets and grow insolent to their superiors. Others objected to cost,
compulsion and the burden on magistrates. Mr. Rose would have the poor taught to read but doubted writing, since
those who wrote well would leave the plough for the counting-house. Whitbread, Romilly, Lushington, Lord Milton and
Lord Henry Petty answered for diffusion. The Chancellor of the Exchequer preferred a commission of inquiry and
warned of harm to voluntary schools. The bill was read a second time without a division, and the Commons voted 47 to
13 to go into committee.

## Key facts it relies on

- What the bill would require: Lord Henry Petty described it as going "no farther than barely to furnish youth who were destitute of all other means, with a certain source of obtaining a very plain and limited education." c. 805 [quote]
- Administration: Whitbread said "The business was committed to the magistrates, who were the most proper persons to carry the act into execution," and magistrates could suspend it where schools existed. c. 803 [quote]
- Giddy's objection: schooling "would teach them to despise their lot in life, instead of making them good servants in agriculture, and other laborious employments to which their rank in society had destined them." c. 798 [quote]
- Giddy also said "it would enable them to read seditious pamphlets, vicious books, and publications against Christianity." c. 798 [quote]
- Reading without writing: Rose "had no doubt that the poor ought to be taught to read; as to writing, he had some doubt, because those who had learnt to write well, were not willing to abide at the plough, but looked to a situation in some counting house." c. 800 [quote]
- Wharton asked whether ministers and churchwardens should "hold a critical examination in order to ascertain who were fit to be exalted to the counting-house, and who were only fit for the plough?" c. 801 [quote]
- Whitbread on rank: "in those places where the lower orders were most remarkable for their vices the example was set them by their superiors, who were generally more vicious than they." c. 803 [quote]
- The Chancellor of the Exchequer said the education proposed "would disqualify the persons possessing it from the most necessary, and useful description [...], labour" (the gap is in the web edition). c. 804 [quote]
- Division on going into committee: 47 for, 13 against. c. 806 [paraphrase]

## Critical notes

- Limits: one Commons sitting. The bill's later fate is not in this text, and a summary that says "Parliament
  rejected universal schooling in 1807" cannot rest on this debate, where the Commons voted 47 to 13 to proceed.
  The opponents' words are the reporter's third-person summary.
- Contemporaries named the wrong on the floor: Whitbread blamed vice on the example of superiors (c. 803), and Lord
  Milton and Lushington argued that informed labourers were better subjects (c. 801). The debate is evidence that
  the case for withholding was contested at the time, not only in hindsight.
- Rose's remark (c. 800) is the primary trace of candidate category 2 (partial competence: reading taught, writing
  withheld) in §4.5; it is one speaker's doubt, not a rule.
- Several objections (cost, compulsion, harm to voluntary schools, magistrates' opposition) are not about
  withholding literacy at all.
- Bearing: supports 3.3 (withheld competence argued openly by rank); new category (reading without writing);
  counter (much of the opposition rests on cost and compulsion, and the Commons majority favoured the bill).
