## Summary

Read: pp. 1-7 of ch. 1, single pages of later chapters, and the conclusion to the chapter on children; not the whole book. Johnes argues that the Welsh Not, the token passed to children caught speaking Welsh, was real and was punished, but was a practice of individual teachers and not a state policy. He says its direct goal was to help children learn English and not to end Welsh in their adult lives. He holds that popular memory has turned a varied classroom practice into a story of state coercion. He reports that school inspectors and the 1847 commissioners largely accepted the use of Welsh as a scaffold for teaching English. He still concludes that children beaten and punished for speaking their mother tongue suffered an injustice. The book therefore both confirms the punishment and contests the claim that the state ordered or sanctioned it; its closing pages add that English remained the basis of the educational system and that the state recognised no right to education in the mother tongue (p. 360).

## Key facts it relies on

- The Welsh Not was real and many children were physically and psychologically punished for speaking Welsh at school. [quote] p. 3: "The Welsh Not was real and many children were physically and psychologically punished at school for speaking Welsh."
- The practice was implemented by teachers on their own, without parental opposition. [quote] p. 3: "It will show that the Welsh Not was implemented by individual teachers, largely of their own accord and without opposition from parents."
- Its aim was learning English, not ending Welsh in adult life. [quote] p. 3: "its direct goal was not to stop children speaking Welsh in their future lives but to help them learn English."
- Popular memory treats it as official policy. [quote] p. 2: "It started to be assumed that it was some kind of official policy."
- The 1847 Blue Books commissioner Lingen found the English-only schoolbooks a barrier to Welsh-speaking children and still held the purpose to be superseding the language. [quote] p. 80: "impossible to exaggerate the difficulties"; same page: "if not lost, at least stowed away" (Lingen's words as Johnes quotes them).
- Johnes criticises Phillipson and Welsh historians for treating the suppression of Welsh as official policy without documenting a policy. [paraphrase] p. 23.
- Lingen later headed the education department and was not sympathetic to Wales. [paraphrase] p. 201.
- Conclusion on the injustice. [quote] p. 270: "But none of this changes the bare fact that there were children beaten and punished for speaking their mother tongue."
- Added at the Fable read. A practice, not a policy, and never sanctioned by the state. [quote] p. 28: "It sees the Welsh Not, not as a policy, but as a practice."; p. 360: "The Welsh Not was not imperialism in a direct sense, not least because the state never sanctioned it"
- Added at the Fable read. The state's grant examinations were in English from 1862, and the Code did not bar Welsh from the classroom. [quote] p. 110: "The Revised Code made no mention of the Welsh language but its examinations were in English, which created a sense that Wales was being treated unfairly."; same page: "the Revised Code did not prevent the use of Welsh in classrooms"
- Added at the Fable read. Attendance became a legal requirement in 1880, for ages five to ten. [paraphrase] p. 111.

## Critical notes

Runs against the linguistic-imperialism literature and earlier historians of education in Wales (Phillipson; W. Gareth Evans; H. G. Williams), whose claims Johnes names and calls wrong on p. 23. His view that the Welsh Not was teacher-led narrows any claim that a state rule enforced one language in Wales. Limits: the drafter read eleven pages of 458 and did not test his logbook evidence. The author is a Welsh-history specialist writing against a popular narrative; the University of Wales Press edition is open access (front matter, per scout).
Bearing: narrows 3.7 (the British case supports a punishment and a language standard, but not a statutory enforcement rule); counter to any claim that the state ordered or sanctioned the punishment of Welsh-speaking in school. It is not counter to a claim that the state set English as the examined standard: grant examinations were in English from 1862 (p. 110), and the law required attendance from 1880 (p. 111).
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-1-2): the Bearing line claimed the book counters "any claim that the state compelled the language standard in Wales"; pp. 110-111 and 360 do not support that breadth, and the line is narrowed to the punishment. The Summary's last sentence dropped "or the school system" and gained the p. 360 point. The basis sentence said "ch. 1"; the drafter read pp. 1-7 of a chapter that runs to p. 36. "Welsh-nationalist" was the drafter's label for Evans and Williams, whom Johnes calls historians of education in Wales (p. 23), and "about eight pages" miscounted the eleven in `pages_read`.
