## Summary
Read in full. Graff, the historian who named "the literacy myth", argues that the new literacy studies of the 1960s to 1980s (his own work, Scribner and Cole, Heath, Street, Brandt) replaced the myth of literacy's unique, autonomous power with context-bound research, but that the myth has returned in the proliferating "new literacies" and "multiple literacies". He holds that many of these labels come from organizations founded to promote and sell them, that they seldom cite the new literacy studies, and that most are metaphors rather than literacies. His list of such "literacies" includes digital, data, and coding and computational literacy. He says the myth was never an argument that literacy had no effects, since otherwise it "would never have achieved its hegemony". His evidence for the myth's return is a set of examples (a 2021 newspaper advertisement for "FL4ALL", an online society of literacy educators, Psychology Today pieces, the 2020 Routledge Handbook of Literacy Studies), not a count or a study, and he says the question whether the influence of the new literacy studies has been diminishing "demands more complete study". The essay is short and polemical, and it does not discuss artificial intelligence.

## Key facts it relies on
- Graff restates his 2010 definition: "The Literacy Myth refers to the belief, articulated in educational, civic, religious, and other settings, contemporary and historical, that the acquisition of literacy is a necessary precursor to and inevitably results in economic development, democratic practice, cognitive enhancement, and upward social mobility". [quote] p. 48.
- "Literacy is never a “neutral skill”; it is always historically determined and value laden." [quote] p. 48.
- "By reference to myth, I did not argue that these foundational assumptions were completely false." [quote] p. 48.
- Under the myth, "Individuals, age groups, gender groups, racial and ethnic groups, tribes, territories, regions, nations, and even continents were labeled essentially as superior or inferior." [quote] p. 48.
- "Many of these proliferating “new literacies” derive from formal organizations founded to promote and sell them, figuratively and literally." [quote] p. 49.
- "Most of them resemble illogical, unfounded metaphors rather than reputable literacies." [quote] p. 49.
- His list of 36 "literacies" (counted from the list) names "data literacy; digital literacy; coding and computational literacy" and none for artificial intelligence. [quote] p. 49.
- "There is reason to believe that the influence of the new literacy studies has been diminishing. This question demands more complete study." [quote] p. 49.
- "the literacy myth continues to compete with established, trusted research and understanding." [quote] p. 51.
