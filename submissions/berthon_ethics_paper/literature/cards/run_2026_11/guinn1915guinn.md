## Summary

Read in full for the opinion (pp. 354-368) in the official U.S. Reports print; the counsel's argument summaries
(pp. 348-354) were skimmed. Oklahoma election officers had been convicted of conspiring to deny Black citizens the
vote in 1910; the Circuit Court of Appeals certified two questions on the 1910 suffrage amendment to the Oklahoma
constitution. That amendment
required every registrant to read and write any section of the state constitution, but exempted anyone entitled to
vote on 1 January 1866, anyone then resident in a foreign nation, and any lineal descendant of such a person. The
Court (White C.J.) held the 1866 standard void under the Fifteenth Amendment because it had no reason except to
revive the conditions the Amendment destroyed. It held the literacy test, considered alone, a lawful exercise of
state power not subject to federal supervision. It then held the literacy test fell with the exemption, because the
amendment's own words commanded that the exempted class never be subjected to it. McReynolds J. took no part.

## Key facts it relies on

- The amendment: no person shall be registered or vote "unless he be able to read and write any section of the constitution of the State of Oklahoma." p. 357 [quote]
- Administrators: "Precinct election inspectors having in charge the registration of electors shall enforce the provisions of this section at the time of registration" (the sentence runs on: ", provided registration be required"). p. 357 [quote]
- The exemption reached anyone entitled to vote "on January 1, 1866, or at any time prior thereto" and "no lineal descendant of such person." p. 357 [quote]
- The United States raised no question about the literacy test as an independent standard; its argument was that the test fell only through its link to the 1866 clause. p. 360 [paraphrase]
- On the 1866 standard: "Certainly it cannot be said that there was any peculiar necromancy in the time named which engendered attributes affecting the qualification to vote which would not exist at another and different period ..." p. 365 [quote]
- On literacy: "No time need be spent on the question of the validity of the literacy test considered alone since as we have seen its establishment was but the exercise by the State of a lawful power vested in it not subject to our supervision, and indeed, its validity is admitted." p. 366 [quote]
- The literacy test fell only because the text commanded that the exempted persons "should not be under any conditions subjected to the" literacy test, which would be "virtually set at naught" if the test survived alone. p. 367 [quote]
- Disposition: first question (was the amendment valid?) answered No; second (was it void as to otherwise-qualified Black citizens unable to read and write?) answered Yes. pp. 356-357, 368 [paraphrase]

## Critical notes

- What the usual summary overstates: *Guinn* is often cited as the case that struck down the grandfather clause and
  with it a literacy test. The Court expressly affirmed the literacy test's validity "considered alone" (p. 366) and
  voided Oklahoma's only on severability. *Davis v. Schnell* (p. 876) cites *Guinn* for the states' right to set a
  literacy test (card davis1949davis).
- The Court states the Government's charge that the 1866 standard is a "somewhat disguised" refusal of the Fifteenth
  Amendment (p. 361) and accepts it (pp. 364-365). Nothing in the opinion names reading tests as a wrong; the wrong it
  names is the exemption that let the test fall on one race.
- Form of correction (question 3): prohibition (a constitutional provision declared void). No helper, check of
  understanding or shift of risk.
- *Louisiana v. United States* (380 U.S. at 148, 151-152) records Louisiana's answer: an interpretation test in 1921,
  "little if any less successful" (card louisiana1965louisiana). The correction was evaded by moving the gate from
  ancestry into the examiner's discretion.
- Bearing: narrows 3.2 (the Court upheld the literacy qualification and condemned only the race-proxy exemption);
  supports 7-D02 only as a prohibition that a state routed around (Louisiana, per 380 U.S. at 148; this opinion
  concerns Oklahoma and says nothing of what followed).
- Corrected by V-A-2: the "necromancy" quotation ended at "vote." mid-sentence; extended to "...qualification to vote which would not exist at another and different period ..." (p. 365) so the cut is marked.
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-2-1, 2026-11-18): the Summary had the convicted officers bringing the certified questions; the opinion says the court below drew the certificate (p. 354; caption "Certificate from the Circuit Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit", p. 347). The administrators quotation closed before the proviso "provided registration be required" (p. 357); the close is now marked. Under Bearing, "the state" that routed around the prohibition is Louisiana, known from the Louisiana opinion and not from this file.
