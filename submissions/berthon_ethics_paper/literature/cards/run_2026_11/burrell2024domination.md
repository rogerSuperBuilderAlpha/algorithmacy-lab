## Summary

Read in full at the Fable read, body and notes; the drafter read the abstract, the introduction and part of section 3 (the extracted text has no page numbers; locators are section names). Burrell argues that machine learning ethics overproduces work on allocative fairness, and offers a sociological account of why. She proposes that algorithmic injustice be defined as domination, drawing mainly on Iris Marion Young; the text never mentions Pettit or republicanism. Domination here means the denial of self-determination and control, and in particular limits on opportunities to voice concerns and be heard. The redefinition widens the remedies beyond code fixes to legislation, participatory audits, user repurposing and resistance, and bans. Her cases of domination are a parole risk score, automated Medicaid allocation and enrolment, and police facial recognition (§3); none is a commercial dealing with a customer, and the word "consumers" appears twice (§3.1, §3.2). The Conclusion says that centring domination raises the burden of justification for delegating important decisions to machines (§4).

## Key facts it relies on

- "the domination of humans by algorithms, i.e., the denial of self-determination and control, and specifically limitations on opportunities to voice concerns and have them be heard". [quote] §1
- The redefinition expands the solution space beyond code fixes to "legislation, participatory assessments, forms of user repurposing and resistance, and activism that leads to bans on certain uses of technology". [quote] Abstract
- Technical fairness discourse "makes it more difficult to challenge upstream decision-making about whether automated decision-making can or should be implemented." [quote] §3
- "Ensuring that human self-determination is preserved as much a possible should not be simply a way that system designers and business firms avoid their own responsibility by delegating it to consumers." [quote] §3.1
- Of the parole case of Glenn Rodriguez: "more deeply it was a problem of algorithmic domination — that no meaningful or formal process of seeking recourse or error correction was provided to Rodriguez or his legal representation." [quote] §3
- "if we center domination as the primary injustice, the burden of justification gets substantially higher." [quote] §4
- She names Anderson's relational "democratic equality" and Young as the two alternatives to distributive fairness. [paraphrase] §3

## Critical notes

- Limits: the account is Young's, relational and set in institutional context and process (§3); the contrast with Pettit's agent-relative test is this card's, since the text does not discuss Pettit. The abstract and §1 supply the definition. The text does not say whether a single transaction can dominate. No discussion of customers: "consumers" appears as the party to whom designers and firms must not delegate their own responsibility (§3.1) and among the "citizens, workers, and consumers" who "have found strategies to resist or even break systems" (§3.2). The Google search and image-labelling cases (§§2, 2.1) involve users of consumer services but are treated as representational harm to racialized groups.
- Contested by: not established; the text names no critic of its own position. Note 15 says communitarian critiques of Rawlsian fairness are left for a future publication.
- Bearing for Q1 and Q3: control and voice, with design measures beyond user toggles. Person acted on gets: record no; brake partial (the absence of human review or override is named as the fault in the Medicaid cases, §3; human review under the GDPR is called a modest move, §3.1; bans, §3.2); envelope no; standing yes (voice and being heard as the core, §1; recourse and error correction, §3); loss cover no.
