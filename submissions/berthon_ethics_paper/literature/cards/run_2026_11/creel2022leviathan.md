## Summary

Read: pp. 26-27, 34 and 37, with pp. 28-29 and 38-41 at the Fable read; pp. 30-33 and 35-36 are unread. Creel and Hellman ask whether an arbitrary algorithmic decision wrongs the person it affects. They analyse the charge of arbitrariness as the claim that the employer, lender or other actor has adopted a screening tool that fails to achieve its own ends as well as it might. On that reading, one actor's arbitrary decision has no obvious moral force and does not wrong the applicant, unless the actor has promised to decide by certain criteria or invited reliance on announced factors, or unless other rights make non-arbitrariness relevant, as in criminal justice (p. 27 and n. 4; p. 34 n. 17). The moral concern arises when the same algorithm, or algorithms trained on the same data, are used across many contexts, so a person is excluded from a broad range of opportunities. Their remedy aims at the systematicity, not the arbitrariness: they recommend introducing randomness, for example random classifier ensembles (pp. 38-41). The text never cites Pettit and never uses the word "republican" (whole file searched); it considers arbitrariness as lack of constraint by rules, sets that sense aside as "likely inapt" for rule-based algorithms (pp. 28-29), and settles on a means-ends sense (p. 34).

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract: "when the same algorithm or different algorithms based on the same data are used in multiple contexts, a person may be arbitrarily excluded from a broad range of opportunities." [quote] p. 26
- "isolated arbitrary decisions are not of moral concern except when other rights make nonarbitrariness relevant." [quote] p. 27
- On arbitrary as "unconstrained by ex ante rules or standards" (p. 28): "This sense of arbitrariness is likely inapt in the context of algorithmic decision-making. An algorithm is, after all, rule based." [quote] p. 29
- On reliance: "Ultimately, we are unsure about whether reliance (at least in most cases) is sufficient to limit the ability of an employer to change her focus." [quote] p. 28
- "If an employer or lender has adopted an inefficient means to achieve its own ends, that is unfortunate but, without more, does not wrong others." [quote] p. 34
- Employers and lenders "may wrong applicants if the selection procedure is not as promised or announced", because they broke a promise or invited reliance, not because the process is arbitrary. [quote] p. 34
- "If arbitrary decision-making is not morally troubling at the individual level, does anything change when that arbitrariness goes to scale?" [quote] p. 37
- A person excluded from important opportunities across employment, loans and education may face a social hierarchy of esteem or domination (citing Anderson 1999); the same sentence offers three other grounds, from Sen and Nussbaum, Raz, and Rawls. [paraphrase] p. 37
- "The practical upshot of this analysis is that a remedy should be aimed at disrupting systematicity rather than arbitrariness." [quote] p. 38

## Critical notes

- This is counter-evidence for a one-off dealing (Q1, Q2): on the authors' account, an arbitrary decision by a single firm does not wrong the person, except through a broken promise or invited reliance, or where other rights apply. They hedge the reliance exception themselves (p. 28). The loss they call morally serious is exclusion at scale, and they grade it by the degree of exclusion and the importance of the domain (p. 38). Their cases are hiring and lending; they "contend" that the argument generalizes (p. 28).
- Different conception of arbitrariness from the republican one; do not read it as a test for domination. The contrast with the republican conception is this card's; the nearest the text comes is the "unconstrained" sense it sets aside (pp. 28-29). The noun "domination" appears once, in a citation of Anderson on hierarchy (p. 37); the verb "dominate" elsewhere means market dominance (pp. 28, 35, 38, 39 n. 24).
- Who contests it: not established from the parts read; no rebuttal was searched for. The authors record one dissent from their concern with a single misclassified person: Kearns et al. "find this problem uncompelling" (p. 39 n. 22).
