"I always assumed that I wasn't really that close to [her]": Reasoning about invisible algorithms in news feeds. *Proceedings of the 33rd Annual ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (CHI '15), 153–162.

## What it argues

Most Facebook users do not know their News Feed is curated, and showing them the curation changes how they use the site. The authors interviewed 40 Facebook users recruited by quota sampling in Champaign, Illinois, and found that 62.5% were unaware the feed hid anything; these "Unaware" participants attributed missed posts to their own scrolling or infrequent visits. The 37.5% who were "Aware" had learned it by one of two routes — inductively comparing how often different friends appeared, or deductively reasoning that a large network must be filtered — and the two groups differed on every active-engagement measure (usage frequency, posting activity, use of feed settings, page management) but on none of the passive ones (membership duration, network size, shown-content percentage). The authors draw the conclusion the design section depends on: "simple exposure to the algorithm output is not enough to gain information about the algorithm's existence. To learn about an algorithm without any outside information, active engagement is required."

FeedVis is the instrument. It pulls a participant's own feed and every friend's posts through the Graph API and renders "the algorithm outputs": the difference between the feed as curated and as it would be unfiltered. Four views do the work. The Content View sets two columns side by side, "All Stories" against "Shown Stories," with hidden posts as white "holes"; the Friend View sorts friends into "rarely shown" (0–10% of posts displayed), "sometimes shown" (45–55%), and "mostly shown" (90–100%); the Friend Rearrangement and Content Rearrangement Views then invite the participant to "tweak" the algorithm by moving friends between categories or flipping stories between shown and hidden. Unaware participants reacted with surprise and anger ("waking up in 'the Matrix'"), and eleven of them discovered that friends they believed had stopped posting, left Facebook, or drifted away had simply been filtered — the title quotation is P3 learning this. Participants moved 43% of friends to a different category on average but only 17% of stories, and by the end 21 of 40 had come to appreciate the filter once they saw that most hidden items were interaction noise. At a two-to-six-month follow-up (30 respondents), 83% reported changed behavior — switching to "Most Recent," liking more selectively, visiting friends' pages directly — and 24 reported equal or higher satisfaction. Six reported lower satisfaction and less trust; one participant quit Facebook because "by neither showing me everything nor making their actions explicit, I felt like I was being lied to."

## Relation to the argument

This is the primary empirical anchor for the feed-control case study and it grounds three of the four affordances directly. **Branching/counterfactual exploration**: FeedVis is a counterfactual instrument by construction — the "All Stories" column is the feed under a null algorithm, and the two Rearrangement Views let the user author an alternative curation and see what it would return. The paper reports the behavioral result of that exploration (43% of friends re-categorized) and its learning effect (awareness → active manipulation). **Visual chunking**: the Friend View's three bins are exactly the kind of coarse partition the paper proposes for novices; the authors note that participants responded differently to friend-based and story-based revelation, which is a chunking-granularity finding. **Intentional frictional transparency**: the discussion sets out the design dilemma in the paper's own vocabulary — an external "temporary x-ray or ombudsman's perspective" versus "regular introductions of 'seams' through explanations of algorithms that make interactions less fluid" — and comes down on the side of engaging users with the process so they feel "not controlled by an algorithm but ... a part of one." The novice-to-expert framing is also here: the Aware minority built their models by comparing feeds and reasoning about network size, which is the expert move FeedVis makes available to everyone else. The 62.5% baseline is the paper's cleanest number for the size of the algorithmacy gap in an everyday system.

## Caution

Forty participants from one US metropolitan area, one platform, one algorithm, in 2014; the authors flag the non-probability sample and call for a controlled experimental design. All follow-up behavior is self-reported, and ten of forty did not respond. The API's 600-query-per-minute limit meant participants with large networks saw a shorter window of posts. The paper reports awareness, reaction, and self-reported behavior change; it does not measure whether participants' mental models of the algorithm became more accurate, and the authors explicitly defer the folk-theory analysis to later work (that is `eslami2016`, "First I 'like' it, then I hide it"). Cite it for the awareness gap, the FeedVis design, and the behavioral consequences of revelation, not for how users theorize the algorithm. Pages 153–162 are taken from the paper's citation in Rader, Cotter & Cho (2018) and dblp, not from a page-numbered copy.
