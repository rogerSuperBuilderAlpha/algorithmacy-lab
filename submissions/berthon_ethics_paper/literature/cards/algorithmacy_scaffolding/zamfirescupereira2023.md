# Zamfirescu-Pereira, J.D., Wong, R.Y., Hartmann, B., & Yang, Q. (2023). Why Johnny Can't Prompt: How Non-AI Experts Try (and Fail) to Design LLM Prompts. *Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '23)*, Article 437, 21 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3544548.3581388 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted PDF, people.eecs.berkeley.edu) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top HCI venue (CHI) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Non-AI-experts explore prompts opportunistically, not systematically, and their failures trace to two sources: over-generalisation from single observations and a social lens carried over from human-to-human instruction. The authors built BotDesigner, a no-code chatbot design tool with a Conversation view for local testing and an Error Browser for regression-testing a prompt change against every labelled conversation, and used it as a design probe with ten participants (professionals and graduate students, none with prompt-design experience) asked to make a recipe chatbot behave like a TV chef. Every participant could iterate on prompts locally, but none used the systematic testing interface, most declared success after a single improved utterance, and error labelling died out once participants saw it did not feed the bot. The social lens showed up as insistence on politeness, a strong bias toward direct instruction over in-line examples even after examples visibly worked, expectations that the bot would "remember" prior conversations, and the assumption that "Do not say X" would be understood as a human would understand it. The paper maps these struggles onto the six learning barriers of end-user programming (design, selection, coordination, use, understanding, information) and onto known novice-versus-expert differences in software engineering and ML (Table 2: implicit vs. explicit specification, overconfident vs. cautious testing, opportunistic vs. systematic debugging). Its design section asks for tools that show cause and effect (highlight prompt changes and the resulting conversational changes), make effective examples salient at the moment of need, and, drawing on Chalmers and on Inman and Ribes, favour "seamful" interfaces that make the app act in non-human ways rather than pass as a social actor.

## Relation to the argument

This is the anchor source for the novice/expert claim in the prompt-engineering case study, and it supports affordances 2, 3 and 4 more than it supports 1. For affordance 2 (branching / what-if): participants wanted exactly this — P4 asked for a side-by-side diff of two templates and the utterances each produced — yet when the tool offered systematic multi-conversation testing, nobody used it, so the paper is evidence for a need and a caution about uptake in one breath. For affordance 3 (visual chunking): the preamble / first-turns / reminder structure of the template is itself a mild chunking, and non-programmers still could not tell which field had which time horizon. For affordance 4 (frictional transparency): section 5.2 explicitly proposes seamful design over seamless design and cites the CHI 2019 "Beautiful Seams" paper, which gives the essay a peer-reviewed bridge from prompt tooling to the seams vocabulary. The novice/expert table (Table 2) is secondary: the paper imports it from the EUP and iML literatures rather than measuring expert prompt writers itself.

## Caution

Ten participants, one task, one model (text-davinci-002), sixty minutes each, with interviewers intervening when participants stalled. The authors say outright that the sample skews toward STEM early adopters and that they "cannot (and will not)" make prevalence claims. There is no expert comparison group: the "expert" column of Table 2 comes from prior work on programming and ML, not from observed expert prompters. Do not cite this paper as showing that experts prompt differently; cite it as showing how novices prompt and where the EUP barriers recur. The "seamful" suggestion is a design proposal in a discussion section, not a tested intervention.
