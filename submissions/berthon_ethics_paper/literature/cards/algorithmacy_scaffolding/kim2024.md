# Kim, S.S.Y., Liao, Q.V., Vorvoreanu, M., Ballard, S., & Vaughan, J.W. (2024). "I'm Not Sure, But...": Examining the Impact of Large Language Models' Uncertainty Expression on User Reliance and Trust. *Proceedings of the 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '24)*.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3630106.3658941 · arXiv:2405.00623v2 · pre-registration osf.io/mnrp9 · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v2) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top venue for accountable-AI empirical work (FAccT); pre-registered, N = 404 · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-15

## What it argues

Natural-language uncertainty expressions in LLM output reduce overreliance, but the wording matters and the effect is modest. Participants answered eight yes/no medical questions with responses from a fictional LLM search engine (built from real Copilot-in-Bing outputs, half correct), in one of four conditions: Control (no hedging), Uncertain1st ("I'm not sure, but it seems…"), UncertainGeneral ("It's unclear, but it seems…"), or No-AI. First-person hedging lowered agreement with the system (74.8% vs 80.9%), lowered confidence in the AI's answer (3.66 vs 3.95 on 5), and raised accuracy (72.8% vs 63.9%); the general-perspective hedge moved the same measures in the same direction but not significantly. An exploratory split by correctness attributes the accuracy gain to reduced, not eliminated, overreliance on wrong answers. Trust beliefs, perceived transparency and anthropomorphism did not differ across conditions, but first-person hedging did lower trust *intention* — willingness to use the system — and one participant called it "very non-committal." Access to the AI at all made people worse than No-AI (63.9% vs 74.2%), which the authors attribute to the system's engineered 50% accuracy.

## Relation to the argument

This is the best-powered study in the library for affordance 1 (exposing system confidence rather than hiding it behind natural language), and it complicates the essay's framing in a useful way: the uncertainty here is *itself* natural language, embedded in the prose, not a numeric or visual target; the authors chose that form on the grounds that people find numbers hard and that search products already hedge. So the paper supports "surface uncertainty" but not "surface it as a number instead of words." It also touches affordance 4: the hedge works partly as friction — instance-level analysis shows longer task time and more self-reported Internet searching on hedged answers. For the novice/expert frame, the finding that a hedge shifts behaviour without shifting stated trust beliefs suggests the scaffold operates below the level of the user's explicit model of the system.

## Caution

Effects are real but small (six points of agreement, nine of accuracy), the general-perspective version did not reach significance, and 38% of collected responses were excluded on pre-registered quality criteria after the authors found MTurk data quality required a "Masters" qualification. The AI system is fictional and fixed at 50% accuracy, uncertainty was inserted at random rather than calibrated to correctness, and the task is one-shot medical fact-finding rather than the iterative generative work the essay's case study concerns. The authors themselves flag that first-person hedging reduces desire to use the system, which is a cost for any design that leans on it. Do not cite this as evidence about confidence *visualisation*; it is evidence about hedging language.
