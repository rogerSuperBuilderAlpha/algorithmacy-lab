# Rosenblat, A., & Stark, L. (2016). Algorithmic labor and information asymmetries: A case study of Uber's drivers. *International Journal of Communication*, 10, 3758–3784.

## What it argues

Uber manages a large, disaggregated workforce through information and power asymmetries built into its app, without the authority of an employment relation. Nine months of archival and real-time analysis of five driver forums (December 2014–September 2015, about 1,350 items) plus seven driver interviews document the mechanisms. Blind acceptance gives a driver about fifteen seconds to take a ride without seeing destination or fare, and cancelling risks deactivation, so drivers work "blind." Surge pricing appears as a heat map keyed to the passenger's location rather than the driver's, and Uber conflates predictive guesses about future demand with real-time measurement, using both to mobilize drivers through nudges ("Are you sure you want to go offline? Demand is very high in your area"). Rates change unilaterally, and drivers distrust the "Uber math" offered in justification. Communication runs one way: data flow up, while drivers reach only customer-service representatives returning template replies drivers call "Uber's robots." Passenger ratings act as the load-bearing control — a driver must hold roughly 4.6 to stay active — and because passengers cannot rate the system separately from the driver, "systematic accountability for the whole interactive process is downloaded onto individual drivers." The authors extend Lee et al.'s term *algorithmic management* to this automated enforcement of policy and call its effect "soft control": managerial outcomes without a managerial relationship.

## Relation to the argument

The gig-work case's foundational field study, and the one that shows the algorithmacy gap being manufactured rather than merely tolerated. Each affordance has a documented negative here. **Exposing optimization targets**: drivers cannot see what the dispatch or pricing optimizes for; the "Uber math" behind rate cuts and the passenger-keyed surge map are targets deliberately withheld, and the paper's phrase for the result — information asymmetry as a control instrument — is the condition the affordance would reverse. **Branching/counterfactual exploration**: blind acceptance removes the branch entirely; a driver cannot compare the ride offered with the rides not offered, and the fifteen-second window forecloses deliberation. **Intentional frictional transparency** has its mirror image in the nudges, which are engineered *anti*-friction: they interrupt exit, not entry, and disclose only what mobilizes. **Visual chunking** is present in a degraded form — the surge heat map chunks demand spatially but on the wrong referent, so drivers who chase it arrive after it has moved. The forum evidence also shows drivers building collective algorithmacy from the outside ("don't chase the surge"), which is the bottom-up scaffolding the paper's affordances would formalize.

## Caution

A single-platform US case from 2014–2015, built from forum posts and seven interviews; forum posters may be more opinionated or more platform-dependent than typical drivers. The specific figures (the 4.6 threshold, the fifteen-second window, commission rates) are period-specific and Uber's terms have changed repeatedly since. The stance is critical and "soft control" is a normative reading; report it as the authors' claim. `rosenblat2015` (SSRN) is the working-paper version of the same study, not independent evidence. For a companion study of the same platforms with a design orientation, use `lee2015`.
