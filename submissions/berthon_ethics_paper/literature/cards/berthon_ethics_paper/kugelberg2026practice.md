## Summary
The authors (Oxford; Warwick) reject "moral machine regulation" (regulate AI so that it does in each case what
individual morality demands) in favour of practice-dependent governance: "Regulation of AI systems should aim to
ensure that the practices in which they are deployed are just and justifiable to those affected, in light of the
values and purposes those practices are meant to serve" (PDF p. 4). Examples are autonomous vehicles in the
traffic system. Locators are PDF pages; the online-first version has no journal pagination.

## Key facts it relies on
- Objection order. (1) Steelman of the opponent: the familiar inconclusiveness objection to moral machine regulation
  is answered on the opponent's behalf; on a democratic interpretation, "inconclusiveness in the form of reasonable
  disagreement does not by itself undermine the ideal of moral machine regulation" (p. 6), and "Although the
  democratic objection to moral machine regulation is not decisive, we take these two problems to be fundamental
  objections" (p. 6). (2) Their own two objections: the forest-and-trees problem (pp. 6-8) and the
  pre-institutional problem (pp. 8-9). (3) A circularity worry answered in passing (p. 9). (4) Objections to their
  own view, weaker first: "Status Quo Bias" (pp. 9-10), then "A powerful objection to practice-dependent
  theorising is that it faces difficulties when it comes to delineation of practices" (p. 10), "Practice
  Delineation" (pp. 10-11).
- On internal standards: "Because real-world institutions are not always the best possible solutions to the
  problems they are meant to solve, and because they do not always deliver on their promises, we can never take
  the status quo as a given." (p. 3)
- General values are admitted: "The relevant moral principles and values need not all be internal to it" (p. 3).
- Reply to the status-quo objection: the approach "can recommend fundamental reform when a practice no longer
  serves the values that justify it" (p. 10).
- Change by AI, quoting Gabriel (2022): AI is not "simply an additional ingredient that supervenes onto a stable
  practice" (p. 10); norms "likely change" when driverless cars arrive (p. 10).
- AI agents across practices: "the practices nevertheless have primacy" (p. 11).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper's examples are hypothetical traffic rules; it gives no empirical case and no historical record.
- It is a JBE paper (Springer), not BEQ.

## Where the argument uses it
- Outline, Objections ("Kugelberg and Kugelberg's order: dismiss the weak objection first, then meet the strong ones
  by name"): **narrower**. The move they make first is to *rebut a weak objection against their opponent*
  (steelmanning moral machine regulation, p. 6) before pressing their own. Against their own view they do go from
  the weaker (status quo bias) to the one they call "powerful" (delineation, p. 10). Both moves are usable; the
  outline's description conflates them.
- A7: **supports with a condition**. Their test, a practice judged by "the values and purposes" that justify it,
  including unmet "promises" (p. 3), is the paper's benchmark 3 in present tense. But they also hold that AI can
  change the practice it enters (p. 10, Gabriel), which is the "something has changed" escape A7 must close. A8
  must show which values of the practice survive the change.
- Enterprise objection (O06): their primacy-of-practice view would assess an LLM agent inside each practice it
  acts in (p. 11), which cuts against treating "enterprise" as one setting.
