## Summary
Cattley's edition of Foxe prints, in English, the thirteen provincial constitutions that Archbishop Thomas Arundel
promulgated for the province of Canterbury (the Oxford constitutions of 1407/1409; Foxe's margin dates them "A.D.
1409", p. 242). Foxe introduces them as "his bloody constitutions above-mentioned: the style and tenor whereof, to the
intent the rigour of the same may appear to all men, I thought hereunder to adjoin" (p. 242). The seventh constitution
forbids anyone "by his own authority" to translate any text of Scripture into English "by way of a book, libel, or
treatise", and forbids anyone to read any such book made "in the time of John Wickliff, or since, or hereafter",
"until the said translation be allowed by the ordinary of the place, or, if the case so require, by the council
provincial" (p. 245). The page is fixed by the running heads "244 THE CRUEL CONSTITUTIONS OF ARUNDEL" and "246 ...",
in two independent scans of the volume (`actsmonumentsofj03foxe` and `actsmonumentsof03foxe`, sha256 of the second
`47bfb219d3fbcf50a20952dc9d96bd928aaf802ddf14d7d997acc4b488648964`, same folder). Foxe's Latin original and the
editorial apparatus were not compared; this card quotes the 1837 English text only.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 242, heading: "The cruel Constitution of Thomas Arundel, Archbishop, against the Gospellers, or followers of
  God's Truth"; margin "Thirteen articles".
- p. 245, the seventh constitution: "we therefore decree and ordain, that no man, hereafter, by his own authority
  translate any text of the Scripture into English or any other tongue, by way of a book, libel, or treatise; and that
  no man read any such book, libel or treatise, now lately set forth in the time of John Wickliff, or since, or
  hereafter to be set forth, in part or in whole, privily or apertly, upon pain of greater excommunication, until the
  said translation be allowed by the ordinary of the place, or, if the case so require, by the council provincial. He
  that shall do contrary to this, shall likewise be punished as a favourer of error and heresy."
- p. 245, the sixth constitution (immediately before): whoever "shall read any such kind of book in schools or
  otherwise, as aforesaid, he shall be punished as a sower of schism, and a favourer of heresy" (of books by Wyclif or
  others not first examined by the universities of Oxford or Cambridge "or, at least, by twelve persons", p. 245).
- pp. 398-399, Foxe's own gloss: the constitution "decreed, that the text of holy Scripture should not be had, or read
  in the vulgar tongue, from the time of Master John Wickliff for ever after, unless the said translation be approved
  first by the ordinary, or by provincial council, under pain and punishment of heresy"; the approval clause "maketh
  more for a show or pretence, than for any just exception".

## Critical notes from the literature
- Foxe is a partisan Protestant martyrologist writing in the 1560s; his translation and framing ("cruel", "bloody")
  are his. For the Latin text a modern reader goes to Wilkins, *Concilia*, iii. 314-319, or to Watson (1995, Speculum
  70: 822-864; `watson1995censorship`, record only, not read), which treats the constitutions as the turning point of
  late-medieval English vernacular theology.
- The constitution licenses reading of a vernacular Scripture by diocesan approval, with excommunication for breach.
  It is a manuscript-era instrument (1409), half a century before print reached England, and it does what the draft
  attributes to the print stage: it put the licence to read in the hands of a designated few.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2, the "print stage" framing (draft ll. 59, 204, 225-227: "statutes that licensed who could read"; "Reading itself
  was licensed"; "the license to read to a designated few"): **narrower**. Licensed reading of the vernacular Bible is
  older than print in England by at least 134 years. The 1543 Act (`statutesrealm1817vol3`) repeats a medieval pattern
  with a statute. The periodization "scribes' correction then print stage" does not survive this instrument; the
  paper should present licensed reading as a recurring choice, not a print-era invention.
- H24 (four stages): the 1409 licence belongs with the first stage.
