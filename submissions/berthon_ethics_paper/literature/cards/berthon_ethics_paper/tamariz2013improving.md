## Summary
Abstract-only card, paraphrase only. Tamariz and colleagues searched MEDLINE to November 2011 for studies that
modified consent to improve comprehension among research subjects with low literacy. Six of 281 studies qualified
(1,620 participants; mostly older, minority, at or below eighth-grade literacy); only one was randomized. The two
studies that used teach-back or teach-to-goal reached the highest comprehension; the two that only lowered the
reading level of the form produced the lowest. The authors call the evidence extremely limited and name one-to-one
time with a study team member as the most effective strategy, on the strength of a single study. Springer served a
client challenge to my request and Europe PMC holds no open full text (PMC3539038 is not open), so the full article
was not read.

## Key facts it relies on
- Paraphrase of the abstract: six eligible studies, one randomized; teach-back or teach-to-goal gave the highest
  comprehension; simplified readability gave the lowest.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Six studies, one randomized: the review cannot carry a causal claim.
- Sudore et al. (2006; `berthon_ethics_course/sudore2006use.md`), a teach-to-goal study, is probably among the six;
  not confirmed without the full text.

## Where the argument uses it
- B1 / D06 (check understanding): **holds at abstract depth**. For readers with low literacy, checking understanding
  (teach-back) beat rewriting the text.
- A2: **holds at abstract depth**. Making the text easier left comprehension lowest.
