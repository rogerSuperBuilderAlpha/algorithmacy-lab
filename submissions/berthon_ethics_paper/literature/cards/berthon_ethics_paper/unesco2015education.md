## Summary
This is the full 2015 Global Monitoring Report, not the Summary carded as `berthon_ethics_course/unesco2015efa.md`.
UNESDOC serves a JavaScript shell to scripts, so I took the Wayback Machine capture of 5 January 2017 (the `id_`
raw form of the same URL). Chapter 4 reports that the Dakar goal of halving adult illiteracy was missed: about 781
million illiterate adults and an illiteracy rate falling only from 18% (2000) to 14% (2015) (p. 135). Its sharpest
finding comes from a new cohort analysis of DHS data for 30 countries: the literacy of a given cohort of women
barely changed over a decade, so most of the rise in adult literacy came from better-schooled young people
replacing older ones, "not due to the implementation of effective literacy programmes" (pp. 135, 143-144). The
report then explains limited progress: an ambiguous global commitment (MDGs left literacy out), campaigns with
ambitious targets and missed deadlines, the language of "eradicating" illiteracy as a "social illness", governments
declaring themselves "illiteracy-free", and an absence of independent evaluation (pp. 144-147). Printed page numbers
are used; PDF page = printed page + 17 in this chapter.

## Key facts it relies on
- Highlights: "There are about 781 million illiterate adults. The rate of illiteracy is likely to have dropped
  slightly from 18% in 2000 to 14% in 2015, which means that the Dakar target of halving illiteracy has not been
  achieved"; "Only 17 out of 73 countries with a literacy rate below 95% in 2000 halved their illiteracy rate by
  2015" (p. 135).
- Highlights: "Progress in adult literacy rates may be the consequence of younger, better educated people replacing
  older, less educated ones and not due to the implementation of effective literacy programmes" (p. 135).
- Goal 4 as worded at Dakar: "Achieving a 50 per cent improvement in levels of adult literacy by 2015, especially
  for women, and equitable access to basic and continuing education for all adults" (p. 137).
- Conflict of interest in monitoring: national agencies are "responsible for monitoring literacy and reducing
  illiteracy rates at the same time. This dual role can create pressure to show progress or to restrict access to
  data by independent assessors" (p. 143).
- Cohort finding: in Malawi the literacy rate of women aged 20-34 rose from 49% (2000) to 63% (2010), but the
  cohort aged 20-34 in 2000 "remained constant at 49%"; in Ethiopia the cohort fell "from 21% to 16%" (p. 144).
- "If the literacy skills of adults who were above school age did not improve in most developing countries, then
  considerable doubt is cast on the impact of efforts to improve the literacy skills of adult populations since
  2000" (p. 144).
- Expectations raised: "Declared targets have raised expectations that illiteracy would follow a path like that of
  polio and could be ‘eradicated’" (p. 145). Nepal is the exception the cohort analysis confirms (p. 144).
- Campaigns: "Most of the campaigns set ambitious targets, but the deadlines have already passed in some cases with
  targets unmet" (p. 146).
- Risks: campaigns "can raise unrealistic expectations"; their language "often portrays illiteracy as a ‘social
  illness’ which can be ‘eradicated’ with the right intervention", which "can stigmatize illiteracy, demotivate those
  with low literacy skills and lead them to hide their problems, particularly if political leaders declare the
  country to be ‘illiteracy-free’" (p. 147).
- Box 4.2: Yo Sí Puedo reached "more than 7 million people in 30 countries" (2003-2013) and three ALBA states
  declared themselves illiteracy-free, "However, despite its wide adoption, there have been no independent
  evaluations of its cost-effectiveness or its capacity to achieve sustainable reading skills on a large scale"
  (p. 147).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The cohort analysis uses DHS female samples and direct assessment in 30 countries (Barakat 2015, a background
  paper I did not open); the report says the results "hold true" after mortality and migration checks (p. 144).
- UNESCO is grading its own programme (EFA, LIFE); its self-criticism here is notable but institutional.
- The report credits some programmes (Nepal, South Africa's Kha Ri Gude as the one with good monitoring, pp. 144,
  147).

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (promise unmet; H13): **holds**, more strongly than the Summary card. The target was missed, and the gains that
  did occur mostly owe nothing to adult programmes (pp. 135, 143-144).
- A2 (competence oversold; H13): **holds**. The report itself names the overselling: "eradication" talk, declared
  "illiteracy-free" status, no independent evaluation, and the stigma that falls on the learner (pp. 145-147).
- Monitoring conflict (p. 143) is a direct statement of the custody problem: the agency responsible for the result
  also keeps the record of it.
