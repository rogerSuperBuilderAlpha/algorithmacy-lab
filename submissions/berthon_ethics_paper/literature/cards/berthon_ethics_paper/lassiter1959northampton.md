## Summary
A Black citizen of North Carolina sued to have the State's literacy test for voters declared unconstitutional. The
test required an applicant to "be able to read and write any section of the Constitution of North Carolina in the
English language" (n. 1). The Court, unanimous, affirmed the state court's judgment upholding the test. Douglas J.
held that the States have broad powers to set voter qualifications; that literacy "has some relation to standards
designed to promote intelligent use of the ballot" (p. 51); and that the Court "cannot say" the policy is not an
allowable one (p. 53). The opinion reserves two routes of attack it did not reach: a test "fair on its face" used
"to perpetuate that discrimination which the Fifteenth Amendment was designed to uproot", of which "No such influence
is charged here" (p. 53), and a test unconstitutional on its face, as in *Davis v. Schnell* (p. 53). The decision
date and the page range come from CAP's `CasesMetadata.json` for 360 U.S. (saved in the same folder).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 51: "The ability to read and write likewise has some relation to standards designed to promote intelligent use
  of the ballot. Literacy and illiteracy are neutral on race, creed, color, and sex, as reports around the world show."
- pp. 51-52: "Literacy and intelligence are obviously not synonymous. Illiterate people may be intelligent voters.
  Yet in our society where newspapers, periodicals, books, and other printed matter canvass and debate campaign
  issues, a State might conclude that only those who are literate should exercise the franchise." (The CAP OCR
  carries stray punctuation, "the'ballot", "periodicals;", which I have normalised here.)
- pp. 52-53: "We do not sit in judgment on the wisdom of that policy. We cannot say, however, that it is not an
  allowable one measured by constitutional standards."
- p. 53: "Of course a literacy test, fair on its face, may be employed to perpetuate that discrimination which the
  Fifteenth Amendment was designed to uproot. No such influence is charged here."
- p. 54: the North Carolina requirement "seems to us to be one fair way of determining whether a person is literate,
  not a calculated scheme to lay springes for the citizen. Certainly we cannot condemn it on its face as a device
  unrelated to the desire of North Carolina to raise the standards for people of all races who cast the ballot.
  Affirmed."
- p. 50: the Court noted but did not reach the claim that voters grandfathered before 1 December 1908 might still be
  voting without a test, which "would be analogous to the problem posed in the classic case of *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*".

## Critical notes from the literature
- *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301, 333 (1966) (`berthon_ethics_course/katzenbach1966south.md`) reaffirms
  *Lassiter* on the point that literacy tests are not in themselves unconstitutional, and then upholds their
  suspension because in the covered States they had been "instituted with the purpose of disenfranchising Negroes",
  framed to that end and administered discriminatorily (pp. 333-334).
- *Gaston County v. United States*, 395 U.S. 285 (1969) (`berthon_ethics_course/gaston1969gaston.md`) rests on the
  Voting Rights Act's §4(a) and on the County's "separate and inferior schools", not on any constitutional defect in a
  literacy qualification as such.
- The nationwide ban came by statute (the 1970 Amendments, upheld in *Oregon v. Mitchell*), not by overruling
  *Lassiter*.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (the gate was a failure "by the era's own lights"): **narrower**. The judiciary of the era held, unanimously and
  only six years before the Voting Rights Act, that a literacy qualification was a permissible policy. The verdicts
  the paper leans on (*Katzenbach*, *Gaston*) condemned purpose, administration and state-made illiteracy, not the gate
  as a gate. The draft (ll. 252-259) should state the failure as discriminatory design and administration of a test
  the courts otherwise allowed.
- A2 (a gate held by the few; H01): **holds** as context. *Lassiter* shows what the registrars' discretion operated
  within: a facially neutral qualification the Court would not condemn.
