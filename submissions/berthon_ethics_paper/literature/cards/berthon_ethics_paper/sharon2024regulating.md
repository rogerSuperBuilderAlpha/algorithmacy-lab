## Summary
Drawing on Walzer's *Spheres of Justice*, the authors read Big Tech's move into health, education and agriculture
as "sphere transgressions": "conversions of advantages based on digital expertise into advantages and dominance
in other spheres of society" (p. 2652). Beyond privacy (§2.1, "the tip of the iceberg") they name three risks:
non-equitable returns (§2.2), reshaping of critical sectors (§2.3), new dependencies (§2.4). Section 3 then tests
each risk against the EU instruments (GDPR, AI Act, DSA, DMA, DGA, Data Act, EHDS) and finds that "none of them
properly address the risks we identify in Section 2" (p. 2652). Open access (CC BY-NC-ND). Printed pages = PDF
page plus 2648.

## Key facts it relies on
- Cross-test structure: §3.2 privacy, §3.3 non-equitable returns, §3.4 reshaping, §3.5 new dependencies; each
  subsection takes one risk and runs every instrument against it in prose (pp. 2659-2662). There is no table.
- "The following sections discuss whether this EU digital regulatory strategy is able to adequately address each of
  the various risks raised by sphere transgressions." (p. 2658)
- Privacy focus as cover: "the focus on privacy and data protection can actually act as a smokescreen to other
  risks" (p. 2654).
- Mechanism of the mismatch: the strategy's two orientations, fundamental rights through data protection and fair
  markets, each miss sphere transgressions (pp. 2662-2663).
- Concession: "our aim is not to construct a facile critique of the EU's digital regulatory strategy. We acknowledge
  that these instruments address many relevant issues and achieve many positive things." (p. 2663)
- Domination vocabulary: when advantages in one sphere translate into another, "there is a risk of domination of
  some members of society over others" (p. 2653).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The examples are drawn mostly from the authors' own health-sector research (p. 2652); the cross-test is
  qualitative and the authors do not claim completeness ("Not to mention that there are of course other angles of
  critique", n. 8).
- The AI Act, DA and EHDS were still proposals when written (p. 2658), so the instrument readings are of drafts.

## Where the argument uses it
- Outline, present case ("Lay it out as a failure × move grid (Sharon and Gellert's risk × instrument
  cross-test)"): **narrower**. The method is a risk × instrument cross-test, but they write it as one subsection per
  risk, not as a grid. A grid in the paper would be an addition, not a copy.
- A6: the cross-test is a model for testing each named property (who keeps the record, who holds the brake) against
  each move in turn rather than by overall resemblance.
- A1/A7: they ground the critique in Walzer's spheres (a society's shared meanings of goods), the same author the
  internal-critique frame rests on. That is a second published use of Walzer against tech firms.
