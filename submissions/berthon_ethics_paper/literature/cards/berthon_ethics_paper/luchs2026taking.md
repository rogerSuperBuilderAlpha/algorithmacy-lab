## Summary
Luchs (Groningen; open access, CC BY) reads Google Cloud's claim to "democratize AI" against three historical
moments of the same claim in computing: time-sharing and the "computer utility" (1960s), abstraction in
programming around the software crisis, and the platform of the 2000s. Each moment shows a "duality": access
widened, and power centralised in whoever provides the resource. She then runs a critical discourse analysis
(CDA) of Google Cloud documents (blog, "AI simplified" videos, Vertex AI docs, product listings), "structured
around the themes identified in the media-genealogical analysis" (p. 3), and closes by drawing alternatives from
the same history (Community Memory's "people's utility"; free software and open data as commons). Page numbers
below are the printed ones (PDF page minus one).

**Measured section shares (my count, body text only).** I counted words by section in the pdftotext extraction,
excluding the abstract, running heads, licence and contact lines, figure captions, notes and references.

within about 3 points but overstate the genealogy and understate the present case and the conclusion.

## Key facts it relies on
- Opening move: Fei-Fei Li's 2017 Google Cloud Next keynote; Google's mission to "democratize AI" by turning it
  from "a precious resource into something that can benefit everyone" (pp. 1-2).
- "The attempt to 'democratize' computing is by no means new." (p. 2)
- Method: media genealogy (Apprich and Bachmann 2017) identifies "the mechanisms and processes that have led to
  the development of power and truth structures" and shows developments "are historically contingent, thereby
  bringing alternatives to the fore" (p. 2).
- Thesis: "each of these moments of 'democratization' is marked by a certain duality" (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
- Each genealogical subsection ends in a lesson posed as a question, e.g. "Who provides the technology, under which
  interests, and what kind of access is granted?" (p. 5).
- The CDA "is structured around the themes identified in the media-genealogical analysis" (p. 3); the present
  section opens by naming the three themes (p. 8).
- Factual slip: "the increased marketing and proliferation of generative AI applications since the release of
  ChatGPT in 2018" (p. 13). ChatGPT was released by OpenAI on 30 November 2022.
- Dating of the software crisis: "officially declared in 1968" (p. 6); the conclusion places abstraction "during the
  software crisis in the 1970s" (p. 14). A crisis declared in 1968 can continue in the 1970s; this is not an
  inconsistency.
- Close: "the media-genealogical analysis challenges this rhetoric" (p. 14); alternatives are Community Memory's
  "people's utility" and digital commons with "co-determination of their design" (p. 14).
- No normative framework, no concessions section, no objections section and no duties; the alternatives are
  two paragraphs (p. 14).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The ChatGPT date is wrong (p. 13): ChatGPT launched on 30 November 2022 (OpenAI's own release announcement; the
  date is uncontested). If the paper cites Luchs for anything near that sentence, it should not repeat the year.
- The article history reads "Received 31 August 2025; Accepted 22 September 2026", and a note says the article
  "has been corrected with minor changes" (p. 1). I found no published response to it (it is four weeks old).
- Luchs's genealogy uses secondary histories of computing (Campbell-Kelly et al., Chun, Hu, Kennedy) plus a few
  primary quotations (McCarthy 1962, Baran 1967); it is not archival work. The paper's own record section is
  denser in primary sources, so Luchs is a model of shape, not of evidential standard.
