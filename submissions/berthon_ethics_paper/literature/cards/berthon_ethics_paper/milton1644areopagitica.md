## Summary
Upgrade of the writer-grounded card, which rested on the modernised, unpaginated Project Gutenberg text. This card reads Arber's 1868 reprint, which keeps the 1644 spelling and gives page numbers, and dates the tract "[24 November] 1644" on its title page. Milton asks Parliament to revoke the Order of 14 June 1643 that no book be printed unless first licensed. He leaves untouched the Order's clauses that preserve "every mans Copy" and provide for the poor (p. 34). He calls licensing a monopoly of knowledge "like our broad cloath, and our wooll packs" (p. 58), suspects that the Order owed its passage to "the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of book-selling" (p. 79), and warns that a magistrate is misinformed the sooner "if liberty of Printing be reduc't into the power of a few" (p. 79). He excludes "Popery, and open superstition" from toleration (p. 76). The OCR renders long s as "f"; I normalise "f" to "s" where the long s is meant and change nothing else.

## Key facts it relies on
- The Order he attacks: "That no Book, pamphlet, or paper shall be henceforth Printed, unlesse the same be first approv'd and licenc't by such, or at least one of such as shall be thereto appointed" (p. 34; italic in the original; OCR "ns", "JJiall be heficeforth", "befirjl appi'ov'd and lie end t" repaired against the context and the Gutenberg text).
- The proviso he accepts: "For that part which preserves justly every mans Copy to himselfe, or provides for the poor, I touch not, only wish they be not made pretenses to abuse and persecute honest and painfull Men" (p. 34).
- "Truth and understanding are not such wares as to be monopoliz'd and traded in by tickets and statutes, and standards. We must not think to make a staple commodity of all the knowledge in the Land, to mark and licence it like our broad cloath, and our wooll packs." (p. 58).
- "it may be doubted there was in it the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of book-selling; who under pretence of the poor in their Company not to be defrauded, and the just retaining of each man his severall copy, which God forbid should be gainsaid, brought divers glosing colours to the House, which were indeed but colours, and serving to no end except it be to exercise a superiority over their neighbours" (p. 79; OCR "patejitees", "fiwnopolizers", "glofnig" repaired).
- "for what Magistrate may not be mis-inform'd, and much the sooner, if liberty of Printing be reduc't into the power of a few" (p. 79).
- The limit: "I mean not tolerated Popery, and open superstition, which as it extirpats all religions and civill supremacies, so it self should be extirpat" (p. 76).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Milton accepts copy-registration ("which God forbid should be gainsaid"); his target is licensing and the patentees' use of the Order, not the Stationers' Register as such. This is evidence for Johns's point that contemporaries separated licensing from privilege and registration (`johns2002how`, p. 119).
- His toleration excludes Catholic worship; he serves the paper as an objector to licensing and monopoly, not as a champion of open access.
- Arber's page numbers are those of the 1868 reprint, not of the 1644 quarto.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **holds** for licensing and the patentees' monopoly; the passages are now located by page in an original-spelling reprint.
- A2 (custody by an interested party; the gate held by the few): holds; "reduc't into the power of a few" is the contemporary's own statement of the gate.
- Refutation sought (the Register as author protection): **complicates**. Milton defends "every mans Copy" while attacking licensing; registration of copies was a protection he endorsed.
