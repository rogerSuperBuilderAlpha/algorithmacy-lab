## Summary
Deazley's commentary on the Licensing Act 1662 (13 & 14 Car. II c. 33; the page's full title gives "3 & 4 Car.II,
c.33", a slip) covers its passage, its renewals (1664, 1665, lapse 1679, renewal 1685) and its final expiry "in May
1695 when, with the close of the last session of William III's (1650-1702) second parliament, it finally expired and
pre-publication censorship of the English press ceased" (section 5). Section 6, "John Locke and the Lapse of the
Licensing Act", traces the Commons' decision: a committee's January 1695 proposal to renew the Act was rejected by the
House on 11 February 1695 "without division"; the Lords amended the Continuation Bill to restore it; the Commons'
committee under Edward Clarke prepared reasons against the amendment; on 18 April 1695 the Lords gave way. Deazley ties
the reasons to Locke's 1694 memorandum and judges that they "had little to do with freedom of speech and the liberty
of the press": they concerned restraint of trade, the Stationers' abuse of their position and their monopoly of the
"Classick Authors" (section 6). The page text was extracted to `commentary_uk_1662.txt` in the same folder for reading.

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (section 2): "In force between 1662 and 1679, and then again between 1685 and 1695, the Act represents the
  last occasion on which the censorship of the press was formally and strategically linked to the protection of the
  economic interests of the Stationers' Company."
- Section 5: "Thereafter the Act continued in force until May 1695 when, with the close of the last session of
  William III's (1650-1702) second parliament, it finally expired and pre-publication censorship of the English press
  ceased."
- Section 6: "The decision to allow the Licensing Act to lapse for the final time was taken by the Commons."
- Section 6: the renewal proposal "was however rejected by the House on 11 February 1695 without division"; the Lords
  re-included the Act; "the Commons appointed a committee to prepare a set of reasons objecting to the amendment";
  "The following day, 18 April 1695, the House of Lords agreed to allow the Bill to proceed without the clause
  restoring the 1662 Act."
- Section 6: "While the committee's report on the Licensing Act did acknowledge the unwelcome prospect of a 'great
  Oppression' re-emerging with the appointment of a new licensor, their arguments, like Locke's, had little to do with
  freedom of speech and the liberty of the press."
- Section 6: the committee "preferred instead to focus on the more practical and commercial implications of reviving
  the Act. These concerned the restraint to trade that the Act represented, the Company of Stationers' abuse of their
  position, and the monopoly they enjoyed over work by 'Classick Authors'."
- Section 6, citing Kenyon: it was the company's control over the trade "rather than any abstract love of freedom"
  that persuaded the Commons to abandon the legislation.
- Section 6: Locke's 1694 commentary attacked "the 'lazy, ignorant Company of Stationers'" and their books
  "scandalously ill printed both for letter paper and correctness" sold at "excessive rates".
- Section 7 cites Astbury, "The Renewal of the Licensing Act in 1693 and its Lapse in 1695", *The Library*, 5th ser.,
  33 (1978): 296-322, as the authority for the lapse (n. 46).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Deazley summarises Astbury and Kenyon; I did not open either (Astbury: `astbury1978renewal`, OUP paywall).
- His "pre-publication censorship ... ceased" refers to the licensing system; seditious libel prosecutions continued.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected to the press regime; draft ll. 220-224): **holds, narrower**. The Commons' reasons are
  a House's stated objections, but the owning literature reads them as trade and monopoly grievances, not as an
  objection to custody of what could be read. The draft's use (monopoly, the Company's self-dealing, the "People
  compelled to buy their Freedom of Trade") stays within that reading; it should not describe the lapse as a verdict on
  licensing as such.
- H11 (lapse date): **holds**: May 1695, by expiry after the Commons refused to revive.
