## Summary
Cedillo-Lazcano (UDLAP law school) asks whether the governance of generative AI is unprecedented or "a new
iteration of the endogenous self-structuration processes" that tied technical innovation to intellectual property
law (abstract, PDF p. 1). His historical case is the Stationers' Company, read as a guild whose ordinances acted as
"proto-IP Law" and as the model of self-regulation that later became statute (Statute of Anne, Pope v Curl). He
treats today's "code and licensing" as the new lex mercatoria (section 2.1) and proposes Creative-Commons-style
"pictographic" AI licences and copyright for complex prompts. He concludes that GenAI "calls for adaptive
governance rather than systemic disruption" (abstract). The paper is about copyright and creators; it never
mentions censorship, licensing as press control, or readers (a text search for "censor" returns nothing; "reader"
occurs only as an address to the paper's own reader).

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (PDF p. 1): "clashes between creative industries and emergent technologies have consistently been
  resolved through lex mercatoria before sovereign regulation crystallised."
- Aim: to "show how the private sector can contribute to the efforts that States want to put in place to face the
  imperfections that define the AI market" (PDF p. 3).
- The Stationers as the guild that "will illustrate the transition between selfstructuring efforts to a proper
  legislative act" (PDF p. 6; "selfstructuring" as extracted).
- On the Company after print: "the prerogatives of the authors and the frictions related to them were addressed
  through the ‘ordenaunces of this Compnye’; in other words, lex mercatoria" (PDF p. 7), citing Blagden (1960) for
  the December 1565 resolution.
- The Statute of 1710 "empowered individual authors to control their intellectual outputs through a new paradigm
  of licensing that emerged to challenge the model structured around the practices of the Stationers and
  recognised in the Licensing Act 1662" (PDF p. 7).
- New technologies foster "the emergence of new standard-setting organisations and guilds", moving "from a model
  of selfstructuration and self-regulation, to one centered on co-regulation", with ISO photography standards as
  the example (PDF p. 9).
- Conclusion: "if we change our way to see the creative process as the Stationers did centuries ago, and train new
  generations accordingly, new professions with their respective guilds will emerge" (PDF p. 22).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The reading of the Stationers is the producers' and authors' one (Blagden, Holdsworth). It omits the Company's
  role as the Crown's press censor and the 1586/1637 decrees, which N2 carded (`deazley2008commentary1586`,
  `starchamber1637decree`), and the Commons' 1695 objection (`commons1803journal`).
- Small open-access venue; secondary sources only for the history.

## Where the argument uses it
- **Gap search (N6 Part 2).** The nearest work found that draws the Stationers' Company forward to generative-AI
  governance. It does so for copyright and in the guild's favour; it does not address vendor or deployer
  governance of agents, readers, or the people acted on. **Narrows** any claim that the Stationers analogy has not
  been applied to AI.
- **A2/A3, counter-case (b).** The strongest published reading that the Stationers' self-regulation worked:
  guild custom resolved conflicts and seeded statute. It **complicates** "custody by an interested party" as a
  failure by internal standards, but only for the trade and authors; it says nothing that shows the institution
  served the person bound by the printed text.
- **A7.** It runs the bridge the other way: past guild practice licenses present self-regulation ("adaptive
  governance"). Same historical evidence, opposite verdict; the paper must say why its standard (contemporaries'
  objections, broken professions) and not Cedillo-Lazcano's (eventual codification) is the right one.
