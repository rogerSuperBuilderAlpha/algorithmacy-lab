## Summary
Nishimura and colleagues review 39 randomized trials (54 interventions, searched to September 2010) that tried to
raise research participants' tested understanding of consent information, extending Flory and Emanuel's 2004 review.
They group interventions as multimedia, enhanced consent forms, extended discussion, test/feedback, and
miscellaneous, and meta-analyse 22. Enhanced forms and extended discussion raised understanding; multimedia did not
reach significance; test/feedback trials were too few and methodologically weak to judge. No category worked
consistently, and the authors conclude that "there is still no substitute for a good conversation". They warn that
the findings "may not be applicable to illiterate or socially disadvantage groups". BMC's own site served a client
challenge to my request, so I read the Europe PMC XML of the same open-access article.

## Key facts it relies on
- Baseline (Background, citing an earlier review of 1961-2006): "only 54% of patients and research participants
  adequately understood the aim of a given study; 50% understood the process of randomization".
- Abstract results: enhanced consent form "significant increase (SMD 1.73, 95% CI, 0.99 to 2.47)"; extended
  discussion "significant increase (SMD 0.53, 95% CI, 0.21 to 0.84)"; multimedia "non-significant increase in
  understanding scores (SMD 0.30, 95% CI, -0.23 to 0.84)".
- Results section gives different pooled figures for two categories: extended discussion "SMD 1.03, 95% CI, 0.79 to
  1.26" and multimedia "SMD 0.32, 95% CI, -0.20 to 0.85". The abstract and the body disagree; I cannot tell which is
  right from the text.
- By count: significant improvement in "31% of multimedia interventions", "41% for enhanced consent form; 50% for
  extended discussion; 33% for test/feedback; and 29% for miscellaneous" (abstract).
- A read-aloud trial (Table 4, miscellaneous): Ford et al. 2008, "Standard paper document read aloud by study staff
  with family member or care-giver present", elderly patients with Parkinson's disease, real trial, n = 136,
  understanding 48% (control) vs 52% (intervention), p = 0.012, assessed at about one week.
- Discussion: "Still, even the most exquisitely-designed form, be it on paper or computer screen, should not be
  expected to suffice. Arguably there is still no substitute for a good conversation"; "No category of intervention
  managed to have consistent efficacy."
- Limits: the findings "may not be applicable to illiterate or socially disadvantage groups, given the limits of our
  search"; enhanced-form gains were larger in simulated than real settings.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Abstract/body inconsistency on the extended-discussion and multimedia SMDs (above). Tam et al. (2015, Discussion;
  `berthon_ethics_paper/tam2015participants.md`) cite this review only for the direction of effects.
- Heterogeneity was large (enhanced forms I² = 97%), controls varied, and most understanding tests were
  study-specific (Results; Limitations).
- Flory and Emanuel (2004) is cited here as the prior review; I read only its abstract (`flory2004interventions` in
  the facet bib).

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (failure: disclosure in place of understanding): **holds**. Even redesigned disclosure leaves understanding
  partial and inconsistent; the best results come from a person talking with the participant.
- Refutation sought (read-aloud with protective effect): **narrows the thesis slightly**. One trial of reading a
  form aloud with a family member present shows a small, significant gain (48% to 52%); reading aloud is not
  worthless, but the effect is small.
- B1 / D06 (check understanding): **holds**. The review's own preference for "rich conversations" and test/feedback
  points the duty at checking, not at better text.
