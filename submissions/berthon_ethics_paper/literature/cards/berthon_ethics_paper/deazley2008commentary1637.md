## Summary
Deazley's commentary on the 1637 decree. He calls it "almost universally acknowledged as representing the high point of the Company of Stationers' control and authority over the book trade", sets out its new provisions (caps on master printers, founders and presses; import controls; licensing by subject; compulsory entry in the Register; the author's and printer's names on every book; a deposit copy for Oxford), records that the Long Parliament abolished Star Chamber and High Commission in July 1641, and traces the decree's afterlife as the blueprint of the Licensing Act 1662, "which act would (intermittently) remain in force until its expiration in May 1695", and through that lapse to the Statute of Anne 1710.

## Key facts it relies on
- "The Star Chamber Decree of 1637 is almost universally acknowledged as representing the high point of the Company of Stationers' control and authority over the book trade throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries." (section 4).
- Blagden, quoted: "There is no doubt ... that the decree like the 1586 decree was promoted by the Company for the benefit of Stationers and obtained the sanction of the government because it promised more effective safeguards than those already in existence, against the printing and distribution of schismatical publications" (section 4).
- Loewenstein, quoted: "with two exceptions ... everything in the new decree served the monopolies of the London stationers" (section 4).
- Registration: "whereas prior to the decree the registration of printed works with the Stationers' Company was simply encouraged, now acquiring the stationers' licence, in addition to the licensor's authorisation, became compulsory" (section 4).
- Author's name: printers must set "the name or names of the author or authors, maker or makers of the same"; "With this ordinance enters the 'rhetoric of authorial responsibility' within the history of British publishing" (section 4).
- Practice: state censorship "transformed into monopolistic competition" (section 5, quoting a source in note 25).
- End: "in July 1641 the Long Parliament abolished both the Star Chamber and the High Commission"; the Licensing Act 1662 ran "until its expiration in May 1695" (section 5).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Secondary throughout; the quoted judgements are Blagden's and Loewenstein's, through Deazley.
- "Authorial responsibility" is liability for content, not authors' property; Deazley does not call it protection of authors.
