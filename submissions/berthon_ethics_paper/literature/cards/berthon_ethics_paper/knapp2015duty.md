## Summary
Knapp asks what American courts mean when they say there is a "duty to read" ("DTR"). The principle "is commonly
expressed in American jurisprudence in two ways: as a 'duty,' and as a 'presumption'" (p. 1085). It "is not a 'duty'
imposed by contract, but rather a statement about how parties" are treated (p. 1085, continuing on p. 1086). Part II
lists the defences that overcome it (interpretation, lack of assent, mistake, fraud or misrepresentation, other
doctrines, pp. 1089-1092). Part V tells judges not to call it a duty, not to call it a conclusive presumption, and not
to let it shield fraud; the rule "would better be denominated as 'a presumption of knowing assent'" (abstract, p.
1083). The copy read is the journal's typeset PDF as reposted on a law-firm site (running heads "HASTINGS LAW JOURNAL
[Vol. 66:1083"); the journal's own repository was not reached. Hastings Law Journal articles carry no DOI that I
could find.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 1083 (abstract): the Article "concludes by suggesting that the 'duty to read' rule would better be denominated
  as 'a presumption of knowing assent,' and asks what role that principle should play in present day contracts
  jurisprudence."
- p. 1085: "The notion that there is in general contract law a 'duty to read' ('DTR') persists in the decisions of
  American courts. That was certainly true in 1966, when Stewart Macaulay wrote his classic article on that
  subject." (n. 1: Macaulay, "Private Legislation and the Duty to Read", 19 Vand. L. Rev. 1051 (1966)).
- p. 1085: "The duty to read, although regarded as a part of contract law, is not a 'duty' imposed by contract, but
  rather a statement about how parties" (sentence continues on p. 1086).
- p. 1108: "This is not only technically incorrect, but it also encourages judges (and others as well) to moralize or
  be condescending to persons who do not read everything they sign. Nobody does that, and in fact nobody is expected
  to."
- p. 1109: "Nobody reads everything she signs; Nobody is able to read everything she signs; and Nobody wants her to
  read everything she signs. What drafters do want is to be able to treat her as if she had read everything."
- p. 1109: "There are countless court decisions that accept as a fact the proposition that a contracting party has
  not read what she signed, and go on to give that party some relief based on that fact."
- p. 1110: a modernised rule should rest "on the candid admission that in many cases it is necessary and appropriate
  as a matter of policy to hold a person to a contract to which she has manifested a form of assent without having
  understood or even read its terms."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Knapp writes about American law; the English rule in *L'Estrange* (`berthon_ethics_course/lestrange1934graucob.md`)
  is stated "in the absence of fraud, or ... misrepresentation" (p. 403), the same qualification Knapp's Part II lists.
- His remedy is a label and a set of judicial cautions, not a change of medium or a check of understanding; the
  draft's corrections (B1) go further than his.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2, H19 ("Courts developed a duty to read", draft l. 231; "*Upton*'s rule", l. 398): **narrower**. The literature
  that owns the concept treats the "duty" as a misnomer for a presumption of assent with standard defences; the draft
  may keep the phrase as courts use it but should not present it as an absolute obligation. Knapp's inventory of
  defences shows the burden of reading was never placed on the signer without exceptions.
- B1 (corrections): Knapp's "presumption of knowing assent" is a rebuttable allocation of risk, a weak form of the
  draft's "carry the risk" correction; it never asks anyone to check understanding.
