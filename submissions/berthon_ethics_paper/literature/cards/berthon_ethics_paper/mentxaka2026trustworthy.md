## Summary
Mentxaka and colleagues propose a "dual taxonomy" of AI's effects on democracy: seven risks (AIRD: subversion of
autonomy, unfairness, power asymmetries, authoritarianism, undermining public discourse, undermining democratic
principles, erosion of trust) and seven conditional contributions (AIPD: efficiency, accessibility, security, informed
citizenry, transparency and accountability, global dialogue, evidence-based policymaking). Each risk is mapped onto the
seven requirements of the EU High-Level Expert Group's 2019 *Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI* and onto governance
responses and responsible actors (Fig. 5; Table I). Two worked examples apply it: electoral misinformation and
AI-assisted public-service allocation (Tables II-III). The authors call the framework "conceptual" and say it "has not
yet been empirically validated" (p. 1). It is a synthesis of literature, not a study.

## Key facts it relies on
- "The framework is conceptual and has not yet been empirically validated" (abstract, p. 1).
- "These mappings support structured assessment; they do not establish the causal effectiveness of the proposed
  measures" (p. 2).
- Its paraphrase of the HLEG principles (not the guidelines' own words): fairness includes "allow people to object to
  automated decisions"; explainability includes "enabling users to challenge decisions"; Rq7 accountability includes
  "mechanisms for responsibility assignment, impact minimization, reporting, and redress" (p. 3).
- "However, explaining a model's output does not establish how an intervention would affect democratic outcomes"
  (p. 4).
- "prior work shows that opacity is not only technical, but also institutional, strategic, expert, and emergent", and
  opaque AI systems may "shield decisions from scrutiny" (p. 4).
- Citing Buhmann and Fieseler (2023): they "show how strategic, expert, and emergent opacity consolidate technocratic
  control and reduce citizens' ability to question AI-shaped decisions" (pp. 8-9; secondary for that claim).
- "Subversion of autonomy can be mitigated by integrating human expertise into models and allowing proper human
  supervision and control mechanisms over systems, so the requirement for human agency and oversight is met" (p. 11).
- "Providing explanations of AI outputs fosters transparency, enabling individuals to make informed choices while
  preserving their autonomy" (p. 11).
- "Erosion of trust can be minimized by certifying compliance with trustworthy AI requirements so accountability is
  met" (p. 12).
- Public-service evaluation "should compare processing time, erroneous denials, appeal outcomes, and distributional
  effects with the existing process" (p. 12).
- Fig. 5 (image, p. 13): every mapping takes the form "X can be palliated by [ _ ] so requirement Z is fulfilled".
- Table I (image, p. 13), "Responsible actors" column: regulators, platforms, providers, auditors, public agencies,
  courts, public institutions, oversight bodies, electoral authorities, certification bodies, civil society. The
  affected person is not listed as an actor.
- Table III (image, p. 14), public-service allocation: observed issue "bias, opacity, weak contestability, and unequal
  group effects"; governance responses include "explanations, appeals, outcome monitoring, and independent audits".
- Transparency (Rq4) is mapped to six of the seven risks (Table IV and text, p. 14); "These counts describe the
  authors' mapping, not the prevalence or severity of harms" (p. 14).
- Lesson 2: "Trust is not only about system reliability or transparency in isolation; it depends on power relations,
  perceived legitimacy, and the capacity for democratic oversight" (p. 15).
- "Critics warn that regulation may stifle innovation, create bureaucratic hurdles, or disproportionately burden
  smaller developers" (p. 16).

## Critical notes
This is our reading for the Nadella paper, not the authors' claims.

- **Explanation conceded, then relied on.** The authors grant that explanation does not establish effects and that
  opacity is institutional and strategic (p. 4), yet transparency is the requirement they assign most often (6 of 7,
  p. 14). Several mitigations hand explanations to the individual (p. 11), which is the disclosure model with the
  reading left to the person.
- **Trust is relational in Lesson 2 but not in the mapping.** Lesson 2 makes trust depend on power relations (p. 15);
  Fig. 5 never asks who holds a control. "Human agency and oversight" counts as mitigation (p. 11) without asking
  whose oversight. Oversight held by the operator is the insider-risk move.
- **Requirement met stands in for harm reduced.** Fig. 5's template treats meeting a requirement as mitigation, which
  the authors concede is not shown (p. 2). Certification (p. 12) leaves open who certifies and who may challenge the
  certificate (Lazar's authority objection).
- **The affected person never holds a control.** Table I's actors are institutions; the citizen is protected but holds
  no record, brake or standing. Credit: Table III lists appeals.
- **Scope.** Its subject is democracy and public institutions, not firms acting on customers. Carry the critique over as
  a structural point, not as a charge that it ignores retail relations.
- Co-author Coeckelbergh is a plausible BEQ referee; keep any critique precise and credit the authors' own caveats.
- Authors include European Commission JRC staff; the paper states its views are not the Commission's (p. 16).
