## Summary
I read the whole opinion; the earlier card stopped at p. 12, after the canon of construction. The case turned on a
strip of land at Thief River reserved to the Chippewa chief Moose Dung by article 9 of the 1863 Old Crossing treaty.
His son leased it in 1891 to the Meehans, lumbermen, for $25 a year; in 1894 he leased it again to Jones at $200 a
year, a lease the Interior Department approved at $400 a year, "upon condition ... that the annual rent should
be four hundred dollars" (pp. 6-8). The Court states the canon that treaties are read "in the
sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians" (pp. 1, 10-11), reviews a line of reservation
cases (pp. 12-22), and holds that article 9 granted an alienable fee (p. 23). It then says it need not decide
whether the negotiation journal and the interpreter's testimony were admissible, because the treaty's own terms
settle the point (pp. 23-24). Inheritance followed Chippewa custom, proved by the half-Chippewa interpreter
Morrison and by the son (pp. 25, 29-31). The Court discounted an 1895 affidavit signed by the son's mark because he
treated it as "mere matter of form" (p. 31). The Meehans' 1891 lease stood (p. 32).

## Key facts it relies on
- Syllabus: a treaty "must be construed, not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers, but
  in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians" (p. 1).
- Record of the signing: all Indian signatures "were by marks only" (p. 4). The negotiation journal records that
  Moose Dung "stood for an hour weighing and deliberating on every separate provision of this treaty, asking for
  this explanation and that modification" before he "touched the pen" (p. 5).
- The 1891 lease was acknowledged "before a notary public, after its contents had been fully explained to him
  through an interpreter" (pp. 6-7).
- The canon's premise: the United States negotiates "assisted by an interpreter employed by themselves", and the
  Indians' "only knowledge of the terms in which the treaty is framed is that imparted to them by the interpreter
  employed by the United States" (pp. 10-11).
- McLean J. in Worcester, quoted: "How the words of the treaty were understood by this unlettered people, rather
  than their critical meaning, should form the rule of construction" (p. 12).
- Holding: article 9 "was intended by the United States, and was understood by the Indians, and took effect, as a
  present grant" of an alienable fee (p. 23).
- The Court did not rest on the evidence of understanding: "it is unnecessary to consider the competency of the
  extrinsic evidence, offered by the plaintiffs, of what took place between the representatives of the parties at
  the negotiations" (pp. 23-24).
- The 1895 affidavit signed by mark, procured by the Indian agent, "was evidently considered by him as mere matter
  of form with which he was obliged to comply in order to get any part of the rent" (p. 31).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The canon moves the risk of the text to the drafter after the fact; it is a rule of construction, not a check
  at signing. Here the Court construed the text and declined to rest on the record of what the Indians understood
  (pp. 23-24).
- The beneficiaries of the canon in this case were white lessees (the Meehans), and the effect was to defeat a
  later lease that the Interior Department approved at $400 a year (made at $200), sixteen times the Meehans' $25
(pp. 6-7, 32). The canon protected
  the Indian's power to alienate, and the party who gained was the earlier buyer.
- The premise is paternalist ("a weak and dependent people", p. 11).

## Where the argument uses it
- B1 (corrections share a property; H23): **narrower**. The case does move risk to the drafter, which fits B1's
  "moved risk". It does not check understanding: the Court declined to decide on the negotiation evidence. The one
  place it weighs a signer's understanding is to discount a document signed by mark as a formality (p. 31).
- A2 (record over voice; H07): **complicates**. The journal of the treaty council, kept by the United States side,
  is the record of Moose Dung's deliberation (p. 5); the Court admitted the voice through the drafter's own record.
