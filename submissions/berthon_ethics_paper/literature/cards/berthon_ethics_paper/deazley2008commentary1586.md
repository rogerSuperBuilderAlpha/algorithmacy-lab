## Summary
A legal historian's commentary on the Star Chamber decree of 23 June 1586 ("The newe Decrees of the Starre Chamber for Orders in Printinge", Arber vol. 2 p. 807). Deazley names two impulses: Elizabeth's concern with religious controversy, and unrest in the trade at royal printing privileges that put the lucrative titles in a few hands. He quotes, from Arber vols. 1-2, the unprivileged printers' complaints of 1577 and 1582, John Wolfe's claim that "it was lawful for all men to print all lawfull bookes", Robert Bourne's answer of 1586 that patents left "almost no liberty lefte for printinge but for ballettes and toyes", and the patentees' reply that without privileges "no bookes at all shoulde be printed". He concludes that the decree mainly cemented the Company's control: it registered presses, confined them to London and the universities, froze new presses until the Archbishop and Bishop of London judged the number fit, extended Company search to presses, put licensing in the hands of the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London, and made breach of the Company's own ordinances a Star Chamber matter. The contemporary voices are quoted from Arber; they are second-hand here.

## Key facts it relies on
- The 1577 complaint: the privileges "will be the overthrowe of the Printers and Stationers within this Cittie ... [b]esides their wyves Children Apprentices and families, and thereby th[e] excessive prices of bookes preiudiciall to the state of the whole Realme besides the false printinge of the same" (section 4, quoting Arber 1: 111).
- Scale: "In 1577 for example, a time when there were close to 200 printers within the city of London, control of the most commercially lucrative texts, as well as particular classes of books, lay in the hands of no more than ten stationers" (section 4).
- Wolfe was imprisoned in 1582 for setting himself "against the Quenes hole prerogative touching printing", asserting that "it was lawful for all men to print all lawfull bookes what commandement souever her Maiestie gaue to ye contrary" (section 4, quoting Arber 2: 774, 781).
- Bourne, 3 February 1586: "there is almost no liberty lefte for printinge but for ballettes and toyes and such like" (section 4, quoting Arber 2: 802), and a call for trial of the patents "according to the ordinary course and rules of the common Lawes" (Arber 2: 803).
- The patentees' defence, 4 May 1586: without privileges "no bookes at all shoulde be printed ... for comonlie the first printer is at charge for the Authors paynes" and "if every man maie print, that is so disposed, it may be a meanes, that heresies, treasons, and seditious Libelles shall be too often dispersed, whereas if onlie knowne men doo prynte this inconvenience is avoided" (section 5, quoting Arber 2: 804-805).
- Deazley's reading: "Here, for the first time, the stationers hold themselves out as the guardians of the press" (section 5).
- The decree: no press to be used "tyll the excessiue multitude of Prynters hauinge presses already sett vp, be abated ... as the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London for the tyme being shall therevpon thinck requisite" (section 5, ordinance 3); all works "first seen and pervsed by the Archbishop of Canterbury and [the] Bishop of London" (ordinance 4); and the licensing clause now reached printing "contrary to any allowed ordynaunce sett Downe for the good governaunce of the Company of Staconers" (ordinance 4).
- Evidence the system leaked: "in 1585-86, only 30 works were entered on the Register, by comparison with the yearly average of 147 entries between 1576 and 1640" (note 7, citing Greg 1944), and Clegg's figure that in 1585 "only 13 percent" of entered works were marked as officially licensed (note 7).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Secondary for every period quotation: I did not open Arber vol. 2, which is lending-only on archive.org (see the facet log). Quote these voices in the paper only after a library check of Arber vol. 2 pp. 770-807.
- Deazley notes Feather's view that trade monopoly was "the singular reason" for the decree and disagrees (note 12): religion also drove it.
- The patentees' first-printer argument is the period form of Johns's "security" defence of privilege (`johns2002how`, p. 112).

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **holds** for the press monopoly, at secondary depth: printers of 1577-1586 named the privileges a wrong to themselves, their families and the realm, and one challenged their legality at common law.
- A2 (custody by an interested party): holds; the Company sought and shaped the decree ("the efforts of the dominant members of the Stationers' Company to influence the substance of the decree", abstract) and gained state sanction for its own ordinances.
- Refutation sought: the patentees' defence (privilege pays for authors' work and costly books) is the strongest contemporary case that the system served books; it serves first printers, not readers, and the paper should state it.
