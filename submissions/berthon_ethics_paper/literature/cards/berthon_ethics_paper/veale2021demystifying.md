## Summary
Crossref confirms the article (CRi 22(4): 97-112, 2021). The arXiv copy (CC-BY) carries the CRi pagination and running heads ("CRi 4/2021 ... 105"), so page locators are the journal's. The authors analysed the Commission's April 2021 proposal; several provisions changed before enactment (complaints now in Art 85, explanation in Art 86). On standards they wrote that CEN and CENELEC are "Arguably the most important actors in the Draft AI Act" though "neither are mentioned in the text" (p. 104), that harmonised standards "are not as voluntary as the Commission argues", and that "standardisation is arguably where the real rule-making in the Draft AI Act will occur" (p. 105). Consumer organisations "struggle to participate", affected communities "will have absolutely no experience of standardisation", and the European Parliament "has no binding veto over harmonised standards" (p. 105). On remedies, under the draft "individuals affected by AI systems have no right to complain to an MSA" and the draft "creates no legal right to sue a provider or user" (p. 111).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 104: "Arguably the most important actors in the Draft AI Act are the double-act of CEN (European Committee for Standardisation) and CENELEC (European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardisation). This may surprise readers; neither are mentioned in the text."
- p. 105: "In theory, providers do not have to follow such harmonised standards. ... Harmonised standards are both cheaper for producers, and a safer bet. They are not as voluntary as the Commission argues."
- p. 105: "Consequently, standardisation is arguably where the real rule-making in the Draft AI Act will occur."
- p. 105: "Standards are not free – copyright is owned by national standards bodies, and each usually costs a few hundred Euros to purchase."
- p. 105: "The vast majority will have absolutely no experience of standardisation, and may lack EU-level representation. Moreover, the European Parliament has no binding veto over harmonised standards mandated by the Commission."
- p. 111: "individuals affected by AI systems have no right to complain to an MSA in the same way that, for example, they have a right to lodge a complaint to and seek a judicial remedy against a supervisory authority under data protection law. The Draft AI Act creates no legal right to sue a provider or user for failures under the Draft AI Act".
- p. 111: "As only those with obligations under the Draft AI Act can challenge regulators' decisions, rather than those whose fundamental rights deployed AI systems affect, the Draft AI Act lacks a bottom-up force to hold regulators to account for weak enforcement."

## Critical notes from the literature
- The enacted Act (`eu2024aiact`) answers part of the p. 111 critique with Art 85 (complaint) and Art 86 (explanation); it still creates no right to sue under the Act, and complaints are surveillance input. The p. 105 price point is overtaken for OJ-cited standards by `cjeu2024publicresource` (2024).
