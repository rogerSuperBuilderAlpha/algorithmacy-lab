## Summary
The Record Commission's text of the Act of 1542-3 "for thadvauncement of true Religion", read in full (pp. 894-897; OCR line numbers 127611-128115 of the local file). The Act abolishes Tyndale's translations and religious books contrary to the doctrine set forth since 1540; licenses religious printing "by the King and his Clergye"; confines open reading and teaching of Scripture in church to those appointed by the King or an Ordinary; and bars women, artificers, apprentices, journeymen, serving men "of the degrees of yeomen or undre", husbandmen and labourers from reading the English Bible or New Testament, privately or openly, on pain of a month's imprisonment. Noblemen and gentlemen householders may read to their households; merchant householders, and all others outside the barred ranks, to themselves; noblewomen and gentlewomen to themselves alone. Its own preamble to the reading clause records that the King had "set foorthe the Byble ... to be reade by his loving Subject". The bar is open-ended at the King's pleasure. Page markers in the OCR are the printed page numbers; side-notes are interleaved.

## Key facts it relies on
- Ground of the whole Act: subjects "pretending to be learned" have spread error "by printed bokes printed balades playes rymes songes", whence "diversite of opinions sayengs variaunces argumentes tumultes and scismes" (p. 894; OCR "sayengf variaunc€ argument tumult€").
- Licensed printing: printers may print books of religion the King allows, with the subscription "by the King and his Clergye" and the printer's name, dwelling, day and year (p. 895).
- Open reading: no one "shall take upon him or them to reade preache or teache openlie to other in any churche or open assemblye ... the Byble or any parte of Scripture in Englishe ... oonles he be so appointed therunto by the King Majestie or by any Ordinarye" (p. 895).
- The professed purpose, in the Act's own words: the King "hathe heretofore caused to be set foorthe the Byble and New Testament in the Englishe tongue to be reade by his loving Subject, to thintent that they might therbye the better knowe theyre duetie to Almightie God and to his Majestie" (p. 896; OCR "Subject" for "Subjectes").
- Stated ground of the bar: "a greate multitude of his saide subject, moste spaallie of the lower sorte have so abused the same, that they have therbye growen and increased in divers naughtie and erronyous opynions" (p. 896; OCR abbreviations kept).
- The bar: "no woomen nor artificers prentises journeymen serving men of the degrees of yeomen or undre, husbandemen nor laborers shall reade ... the Byble or Newe Testament in Englishe, to himselfe or (") any other pryvatelie or openiie, upon paine of oone monethes imprysonement" (p. 896; OCR "openiie").
- The release is the King's alone: "oonelesse the Kinges Majestie ... pceyving such reformacion and amendement in theyre lyeves and behavour ... shall of his clemencye thinke good, otherwyse to enlardge and give libertie for the reading of the same" (p. 896; OCR "King¢", "reformacén").
- Exceptions: nobleman and gentleman householders may "reade or cause to be red ... to his owne famylie"; merchant householders "reade to himself privatelye"; "everye noble wooman and gentlewooman maie reade to themselves alone and not to others" (p. 896).
- Dispute barred: no person "other thenne be above lymited" may "openlye to dispute or argue, to debate discusse or expounde Holye Scripture" (p. 896).
- Informers: half of the money penalties to whoever "will detecte and sue" (p. 896).
- Procedural protections for the accused: a person complained on "shalbe admytted to purge and trye his or theyre innocencye by other witnes, as manye or moo in noombre" (p. 897); prosecution within one year (p. 897); no penalty for offending new royal doctrine until "oone moneth be paste ... next after suche doctryne shalbe put in printe" (p. 897).
- The King "maie at any tyme hereafter at his Hieghnes libertie and pleasure chaunge and altre this present Acte" (p. 897; OCR "pnt").
- The side-note at the head of the Act points to its repeal: "See Stat. 1 Edw. VI. c.12. § 2." (p. 893, foot).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Facet B (`nadella/sources/bec_facet_B_print_1450_1800.md`, failure 2) quoted the reading clauses and found no contemporary objector. This card adds the Act's own record of the earlier professed purpose (reading "by his loving Subject"), the open-ended royal release, the bar on disputing Scripture, and the procedural protections.
- The procedural clauses (purgation by witnesses, a one-year limit, a month's notice of new doctrine) protect the accused, not the reader. Foxe (`foxe1838acts`, vol. 5 p. 527) counts the purgation clause as a mitigation of the Six Articles regime. Neither clause widens access to the text.
- The Act's reading clause bars reading by rank and sex; it says nothing on income. The 1546 *Supplication of the Poore Commons* (`furnivall1871four`, p. 64) describes "a lawe" barring the Scripture from any house "onlesse he maye spend x. pound by yere"; that figure is not in this text.
- No count of persons affected is given.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (second moment, "The press held by its licensers"): holds. A dated statute withdraws vernacular reading from the lower ranks and women, and leaves its restoration to the King's "clemencye".
- A3 (a professed standard broken): holds, narrower than "contemporaries objected". The Act itself records the professed purpose it then withdraws for the "lower sorte". The contemporary objection is in `furnivall1871four`.
- A2 (custody; gate held by the few): holds. Open reading needs appointment by King or Ordinary; the release from the bar is the King's alone.
- Refutation sought ("any reading of 1543 as protective"): the Act protects the accused procedurally, and states order and right doctrine as its aims; nothing in it serves the barred reader.
