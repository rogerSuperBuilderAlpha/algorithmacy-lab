## Summary
Two depositors left bags at a railway cloak-room, paid 2d., and took tickets marked "See back"; the back limited the
company's liability to £10, and a placard said the same. Both swore they had not read the ticket or seen the notice
and took the ticket for a receipt. Juries found for them on two questions: did the plaintiff read or know the
condition, and was he under any obligation to read it (pp. 416-418). The Court of Appeal ordered new trials.
Mellish L.J. (with Baggallay L.J.) held the second question wrong in form: the depositor was under no obligation to
read, and the right question was whether the company "did what was reasonably sufficient to give the plaintiff
notice of the condition" (pp. 423-424). In framing that test he let the company assume a reader of English and
measured notice against "people in general", not the particular depositor (p. 423). Bramwell L.J. would have entered
judgment for the company outright: handing over a printed paper is equivalent to saying "Read that" (pp. 427-428).
Baggallay L.J. said that on the facts he would himself have found for the plaintiffs and put the onus on the company
(pp. 425-426). The text is archive.org OCR of the Law Reports; I checked pp. 423 and 427 against the page images
(leaves 451 and 455).

## Key facts it relies on
- Signed writing: "in the absence of fraud, it is wholly immaterial that he has not read the agreement and does not
  know its contents" (Mellish L.J., p. 421).
- Bill of lading: a shipper ignorant of what a bill of lading is "must bear the consequences of his own exceptional
  ignorance, it being plainly impossible that business could be carried on if every person who delivers a bill of
  lading had to stop to explain what a bill of lading was" (p. 422).
- The reader the company may presume: "I think they are entitled to assume that he can read, and that he
  understands the English language" (p. 423).
- The standard is the general public: "a particular plaintiff ought not to be in a better position than other
  persons on account of his exceptional ignorance or stupidity or carelessness" (p. 423).
- No duty to read: "The plaintiff was certainly under no obligation to read the ticket" (p. 423); the question is
  "whether the railway company did what was reasonably sufficient to give the plaintiff notice of the condition"
  (p. 424).
- Baggallay L.J.: "the onus of proof is, however, upon the company in respect of these matters" (p. 425); on the
  evidence he "should ... have decided in favour of the plaintiffs in both cases" (p. 426).
- Bramwell L.J.: "The putting of it into their hands was equivalent to saying, “Read that.”" (p. 427); "The truth
  is, people are content to take these things on trust" (p. 428); there is "an implied understanding that there is
  no condition unreasonable to the knowledge of the party tendering the document and not insisting on its being
  read" (p. 428).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Parker is the source of the "reasonable notice" rule for unsigned documents, and it sits beside L'Estrange
  (signed documents, 1934): the signer is bound regardless (p. 421 already says so in dictum).
- The case cuts two ways for the argument. Mellish L.J. denies a duty to read and asks whether the company gave
  reasonable notice (pp. 423-424); Baggallay L.J. puts the onus on the company (p. 425). It also defines adequate notice by what "people in general" would infer and lets the issuer presume literacy in
  English, which leaves the person who cannot read outside the test.
- I read the case in the 1877 report only; later treatment (e.g. Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, 1971) is not on this
  card.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (failure: the burden of reading on the person bound; H19): **narrower**. Parker shifts the question from the
  reader's diligence to the issuer's notice, but by presuming an English reader it keeps the burden on anyone who
  is not one (p. 423). Bramwell's "Read that" (p. 427) is the strongest contemporary statement of the burden view.
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **does not hold on this source**. Two juries and Baggallay L.J. would have found for
  the depositors (pp. 417, 426), but the verdicts and Baggallay's view show the facts were contested, not that anyone
  objected to the rule; Baggallay agreed to a new trial.
- H24 (four stages): Parker sits at stage two (notice), not stage three: nobody checks what the depositor understood.
