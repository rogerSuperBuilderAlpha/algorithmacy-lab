## Summary
A legal-realist critique of Nissenbaum's contextual integrity (CI). If CI were adopted as a legal rule of decision,
neutral judges applying it would, over the long run, favour the "more sophisticated, well-funded, repeat player"
(abstract, p. 1), so CI "would undermine the very values it was intended to protect" (p. 1). The paper states CI
fairly (§1), adopts a neutral-judge stance (§2), then gives eight mechanism subsections (§3.1-3.8), one of them a
worked case (§3.5, Google search treated as a public library), and ends with three refinements that might save
CI at the cost of its identity (§4). About 11,500 words including heavy footnotes; BEQ open access, CC BY.

## Key facts it relies on
- Self-description as internal critique: "The goal of this article is to advance an internal critique of
  contextual integrity, not to advocate for any alternative theory." (p. 4) The internal standard is CI's own
  aim (protecting privacy), not an outside theory.
- Concession at the opening of the argument: "Contextual integrity's adherents offer many persuasive examples of
  the privacy-protective prescriptions the theory can generate" (Street View faces, public records, Facebook ads);
  "These conclusions all accord with widely shared opinions among privacy scholars." (p. 7)
- Charitable stance: the exercise "stacks the legal realist deck in favor of contextual integrity by focusing on how
  neutral judges would decide cases" (p. 8); the article "assumes away any influence pro-corporate interests have
  on the judicial selection process" (p. 9).
- Short-term concession: "In the short term, a legal rule of contextual integrity may, as its proponents predict,
  favor David-the-consumer over Goliath" (p. 8).
- Worked case (§3.5 "Heads Google Wins, Tails You Lose", pp. 15-16): CI's proponents liken search to a public
  library to protect search history; Diamantis follows the analogy to the library card, where patrons must hand
  over a driver's licence, so "if Google is like a public library, then it would be well within its rights to
  require each of us to provide verifiable personal identification" (pp. 15-16); "Surely that is a result every
  friend of privacy wants to avoid." (p. 16)
- Mechanisms: value indeterminacy (§3.2); context indeterminacy (§3.3); complexity favours the resourced litigant
  (§3.4); gradual norm shift, "frog in hot water" (§3.6); "one-way ratchet" of errors (§3.7); "persistent
  particularism", no precedent (§3.8, framed as a reply to the "growing pains" objection).
- Closing concession: "For all I have said, contextual integrity may still be the best theory available." (p. 19)

## Critical notes from the literature
- Diamantis names earlier critics (Birnhack 2011; Rule 2019; Prégent 2025; McDonald and Forte 2020; Richards 2021)
  and says CI's architects "have yet to meaningfully [answer]" them (p. 3; a word is missing in the original).
- The concession is not one opening section: it is distributed (p. 7 good prescriptions; p. 8 short-term and
  neutral-judge assumptions; p. 19 may be the best theory). There is no separate objections section; §3.8 and §3.1
  carry objection replies inside the mechanism list.
- No historical record is used as evidence.

## Where the argument uses it
- Outline, "Standards of judgement" ("Diamantis's concession move"): **holds, narrower in form**. Diamantis concedes
  the target's good outputs before the critique and grants it the most favourable conditions (a neutral judge);
  he does not concede in a single "at the outset" paragraph. The paper's parallel would be: grant P9's own
  admission and assume the authorised person and the auditors act in good faith, then show the positions still
  favour the deployer.
- Outline, present case ("Diamantis's worked case"): **holds**. The move is to follow the target's own analogy or
  mechanism one step further, to a concrete outcome that turns against the weaker party (pp. 15-16).
- A7: Diamantis is a BEQ precedent for calling a critique "internal" when it measures a proposal by the values
  it professes (p. 4). That is the present-tense version of internal critique; it does not show that a past
  era's standards bind a present actor.
