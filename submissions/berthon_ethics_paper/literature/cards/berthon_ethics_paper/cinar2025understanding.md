## Summary
Abstract-level card; paraphrase only. Cinar (McGill Faculty of Law, doctoral candidate) reads English legal
responses to the printing press from the late fifteenth to the early eighteenth century, with legal history and
Braudel's longue durée. The abstract names four characteristics of technology regulation in the period: initial
support followed by increasing constraint; regulation driven by interactions among interest groups; the need for
disruptive externalities before corrective action; and the influence of economic power on the regulatory landscape.
It draws two insights: influence over technology regulation required both economic power and a direct stake in the
technology, and the involvement of diverse interest groups was needed to mitigate complications. A McGill news item
(19 Nov 2025, reprinted on trade sites; the McGill page now 404s) reports, at second hand, that Cinar compares this
with large technology firms shaping AI governance and that the paper concedes AI's scale, speed and complexity have
no direct historical parallel; I could not confirm either statement in the article.
