## Summary
Abstract-level card; paraphrase only. Mahardhika proposes the "Gutenberg Syndrome" as a falsifiable framework for
institutional responses to technologies that widen access to cognitive production. It arises when four conditions
coincide: a large fall in the cost of producing or circulating symbolic content; reduced dependence on established
epistemic intermediaries; displacement of their function or authority; and demands for renewed control framed as
safety, integrity or public protection. He distinguishes it from regulatory capture, professional closure, moral
panic and the regulatory moat. Printing is the historical case (wider access, and also propaganda, confessional
conflict and new censorship). Generative AI differs by automating production, transformation and personalisation,
not reproduction alone. Article 50 of the EU AI Act is a "mixed" contemporary case. Legitimate regulation should
pass four tests: harm proportionality, technological neutrality where feasible, institutional neutrality, and
responsibility attribution, with a rebuttable presumption of tool-neutral liability.

## Where the argument uses it
- **Gap search (N6 Part 2).** A 2026 work that draws the print-era institutional response forward to AI
  regulation. Its target is the displaced intermediaries' demand for control under a safety banner, and its test
  case is a transparency rule (AI Act art. 50), not vendor or deployer governance of agents or the person bound.
- **Counter-case (b), (f).** It reads print-era control as the incumbents' reaction, which **supports** A2's
  custody failure; but it turns the lesson against safety-framed control in general, which bears on P6's call for
  containment and standards. Its "tool-neutral liability" test is an independent form of P3's refusal to outsource
  responsibility.
