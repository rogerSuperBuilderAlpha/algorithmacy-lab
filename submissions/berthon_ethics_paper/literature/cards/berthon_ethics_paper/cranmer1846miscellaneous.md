## Summary
Cranmer's prologue to the Great Bible, in the Parker Society edition (ed. Cox, 1846), modernised spelling. The editor's note (p. 118) says the prologue "is not found in the edition of the bible, which was issued a. d. 1539", and describes the copy his text follows with the dates 1540 and 1541; the OCR of the two-column note is interleaved, so I take from it only that the prologue postdates 1539. Cranmer addresses two "sorts of people": those who refuse to read Scripture in the vernacular or discourage others, and those who misuse it by "inordinate reading, undiscreet speaking, contentious disputing" (p. 118). He concludes that Scripture is "to be read of all sorts and kinds of people, and in the vulgar tongue" (p. 122), and lists the readers by rank and sex: women, tenants, "mean men", artificers, husbandmen. Three years later 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1 barred several of the groups Cranmer had named: women below gentle rank, artificers, husbandmen. The same prologue also carries the bridle that the Act would make law: through Gregory Nazianzen it warns that "It is not fit ... for every man to dispute the high questions of divinity" (p. 122).

## Key facts it relies on
- The two sorts: "In the former sort be all they that refuse to read, or to hear read the scripture in the vulgar tongues; much worse they that also let or discourage the other from the reading or hearing thereof. In the latter sort be they, which by their inordinate reading, undiscreet speaking, contentious disputing, or otherwise, by their licentious living, slander and hinder the word of God" (p. 118).
- The readers named: "Here may all manner of persons, men, women, young, old, learned, unlearned, rich, poor, priests, laymen, lords, ladies, officers, tenants, and mean men, virgins, wives, widows, lawyers, merchants, artificers, husbandmen, and all manner of persons, of what estate or condition soever they be, may in this book learn all things what they ought to believe" (p. 121; OCR "W1Ves" for "wives").
- The conclusion: "it is convenient and good the scripture to be read of all sorts and kinds of people, and in the vulgar tongue" (p. 122), resting finally on royal authority: the king "hath approved with his royal assent the setting forth hereof" (p. 122).
- The bridle, quoting Nazianzen on talk of Scripture in "every market-place, every alehouse and tavern, every feast-house" and "It is not fit ... for every man to dispute the high questions of divinity" (p. 122).

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is an edition of 1846 in modern spelling, taken by the editor from a later edition than 1539 (editor's note, p. 118, interleaved OCR); I did not see the 1540 print.
- Cranmer's prologue is a professed standard of the Crown's own Bible, not an objection to 1543. It predates the Act. Cranmer remained archbishop through the Act's passage; I found no text in which he objected to it.
- The prologue's warning against disputation anticipates the Act's ban on disputing Scripture (`statutesrealm1817vol3`, p. 896); the distinction it draws is between reading and disputing, not between ranks.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (a professed standard broken): **holds**. The officially printed preface of 1540-1 names women, artificers, husbandmen and "mean men" as proper readers; the 1543 Act bars them. Pair with the Act's own preamble ("to be reade by his loving Subjectes", `statutesrealm1817vol3`, p. 896) and the 1546 petition (`furnivall1871four`).
- Complicates any claim that 1543 broke with a standard of open disputation: the same prologue restricts disputation.
