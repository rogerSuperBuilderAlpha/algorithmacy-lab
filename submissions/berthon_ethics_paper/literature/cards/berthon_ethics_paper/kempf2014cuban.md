## Summary
Kempf (UCLA) reviews the 1961 Cuban literacy campaign at its fiftieth anniversary as the "formal genesis of
contemporary Cuban national ideology". He reports, from secondary sources, that the campaign cut illiteracy from
almost 24% to 3.9% within a year, that it aimed at a first-grade reading level for all, and that it was followed by
a series of "battles" for higher grades ending with the Battle for the Ninth Grade (1980-1985). He stresses that the
primer and instructors' manual carried the revolution's politics ("Fidel is Our Leader" among 26 themes) and that
the campaign put young, often Black and female, urban volunteers into rural homes as teachers. He calls its success
and duration "unparalleled". The doi.org link resolves to the journal; I found no Crossref record for it.

## Key facts it relies on
- Scale and result: "In 1961, over 120,632 volunteer teachers, along with 147,788 trained and paid teachers
  participated in a nationwide literacy campaign which reduced the illiteracy rate from almost 24% to 3.9% (Huberman
  and Sweezy 1969, pp. 25–26)" (p. 3).
- "The success and duration of the literacy campaign were unparalleled, however, both before and since" (p. 4).
- Politics: "The instructor’s manual lists 26 political themes meant to inform the pedagogy and context of teacher
  literacy instruction" (p. 4), listed in n. 2 from "The Revolution" and "Fidel is Our Leader" onward.
- Follow-through: the campaign "was oriented toward standardized results across the island with the initial aim of
  a minimum first grade reading level for all Cubans"; "The final such literacy campaign (the Battle for the Ninth
  Grade) was waged, and ‘won’ between 1980–1985" (p. 7).
- "Whatever conscientization occurred through the campaigns was oriented to support the revolutionary project"
  (p. 7).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Kempf's figures are secondary (Huberman and Sweezy 1969). They reconcile with Bhola's (1984, p. 97, as recorded in facet V5:
  268,420 instructors in all: 120,632 popular instructors, 13,016 workers' brigades, 100,000 Conrado Benítez brigades and
  34,772 teachers; `berthon_ethics_course/bhola1984campaigning.md`): 120,632 + 147,788 = 268,420, and 13,016 + 100,000 +
  34,772 = 147,788. The 3.9% end rate also agrees. Only the labels differ: Kempf calls the brigades and teachers together
  (147,788) "trained and paid teachers", which Bhola's breakdown does not support.
- All figures trace to the Cuban government's own declarations. The 2015 GMR finds that the Cuban-designed successor
  model, Yo Sí Puedo, has had no independent evaluation (`berthon_ethics_paper/unesco2015education.md`, p. 147); it
  says nothing about the 1961 figures.
- Kempf is sympathetic but explicit about the ideological content.

## Where the argument uses it
- Refutation sought (a literacy campaign that met its promise): **narrows A2/A3**. By its own declared measure Cuba
  met a literacy promise within a year and followed it with decades of further "battles". Competence oversold and
  unmet is not a law of literacy campaigns; it is a choice about resourcing and follow-through.
- B1 / H25 (scaffolds served their sponsors): **holds**. The scaffold was owed and delivered at scale, and it carried
  the sponsor's politics (p. 4).
- The success rests on official figures that no independent evaluation has tested; the paper should say "declared"
  when it uses them.
