## Summary
In January 1998 Los Angeles County turned restaurant inspection scores into letter grades that restaurants had to
post at the entrance. Simon and colleagues compare foodborne-disease hospitalizations (California discharge data,
1993-2000) in the county with the rest of California and find a 13.1% drop in the first year after grading began,
sustained through 2000. They name confounds: a television exposé before the programme, more frequent inspections,
retrained inspectors and education for owners. The design is ecologic. I read the authors' deposited copy in the
University of Maryland repository (DRUM); Europe PMC records no DOI for the article.

## Key facts it relies on
- The device: "Numeric inspection scores were translated into letter grades (90–100 = A, 80–89 = B, 70–79 = C), and
  establishments were required to post their grade (or numeric score if below 70) within 5 feet of the point of
  entry" (p. 32).
- Result (abstract, p. 32): the programme "was associated with a 13.1 percent decrease (p < .01) in the number of
  foodborne-disease hospitalizations in Los Angeles County in the year following implementation of the program
  (1998). This decrease was sustained over the next two years (1999–2000)."
- Confounds (p. 35): "The program was implemented following a hidden-camera exposé of unsanitary restaurants by a
  local television news program"; grading "also was accompanied by an increase in the frequency of inspection of some
  restaurants, additional training of restaurant inspectors, and enhanced efforts in educating restaurant owners and
  staff".
- Design limit (p. 35): "it was ecologic in design and therefore could not assess restaurant exposures and disease
  transmission at the individual level".

## Critical notes from the literature
- Jin and Leslie (2003, QJE 118(2), 409-451; abstract only, SSRN copy blocked) report that the cards raised
  inspection scores, made demand sensitive to hygiene, and cut hospitalizations, and that the gain "is not fully
  explained by consumers substituting" between restaurants: the cards worked mainly by changing what restaurants did.
- OpenAlex lists later work on grading (e.g. "New Evidence on Information Disclosure through Restaurant Hygiene
  Grading", AEJ: Economic Policy, 2019, doi 10.1257/pol.20180230); I did not open it, so this card makes no claim
  about whether the 1998 effect held elsewhere.
- "hidden-camera" breaks across a line in the PDF; the hyphen is the authors'.

## Where the argument uses it
- Refutation sought (a disclosure regime with measured protective effect): **refutes a strong form** of "disclosure
  does not protect". Grading is a disclosure that cut hospitalizations.
- B1 (corrections share a property): **holds, and sharpens it**. The protective disclosure is one in which an
  independent inspector does the reading and the artefact carries a one-letter verdict at the door; the diner reads
  nothing. The work moved from the person to the artefact and to a party owed to the public, which is B1's property.
- A2 (disclosure in place of understanding): **narrower**. The failure is disclosure that leaves the reading to the
  person bound, not disclosure as such.
