## Summary
Lazar argues that opaque computational systems "are being used to govern" (p. 1), that governing power must meet
procedural legitimacy and proper authority, and that both require publicity: "reasonably competent members of the
governed community must be able to determine that they are being governed legitimately and with proper authority"
(p. 1). Explanations are therefore democratic duties owed to the political community, not only to decision
subjects (p. 5, n. 15, distinguishing his view from Vredenburgh's right to explanation). Power is "one-way
control" (p. 5); legitimacy has three components, ex ante limits, in medias res constraints and ex post
contestability (p. 11).

## Key facts it relies on
- "Power is one-way control: the ability to shape others' prospects, options, and (evaluative and doxastic)
  attitudes without their being able to do the same to you." (PDF p. 5)
- Publicity requirement: "reasonably competent members of the governed community must be able to determine that
  they are being governed legitimately and with proper authority" (PDF p. 1).
- Procedural legitimacy: "ex ante limitation of what power can be used to do; in medias res constraints on
  precisely how power can be exercised; ex post contestability and accountability" (PDF p. 11).
- On Vredenburgh (2022): an argument "which focuses not on the rights of decision subjects, but on duties owed to the
  political community at large" (PDF p. 5, n. 15).
- Against republican liberty: his reasons "concern both the impossibility and undesirability of eliminating the
  possibility of republican domination" and its tendency to conflate distinct concerns (PDF p. 8, n. 27).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Preprint; the OUP chapter may differ. Lazar's examples are state and platform systems (COMPAS, PageRank, GPT-4),
  not enterprise agents acting on customers.
- The companion Tanner lecture (`lazar2024governing`) develops the same frame for intermediaries.

## Where the argument uses it
- A1 (who keeps the record; who may contest): **supports and sharpens**. Lazar's publicity requirement is a
  published version of the draft's claim that a record kept by one side, and reasoning disclosed to no one outside
  the supplier (A4), defeat contestability. His three components give the draft's "brake" (in medias res), "record"
  (publicity) and "contest" (ex post) a vocabulary a political philosopher will recognise.
- N08: the right to explanation runs to the community as well as to the decision subject; the draft's "notice
  without standing" fits his frame as a failure of publicity.
- A7: **contested**. Lazar reaches the draft's conclusions without non-domination and gives reasons for not using it.
