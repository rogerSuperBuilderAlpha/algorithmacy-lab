## Summary
Israeli traces the colonial Requerimiento's "ritual core" (p. 39), a demand presented to an addressee and recorded
by a notary, to ordinary dispute practice in fifteenth-century Castile. Notarial *testimonios de requerimiento*
show the form: a claimant or procurator, two witnesses and an escribano who read the claimant's documents aloud and
drew up the record (p. 42). Castilians of every rank used it, from the princess Isabel claiming the castle of Zafra
(pp. 42-43) to villagers petitioning the Royal Council (pp. 48-52). It warned, publicised, and pressed officials
(pp. 52-55). Its roots lie in Romano-canonical judicial warnings and the *denunciatio evangelica* (pp. 55-60). The
Castilian form was dialogical: the addressee answered, the notary had to record the answer, addressees could take
time and counsel, and in one case each side brought its own notary (pp. 46-48). Transplanted to the Caribbean, the
procedure lost the shared political community, the answer and the court: royal instructions to translate it were
probably not followed, and the colonial record served "self-legitimation" (pp. 61-62). This card supersedes the
partial card (pp. 37-40, 46-47) with a reading of the whole article.

## Key facts it relies on
- Ritual core: "the presentation of a demand or warning to an addressee and its official recording" (p. 39).
- The notary "would authenticate and read aloud any document presented by the claimant, as well as attest to the
  act of claim making by drawing up the testimonio" (p. 42).
- Effect: "Once a requerimiento had been conducted, the addressee could not pretend to be ignorant of the demand,
  and could be considered as having been given a fair opportunity to make amends" (p. 46).
- The record was the legal product: "the lack of a testimonio could render the entire procedure meaningless"
  (p. 46). Notaries sometimes refused to record or to hand over the testimonio, which "neutralized" the act
  (pp. 46-47).
- Dialogue: "the notary was obliged to document the addressee’s response and incorporate it into his testimony"
  (p. 47).
- Each side's notary (Seville, 1417): "the involvement of two notaries, one from each side"; "the two parties seem
  to have participated together in producing the record" (p. 48). Written statements gave "disputants a greater
  degree of control over how their words were recorded" (p. 48).
- Loss in transplant: "In Castile, parties engaging in requerimientos tended to belong to the same political
  community" (p. 61). Royal instructions said the demand should be "translated into their native languages", but
  "it is doubtful that such instructions were actually fulfilled"; "many believed that the performative capacity
  of the colonial requerimiento depended on the recitation of the document alone" (p. 62).
- Record without a court: "It is not clear at all that these records ever ended up in courts"; "the ultimate
  function of record making was self-legitimation" (p. 62).
- Seed's Islamic-summons hypothesis (Ceremonies of Possession, pp. 69-99) is reported and qualified via Devereux
  and Herzog (pp. 39-40 and n. 8).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Israeli's evidence for the colonial side is thin by design (the article is about Castile); the colonial claims on
  p. 62 rest on Faudree (2015) and Konetzke's documents, which I did not open.
- His Castile is a positive picture of the procedure among peers; he gives one case of notaries refusing for
  partisan reasons (pp. 46-47) but no count of how often.
- Muldoon (1980) and Pagden (2018) place the text in canon-law debates on infidel dominium (p. 40); that reading
  makes the colonial requerimiento coherent by its own jurists' standard, which Israeli does not dispute.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (failure: disclosure in place of understanding; record kept by one side; H18, H07): **holds**. The colonial
  form kept the reading and the notary's record and dropped the answer, the translation and the court (pp. 61-62).
- A3 (standard broken): **holds**. The professed standard was Castile's own: the addressee hears, answers and has
  the answer recorded (p. 47), plus royal orders to translate (p. 62). The colonial practice broke both.
- B1 (corrections share a property; H10): **holds, narrower**. The Castilian procedure's checks (the recorded
  answer, time to take counsel, a notary for each side) ran between peers in one community; they put a hearing and
  a second record in the weaker side's hands, but they did not check understanding.
