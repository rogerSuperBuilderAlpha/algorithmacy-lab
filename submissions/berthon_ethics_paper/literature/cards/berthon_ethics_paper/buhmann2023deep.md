## Summary
Buhmann and Fieseler sort concerns about AI into evidence, outcome and epistemic concerns, and split the epistemic ones
into strategic, expert and true opacity (pp. 150-152). They build a framework of responsibilities (nonmaleficence,
beneficence, governance; Table 1, p. 154), argue from political CSR that AI firms carry an obligation to take part in
deliberation (pp. 155-157), test that against three deliberative principles (participation, comprehension,
multivocality; p. 157), and propose "distributed deliberation" across three venues: AI expert discourse, the public
sphere, and AI mini-publics that translate between them (Figure 1, p. 162; pp. 159-166). The discussion weighs the risk
that corporate deliberation serves as deflection (pp. 166-167). It is a BEQ paper and a sibling of our venue.

## Key facts it relies on
- Strategic opacity: algorithms "intentionally kept secret, obfuscated, or 'blackboxed'", with motives including
  "avoiding accountability and evading regulations" (p. 151).
- Expert opacity "can also arise inadvertently through attempts at disclosure and transparency that overwhelm citizens
  on account of the sheer volume and complexity of information made available to them" (p. 151).
- "the impossibility of attaining complete 'AI transparency' can be used as an excuse for organizations not to fulfill
  ethical duties to deliver conventional explanations and straightforward accounts based on fixed legal frameworks"
  (p. 157).
- Participation principle: "subjects who potentially suffer negative effects should have equal access to communicative
  fora" (p. 157).
- Without meta-evaluation, "the choice of translational tools is left to developers, increasing the likelihood of
  convenient rather than ethical solutions" (p. 158).
- Following Moore: "there needs to be both a real possibility of contesting and withdrawing legitimation and a real
  opportunity to influence the content of expert deliberation" (p. 161).
- "For reasons of self-interest, therefore, including the desire to maintain power imbalances and information
  advantages, AI industry actors, just like other corporate actors (Hussain & Moriarty, 2018), would seem unlikely to be
  willing to solve challenges deliberatively." (pp. 166-167)
- "Normative stakeholder engagement in this case would thus constitute merely a means of deflection." (p. 167)
- Ethics washing may be "part of an early stage on a longer path toward the substantive adoption and
  institutionalization of corporate social responsibility" (p. 167).
- The model "means that citizens do not need to have the expert capacity to undertake independent examination of
  algorithmic systems" (p. 171).
- Conclusion: "a politically disengaged AI industry may pose a much greater threat to the prospects of responsible
  innovation in AI" (p. 171).

## Critical notes
This is our reading for the Nadella paper, not the authors' claims.

- **Same diagnosis, different remedy locus.** Their strategic and expert opacity name what the history of literacy
  shows: the institution keeps the record unreadable, or floods the reader. Their remedy is deliberation about systems
  at the level of the public and mini-publics; ours is a control in the hands of the person an agent acts on. The two
  are complements, not rivals. Credit them and say where we differ.
- **Disclosure overload.** p. 151 supports the reading duty (the reading left to the person is a burden, not a remedy).
- **Transparency as excuse.** p. 157 supports the claim that "the record is too complex to share" cannot discharge the
  duty.
- **Deflection.** pp. 166-167 state in BEQ the worry behind A5 (the move is a real correction for the organization and
  still leaves the person the loss). They answer it optimistically (p. 167); we need not.
- **The citizen need not examine the system (p. 171).** This is the reverse of the duty-to-read failure; usable as a
  sibling view. Their citizen is a member of a public, not a party to a transaction.
- Mentxaka et al. (2026) cite this paper at pp. 8-9 for opacity reducing "citizens' ability to question AI-shaped
  decisions"; cite Buhmann and Fieseler directly.
