## Summary
Lazar models "the Algorithmic City", the network of algorithmically mediated social relations, and the power by
which it is governed. He distinguishes extrinsic power, which shapes relations from outside like a river's banks,
from intermediary power, which "constitutes (in whole or part) the social relations that it mediates" (p. 14).
Algorithmic intermediaries combine agential and structural intermediary power at unmatched scale (p. 15), and LLMs
"govern their users" (p. 19). He grounds the evaluation in three democratic values, individual liberty, relational
equality and collective self-determination, and explicitly declines republican non-domination (p. 20, n. 80).
Governing power must answer three questions, what, how and who: procedural legitimacy and proper authority, the
latter derived from authorisation by the governed community (p. 23). AI firms "settle questions of safety
unilaterally and intransparently" and so fail even loose standards of legitimacy and authority (p. 24). Because
intermediaries constitute the relations they govern, their designers "must take responsibility for what people do
within those relationships" (p. 37).

## Key facts it relies on
- "Intermediary power is more like the bonds knitting the water's molecules together. It constitutes (in whole or
  part) the social relations that it mediates ... since refusal is impossible unless it is intentionally designed
  in." (p. 14)
- Intermediaries "monitor the behaviour of their mediatees, learn and shape their beliefs and desires, and then shape
  their relationship, typically in ways that profit the intermediary." (p. 15)
- "LLMs like Claude and ChatGPT govern their users—insofar as the AI system itself determines, within boundaries,
  the norms that constrain that use." (p. 19)
- Against the republican frame: "the tendency of the republican ideal of non-domination to conflate an internally
  diverse suite of normative concerns into a single value that has less explanatory power than separately focusing
  on (probabilistic negative) liberty, relational equality, and collective self-determination." (p. 20, n. 80)
- "If A has power over B, but A must abide by standards of procedural legitimacy, then B also has power over A, since
  A's power is limited, and B can force A to comply with those limits." (p. 23)
- "Benevolent, procedurally legitimate authoritarianism remains authoritarian and is inadequate for governing."
  (p. 23; the Facebook Oversight Board "lacks authority to govern")
- "the ultimate source of the authority to exercise power is the community subject to that power" (p. 23).
- AI companies "invariably settle questions of safety unilaterally and intransparently, in ways that would not pass
  even the loosest standards of procedural legitimacy and proper authority." (p. 24)
- "Designers of algorithmic intermediaries must take responsibility for what people do within those relationships by
  way of those intermediaries, because they have provided them with the materials with which to act." (p. 37)

## Critical notes from the literature
- A preprint of a lecture; the published P&PA text is the version of record and was not opened.
- Lazar's cases are platforms, recommender systems and generative AI; he does not treat enterprise agents acting on
  non-users, and his "mediatees" are the parties on both sides of a mediated relation.
- He rejects non-domination on two grounds (impossibility and undesirability of eliminating the possibility of
  interference; conflation), which is one side of a dispute with Pettit (2008) and Hamilton (`hamilton2025digital`).

## Where the argument uses it
- A7, the second route: **contests the choice of frame**. Lazar supplies a published rival to non-domination for
  exactly the paper's object (intermediaries governing the people they act on): procedural legitimacy (ex ante
  limits, in medias res constraints, ex post contestability) and proper authority. A BEQ referee may ask why the
  paper needs the republican vocabulary when "contest" already names Lazar's ex post condition.
- A5 (envelope declared to the deployer): **supports the paper's point more strongly than Hamilton does**. A
  self-declared, benevolent, rule-following envelope "remains authoritarian" (p. 23) because its authority is
  unilateral; firms' unilateral safety settings fail legitimacy (p. 24).
- CB (duties on designers): **cuts both ways**. P. 37 supports placing responsibility on designers; p. 23 implies
  that duties the firm adopts for itself do not confer authority, so the paper's six duties need an external
  authoriser (regulation, the people acted on) to meet Lazar's "who" question.
- K01 (algorithmacy as a relation through an intermediary): p. 14's "refusal is impossible unless it is intentionally
  designed in" is a published statement of the draft's structural-refusal duty and of its first question.
