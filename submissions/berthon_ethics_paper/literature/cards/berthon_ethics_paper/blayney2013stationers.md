## Summary
Secondary card: paraphrase only; no words of Blayney's are quoted here. Blayney's two volumes are the standard
modern history of the London book trade from 1501 to the Company's incorporation on 4 May 1557. The Cambridge Core
description (saved at `blayney/cambridge_book.html`, sha256
`a63f3fe2bfedb63f39fd4e0ffc0bf0bfb941ec224051e3f03930a3d6f3e5bebe`) presents the work as revisionist on privileges and
patents and on "the regulation of printing by the Church, Crown and City"; its chapters run from "Royal privilege and
clerical scrutiny" (1510-1520) and "The Church clamps down" (1521-1528) through "From catastrophe to charter"
(1553-1557) and "The road to incorporation" (1554-1557). Smyth's review reports Blayney's account of the charter as
having two sides: for the Stationers it was a means to enforce a new commercial monopoly; for the Crown it belonged to
a wider effort to regulate the trade and accompanied Mary's purge of printers who had prospered under Edward VI's
Reformation. Smyth also reports Blayney's demolition of received errors, among them W. W. Greg's description of the
1557 charter as a masterstroke of Elizabethan politics and Blagden's company history, and the date of incorporation as
4 May 1557. Crossref resolves the DOI (monograph, issued 2013-11-21). OpenAlex gives no open copy.

## Key facts it relies on
- (Smyth, LRB 2015, paraphrase) Incorporation on 4 May 1557 gave the Stationers a nationwide printing monopoly and
  the right to seize, burn or amend illegal books.
- (Smyth, paraphrase) Blayney distinguishes the Company's motive (enforcing its commercial monopoly) from the Crown's
  (regulating the trade after a Marian purge of Edwardian printers).
- (Smyth, paraphrase) Blayney corrects Greg's "Elizabethan politics" label for a Marian charter and finds Blagden's
  history riddled with errors.
- (Cambridge Core description, paraphrase) The book revises accepted beliefs about privileges, patents and regulation
  by Church, Crown and City.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The chapters on the charter (10-11) were not read. What this card can carry is that the owning literature now
  treats the 1557 charter as a trade incorporation sought by the Company, with the Crown's censorship interest as the
  context rather than the whole explanation. The older reading (Arber, Siebert) is what the draft states.
- Smyth's review is itself a secondary account; the author should read Blayney vol. 2, chs. 10-11 before the paper
  characterises the Crown's purpose.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2, H11 (draft ll. 187-189: "Custody of the medium was given to the trade that profited from it, as an instrument
  of the Crown's control of what could be read"; l. 313): **contested**. Blayney's account supports the first half (the
  trade held the medium and sought the charter for its monopoly) and complicates the second (the charter as the
  Crown's instrument of reading control). The paper must name both readings.
- A6 (the property the verdict turned on, the custodian): **holds** in the narrower form: the custodian was the trade;
  whose instrument it was is disputed.
