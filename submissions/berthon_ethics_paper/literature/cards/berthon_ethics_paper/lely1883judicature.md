## Summary
Lely and Foulkes's fourth edition prints the Rules of the Supreme Court 1883 with notes. Order XXXVIII
("Affidavits and Depositions"), rule 13, governs affidavits by deponents who cannot read: the officer who takes the
oath must certify in the jurat that the affidavit was read to the deponent in his presence, that the deponent
"seemed perfectly to understand it", and that the deponent signed in his presence; without that certificate the
affidavit may not be used unless the court is "otherwise satisfied" it was read over and "appeared to be perfectly
understood" (pp. 276-277). The editors' margin note says the rule was "Extended to blind persons in 1883" (p. 277).
This is the older Rules of the Supreme Court form behind the modern Practice Direction 32 jurat
(`berthon_ethics_course/moj1998practice32.md`). A second, independent edition, R. W. Andrews, *The Supreme Court of
Judicature Acts ... with Rules of Court and Forms issued up to 1885* (London: Reeves, 1885), p. 331, prints the same
rule as Ord. XXXVIII r. 13 and adds a case note (below). Andrews's OCR is at
`/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/berthon_ethics_local/pdfs/nadella/N3/rsc1883/supremecourtjud00stongoog_djvu.txt`
(sha256 `28ca3b7a4dcbc8f59f7b60fd90264e5a493935ad7df532caa4781c183079ab50`).

## Key facts it relies on
- Rule 13: "Where an affidavit is sworn by any person who appears to the officer taking the affidavit to be
  illiterate or blind, the officer shall certify in the jurat that the affidavit was read in his presence to the
  deponent, that the deponent seemed perfectly to understand it, and that the deponent made his signature in the
  presence of the officer" (p. 276; checked against the page image, leaf 364).
- Sanction: "No such affidavit shall be used in evidence in the absence of this certificate, unless the court or a
  judge is otherwise satisfied that the affidavit was read over to and appeared to be perfectly understood by the
  deponent" (pp. 276-277).
- Margin note: "Extended to blind persons in 1883" (p. 277), so the illiterate-deponent rule predates 1883.
- Andrews (1885, p. 331), case note to the same rule: "The statement of a managing clerk that he had read over and
  explained the affidavit to an illiterate witness was held insufficient to satisfy the Judge, and the affidavit was
  therefore directed to be taken off the file" (Re Longstaff, W. N. 1884, 216). The OCR garbles this note
  ("Btatement", "manaffing"); I read the words from the page image (archive.org leaf n395, saved as
  `rsc1883/andrews_p331.jpg`, sha256 `d8139a8a…`).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The check is an impression ("seemed perfectly to understand"), recorded by the officer; the deponent is not asked
  to restate anything. It is stage two of H24 (disclosure, certified) with a light stage-three element.
- The rule protects the court's record as much as the deponent: an affidavit is evidence the deponent offers, not
  a contract imposed on him.
- I did not open Re Longstaff itself (Weekly Notes 1884); the case note is Andrews's. The note gives no reason for
  the ruling; the rule itself requires the officer's certificate, which may be why the clerk's statement failed.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (failure: disclosure in place of understanding; H22): **narrower**. The 1883 rule demands more than disclosure:
  an independent officer must certify an impression of understanding, and a managing clerk's own statement that he
  had read and explained the affidavit did not satisfy the judge (Re Longstaff, per Andrews p. 331); the reason is not
  given. That is a weak check of understanding, carried by someone other than the party who
  benefits.
- B1 (corrections share a property): **holds in part**. The reading and the certificate are moved off the drafting
  party and onto an officer of the court (the rule's certificate; Re Longstaff shows a managing clerk's statement did
  not satisfy the judge, for a reason not given), and the certificate is about understanding. The check is an
  impression, not a test.
