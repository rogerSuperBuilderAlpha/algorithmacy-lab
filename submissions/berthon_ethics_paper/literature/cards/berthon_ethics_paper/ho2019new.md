## Summary
Replication of Jin and Leslie (2003) on the Los Angeles grade-card natural experiment, by the authors of "Fudging the
Nudge". Using expanded hospitalization data (1990-2010) and new data on mandatorily reported foodborne illness, Ho and
colleagues find that the reported 20 percent fall in foodborne hospitalizations after 1998 does not survive: the largest
salmonella outbreak in California's history hit Southern California before grading began, and placebo tests detect the
same "treatment effect" in Southern California counties that never adopted grading. Note 1 states that the same problems
affect Simon et al. (2005), the paper the draft cites as D07's precedent. Printed page = PDF page + 403, confirmed by the
running heads ("VOL. 11 NO. 4 ... 405" at PDF p. 2). Jin and Leslie's reply (AEJ:EP 11(4): 429-443, doi
10.1257/pol.20180543; abstract only, Crossref) defends the original conclusion, so the matter is contested.

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (p. 404): "Expanding hospitalization data and collecting new data on mandatorily reported illnesses, we
  show that this finding does not hold up under improvements to the original data and methodology. The largest
  salmonella outbreak in state history hit Southern California before Los Angeles implemented grading."
- Abstract (p. 404): "Placebo tests detect the same treatment effects for Southern California counties, none of which
  changed restaurant grading."
- n. 1 (p. 405): "A different version of the foodborne illness analysis was also published as Simon et al. (2005).
  Online Appendix A shows that the same issues identified here affect that analysis."
- Section IV (p. 418): "In sum, using the same model as JL, Southern CA exhibits statistically identical grading
  effects, even though no Southern CA county adopted grading in this time period."
- Conclusion (p. 425): "First, our findings show that there is little credible evidence for the impact of restaurant
  grading—what many regard as the archetypal disclosure—on foodborne illness. Although we do not address JL’s findings
  on consumer behavior and restaurant hygiene scores, our analysis suggests that much work remains to be done to
  empirically ground our understanding of information disclosure."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Jin and Leslie (2019, reply, abstract): "Robustness checks support our original conclusion—foodborne
  hospitalizations, as defined in JL, declined in Los Angeles County (LA) relative to the rest of California (CA)
  after LA adopted restaurant hygiene grade cards in 1998. More precisely, the decline in LA is pronounced against
  central and Northern CA, but insignificant when compared with the rest of Southern CA." (Crossref abstract; the
  reply itself was not opened: AEA PDF served HTML.)
- Ho et al. do not contest JL's findings on consumer demand and hygiene scores (p. 425), so the grade card's effect on
  restaurants' behaviour is not what fails; the health outcome the draft cites is.

## Where the argument uses it
- **D07 / CB (draft lines 281-283, 530-532).** The draft's precedent for "change the medium" is the LA grade card and
  its "13.1 percent fall in foodborne-disease hospitalizations (Simon et al. 2005, 32)". Ho et al. say the Simon
  analysis has "the same issues" (p. 405 n. 1) and that there is "little credible evidence" of a health effect (p.
  425). Verdict: **contested**; the draft cannot cite the hospitalization figure as established. What survives is the
  grade card as a device that moved the inspector's reading to the door (JL's unrefuted findings on hygiene scores and
  demand), not a measured protective effect.
- **A2 (disclosure failure) and N3's refutation entry.** N3 Q9 and ADJUDICATION.md treat Simon 2005 as the one
  disclosure regime with a measured protective effect; this card narrows that to "associated with, contested on
  replication".
