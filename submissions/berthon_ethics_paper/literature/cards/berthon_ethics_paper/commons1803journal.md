## Summary
The Commons' reasons, reported by Mr Clark and agreed by the House on 17 April 1695, for refusing the Lords' clause that would have revived the Licensing Act of 1662. Copyright History (Bently and Kretschmer) catalogues the same text as "Reasons for objecting to the renewal of the Licensing Act, London (1695)", citing *Journal of the House of Commons* vol. 11 pp. 305-306. The Commons did not object to punishing sedition; they objected that the Act did not do so and served the Stationers' Company: it confirmed patents and the Company's monopoly of classic authors, gave the Company "an Opportunity to enter a Title to themselves, and their Friends, for what belongs to, and is the Labour and Right of, others" (reason 3), subjected houses to search on the Company's warrant, and left fees and penalties unbounded. The Act lapsed that year (Deazley, `deazley2008commentary1637`, gives "its expiration in May 1695"). This is the contemporary objection, by a House of Parliament, to custody of the press by an interested party, and it is the instrument of the lapse.

## Key facts it relies on
- Reason 1: the Act "in no-wise answered the End for which it was made"; it carries no penalty for seditious books, "whereas there are great and grievous Penalties imposed by that Act for Matters wherein neither Church nor State is any ways concerned."
- Reason 3: by requiring entry in the Register before printing, "both Houses of Parliament are disabled to order any thing to be printed; and the said Company are impowered to hinder the printing all innocent and useful Books; and have an Opportunity to enter a Title to themselves, and their Friends, for what belongs to, and is the Labour and Right of, others."
- Reason 8: the Act "confirms all Patents of Books ... whereby the sole Printing of all, or most of, the Classick Authors are, and have been for many Years past, together with a great Number of the best Books, and of most general Use, monopolized by the Company of Stationers"; scholars "are forced not only to buy them at the extravagant Price they demand, but must be content with their ill and incorrect Editions".
- Reason 9: no fee fixed for a licence, "by colour whereof great Oppression may be, and has been, practised."
- Reason 12: those not licensed by the bishop cannot sell books, except freemen of the Company, "so that neither Church nor State is taken care of thereby; but the People compelled to buy their Freedom of Trade in all Parts of England from the Company of Stationers in London."
- Reason 16: houses "as well Peers as Commoners" open to search day or night on a Secretary's warrant, and Company freemen's houses "on a Warrant from the Master and Wardens of the said Company".
- The Streater proviso: one man "may print what he pleases, as if the Act had never been made; when the Commons see no Cause to distinguish him from the rest of the Subjects of England."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Locators are the Journal's pp. 305-306 as given by Copyright History; the BHO page spans pp. 305-308 and does not mark the page break inside the entry, so I cite the entry as pp. 305-306.
- The reasons come from MPs with commercial and political interests of their own (provincial ports, booksellers outside London); they object to the Company's privilege, not to state control of sedition, which they leave to the common law.
- Resolves the date conflict in facet B (Johns 1695 vs Innis 1694): the Commons refused renewal in April 1695.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **holds** for the press monopoly. A House of Parliament named the Company's custody a wrong, in terms of whom it harmed (scholars, provincial traders, householders). Reason 3 names no authors: the Register gave the Company "an Opportunity to enter a Title" to what is "the Labour and Right of, others".
- Refutation sought ("the Register as author protection"): **refutes** the strong form. Reason 3 says the Register gave the Company "an Opportunity to enter a Title" to what is "the Labour and Right of, others". The Register protected entrants, chiefly Company members.
- A2 (custody by an interested party; the gate held by the few): holds; the lapse came by Parliament's refusal, an outside force, which is the kind of correction A2's "Sinks it" column says the record must lack.
