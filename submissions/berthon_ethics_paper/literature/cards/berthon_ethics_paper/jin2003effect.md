## Summary
Abstract-only card, paraphrase only. Jin and Leslie study Los Angeles County's 1998 requirement that restaurants post
hygiene grade cards. The abstract reports that the cards caused inspection scores to rise, made consumer demand
sensitive to hygiene, and reduced foodborne-illness hospitalizations, and that the health gain is not fully explained
by diners switching to cleaner restaurants, so restaurants improved. The QJE version is paywalled; OpenAlex's green
copy is on SSRN, which returned HTTP 403, so I did not read the article.

## Key facts it relies on
- Paraphrase of the abstract: grade cards raised inspection scores, made demand respond to hygiene, cut
  hospitalizations; restaurants, not only consumers, changed.
- Simon et al. (2005; `berthon_ethics_paper/simon2005impact.md`), with Jin and Leslie as co-authors, report the
  hospitalization effect in full text (13.1% in the first year).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Natural experiment in one county; later evaluations of other grading regimes not opened here.

## Where the argument uses it
- Refutation sought (disclosure with protective effect): **refutes a strong form** of "disclosure fails", at abstract
  depth; Simon et al. (2005) carries the claim in full text.
- B1: the effective disclosure is a verdict read by an inspector and posted as one letter; the diner is not asked to
  read a record.
