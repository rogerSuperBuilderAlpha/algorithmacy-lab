## Summary
The SEP entry's §7 sets out Bernard Williams's "relativism of distance" (Williams 1981; *Ethics and the Limits of
Philosophy*, 1985, ch. 9) as a mixed position between relativism and objectivism, with the objectivist reply. The
card exists to give the paper a reliable statement of the strongest historicist objection to judging, or being
judged by, a distant outlook. Williams's own book was not opened (archive.org lending copy only); the paraphrase
of Williams is Gowans's.

## Key facts it relies on
(all §7, "Mixed Positions: A Rapprochement between Relativists and Objectivists?")
- Williams rejected "strict relational relativism" but drew a distinction "between a “notional confrontation,”
  where a divergent outlook is known but not a real option for us, and a “real confrontation,” where a divergent
  outlook is a real option for us—something we might embrace without losing our grip on reality."
- "Williams’s “relativism of distance” says ethical appraisals are appropriate in real confrontations, but not in
  notional ones."
- Example: "we could never embrace the outlook of a medieval samurai: Since this is a notional confrontation, it
  would be inappropriate to describe this outlook as just or unjust."
- The objection to Williams: "it may be wondered why appraisals are inappropriate in notional confrontations: Why
  should the fact that an outlook is not a real option preclude us from thinking it is just or unjust?"
- Gowans points to Fricker (2010) and Gray (2024) for critical discussion.

## Where the argument uses it
- **A3 and A7, counter-case (f).** On Williams's view, calling the Stationers' licensing or the 1543 bar
  "failures" is appraisal in a notional confrontation, which he holds inappropriate. That attacks the first half of
  the bridge (judging the past) rather than the second (binding the present), and N5's Walzer/Fraser/von Samson set
  does not meet it. What survives: A3's three internal standards report contemporaries' own appraisals, which were
  real confrontations for them; the paper's appraisal of Nadella's course is a real confrontation for us. The
  argument must therefore rest A2's verdicts on the contemporaries' objections and broken professions, never on the
  author's own judgement of the past.
