## Summary
Research note on the one prominent attempt to build an independent data steward between a platform and the people its
sensors act on: Sidewalk Labs' proposed "Urban Data Trust" for the Toronto waterfront, dropped in 2019 before the
project was cancelled. Austin and Lie argue it failed as a model because it was two incoherent models (open data and
stewardship), lacked clarity about the problem it solved and about how accountability and oversight were secured,
and, by de-identifying data to escape privacy law, "actually reduced accountability and oversight". The local file is
named for a mistaken first attribution; the authors are Austin and Lie, confirmed by Crossref and by the citation line
on p. 255. Page numbers confirmed by the footer "Surveillance & Society 19(2)" and the running page numbers.

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract (p. 255): "The Urban Data Trust was a failed model because it lacked clarity regarding the nature of the
  problem(s) to which it is a solution, how accountability and oversight are secured, and its relation to existing
  data protection law."
- p. 257: "we argue that this strategy was problematic as it actually reduced accountability and oversight in relation
  to privacy while increasing complexity."
- p. 257: "we argue that its resulting model was in reality two models put together incoherently. One model was an
  open data vision, which contemplated broad public access to data. The other was a stewardship model that
  contemplated applying a range of norms to data use decisions."
- p. 256: "Sidewalk Labs’ Urban Data Trust model failed before the project was cancelled, and yet, despite this
  failure, the popularity of the idea of data trusts persists at the municipal, provincial, and federal levels in
  Canada."

## Critical notes from the literature
- The trust was proposed by the operator (Sidewalk Labs), which is the configuration D04 is meant to rule out ("a body
  the operator does not control"); the failure therefore supports D04's independence condition rather than refuting
  the duty. Delacroix and Lawrence (`ade/delacroix2019`) argue for bottom-up trusts; this is the top-down case.

## Where the argument uses it
- **D04 (draft lines 524-526, 537-538).** The strongest failed precedent for counter-delegation in a platform setting:
  an operator-designed intermediary that reduced oversight. Verdict: D04 **holds**, narrowed to its condition that the
  body be constituted independently of the operator and inside existing law; the Easton clerk precedent satisfies
  that, the Urban Data Trust did not.
