## Summary
Secondary card, paraphrase only. I could not open Seed's book (no open copy; library item). Crider's review reports
that chapter 3, "The Requirement: A Protocol for Conquest", treats the Requerimiento as a uniquely Spanish ceremony
of possession: a text that Spanish expeditions were obliged to read aloud before subjugating native peoples, usually
without translators, composed by Palacios Rubios. Seed argues that its form drew on Islamic and Jewish Iberian
traditions, in particular the summons that preceded jihad, more than earlier scholars allowed. Crider also reports
that Seed contrasts French possession ceremonies, which attended to the responses of the native peoples and built
alliances on their stated consent. He criticises the chapter for spending its energy on the genealogy of a single
text and for letting the Requerimiento stand for all Spanish practice.

## Key facts it relies on
- Paraphrase of Crider (review, pp. 2-3 of the H-Net PDF): chapter 3 reads the Requerimiento as a protocol for
  conquest, read aloud, usually untranslated; Seed traces its form to the Islamic summons.
- Paraphrase of Crider (review, p. 2): the French ceremonies Seed describes stand out for their attention to native
  responses.
- Israeli (2022, pp. 39-40, n. 8; `berthon_ethics_paper/israeli2022requerimiento.md`) cites Seed, Ceremonies,
  pp. 69-99 and p. 78, and reports that Devereux and Herzog dispute the Islamic derivation.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Israeli (2022) proposes a different origin, Castilian civil dispute practice, which explains the notary and the
  record better than the jihad summons.
- Crider: the Requerimiento was mainly a relic of the first half of the sixteenth century, confined to first
  encounters.

## Where the argument uses it
