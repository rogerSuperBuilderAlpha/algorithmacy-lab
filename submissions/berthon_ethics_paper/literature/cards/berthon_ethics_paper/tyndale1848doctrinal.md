## Summary
The Parker Society edition (ed. Walter, 1848) of Tyndale's treatises and prefaces. In the preface he set before Genesis (dated in the edition "Ann. 1530. Janua. 17"), Tyndale says what moved him to translate: the clergy keep "the world still in darkness" by withholding the text in the mother tongue, and it is "impossible to establish the lay-people in any truth, except the scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother-tongue, that they might see the process, order, and meaning of the text". The title page dates his martyrdom 1536, so he could not object to the 1543 Act, which banned his translations by name. His preface is the reformers' own statement of the standard the Act broke: the lay reader with the text before his eyes.

## Key facts it relies on
- "Which thing only moved me to translate the new Testament. Because I had perceived by experience, how that it was impossible to establish the lay-people in any truth, except the scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother-tongue, that they might see the process, order, and meaning of the text" (p. 394).
- On the clergy's motive: "Finally, in this they be all agreed, to drive you from the knowledge of the scripture, and that ye shall not have the text thereof in the mother-tongue, and to keep the world still in darkness, to the intent they might sit in the consciences of the people, through vain superstition and false doctrine" (p. 393).
- On interpretation: they "amaze them, expounding it in many senses before the unlearned lay-people, (when it hath but one simple, literal sense, whose light the owls cannot abide,)" (pp. 393-394).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Modernised spelling by the Parker Society editor; page numbers are the 1848 edition's.
- The 1543 Act abolished "the craftye false and untrue translacion of Tyndale" (`statutesrealm1817vol3`, p. 894): Tyndale is the Act's target, not a witness to it.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (a professed standard): supports as the reform's own pre-1543 standard of lay access; not a contemporary objection to the Act.
- A5 (whom a move serves; what task it leaves the person): Tyndale's ground, that the reader must "see the process, order, and meaning of the text" for himself, is the period's own statement of the correction's test.
