## Summary
The authors argue that philosophers of sweatshop ethics wrongly assume brands employ or control sweatshop workers
("vertical integration" and "control" assumptions). Under the realistic assumption of "constrained influence",
big buyers still have responsibility for "social upgrading", which they can discharge only by acting collectively
and "ceding power to third parties in formalized and accountable ways" (p. 22). The International (formerly
Bangladesh) Accord is offered as the working example. About 12,000 words before the references, including footnotes.

## Key facts it relies on
- Working-precedent move: "The principles of collective action among buyers ceding power via legally binding
  commitments ... might sound overly ideal. However, there is at least one supply chain governance regime that
  contains all of these elements, if in imperfect form." (p. 19)
- Accord features (pp. 19-20): 260 signatory buyers; joint worker-management safety committees, a right to refuse
  unsafe work, an independent complaints mechanism; tripartite governance; Art. 31 (commercial terms that make safe
  workplaces "financially feasible"); legally binding under Dutch law, with cases settled "for millions of dollars"
  (quoting Reinecke and Donaghey 2023: 83).
- Hedge: "The Accord is a work in progress, but it does represent a concrete approach" (p. 20).
- Federated CSR (Caulfield and Lynn 2024): "Just as constitutions constrain responsible states and decentralize
  power ... big buyers have a responsibility to seek restraint and support appropriate countervailing powers"
  (p. 17).
- Table 2 (p. 18): feature of the supply chain → why it constrains → available response.
- The precedent is also critiqued: ACT on wages is called "feigning" by Coneybeer and Maguire (p. 21).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors flag the worry that the Accord limits buyers' liability (Salminen 2018) and the supporters' reply
  (p. 20). They leave the state's role to future work (p. 22).

## Where the argument uses it
- Outline, conclusion ("Kingston and Carnovale's move of naming a working precedent"): **holds**. The move is: state
  the duty in principle, concede it "might sound overly ideal", then name one existing regime that already has all
  the elements "if in imperfect form", with its documented limits.
- B2/CB: the "ceding power to third parties in binding ways" duty is a close business-ethics analogue of the
  paper's duties that move the record or the brake to a party answerable to the weaker side (D03, D04). It is
  supply-chain, not AI; use it as a structural precedent, not evidence about LLM tools.
