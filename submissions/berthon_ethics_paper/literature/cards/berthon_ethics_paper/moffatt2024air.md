## Summary
A customer asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot said he could claim the reduced
rate retroactively within 90 days; Air Canada's own bereavement page said otherwise, and the airline refused the
refund. The Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation and awarded $650.88 plus interest and fees. It rejected two
defences that the paper's argument pairs with print-era failures: that the chatbot was answerable for its own words
(para 27), and that the customer should have checked the chatbot's answer against another page (para 28). The
decision is a small-claims tribunal ruling (not binding precedent on courts). The PDF was obtained from the
Internet Archive's capture of the Tribunal's own document URL (the live site returned 403 to scripted requests);
the PDF carries the Tribunal's header, file number and paragraph numbering.

## Key facts it relies on
- Date issued 14 Feb 2024; File SC-2023-005609; Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers (p. 1).
- An Air Canada representative "admitted the chatbot had provided “misleading words.”" (para 22, p. 5).
- Duty of care: "given their commercial relationship as a service provider and consumer, I find Air Canada owed
  Mr. Moffatt a duty of care" (para 26, p. 5).
- "In effect, Air Canada suggests the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions.
  This is a remarkable submission." (para 27, p. 6).
- "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no
  difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." (para 27, p. 6).
- "It also does not explain why customers should have to double-check information found in one part of its
  website on another part of its website." (para 28, p. 6).
- "There is no reason why Mr. Moffatt should know that one section of Air Canada’s webpage is accurate, and
  another is not." (para 29, p. 6).
- A contractual (tariff) defence failed because Air Canada did not produce the tariff (para 31, pp. 6-7).
- Damages $650.88 (para 40, p. 8); pre-judgment interest $36.14 (para 42, p. 8).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Small-claims tribunal (BC CRT); the award is small and the reasoning is short. Secondary commentary (law-firm
  blogs, not opened) treats it as a reminder that firms answer for their AI tools, not as a precedent.
- The chatbot was a customer-facing information tool, not an agent taking actions; the extension to agentic
  actions (P2, P19) is an inference.

## Where the argument uses it
- **A8 / O02 ("the law already provides"), counter-case (N6 §d, item 3).** For the customer acted on by a deployer's
  AI, ordinary negligence law already gave standing and a remedy, and the tribunal refused to put the verification
  burden on the customer (para 28). That **narrows** M10 ("distrust as the reader's duty") and M9 ("notice without
  standing") where the person acted on is a consumer with a commercial relationship: the duty-to-read posture the
  vendors' terms adopt (N1 C3) did not survive this tribunal. It **supports** P3's own line that responsibility
  cannot be outsourced (to the vendor or to the tool).
- **A4 (H19, duty to read).** The tribunal's reasoning is a present-day correction of the H19 failure; it holds
  only where a duty of care and reliance can be shown, and only after the harm.
