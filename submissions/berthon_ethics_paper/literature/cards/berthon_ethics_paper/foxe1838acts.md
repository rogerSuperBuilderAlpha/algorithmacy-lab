## Summary
The Cattley edition (vol. 5, 1838) of Foxe's martyrology, at the passage on the parliament that, in Foxe's dating, met "a.d. 1544" and qualified the Six Articles. The editor's footnote identifies the statute as 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1. Foxe condemns the Act's book provisions as "the advancement of popery, under the colour of religion", describes the reading bar by rank and sex without separate comment, and credits the Act with one mitigation: the accused could now purge himself by witnesses. Foxe is the main Protestant chronicler of the reign, so his treatment is the test of whether the reformers' own record named the reading bar a wrong. It names the Act a wrong in general and passes over the reading clause in description.

## Key facts it relies on
- On the whole parliament's work: "By the same parliament, moreover, many things were provided for the advancement of popery, under the colour of religion; so that all manner of books of the Old and New Testament, bearing the name of William Tyndale, or any others, having prologues, or containing any matters, annotations, preambles, words, or sentences, contrary to the six articles, were debarred." (p. 527).
- On the reading bar: "In the same parliament, furthermore, it was provided, that the text of the New Testament, or of the Bible, being prohibited to all women, artificers, prentices, journeymen, servingmen, yeomen, husbandmen, and labourers; yet was permitted, notwithstanding, to noblemen, and gentlemen, and gentlewomen, to read and peruse, to their edifying, so that they did it quietly, without arguing, discussing, or expounding upon the Scripture." (p. 527).
- On the mitigation: "whereas before, the offender or defendant might not be suffered to bring in any witnesses to purge and try himself, in this parliament it was permitted to the party detected or complained of, to try his cause by witnesses" (p. 527).
- The editor's footnote: "This act (34, 35 Henry VIII. cap. i.) is printed at length in Gibson's" (p. 527, n. 1; the note continues beyond the lines read).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Foxe dates the parliament 1544; the Act belongs to the session of 1542-3 (`statutesrealm1817vol3`, head of c. 1).
- Foxe's list of the barred differs slightly from the Act: he includes "yeomen" outright where the Act bars "serving men of the degrees of yeomen or undre", and he omits merchants' private reading.
- The Cattley text conflates Foxe's editions; I did not check which edition first carried this passage.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **narrower**. Foxe objects to the Act as "the advancement of popery, under the colour of religion", which covers its book bans; he reports the rank-and-sex bar without calling it a wrong. The direct objection to the reading bar is the 1546 *Supplication* (`furnivall1871four`), not Foxe.
- Refutation sought ("1543 as protective"): Foxe records a real procedural gain for the accused (purgation by witnesses), which the paper should concede.
