## Summary
Tam and colleagues pool 103 studies (135 cohorts, to October 2013) that tested whether clinical-trial participants
understood the components of the consent they gave. Pooled understanding ranges from 75.8% (freedom to withdraw) down
to 53.3% (placebo) and 52.1% (randomization). Meta-regression finds lower understanding with lower education and in
developing countries, and finds that understanding of several components "had not increased over 30 years" despite
many attempts to improve consent. Closed-ended questions probably overstate understanding.

## Key facts it relies on
- Scope (abstract): "The analysis included 103 studies evaluating 135 cohorts of participants."
- Range (abstract): "75.8% for freedom to withdraw at any time" ... "53.3% for placebo and 52.1% for
  randomization"; "54.9% could name at least one risk".
- Trend (abstract): covariates such as "age, educational level, critical illness, the study phase and location"
  affected understanding, and analyses "indicated that the proportion of participants who understood informed
  consent had not increased over 30 years".
- Discussion: understanding of risks, placebo and freedom to withdraw "had not changed over 30 years. This is despite
  considerable progress in medical research methods over this time and many attempts made to improve the quality of
  informed consent." (Reference numbers 144 and 145 omitted.)
- Discussion: "Participants from developing countries and those with a low level of literacy were less likely to
  understand randomization."
- Discussion: "the use of closed-ended questions could have led to understanding being overestimated".

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors offer four explanations for the flat trend, including lack of statistical power (Discussion).
- Clinical trials only; patients with cognitive deficits excluded, which raises the pooled figures relative to
  earlier reviews (Discussion).

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (failure: disclosure in place of understanding): **holds**. After decades of signed, disclosed
  consent, roughly a quarter to a half of participants miss core components, and the share has not improved.
- A3 (a professed standard broken): **holds**. Consent law and research ethics profess informed consent; the measured
  understanding falls short of it, worst for less educated and less literate participants.
