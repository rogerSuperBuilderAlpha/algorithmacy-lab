## Summary
The Early English Text Society edition (1871) of four reforming petitions. The third, *A Supplication of the Poore Commons*, is dated 1546 by the editor (Cowper's preface: "The dates of their publication are 1544 for the second, and 1546 for the third"; Furnivall's note calls it "the Supplication of the Poore Commons, 1546"). Addressed to Henry VIII, it names a law that withholds the Scripture from those without means and asks the King to restore the English Bible "as you dyd at the fyrst translation therof". It objects to "a lawe" passed in Parliament that kept the English Scripture from those who could not "spend x. pound by yere" (p. 64). Its likeliest target is 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1, printed about three years earlier, but the identification is probable, not shown: the petition never names the Act, and its criterion matches no text found. The petition describes a possession test (none may "haue the Scripture in his house" unless he can spend £10 a year), where the Act bars *reading* by rank and sex and has no income or possession test (`statutesrealm1817vol3`, pp. 896-897). The editor's running heads read "ONLY THE RICH MAY READ" (p. 64) and "THE BIBLE OUGHT TO BE READ" (p. 66). Quotations below are from the OCR of this copy, with page confirmed by a second archive.org scan of the same edition (`asupplicacyonfo01cowpgoog`, sha256 66ed2ae478c4ccaf6740666c18eb54101b1ef50b6b8d530583361dc0f3a9171e) showing the same words; OCR damage is in the margin glosses only.

## Key facts it relies on
- The law named: "Finally, thei haue procured a lawe, that none shal so hardy haue the Scripture in his house, onlesse he maye spend x. pound by yere. And what meaneth this, but that they would famysh the soules of the residue, witholdyng theyr food from them?" (p. 64; OCR "Scriptiue"/"ScriptuTC" and a lost question mark repaired by comparing the two scans).
- The appeal on fairness: "We appeale to your Highnes iudgement in this behalfe, whither this lawe be indifferent or not." (p. 64).
- The question of standing: "why do these men disable them for readers of the Scriptures, that are not indued with the possessions of this worlde?" (p. 65).
- The Parliament that passed it anticipated as an objection: "they were not all sturdy beggers that were in the Parlament when this lawe was stablished. For many of them, and the most parte were seculer men" (p. 65), answered with the claim that "what man soeuer doth wyttyngly and willingly forsake the knowledge of the lyuely Worde of God ... is none of the flock of Christ" (p. 65).
- The request: "as fauorably restore vnto vs the Scripture in oure English tonge, as you dyd at the fyrst translation therof set it abrode" (p. 66).
- The broken professed standard: the King's command that "there were in euery parysh churche ... one Byble at the least set at libertie, so that euery man myght frely come to it, and read therin" (p. 67), which, the author says, priests evaded by moving the Bible "into the quyre, other elles into som pue, where pore men durst not presume to come" (p. 67).
- The clerical position it attacks: "Thei say that it sufficeth a laye man to beleue as thei teach, and not to meddle with the enterpretation of the Scriptures" (p. 64).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The tract blames the clergy ("thei haue procured a lawe"), not the King, for the law; it petitions the King to undo it. Its objection is therefore to the custody of the text by the clergy and the propertied, framed as loyal petition.
- The income figure (£10 a year) does not match the 1543 Act's criteria. Either the author misdescribed the Act, or he referred to an instrument I did not find. I found no other law of 1543-6 with that threshold in the volumes I opened.
- The editor (preface, page not recorded) suggests Henry Brinklow as author of the second and third Supplications; he gives no proof. Authorship is anonymous on the original.
- Facet B (`bec_facet_B_print_1450_1800.md`, failure 2) recorded "Who objected at the time. Not found." This card is the likeliest candidate for that objector, but its objection to the 1543 Act is probable, not established, because the petition never names the Act and its £10 possession test is not the Act's rank-and-sex reading bar.

## Where the argument uses it
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **probable, not established** for the 1543 bar. A contemporary petition calls the law unfair ("whither this lawe be indifferent or not"), names whom it shuts out (those without "the possessions of this worlde") and asks for restoration. Its criterion (a £10 possession test) is not the Act's (a reading bar by rank and sex), and the petition never names the Act, so its identity with 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1 is probable, not shown; the paper should quote its objection, not its account of the Act, and should not call it a direct objector to the Act.
- A2 (custody by an interested party; a gate held by the few): holds as a contemporary's own diagnosis that clergy kept the text from "pore men".
- Professed standard broken (A3, second test): holds through its appeal to the King's injunction that every man might "frely come to it, and read therin" (p. 67), corroborated by Cranmer's prologue (`cranmer1846miscellaneous`, p. 121) and the Act's own preamble (`statutesrealm1817vol3`, p. 896).
