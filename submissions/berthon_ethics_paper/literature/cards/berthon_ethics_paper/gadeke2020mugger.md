## Summary
Gädeke asks whether a mugger who points a gun at you dominates you, and answers that it depends on the social
structure around the encounter. Pettit's ecumenical account (the mere capacity to interfere arbitrarily is
domination; Pettit himself counts the mugger) faces a dilemma: either everyone the mugger could shoot is dominated,
which is too broad, or only the person at gunpoint is, which collapses domination into interference (pp. 200-204).
Her solution: domination is "a structurally constituted form of power" (p. 199), a robust capacity to interfere
that rests on norms and practices and "serves as the basis of a persisting status asymmetry" (p. 200); episodic,
opportunistic power is not domination. Interpersonal domination is triadic (dominator, dominated, peripheral agents
who reproduce the enabling norms, p. 207) and is "a position of power", not an action (p. 207). Systemic domination
names the disempowerment people suffer from the norms themselves when no particular dominator is present (p. 210).
Section III argues the distinction matters for conceptual clarity, for the freedom of persons, and for critical
social analysis, which otherwise misconstrues domination "as an anomaly perpetrated by individual wrongdoers" (p. 219).

## Key facts it relies on
- Thesis: "Domination, I maintain, is a structurally constituted form of power." (p. 199)
- "Purely interactional forms of power, which are based on an opportunistic capacity to interfere, however, are not
  instances of domination. Hence, whether and whom the mugger dominates depend on whether his power is
  structurally enabled and thus serves as the basis of a persisting status asymmetry." (p. 200)
- Pettit's definition as quoted: someone dominates another to the extent that "(1) they have the capacity to
  interfere (2) on an arbitrary basis (3) in certain choices that the other is in a position to make" (p. 201,
  citing Pettit 1997b, p. 52).
- "Paradigmatic cases of domination, like slavery and marriage, are institutionally stabilized forms of
  relationships." (p. 202) "Pettit himself explicitly considers the mugger to be an instance of domination." (p. 202,
  n. 13 citing Pettit 1997a, pp. 68-9; Lovett and Pettit 2018, p. 375)
- Richardson's objection: "it is implausible to say that a kidnapper dominates all potential victims, which might
  include all of us" (p. 203).
- "Domination, I propose, is best conceived of as generally characterized by the structurally constituted capacity
  to interfere (arbitrarily)." (p. 205) Note 23: "Not every structurally constituted capacity to interfere is
  dominating. The power of a judge is structurally constituted, yet whether it is subject to effective controls—and
  thus whether it is dominating—is a separate matter." (p. 205, n. 23)
- "interpersonal domination is not a dyadic, but a triadic relationship between dominator, dominated, and what
  Wartenberg calls peripheral agents" (p. 207). Domination "is not itself an action ... Rather, it is a position of
  power." (p. 207)
- The ordinary criminal: "Yet he won't dominate any of them, because his power is not structurally enabled and thus
  does not serve as the basis of a persisting status asymmetry." (p. 208)
- "I propose to call this kind of disempowerment systemic domination." (p. 210)
- On Pettit's breadth: "Pettit holds that, in principle, a vulnerability class may consist of just one person."
  (p. 215, n. 53, citing Pettit 1997b, p. 122)
- Why it matters: the alternative risks "misconstruing it as an anomaly perpetrated by individual wrongdoers" (p. 219).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Gädeke's is one side of a live dispute. Pettit (1997, pp. 68-9, as she reports) and Lovett and Pettit (2018)
  count episodic power as domination; Waldron, Shapiro and Friedman (p. 204) reduce domination to the prospect of
  interference; McCammon's "cheap domination" is the position she rejects (p. 208, n. 30). She explicitly does not
  give an account of arbitrariness (p. 201, n. 4).
- Her cases are marriage, slavery, sexual harassment and street crime; nothing on firms, customers or software.
  The transfer to a deploying organisation and the people its agents act on is the paper's, not hers.
- Lovett (SEP 2026, §1.3) describes republican freedom as "the absence of any structural dependence on arbitrary
  power", which is the same demand for structure; see `lovett2026republicanism`.

## Where the argument uses it
- N04, A7 (domination "of a customer by a firm's agent"): **narrows, and names a dispute the draft must
  acknowledge**. On Pettit's ecumenical account a one-off capacity to interfere suffices, and the draft's use is
  standard. On Gädeke's structural account, and on Lovett's "structural dependence", an episodic denial of a refund
  by an agent is domination only if the organisation's capacity is structurally enabled (terms that place
  responsibility on the user, no effective sanction, no forum, no exit) and expresses a persisting status asymmetry.
  The draft's bank, insurer and employer-screening cases (line 468) meet that test; the retail customer who can go
  elsewhere does not without argument.
- A1 (who may contest): her note 23 separates the structural capacity from arbitrariness; a declared envelope is a
  structurally constituted capacity, and whether it is "subject to effective controls" is the separate question the
  draft's fourth question asks.
- A8 (consent, exit): "a position of power" (p. 207) supports the draft's reply that the organisation dominates
  whether or not it misuses the agent; the triadic account (p. 207) adds that the model supplier and the law that
  leaves the terms unenforced are peripheral agents, which the draft can use to place Microsoft.
