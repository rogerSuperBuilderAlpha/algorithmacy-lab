## Summary
Crossref confirms title, venue and date (16 July 2024). The EconStor copy is the published version with a repository cover sheet ("Internet Policy Review 13(3) | 2024" in the running head). The authors explain how the AI Act delegates its requirements to harmonised standards (hENs) drafted by private standardisation organisations, and argue that the New Legislative Framework, built for product safety, fits fundamental rights badly. Three passages carry this facet. On composition: "the largest group of ISO stakeholders is the industry", which "can steer the choices of standard organisations towards their preferences" (p. 6); ISO and IEC standards "take precedence where they exist" in European adoption (p. 6). On bindingness: hENs are "voluntary in theory", but "in practice it is difficult if not impossible for manufacturers to choose a different avenue" (p. 11). On the affected: "Those impacted by the use of AI have no role to play in standardisation or certification processes (Edwards, 2022)", a point the authors report from Edwards; consumer, worker and small-business associations "do not officially have the right to participate in the work of ISO and IEC", and the industry-led composition "raises risks of regulatory capture" (p. 16).

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 6: "Additionally, the largest group of ISO stakeholders is the industry (Morikawa & Morrison, 2004)"; "this can also be problematic as the industry can steer the choices of standard organisations towards their preferences".
- p. 6: "As far as hENs are concerned, ISO and IEC standards take precedence where they exist".
- p. 11: "Even if Regulation 1025/2012 considers hENs to be voluntary in theory, as there are other ways to demonstrate compliance, in practice it is difficult if not impossible for manufacturers to choose a different avenue. Recourse to hENs is thus quasi obligatory for economic players".
- p. 16: "standardisation organisations are private law bodies, mostly led by the industry."
- p. 16: "Those impacted by the use of AI have no role to play in standardisation or certification processes (Edwards, 2022)."
- p. 16: "This industry-led composition also raises risks of regulatory capture and conflicts of interest, since industrial stakeholders are drafting the very same laws by which they will be governed."
- p. 16: a CE marking relating to fundamental rights "may be incorrectly interpreted by citizens as meaning that a given AI system respects fundamental rights."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Their industry-share figure for ISO is from Morikawa and Morrison (2004), not a count of SC 42 or JTC 21; for JTC 21 see `corporateeurope2025bias` and `europeancommission2025reply`.
