## Summary
Lecture III takes Amos as the first connected critic. The prophet's message "depends upon previous messages"; he
invokes the covenant and the law that his audience already knows, so criticism and repentance are "parasitic upon a
previously accepted and commonly understood morality" (pp. 59, 63). Jonah in Nineveh shows the contrast: a detached
critic with no shared history can invoke only a minimal universal code against "violence" (pp. 64-66). The prophets
also reinterpret the tradition ("They are parasitic upon the past, but they also give shape to the past upon which
they are parasitic", p. 70), and changed social conditions can give an old law "a new relevance" (p. 70).

## Key facts it relies on
- "The first thing to notice is that the prophetic message depends upon previous messages." ... "The past tense is
  significant." (p. 59)
- Repentance "implies that repentance is parasitic upon a previously accepted and commonly understood morality"
  (p. 63); Greenberg quoted: prophetic admonition "presupposes common ground on which prophet and audience stand"
  (p. 63).
- Detached critic: "Conversation is parasitic on commonality, and since commonality is minimal here, we can imagine
  only a minimal conversation." (p. 65) Amos judges foreign nations only for violations of "a minimal code" (p. 66).
- "The house of Israel is here, and it needs only to be ordered in accordance with its own laws." (p. 68)
- Historical limit, on the Exodus law of the pledge (Amos 2:8): "it isn't universally known, not the law and not the
  morality behind the law. We have different ideas about the pledge (the pawn), and it's not obvious that our ideas
  are unjust." (p. 69)
- "He speaks against a background of urban growth and class differentiation that gave that law, and all the Exodus
  laws, a new relevance." (p. 70)
- n. 17: "Judgment would be entirely arbitrary if it did not refer to standards with which the people were, or were
  supposed to be, familiar." (p. 70)
- Lecture II (rechecked): "Social criticism, for such people, is an internal argument" (p. 34); ruling ideology
  "sets standards that the rulers will not live up to" (p. 35).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Year and imprint (1988, University of Utah Press) remain unconfirmed by a front-matter record (earlier card).
- Walzer's internal critique is "internal" to a community sharing a tradition. Celikates and Flynn (SEP, §2.1) call
  this internal critique, as distinct from immanent critique; von Samson (2025) and Fraser (2023) press the
  objection that embedded norms lack authority without independent reasons.

## Where the argument uses it
- A7, refutation sought: **narrows, does not sink**. Walzer supports the shape of A7: a critic may hold a present
  actor to its predecessors' standards, in the past tense, *when the actor belongs to the community that holds them*
  (pp. 59, 63, 68). He also states the limit A7 must meet: a past rule does not bind those who do not share it, and
  he will not judge present practice by the Exodus pledge law (p. 69). The paper therefore needs to show that
  Microsoft and Nadella stand inside the tradition whose verdicts it cites (Anglo-American contract, consumer and
  voting law; professed responsible-AI standards), or fall back on the thin universal code, which in this paper is
  non-domination.
- A8 ("unless something has changed"): Walzer's p. 70 runs the other way: changed conditions (urban growth, class
  differentiation) gave the old law new relevance. Scale is an argument for the old standard, not only against it.
- Outline, "Standards of judgement": the three benchmarks rest on lecture II; lecture III is the citation for using
  them across time.
