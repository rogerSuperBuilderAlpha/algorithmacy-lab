## Summary
Hall's text of Henry VIII's speech to Parliament at the end of 1545, two years after the 1543 Act. The King blames clergy and laity alike for discord, tells the laity to bring complaints about preachers to the Council rather than judge "of your awne phantasticall opinions", and states the terms on which lay reading is held: it "is licensed you so to do, onely to informe your awne conscience" and instruct one's family, not to dispute. He deplores that Scripture is "disputed, rymed, song and iangeled in euery Alehouse and Tauerne". It is the Crown's own contemporary statement of the rationale behind the 1543 restriction: reading as a revocable licence, held for conscience and household, not for argument. The 1809 reprint has page numbers; the speech falls between running heads "864" and "867", the quoted passage on p. 866.

## Key facts it relies on
- "And although you be permitted to reade holy scripture, and to haue the word of God in your mother tongue, you must vnderstande that it is licensed you so to do, onely to informe your awne conscience, and to instruct your childre and famely, & not to dispute and make scripture, a railyng and a tauntyng stocke, against Priestes and Preachers (as many light persones do.)" (p. 866; OCR "railvng" read "railyng").
- "I am very sory to knowe & here, how vnreuerently that moste precious iuel the worde of God is disputed, rymed, song and iangeled in euery Alehouse and Tauerne, cotrary to the true meaninge & doctrine of thesame." (p. 866).
- On correction: laity who think a preacher errs should "come and declare it to some of our Counsayl or to vs, to whom is committed by God the high auchtority to reforme and ordre such causes" (p. 866; OCR "Counsavl").

## Critical notes from the literature
- Hall reports a speech; the wording is the chronicler's, printed 1548, and this is an 1809 reprint collated with 1548 and 1550. Treat it as Hall's account of the King's words.
- The speech names the reader's licence as a royal grant, which fits the 1543 Act's release clause (reading restored only if the King "shall of his clemencye thinke good", `statutesrealm1817vol3`, p. 896).

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (custody by an interested party): holds. The ruler describes vernacular reading as "licensed" by him and limited to private conscience, with correction routed to "our Counsayl or to vs".
- Refutation sought ("1543 as protective"): this is the Crown's protective framing (order, reverence, an end to tavern disputation). It is a stated ground, not a contemporary objection; the paper can cite it as the defence the failure has to answer.
