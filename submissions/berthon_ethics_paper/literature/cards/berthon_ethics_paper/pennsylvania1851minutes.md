## Summary
The minutes record a treaty council at Easton in July and August 1757 between Governor William Denny, the Crown's
deputy agent George Croghan, and Teedyuscung, "King of the Delawares", who spoke through his own interpreter, John
Pumpshire. Teedyuscung demanded a clerk of his own choosing to take minutes alongside the Governor's clerks and to
compare them at the table; he said he would not treat without one. Four provincial commissioners (Masters, Fox,
Galloway, Hughes) backed him in a written remonstrance of 23 July, calling the demand reasonable because "he and his
People are illiterate Persons" (pp. 656-657). Croghan and Weiser suspected Quaker influence (pp. 657-658). The
Governor called the demand "entirely unprecedented and against my Judgement" but withdrew his objection (pp. 663-664).
At the public treaty of 25 July Teedyuscung seated Charles Thomson ("Thompson" in the minutes), master of the
Quaker school in Philadelphia, beside the official clerk Trent, and Thomson "began to take Minutes, without asking
Permission of the Governor" (p. 665). On 1 August Teedyuscung said his councillors had agreed every word of their
reply with their secretary, had it read back "three or four Times", and offered the written text (p. 689); he
defended the clerk as a way "to exceed my Ancestors" (p. 690). Page images of the six pages were checked against the
OCR (archive.org leaves n677, n678, n685, n686, n710, n711).

## Key facts it relies on
- The demand (commissioners' remonstrance, 23 July 1757): Teedyuscung "demanded that he might have a Clerk, to be
  appointed by himself, to take down the Minutes of the Treaty, who should compare his Proceedings with the other
  Clerks before they leave the Table; This he insisted was the most certain means to searching out the Truth, and of
  obtaining Justice to the Persons who should prove to be injured" (p. 656).
- Refused, he said the Governor would "lead him on Blindfold and in the Dark" (p. 656) and asked "that this Demand
  and Refusal might be taken down in Writing, that it might be represented to the great King" (p. 657).
- The commissioners: "He appears to us to have an undoubted Right, as a King and Chief of a Nation, to have a Clerk"
  to take down the minutes, "And this seems more natural, as he and his People are illiterate Persons" (p. 657). (The
  words between, "of"/"or Secretary", are unclear in both the OCR and the image, so I do not quote them.)
- The Governor: "No Indian Chief, before you, ever demanded to have a Clerk, and none has ever been appointed for
  Indians in former Treaties" (p. 664); "his Demand to appoint a Clerk of his own was entirely unprecedented and
  against my Judgement, yet as he so warmly insisted on it, I should no longer oppose it" (p. 664).
- The settled method: the minutes "should be taken by Mr. Trent, and read and compared every day in Council, and
  that a Copy of the whole should be delivered to the Governor at the end of the Treaty" (p. 665).
- Memorandum: Teedyuscung "called for Charles Thompson, Master of the Publick Quaker School in the City of
  Philadelphia; placed him by Mr. Trent at the Table, and said he had chosen him for his Clerk; Whereupon he sat
  down and began to take Minutes, without asking Permission of the Governor, who took no further notice of it"
  (p. 665).
- Checking the text (1 August 1757): the councillors "fully understood one another, and agreed on every word" and
  then had their secretary write it down; "we ordered him to read it [o]ver three or four Times, and approved it"
  (p. 689; the page image clips the first letter).
- Teedyuscung: "Don’t you see that I aim, by having a Clerk of my own, to exceed my Ancestors by having everything
  for the best" (p. 690). Croghan answered that "this is quite a new Method, and what was never before practised"
  (p. 690).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The minutes are the Governor's council's own record, written by the side that resisted the clerk; Croghan's and
  Weiser's claim that Quakers prompted the demand (pp. 657-658) is theirs.
- Hagedorn (1988; `berthon_ethics_course/hagedorn1988friend.md`) covers interpreters at Anglo-Iroquois councils;
  Merrell (*Into the American Woods*, 1999) treats Teedyuscung's clerk at length. I could not open Merrell (lending
  only) or Fleming (2016, Gettysburg Historical Journal; Cloudflare challenge).
- Thomson's minutes, if they survive separately, were not opened.

## Where the argument uses it
- B1 (corrections share a property; H10): **holds**. The bound party, unable to read, put his own record-keeper at
  the table, with a duty to compare minutes before the parties rose, and had his own text read back until his
  councillors approved it. The work of keeping and checking the record moved to someone owed to the weaker side.
- A2 (record kept by one side; H07): **holds** as the failure's mirror. The Governor's council treated a second
  record as an affront and called it unprecedented (p. 664).
- A3 (contemporaries objected): **holds**. Teedyuscung and four provincial commissioners objected in writing, in
  1757, to a treaty record kept by one side (pp. 656-657).
- Duty D04 (counter-delegation: the person's own record and reader): a direct historical precedent.
