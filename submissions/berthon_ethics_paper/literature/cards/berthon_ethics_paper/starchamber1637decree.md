## Summary
The Star Chamber decree of 11 July 1637, reprinted by Arber in 1868 from the King's Printer's edition of 1637. It confirms the Elizabethan decrees "with these Additions, Explanations, and Alterations", requires every book to be licensed by named officers and "first entred into the Registers Booke of the Company of Stationers" on pain of being disabled for ever from printing, assigns licensing by subject (common law to the chief justices, state history to the Secretaries, heraldry to the Earl Marshal, all else to the Archbishop of Canterbury or Bishop of London or the university chancellors), fixes "but Twentie Master Printers", names them, and gives the Archbishop or Bishop of London, with six High Commissioners, the filling of vacancies. Item XXXIII sends one copy of every new book to Bodley's library at Oxford. The decree is the 1662 Licensing Act's model (Deazley, `deazley2008commentary1637`). The OCR renders long s as "f"; quotations below normalise it.

## Key facts it relies on
- Ground: "diuers libellous, seditious, and mutinous bookes haue beene vnduly printed, and other bookes and papers without licence, to the disturbance of the peace of the Church and State" (preamble; p. 9 by count back from the running head "10", the OCR printing no head on this page).
- Item II: no book to be printed "vnlesse the same Booke or Pamphlet ... shall be first lawfully licenced and authorized onely by such person and persons as are hereafter expressed, and by no other, and shall be also first entred into the Registers Booke of the Company of Stationers; vpon paine that euery Printer offending therein, shall be for euer hereafter disabled to use or exercise the Art or Mysterie of Printing" (pp. 9-10).
- Item IV: the licenser certifies "that there is nothing in that Booke or Books contained, that is contrary to Christian Faith, and the Doctrine and Discipline of the Church of England, nor against the State or Gouernment, nor contrary to good life, or good manners" (p. 11).
- Item XV: "there shall be but Twentie Master Printers allowed to haue the vse of one Presse or more", named; vacancies filled by "the Lord Arch-Bishop of Canterbury, or the Lord Bishop of London ... taking to him or them six other high Commissioners", "Prouided that they exceed not the number of Twentie, besides His Maiesties Printers, and the Printers allowed for the Vniuersities" (p. 16).
- Item XXXIII: one copy of every new book to the Common Hall of the Company "to be sent to the Librarie at Oxford" (p. 23).
- The Commons order of 29 January 1642 (reprinted after the decree; p. 24 is inferred by sequence: the order follows "FINIS" with no running head in the OCR, so cite it as Arber 1868, after p. 23): the Master and Wardens are to see that printers "doe neither print, nor reprint any thing without the name and consent of the Author"; a printer who does so is to "be proceeded against, as both Printer and Author thereof".

## Critical notes from the literature
- Arber's reprint follows one 1637 print; I did not compare it with Arber's *Transcript* vol. 4 p. 528 (the text Copyright History cites), which is lending-only on archive.org.
- Deazley (`deazley2008commentary1637`) reports Blagden's view that the decree "was promoted by the Company for the benefit of Stationers" and Loewenstein's that "with two exceptions ... everything in the new decree served the monopolies of the London stationers"; those are quoted secondary judgements.
- The Commons order of 1642 requires the author's name and consent and treats a printer who omits them as the author; it serves responsibility for content and, secondarily, the author's consent (`deazley2008commentary1637` reads the name rule as "authorial responsibility"). It is Parliament's, issued after Star Chamber's abolition in 1641, not the Company's.

## Where the argument uses it
- A2 (custody by an interested party; the gate held by the few): **holds**. The decree makes the Company's Register a condition of print, caps the trade at twenty masters, and gives the bishops the power to fill the places.
- Refutation sought (licensers serving authors or readers): the decree serves Church, State and the Company; the author-consent rule appears only in the Commons order of 1642. Narrows any claim that the licensing regime protected authors.
