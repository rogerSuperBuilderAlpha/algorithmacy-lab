## Summary
The revised Product Liability Directive is the strongest answer I found to "notice without standing": a remedy that the injured person holds. "Product" now "includes ... software" (Art 4(1)). Any natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product "is entitled to compensation" (Art 5(1)), and claims may be brought by a person acting on behalf of injured persons (Art 5(2)(b)). Defectiveness considers the product's "ability to continue to learn or acquire new features after it is placed on the market" (Art 7(2)(c)) and "relevant product safety requirements" (Art 7(2)(f)), which would include AI Act requirements. Courts presume defectiveness where the defendant fails to disclose evidence, where mandatory safety requirements were breached, or where the claimant faces "excessive difficulties, in particular due to technical or scientific complexity" (Art 10(2), (4)). The limits matter for the argument: compensable damage is death, personal injury including "medically recognised damage to psychological health", damage to non-professional property, and destruction or corruption of non-professional data (Art 6(1)). Discrimination, loss of a job or a loan, and other fundamental-rights harms that AI Act Art 3(49)(c) counts as serious incidents are not on the list. It applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026 (Art 2(1)), the transposition deadline (Art 22(1)).

## Key facts it relies on
- Art 4(1): "'product' means all movables ... it includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials and software".
- Art 5(1): "Member States shall ensure that any natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product (the 'injured person') is entitled to compensation in accordance with this Directive."
- Art 6(1): compensation "shall apply in respect of only the following types of damage: (a) death or personal injury, including medically recognised damage to psychological health; (b) damage to, or destruction of, any property, except ... (iii) property used exclusively for professional purposes; (c) destruction or corruption of data that are not used for professional purposes."
- Art 7(1): defective where "it does not provide the safety that a person is entitled to expect or that is required under Union or national law"; 7(2)(c), (f), (h) as summarised.
- Art 10(4): a court "shall presume the defectiveness of the product or the causal link ... or both" where "the claimant faces excessive difficulties, in particular due to technical or scientific complexity" and shows it is "likely".
- Art 2(1): "This Directive shall apply to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026."

## Critical notes from the literature
- I found no commentary this round. The Commission's proposed AI Liability Directive, which would have covered fault-based claims beyond these damage heads, was not checked this round; its status (reported withdrawn in 2025) is unverified here and should be confirmed from the OJ before use.

## Where the argument uses it
- O02: the objection's best support. For physical injury, psychological injury and data loss caused by AI software, EU law (from December 2026) gives the injured a claim, with burden-easing for technical complexity. The paper must concede this.
- A4, M9: the concession is bounded. The Directive is a remedy after harm, with no notice duty; it does not reach the fundamental-rights incidents of AI Act Art 3(49)(c); it is national-court litigation by the injured. Notice "to those affected" (P20) and this remedy are not linked anywhere I read.
