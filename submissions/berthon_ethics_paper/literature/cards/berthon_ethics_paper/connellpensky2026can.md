## Summary
Abstract only; paraphrase only. Crossref confirms the article (Computers & Education 250, article 105640, September 2026). OpenAlex lists it as hybrid open access under CC BY-NC, but the only location is the publisher's page, which returned HTTP 403 to a plain request; I did not try further. Per the abstract, a 90-minute set of open online modules with embedded practice and feedback was tested on 1,368 undergraduate and graduate students in 65 course sections, randomised by section to modules or no modules between pre- and post-tests. The modules improved genAI knowledge, prompt-writing skill, fact- and source-checking skill and self-efficacy; they had a null effect on critically evaluating bias in genAI output. Gains were reported as equitable across sex, race and ethnicity, level, first-generation status and discipline.

## Key facts it relies on
- Paraphrase of the abstract: cluster-randomised by course section; 1,368 students; 65 sections; 90-minute modules; gains in knowledge, prompting, fact- and source-checking and self-efficacy; null on bias evaluation; gains equitable across the listed groups.

## Critical notes from the literature

## Where the argument uses it
- M11, E16: complicates. A well-designed training can raise people's ability to check AI output, equitably, in a randomised design. Against Chiang and Yin's null for low performers (`chiang2022exploring`), this is the strongest refutation evidence I found that skilling works. The limits for the paper: the trainees are university students learning to *use* genAI, the developer of the systems did not run it, and the null on bias evaluation sits where the people acted on are most exposed.
