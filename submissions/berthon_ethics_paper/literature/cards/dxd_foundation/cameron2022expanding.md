## What it argues
Cameron and Rahman ask how platforms' "algorithmically mediated customer control" changes the relation between control and workers' covert resistance (PDF p. 4). Customers rate workers; algorithms tally ratings and use them for matching, visibility and deactivation.

Their model has three stages (Figure 1; PDF pp. 15–16). Before a task, the platform matches but the customer cannot yet rate, so workers have the most latitude: drivers phone riders to vet them and cancel those who fail "the attitude test," or talk riders into cancelling; freelancers ask for "a promise of 5 stars" before starting (PDF pp. 18–20). During a task, the customer monitors, and workers use interactive tactics that exploit customers' ignorance of the algorithm: splitting one project into several contracts to collect several ratings, holding work hostage for a good rating, ending work early so that no rating can be given (PDF pp. 21–25). After a task, workers have least latitude: drivers rate a hostile rider low first ("mediated retaliation") in the belief that the platform discounts symmetric low ratings, and workers file disputes they call "Hail Mary" moves (PDF pp. 26–29).

Two findings bear on learning. "Some workers thus believe the rating algorithm was not an actual performance evaluation to help them learn how to improve but, instead, a reflection of customer whims" (PDF p. 17). And what workers knew of the algorithm they inferred from experience: "While drivers did not have firsthand information about how the algorithmic rating system worked, they developed last-ditch efforts" (PDF p. 27). The best workers develop "deep knowledge of the labor process," including "deciphering how the platforms' algorithms collect and use their data" (PDF p. 32). The tactics in the first two stages "largely rely on fragile information asymmetries" that the platform could remove by changing its monitoring (PDF p. 34).

Scope. The paper studies individual, covert resistance. It contrasts itself with work on "collective resistance in platform settings," saying "we know less about how individual workers resist" (PDF p. 4), and lists "covert resistance enacted at an individual level" as a boundary (PDF p. 38).

## Relation to the argument
**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The paper is qualitative support for learning a mediator by acting on it: workers form working theories of matching and rating from what happens to them and act on those theories. It also supports P9's condition and Part 1's warning: the one feedback signal the platform gives, the customer rating, was judged by some workers to reflect customer whims rather than to help them improve (PDF p. 17). Those workers learned around the signal, not from it.

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The passage saying "Platforms could, of course, change this policy at any moment" (PDF p. 34) is the owner's unilateral power over the convention, observed from the worker side. The authors add that after data collection FindWork "implemented an algorithm that reminded workers and customers that they could not move their conversations off platform," though it "stopped short of taking action" against violators (PDF p. 34).

**What it does not support.** Collective learning or collective pushback. The workers' theories travel through discussion boards (many quotations are coded "DB"), but the analysis is of individual tactics.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 266 cites the paper for "Workers pool what they observe and push back together (Cameron & Rahman, 2022)." Not supported as written. The paper says it studies individual covert resistance and sets collective resistance aside (PDF pp. 4, 38). Its data do include discussion-board posts in which drivers share tactics (e.g., the airport-queue cancellation trick, PDF p. 20), which would support "workers share what they observe" but not "push back together." For pooled theories and collective action, the draft's neighbours on line 266 (Lee et al. 2015; Kellogg, Valentine & Christin 2020) or the works this paper cites for collective resistance (Chen 2018; Lei 2021; Rizzo & Atzeni 2020) are closer. The paper does support the line's preceding sentence, that drivers "form working theories from what the system does in response to their own moves" (PDF pp. 27, 32), though the draft does not cite it there.
