## What it argues

The Directive's purpose is "to improve working conditions and the protection of personal data in platform work" by facilitating correct employment status, "promoting transparency, fairness, human oversight, safety and accountability in algorithmic management", and improving transparency about platform work (Art. 1(1)). It has two layers of scope: minimum rights for platform workers who are, or are deemed to be, employees, and data-protection rules on algorithmic management that also cover "those who do not have an employment contract" (Art. 1(2)).

The provisions that bear on the paper:

- **Employment presumption.** A rebuttable presumption of employment "where facts indicating direction and control … are found", with the burden on the platform to rebut (Art. 5(1)); for ongoing contracts it applies only from 2 December 2026 (Art. 5(6)).
- **Banned processing (Art. 7(1)).** No processing of data on "emotional or psychological state", private conversations, data collected while not working, prediction of the exercise of fundamental rights, inference of protected characteristics, or biometric identification against a database. It applies from recruitment (Art. 7(2)).
- **Disclosure (Art. 9).** Platforms must inform workers, their representatives and, on request, authorities of automated systems in use, including "the main parameters … and the relative importance of those main parameters" and the grounds for restricting, suspending or terminating an account (Art. 9(1)(b)(iii)–(iv)), "in a transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form" (Art. 9(2)).
- **Human oversight (Art. 10).** Evaluation of the impact of individual automated decisions at least every two years, with workers' representatives (Art. 10(1)); overseers with "authority necessary … including for overriding automated decisions" and protection from dismissal (Art. 10(2)). Where oversight "identifies a high risk of discrimination" or finds that decisions "have infringed the rights" of a worker, the platform "shall take the steps necessary, including, if appropriate, the modification of the … system or the discontinuation of its use" (Art. 10(3)). "Any decision to restrict, suspend or terminate the contractual relationship or the account … or any other decision of equivalent detriment shall be taken by a human being" (Art. 10(5)).
- **Human review (Art. 11).** A right to an explanation and a contact person (Art. 11(1)); a right to request review, answered "within two weeks of receipt of the request" (Art. 11(2)); where a decision infringes rights, the platform "shall rectify that decision without delay and in any case within two weeks of the adoption of the decision. Where such rectification is not possible, the digital labour platform shall offer adequate compensation" (Art. 11(3)). Article 11 does not apply to persons who are also business users under the P2B Regulation (Art. 11(5)).
- **Information and consultation (Art. 13).** Consultation of workers' representatives, in the sense of Directive 2002/14/EC, extends to decisions likely to introduce or substantially change automated systems (Art. 13(2)); representatives "may be assisted by an expert of their choice", paid by the platform where it has more than 250 workers in that Member State (Art. 13(3)).
- **Enforcement.** Data-protection authorities enforce Arts. 7–11, with GDPR Art. 83(5) fine ceilings (Art. 24(1)); access to evidence, including confidential evidence, by court order (Art. 21); private communication channels the platform may not monitor (Art. 20).
- **Room above the floor.** Member States may adopt provisions "more favourable to platform workers" (Art. 26(2)) and should promote collective bargaining, including on algorithmic management (Art. 25); collective agreements may set more specific rules (Art. 28).
- **Dates.** Transposition "by 2 December 2026" (Art. 29(1)); entry into force on "the twentieth day following that of its publication" (Art. 31), publication being 11 November 2024, so 1 December 2024; Commission review by 2 December 2029 (Art. 30).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P12 and ruling D8.** The Directive is a direct case of a convention moving toward the weaker party under outside force: it constrains what the platform may process (Art. 7), who may take termination decisions (Art. 10(5)), and what must happen to a rights-infringing decision (Art. 11(3)). It also shows that the outside force does not take the convention out of the platform's hands. Overseers are the platform's own staff (Art. 10(2)); workers' representatives are informed and consulted, and the Directive grants no co-decision. That is the draft's "no veto", and it is accurate for the Directive as such.

**Ruling D3 and the "beyond disclosure" framing.** The Directive is not purely structural. Its Article 9 is a disclosure regime of the first-generation kind (main parameters and their relative weight), and Article 11(1) is an explanation right. The structural provisions sit beside these. The paper's sentence that recent statutes "move beyond disclosure" holds if it says they add structural duties to disclosure, not that they replace it.

## Caution

Recitals not read, so interpretive glosses there (for example on what "equivalent detriment" covers) are unchecked. A directive binds only through national transposition, due 2 December 2026; no national law is examined here, and on today's date (14 October 2026) most Member States' measures may not yet be in force. The Directive regulates platforms that organise work; it does not reach consumer-facing platforms or marketplaces generally, so it cannot carry claims about "platforms" at large.
