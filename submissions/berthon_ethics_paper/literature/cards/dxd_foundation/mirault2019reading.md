## What it argues
Mirault, Snell and Grainger ask why skilled readers read unspaced text as well as they do. They test two candidates: top-down support from sentence structure and bottom-up word identification (p. 22). Shuffling word order removes sentence structure; if structure carries the unspaced reader, the shuffle should hurt unspaced reading more.

They start from the established cost, "slower by about 40% to 70% than reading normally spaced text (Rayner et al., 1998; Rayner & Pollatsek, 1996)" (p. 22), and from the standing conclusion that removing spaces disrupts both saccadic planning and word identification (Perea & Acha, 2009; Rayner et al., 1998; p. 22).

Findings (Results, pp. 24–27):
- Probe-word errors: spacing b = 1.11 (t = 6.84), order b = 0.32 (t = 3.16), interaction b = 0.40 (t = 3.30). Shuffling raised errors much more in unspaced text (b = 0.75, t = 10.81) than in spaced text (b = 0.17, t = 1.46, not reliable; p. 24).
- Total viewing time per word: the cost of shuffling was 49 ms unspaced against 39 ms spaced. In second-pass viewing after a regression, shuffled unspaced words took 517 ms against 435 ms ordered, while spaced words showed no reliable difference (342 against 398 ms; p. 25).
- Regressions: the shuffle effect on regression probability was smaller in unspaced text (p. 25).
- Saccade length rose with the length of the fixated word equally in spaced and unspaced text (no interaction, b = 0.00, t = 1.14); the authors conclude this is "clear evidence that the fixated word was identified", with its length then used to aim the next saccade (p. 28).

Their summary (Abstract, p. 22): skilled readers' ease with unspaced text "is due to a combination of an increased use of bottom-up word identification in guiding the timing and targeting of eye movements, plus an increased interactivity between word identification and sentence-level processing."

## Relation to the argument
It supports premise P4 (skilled readers adapt to missing spaces) and gives a concrete mechanism for it: they identify the word they are on and use its length to place the next fixation, which a reader of spaced text gets from the gap. That helps the paper say what the space does for the reader (premise P5): it supplies for free what the unspaced reader must compute word by word. It also means the cost reported in P3 (40% to 70%) is a cost of serial processing, not of inability.

Footnote 2 on page 23 notes "the interesting parallel here with the theory advanced by Paul Saenger (1997) that, historically, the introduction of spacing between words enabled the development of silent reading," given that unspaced text was traditionally read aloud in public. The authors offer it as a parallel and test nothing about it. For the dispute in ruling D2 it is a cognitive-science nod to Saenger, not an independent test of his chronology (premise P2) or of his causal claim.

## Caution
Twenty students, one language, one-line neutral sentences with no semantic predictability, in a script that normally has spaces. The shuffled condition changes grammar and meaning together, so it cannot isolate syntax. The authors report that the interaction with order appears in some measures and not in others (it is absent in first-fixation and gaze duration). The key order-by-spacing interactions rest on two measures (probe accuracy, total viewing time). The paper does not test learners or historical readers.
