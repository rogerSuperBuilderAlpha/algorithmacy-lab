## What it argues
Book 1 is Quintilian's account of the education that comes before rhetoric. Three parts bear on the paper.

**Start young, and teach reading as slow drill.** He rejects the view that boys should wait till seven to read (1.1.15–19), sets the order of teaching (letter shapes before names, 1.1.24–25; syllables with no shortcut, 1.1.30), and on speed: "You will hardly believe how much reading is delayed by undue haste" (1.1.32, Loeb p. 37). "Reading must therefore first be sure, then connected, while it must be kept slow for a considerable time, until practice brings speed unaccompanied by error" (1.1.33). The difficulty he names is attention, not the page: "it is necessary to keep the eyes on what follows while reading out what precedes, with the resulting difficulty that the attention of the mind must be divided, the eyes and voice being differently engaged" (1.1.34, p. 37).

**Orthography is judged by its cost to learners.** In 1.7 he treats spelling as "the servant of usage" that "undergoes frequent change" (1.7.11), and rejects an old convention of adding an *e* before *i* in endings because "such a practice will be an actual hindrance to those who are learning to read" (1.7.17, p. 139). His stated test, "within the limits prescribed by usage," is that "words should be spelt as they are pronounced," since "the use of letters is to preserve the sound of words and to deliver them to readers as a sacred trust" (1.7.30–31, p. 145). Chapter 1.7 names changes in letters, doubling, diphthongs and abbreviations; it says nothing about the spacing of words.

**Reading aloud is taught by practice and by understanding.** "Reading remains for consideration. In this connexion there is much that can only be taught in actual practice, as for instance when the boy should take breath, at what point he should introduce a pause into a line, where the sense ends or begins" (1.8.1, p. 147); his "golden rule" is that "he must understand what he reads" (1.8.2). At 1.5.6 he notes that mistakes in speaking come from "separation or combination of syllables" — an observation about sound, not layout.

## Relation to the argument
Facet K asks whether anyone in the history of reading instruction says that separating words shortened the training of readers (rulings D1, D2). This book is the clearest ancient account of how reading was taught, and it contains no such claim and no mention of word division. The cost of learning it reports is drill, divided attention between eye and voice, and the reader's task of finding where to breathe and pause, which fell on the pupil and was taught by the teacher. That complicates premise P6 (separation lowered a cost of reading) by showing where a Roman teacher placed the burden. It also gives the paper something positive for D10 (history as a guide): Quintilian does judge an orthographic convention by whether it hinders learners (1.7.17), so the idea that a writing convention can be weighed by its cost to the learner is ancient, though he never applies it to spacing.
