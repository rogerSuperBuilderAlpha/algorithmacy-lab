# Nothaft, C. P. E. (2020). Medieval Europe's satanic ciphers: On the genesis of a modern myth. *British Journal for the History of Mathematics*, 35(2), 107–136.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/26375451.2020.1726050 · **Read depth:** full_text (accepted manuscript, 56 pp., from Oxford ORA; locators are the manuscript's page numbers, not the journal's; the reference list from p. 44 on was skimmed, not checked entry by entry) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** historiographical source-tracing; follows one claim through many modern authors, 1883 to 2017, against medieval statutes and texts · **Parties modeled:** other (authors and their sources) · **Facets:** F · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Nothaft traces and rejects a story with three core claims: zero was feared as devilish; church or state therefore outlawed it; and zero and Hindu-Arabic numerals (HAN) were "only used in hiding", which supposedly explains "cipher" as "secret code" (abstract; p. 8). He calls the first and third claims false "in the sense that no compelling evidence to support them has ever surfaced" and the second false for the Church (p. 8).

Part 1 (pp. 9–17) sets out the actual record. HAN first appear in Latin Europe in a manuscript dated 976 (p. 9); written HAN arithmetic arrives in the first half of the twelfth century through translated Arabic works; the textbooks of Sacrobosco and Alexandre de Villedieu become part of Arts-faculty teaching in the early thirteenth century, and Ambrosetti counts 161 manuscripts of the *Carmen* (pp. 10–11, n. 12). Numerals coexisted with Roman and other systems for centuries; citing Crossley, he reports HAN predominant across all contexts "only around 1500" and adoption "slow, problematic, and spasmodic" (p. 13). Some milieus (astronomy, computus) moved faster than others (p. 13).

The two prohibitions that carry the myth are small. Florence's money-changers' guild (Arte del Cambio, 1299) barred members from writing accounts "by means of or in the letter of the abacus, but let him write it openly and in full by way of letters" (pp. 14–15); motive is "a matter of speculation" (p. 15). The Paduan "1348" rule has no primary source; Nothaft finds the substance in Padua's 1331 statutes, which require booksellers to mark name and price on the cover in clear letters, "non [per] zyphras", and near-identical rules at Bologna (1317/47, 1432) and Florence (1387) (p. 16). These governed how a price was displayed to customers, not how sellers kept accounts, and transparency may have been the aim since "not everyone would have been trained in a scuola d'abbaco" (pp. 16–17). Trieste (1350) barred money changers and merchants from books *per abachum*; Frankfurt (1494) barred clerks from using the numerals in public books (p. 17). Neither is "a blanket 'ban'" (p. 17).

Part 2 (pp. 17–42) follows the myth. Leo Jordan's 1905 article is its source: "leaps of logic" turn William of Malmesbury's remark that the devil taught Gerbert into evidence about HAN (pp. 18–20), and read a harmless Salem algorismus line and Alain de Lille's quips as hostility (pp. 21–23). Smith and Karpinski (1911), Yeldham (1926), Dantzig (1930), Hogben (1936), Menninger (1969) and Murray (1978) repeat it (pp. 24–31). Rotman (1987), Ifrah (1994/98), Kaplan, Teresi, Seife and Green add inventions, such as mathematicians burned at the stake (pp. 31–42). A footnote chain shows citations running back through Taylor (1883) to unknown sources (n. 24, p. 16). The conclusion calls it "a game of telephone" (p. 42).

## Relation to the argument
It cuts against any use of the 1299 Florentine rule as a case of a convention banned by authority. Read plainly, it is a local guild rule about how entries are written, and the 1331 Padua rule is a legibility rule for customers. For the paper's word-separation precedent (D1, D10) the lesson is about method: Nothaft shows how a tidy story about a notation travels from secondary source to secondary source with no primary support. That is a caution for the paper's own history of word separation (P2: the chronology is Saenger's alone). It also supplies one point on numerals as a design change adopted unevenly by trade (Crossley's "slow, problematic, and spasmodic").

Facet check: the facet read only the 1299 and Padua section and the abstract. The full text confirms its account of Article 102 and of the pages. It adds that the Paduan rule dates from 1331, not 1348, and that Nothaft treats the motive for 1299 as unknown; Nothaft lists fraud-prevention as a "popular" explanation for 1299 but does not endorse it (p. 15, n. 22).
