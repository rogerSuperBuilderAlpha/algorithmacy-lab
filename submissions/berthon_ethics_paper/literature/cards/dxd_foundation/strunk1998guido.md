## What it argues

Guido opens the Prologue with a complaint: singers "will never sing by themselves without a master a single antiphon, not even a short one," though they sing every day "for a hundred years" (p. 2). He blames the lack of a common written form: antiphoners "are not one, nor yet a few, but rather as many as there are the masters in the various churches" (p. 2). He urges that no one provide an antiphoner with neumes who does not know "this art" (p. 2).

His remedy is a book written "any intelligent and studious person may learn the chant by means of it; after he has thoroughly learned a part of it through a master, he will unhesitatingly understand the rest of it by himself without one" (pp. 2–3). He offers a demonstration: boys "who until now have been beaten for their gross ignorance of the Psalms" sing the antiphon correctly by themselves (p. 3).

The excerpt then describes the layout (p. 3): each sound "is found always in its own row", lines are "drawn close together", some sounds on the lines and others in the spaces, "certain letters of the monochord are written at the beginning of the lines or spaces", and the lines are gone over in two colours, yellow for the letter C and red for F. The consequence he draws is that neume shapes alone are insufficient: "however perfect the formation of the neumes might be, it is altogether meaningless and worthless without the addition of letters or colors" (p. 3). The reader also has to "learn by heart a fair number of melodies" so as to recognise sounds, because recalling "with understanding" differs from singing "by rote" (p. 4).

The *Epistola* excerpt contrasts two methods for finding an unknown melody. The older one, sounding each letter on the monochord, he calls "childish, good indeed for beginners, but very bad for pupils who have made some progress" (p. 4). His own method is to fix in memory the starting note of each phrase of a familiar melody (six phrases, six notes, p. 5). It works "provided there is someone present to teach the pupil, not merely from a written textbook, but rather by our practice of informal discussion" (p. 4). His one result is anecdotal: "some of them were able before the third day to sing an unknown melody with ease" (pp. 4–5). He ends: "All these matters, which we can hardly explain in writing, we can easily lay bare by a simple discussion" (p. 5).

## Relation to the argument

**P5, P6 and the author's ruling D6.** The excerpt is a medieval designer's own list of a bundle: ruled lines with one pitch per row, letters and colours as keys, modal formulas, memorised melodies, a monochord and a teacher. Guido claims no single element suffices; the neumes are "worthless" without letters or colours, and the written method works only with a person present. This supports the argument map's dropping of "the" move and D6's "complex, BUT we are looking for the most critical" move. His passage identifies the line-and-letter device as what makes the identical-pitch rule readable at a glance (p. 3), which may be the candidate for "most critical", but it is the author's reading and the excerpt says nothing of ranking.

**P6, most for readers still learning.** The claim he makes is about learners: after learning a part "through a master" the pupil continues "without one." That is a reduced need for the teacher, not its removal, and the excerpt states the teacher's presence as a condition of his own method (p. 4). It supports a cost lowered for learners, not a widened population; the excerpt contains no counts.

**The "ten years to one or two" figure.** The excerpts contain no such figure. The numbers Guido gives are rhetorical ("a hundred years," p. 2) and the "before the third day ... in many weeks" report (pp. 4–5), which concerns his memorising method and not the ruled staff. This agrees with the facet note that the claim is unverified.

**P10, whom the convention serves.** The excerpt shows an author who writes the convention and urges others to adopt it, from a position as a reformer who says his way is the rule ("a common rule of art", p. 2). It gives no evidence on who could withdraw it. Specialist work (Haines 2008, `haines2008origins`, p. 331, per the facet entry) separates what Guido invented from what he popularised and notes his self-promotion; I read only the facet's quotation of that, not the article.
