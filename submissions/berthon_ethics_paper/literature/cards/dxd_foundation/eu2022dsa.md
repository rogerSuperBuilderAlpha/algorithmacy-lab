## What it argues

The Regulation sets "harmonised rules for a safe, predictable and trusted online environment" (Art. 1(1)). It keeps the conditional liability exemptions for mere conduit, caching and hosting (Arts. 4–6) and adds duties in tiers. Every intermediary must say in its terms and conditions what it restricts and how: "information on any policies, procedures, measures and tools used for the purpose of content moderation, including algorithmic decision-making and human review, as well as the rules of procedure of their internal complaint handling system" (Art. 14(1)). It must act "in a diligent, objective and proportionate manner" with "due regard" to users' fundamental rights (Art. 14(4)), and "inform the recipients of the service of any significant change to the terms and conditions" (Art. 14(2); no period is stated). Recital 45 gives the reason: "the avoidance of unfair or arbitrary outcomes".

Hosting providers, including platforms, owe a "clear and specific statement of reasons" for removals, demotion, payment restrictions and suspension or termination of service or account made on grounds of illegality or incompatibility with terms (Art. 17(1)). It must give the facts relied on, whether automated means were used, the legal or contractual ground "and explanations as to why", and redress options (Art. 17(3)). The duty applies only where contact details are known and not to "deceptive high-volume commercial content" (Art. 17(2)). Online platforms must also run an "effective internal complaint-handling system" open for at least six months after the decision, free and electronic (Art. 20(1)); handle complaints "in a timely, non-discriminatory, diligent and non-arbitrary manner" and reverse decisions the complaint shows to be unfounded (Art. 20(4)); give a reasoned answer (Art. 20(5)); and decide "under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means" (Art. 20(6)). Micro and small platforms are exempt from this section unless designated very large (Art. 19).

Beyond the platform: users may choose a certified out-of-court dispute body (Art. 21(1)), but that body "shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement" (Art. 21(2)). The platform pays the body's fees if it loses and the user pays nothing unless acting in bad faith (Art. 21(5)). Court access stays open (Art. 21(1)). Users can complain to their Digital Services Coordinator (Art. 53), and may seek compensation for damage from an infringement (Art. 54). Fines reach 6% of worldwide annual turnover (Art. 52(3); for very large platforms, Art. 74(1)), with periodic penalties of 5% of average daily turnover (Art. 76).

For platforms with 45 million or more monthly EU recipients (Art. 33(1)), the Regulation reaches the algorithm. Providers must assess systemic risks from "the design of their recommender systems and any other relevant algorithmic system" (Art. 34(2)(a)), face an annual independent audit at their own expense (Art. 37(1)), and at a regulator's request "explain the design, the logic, the functioning and the testing of their algorithmic systems" (Art. 40(3)). The Commission may inspect and "require ... explanations on its organisation, functioning, IT system, algorithms" (Art. 69(2)(d)). Vetted researchers can obtain data access (Art. 40(4), (8)). Recommender systems must state their "main parameters" in the terms and offer a non-profiling option (Arts. 27, 38); manipulative interface design is prohibited (Art. 25). The Regulation applies from 17 February 2024, and some provisions from 16 November 2022 (Art. 93(2)).

## Relation to the argument

This is the strongest text for D8's platform side, and it complicates the "highly draconian" reading. By statute the owner of a platform no longer holds the convention alone: notice, reasons, complaint, a human decider, an outside body, a regulator with a 6% fine, and a damages route. These are the elements a court-like order has. The limits are equally textual: small platforms are exempt from the complaint system (Art. 19); the outside body cannot bind (Art. 21(2)); the reasons duty is confined to restrictions grounded in illegality or terms (Art. 17(1)); and the change-of-terms duty is bare notice (Art. 14(2)).

For D3, the DSA mixes both kinds of intervention. Arts. 14(1) and 27 are disclosure to users, the literacy-based kind. Arts. 25 and 38 (no manipulative design, a non-profiling option), Art. 37 (external audit) and Arts. 40 and 69 (regulator access to algorithms) place controls outside the user's reading. The Regulation therefore does not support "his only move is disclosure" as a description of regulation, and it parallels the external-controls structure D4 and P11 attribute to the Nadella post. For P10, the Regulation treats the platform as an interested party to be policed from outside (Arts. 37, 41, 52).

**Facet check:** I found no quotation in facet J or audit AUDIT_05 that the text contradicts: Art. 14(2), 17(2), 19, 20(6), 21(2) and the dates are as the audit corrected them. Two omissions matter. First, J's list of platform "holders" leaves out Arts. 53 and 54 (complaint to a regulator, damages) and the 6% fine ceiling (Arts. 52(3), 74), which are the Regulation's main enforcement. Second, J:271 ("bound by more written duties ... than an FX dealer") needs the correction in the FX card: Code Principle 40 asks that substantial changes in terms be agreed before trading, while DSA Art. 14(2) asks only that recipients be informed.

## Caution

A statute shows duties on paper, not compliance; the facet's own evidence of non-compliance is abstract-only. Most obligations apply only to online platforms above the small-enterprise line; the algorithmic access provisions apply only to very large ones. The text cannot be cited for how often reasons are given or complaints upheld. I did not read the recitals in full, so this card does not report the legislator's stated reasons beyond recitals 45, 47, 58 and 59. The mirror site the bib note mentions was not used; this card rests on the Official Journal text.
