# Wong, D., & Floridi, L. (2023). Meta's Oversight Board: A review and critical assessment. *Minds and Machines, 33*(2), 261–284 (online 24 October 2022).

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11023-022-09613-x · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access publisher PDF, journal pagination; reference list skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none of its own; a synthesis of scholarship, news reports and Meta and Oversight Board documents, with one case analysis (Ethiopia, case FB-MP4ZC4CC) · **Parties modeled:** two parties plus a third party (user and platform, with a board funded by but legally separate from the platform) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Wong and Floridi build a framework of strengths and weaknesses for Meta's Oversight Board (OB). The Board reviews removals and keeps-up decisions on Facebook and Instagram. Its rulings on a case bind Meta unless implementing them "could violate the law"; its policy recommendations do not bind, but Meta has committed to respond within sixty days (pp. 263–264). Meta funds it through an independent trust: $130 million at launch and $150 million more in July 2022 (p. 264). The authors place it as "platform self-governance", more independent than most self-regulation because it is legally separate (p. 264).

Three strengths (pp. 266–268). It makes moderation more transparent, for example by exposing "internal rule books" (§3.1). Its recommendations have moved Meta, which agreed to translate its standards into South Asian languages and to tell users which rule they broke (§3.2). It is assertive: of 27 decisions "issued in 2021" it overturned Meta in 20 (74%) and upheld 7 (p. 268). It also read its charter to stop Meta removing a case from review by reversing its own decision (p. 268).

Four weaknesses (pp. 268–271). Jurisdiction is narrow: a binary uphold-or-overturn on single posts, not accounts or groups (p. 269). Impact is small. Meta keeps final authority and could ignore recommendations or "choose not to provide future funding", and the Board "cannot rule on, or mandate changes to, broader platform features such as Facebook's recommendation algorithms, advertising systems, or data collection" (pp. 269–270). In 2021 it issued "20 decisions out of 130 shortlisted cases and approximately over 1 million cases submitted" (p. 269). Precedent is under Meta's control: Meta decides whether a new case is "identical content in parallel context" and "technically and operationally feasible", and precedent only removes content, never restores it (pp. 264, 270). Diversity is low: Domino (2020) counted one of 20 members from Southeast Asia, and over two-thirds of 2021 appeals came from the Global North, with 2% from sub-Saharan Africa and Central and South Asia (pp. 270–271).

The Ethiopia case (§5) shows these at work. The Board upheld removal of a Facebook post on a different ground than Meta used, and of three recommendations Meta implemented one in part, took no further action on one, and agreed only to assess the third (pp. 272–275). The authors propose reforms (§7): let the Board decide when its precedent applies, apply precedent to pending appeals, define the criteria, and add appellate boards of randomly chosen users to decide whether precedent applies (pp. 278–281). They offer no test of the proposal.

## Relation to the argument
Ruling D8 asks how courts, FX markets and platforms differ as holders of a convention. This paper is the platform case of a court-like body, and it shows the body's reach stopping where the platform's design begins: the Board cannot touch the recommendation system, and the owner controls how far precedent travels. That supports premise P10 (a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention) with a case in which the owner built the court. It also shows an owner-funded body that overturns the owner most of the time, so independence in the ruling and dependence in the architecture can coexist.

Facet check: the facet quotes the abstract and reads the Board as "court-like" with Meta controlling jurisdiction and precedent. The text agrees. It adds that the Board's case rulings are binding while its recommendations are not, so "controls" holds most fully for precedent and scope. The text also gives two counts for 2021: 27 decisions (p. 268) and 20 decisions (p. 269), each attributed to the Board's annual report. Do not cite either without checking the report.

The card's reading, not Wong and Floridi's: the Board's tiny caseload against about a million submissions resembles Galanter's overload (`galanter1974why`, pp. 119–122), which would push users to treat the process as symbolic.

## Caution
A descriptive and normative review of the Board's first 18 months or so; the data are Meta's and the Board's own reports. The reform proposals are untested. The two 2021 decision counts disagree. Anything about the Board after October 2022 is outside this paper.
