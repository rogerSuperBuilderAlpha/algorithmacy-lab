# Hakvoort, B., van den Boer, M., Leenaars, T., Bos, P., & Tijms, J. (2017). Improvements in reading accuracy as a result of increased interletter spacing are not specific to children with dyslexia. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, 164*, 101–116.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.jecp.2017.07.010 · **Read depth:** full_text (final published version, UvA-DARE repository copy; journal pagination 101–116) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** two experiments. Exp. 1: 30 Dutch children with dyslexia and 30 age-matched controls (mean age 9 y 11 m for the dyslexia group, 9 y 9 m for controls), each reading the same 44 sentences aloud on paper in standard and increased letter spacing, a week apart. Exp. 2: 189 unselected Grade 3–4 children (mean age 9 y 3 m), on a laptop, sentence-at-once against word-by-word presentation · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and typeset text) · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Hakvoort and colleagues try to replicate Zorzi et al. (2012), who found that extra-large letter spacing helped children with dyslexia in particular. They use harder, age-appropriate sentences to avoid the ceiling on controls' accuracy that Skottun and Skoyles (2012) had pointed to (p. 104). Experiment 1 copied Zorzi's layout: letter spacing +2.5, three space characters between words, doubled line spacing (p. 105).

Result: both groups made fewer errors with increased spacing (F(1,55) = 16.97, p < .001, ηp² = .236), with no group × spacing interaction (F = 0.04, p = .849); speed did not change (F(1,58) = 0.37, p = .547; pp. 106–107). Mean errors per block fell from 10.54 to 10.00 for dyslexic children in block 1 and from 9.86 to 7.54 in block 2; for controls from 5.10 to 3.60 and 5.67 to 3.83 (Table 1, p. 106). The gain was proportionally larger for controls (29.4% and 32.5%) than for children with dyslexia (5.1% and 23.5%). At the individual level, 41.4% of the children with dyslexia and 23.3% of controls showed no gain or a loss in accuracy; effects did not correlate with word-reading fluency (p. 107). Dyslexic children's errors dropped from block 1 to block 2 regardless of condition, which argues against a novelty or "attention boost" account (p. 108).

Experiment 2 changed only letter spacing (+1.2, proportional word spaces). In whole-sentence reading (N = 96) errors fell from 3.87 to 2.76 per sentence set (28.7%) with speed unchanged at 1.63 words/s; in word-by-word reading (N = 93) the error drop from 1.99 to 1.71 (14.0%) was not reliable (t(91) = 1.80, p = .075; Table 3, p. 110; p. 111). Reading ability did not predict the size of the effect (all |r| ≤ .17; p. 111).

The authors' three conclusions: "First, increased LS appears to affect reading accuracy only. Second, the findings do not support claims that increased LS specifically affects poor readers." The third reads "the effect of LS seems to occur at the interword level" in the abstract (pp. 101–102) but "at the sentence level" in the General Discussion (p. 112). The experiments contrast words shown one by one with whole sentences, which fits "sentence level"; the abstract wording appears to be a slip.

## Relation to the argument
This card concerns letter spacing, an adjacent intervention to word spacing, and it sits in facet D as the main cautionary case about spacing as a remedy. It cuts against any reading of the history that says added space helps the weakest readers most (premise P6, "most for readers still learning"): here the same change gave controls at least as much accuracy as the children with dyslexia, and nobody got faster. It also shows how an apparently clean "interface fix" lands unevenly across individuals, which bears on ruling D10 (the history is a guide) as a warning against importing the effect size from one spacing manipulation to another. It does not test word-boundary marking, so it gives nothing to premises P1 to P5 directly. In Experiment 1 the word spaces were also widened, so Experiment 1 cannot separate letter from word spacing. The authors say Experiment 2 "did not allow a direct comparison between interletter and interword spacing", but argue that its identical result suggests the added word spacing was not what caused the Experiment 1 effect (p. 113).

Facet check: the facet quotes the abstract's "interword level" line. The body says "sentence level"; cite the body.

## Caution
Children's accuracy only; no eye tracking, no comprehension measure. Experiments 1 and 2 differ in medium (paper against laptop), spacing amount and word spacing, so they are not directly comparable (p. 113). Dutch, a fairly transparent orthography. The exposure to each condition was brief (about 17 sentences per block). Crowding was not measured: the authors call for future studies pairing a crowding paradigm with a letter-spacing manipulation, since the link is "rarely directly established" (p. 114).
