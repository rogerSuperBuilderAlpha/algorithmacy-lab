## What it argues
Socrates tells Phaedrus a story he says he "heard of the ancients" (274c). The Egyptian god Theuth, inventor of numbers, geometry, astronomy, draughts and "most important of all, letters" (274d), shows his arts to the king Thamus, who judges each. Of letters, Theuth says they "will make the Egyptians wiser and will improve their memories; for it is an elixir of memory and wisdom" (274e). Thamus answers that the inventor is not the judge of what an art does to its users, and says the invention has the opposite effect: "this invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory" (275a). Trust in "external characters which are no part of themselves" will "discourage the use of their own memory within them." Then: "You have invented an elixir not of memory, but of reminding; and you offer your pupils the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom, for they will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things, when they are for the most part ignorant and hard to get along with, since they are not wise, but only appear wise" (275a–b).

Socrates presses the point past 275b (a part of the passage the bib's range does not cover). Writing is "like painting": its creatures "preserve a solemn silence" when questioned (275d); a written word "knows not to whom to speak or not to speak" and "always needs its father to help it" (275e). The better word is "written with intelligence in the mind of the learner," able to defend itself (276a), and a wise man writes only "to treasure up reminders for himself" (276d).

It fits the objection's shape and the paper treats it as the counter-case to the rule that lowering the reader's burden is a gain (ARGUMENT_MAP, exposure 4, "Lowering a cost has costs"). Two limits. ## Caution
A dialogue in an English translation: Socrates tells the myth, and Phaedrus doubts it ("you easily make up stories of Egypt", 275b), so quoting Thamus as Plato's own view needs care. Whether the dialogue endorses Thamus is itself a long scholarly dispute that I did not review.
