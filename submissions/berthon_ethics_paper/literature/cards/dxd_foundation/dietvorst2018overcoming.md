# Dietvorst, B. J., Simmons, J. P., & Massey, C. (2018). Overcoming algorithm aversion: People will use imperfect algorithms if they can (even slightly) modify them. *Management Science, 64*(3), 1155–1170.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643 · **Read depth:** full_text (the authors' manuscript, 38 pages, as hosted by the University of Pennsylvania repository; not the journal typesetting, so locators are manuscript page numbers, "MS p.") · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (manuscript read) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** three incentivised forecasting experiments (Study 1: lab, N = 288; Study 2: MTurk, N = 816; Study 3: MTurk, N = 818), plus supplementary studies I did not see · **Parties modeled:** person–system (forecaster and algorithm) · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
People who have seen an algorithm err will not use it exclusively, but they will use it if they may change its output. Participants forecast the percentile of 20 real high-school seniors on a standardised maths test from nine variables, paid a bonus for accuracy. A statistical model was "off by 17.5 percentiles on average" and participants were told so (MS pp. 7–8).

Study 1 (N = 288, lab). The share choosing to use the model was 32% when they could not change its forecasts, 73% when they could overrule 10 of 20 (χ²(1, N = 145) = 24.19), and 76% when they could adjust each forecast by up to 10 percentiles (χ²(1, N = 146) = 28.40) (MS p. 10). The can't-change group made larger errors and earned smaller bonuses than all three other groups (MS p. 12, fn 11).

Study 2 (N = 816, MTurk). 47% used the model when it could not be changed against 70% across the adjust-by-X conditions (χ²(1, N = 834) = 36.46). The share did not depend on the size of the adjustment: 71%, 71% and 68% for 10, 5 and 2 percentiles (p = .809). The authors conclude that "their willingness to use the model was not detectably altered by imposing an 80% reduction of the amount by which they could adjust" (MS p. 16). Those who could adjust by 2 percentiles used on average only 1.33 of them (MS p. 16).

Study 3 (N = 818). Forced use of the model left participants less satisfied than the two adjustable conditions (t(614) = -6.59 and t(620) = -6.17, both p < .001) (MS p. 24). Over 80% in every condition chose to use the model in some way in round two; 51–74% chose to combine their judgment with the model (MS p. 26). Those who had been able to modify in round one chose "model-only" more often (30% against 12%, χ²(1, N = 823) = 38.45) (MS p. 26) and rated the model better relative to themselves (MS p. 28). In round two, among participants who chose each process (a self-selected comparison, not a random assignment), average absolute error was 17.90 for those who chose adjust-by-10, against 20.25 for use-freely and 24.50 for human-only (MS pp. 30–31). In round one, where assignment was random, adjust-by-10 against use-freely was only directionally better (t(410) = -1.22, p = .224; MS p. 29).

The authors' stated conclusion: "Participants in our studies did often worsen the algorithm's forecasts when given the ability to adjust them. However, we may have to accept this error so that, overall, people make less error" (MS p. 34).

## Relation to the argument
Part 1 cites this paper for the "modification right" (facet H, line 351). Read in full, the paper supports a narrower claim than "restores willingness to decide through an algorithm". What changes is willingness to adopt a forecast, measured as a choice to use the model, and the authors read it as "some control", not more control. The result is about a preference for any lever. That fits ruling D3's logic only partly: a lever is not disclosure and it is not an understanding of the algorithm. It is a control surface, and facet H's caution applies here: raising willingness is not raising performance. Here, performance in round two was better for those who chose the capped adjustment, which the authors tie to those participants staying closer to the model. That comparison is among self-selected choosers, and the random-assignment comparison in round one was not significant, so the paper does not show that the cap caused the gain. If it did, the gain would come from a rule held in the artefact (a cap), not from the person's understanding, which is the closest this paper comes to the argument's P8.

Limits for the argument: the algorithm is a forecasting formula, the user is the sole party, and no third party is affected (a dyad). The authors read the preference as "a desire for some control" (MS p. 2); they do not test whether the control is real.

Facet check: facet H says the abstract was not retrieved and that the claim is "restoring willingness". The full text confirms willingness to use; it adds that willingness was insensitive to the size of the adjustment, and that accuracy fell when adjustment was unrestricted.

## Caution
Manuscript, not the version of record; supplementary studies S1–S5 are referred to but I did not read them. Participants are students and MTurk workers on a low-stakes task; the authors note professionals may differ (MS p. 34). Study 3's difference between adjust-by-10 and use-freely in round one (random assignment) was not significant (MS p. 29); the accuracy gain is in round two and compares participants by the process they chose.
