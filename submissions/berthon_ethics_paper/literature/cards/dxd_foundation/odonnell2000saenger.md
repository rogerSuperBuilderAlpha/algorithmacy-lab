## What it argues
O'Donnell, a late-antiquity scholar, reviews Saenger for medievalists. ¶1 opens with Saenger's closing sentence (p. 275) and calls the book an "intellectual adventure." ¶2 frames the question, "when and how silent reading became a common practice." The easy answer is that Ambrose invented silent reading and Augustine saw it (*Conf.* 6.3.3), but "already thirty years ago, Bernard Knox … showed that the practice was attested long before Augustine," while "it did not become commonplace until long after." The second clause is O'Donnell's assertion, with no evidence offered. He names Saenger's 1982 *Viator* article (13, pp. 366–414) as "seminal."

¶3 gives "the short form of Saenger's answer": when regular spacing of words "became commonplace" in the later Middle Ages, "purely visual and private reading became so easy as to become widespread." Then the verdict on that thesis: "some will dissent from … his claim that the textual practices he observes were the necessary and sufficient condition of the rise of silent reading." The argument is "a superstructure erected on the vast bulk of this work, like a dizzying spire looming over a richly constructed Gothic cathedral" (¶3). The cathedral would have been poorer without the spire, "but some observers might never have noticed the lack."

¶4 summarizes the book's chronicle of word separation "step by step through a wide variety of insular and continental scriptoria." The "fons et origo in some ways is Virgilius Maro, the Irish grammarian of the seventh century," and "no homogeneity of practice spread easily"; the story has "multiple foci, multiple centuries, and almost infinite variation." The weakest link is the brief opening chapter on the physiology of reading: "the stretch from that survey to the manuscript practices he outlines and then to the theory about reading practices he constructs is the most venturesome and vulnerable part of the book. It will surely be tested." He expects the debate "resolved in Saenger's favor," and says the book's value lies elsewhere, as an orientation to palaeography and manuscript culture.
