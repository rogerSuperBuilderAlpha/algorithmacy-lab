## What it argues

What can be stated from what I saw. The book reports the Vai project. The Vai use an indigenous syllabary learned outside school, alongside Arabic literacy learned in Qur'anic study and English learned in school, which let the authors separate the effects of literacy from those of schooling. In the 1978 article they report that "between 20 and 25 percent of Vai men could read and write using their own script", which is "transmitted from one generation to another without schooling or professional teachers" (1978, p. 453).

Their central result, as the 1978 article gives it: on sorting and verbal-reasoning tasks, "improved performance was associated with years of formal schooling, but literacy in the Vai script did not substitute for schooling. Vai literates were not significantly different from nonliterates on any of these cognitive measures" (1978, p. 453). Vai literacy did produce specific skills tied to its uses, such as clearer game instructions and better integration of syllable-by-syllable speech, but "These consequences, however, are all highly specific to activities with the Vai script" (1978, p. 457). And: "We did not find that literacy in the Vai script was associated in any way with generalized competencies such as abstraction, verbal reasoning, or metalinguistic skills" (1978, p. 457). Their framework treats literacy as "a practice" with particular technologies and purposes (1978, p. 457).

On the script itself, the 1978 article says it "is written without division into words or other language units; a string of syllabic characters runs across the page without spacing or segmentation", and that readers resolve the ambiguity by "experimentation in pronunciation", saying syllables aloud "until they click into meaningful units" (1978, p. 456). Facet C recorded a parallel sentence from the 1981 book by search-inside: "A string of syllabic characters runs across the page without division into words or phrases" (scan index only; printed page not seen).

## Relation to the argument

**Line 497 and ruling D1/D10.** The draft cites the book to say that word separation is a design guide, "not ... a monocausal explanation of historical literacy or intellectual development". As far as the proxy shows, Scribner and Cole support the second half directly: literacy as such did not produce general cognitive change; the effects were practice-specific and general gains tracked schooling (1978, pp. 453, 457). They do not address word separation or the history of literacy, so they support the caution by analogy, not on its subject.

**Bears on P5 and P6, both ways.** The Vai case is a living unspaced script read by adults who learned it informally, which shows that an unspaced script can be learned without schooling. It also shows the reading cost the draft attributes to *scriptio continua*: readers find decoding "extraordinarily difficult" and resolve word boundaries by trial pronunciation (1978, p. 456). That fits P5's claim that separation turns an inferential task into a perceptual one. The Vai script also lacks tone and standardised vowel length, so its difficulty is not due to the missing spaces alone (1978, p. 456).

## Also used by facet W (2026-10-15)

Facet W uses the Vai case, through the 1978 proxy article, as the one field description of the mechanism the abstract asserts: readers of an unspaced syllabary resolve ambiguity "by experimentation in pronunciation", saying syllables aloud "until they click into meaningful units" (1978, p. 456). That is voiced boundary detection by lexical matching, in a script where (1978, p. 456) tone and vowel length are also unmarked, so the voicing does more than find word ends. It supports a narrow form of "scaffolding literacy with oracy" for an unspaced script learned informally, and it comes with the same authors' finding that this literacy produced no general cognitive change (1978, pp. 453, 457), which bears on "freed literacy".
