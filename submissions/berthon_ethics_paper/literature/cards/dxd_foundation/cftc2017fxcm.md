# U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission. (2017, February 6). *CFTC orders Forex Capital Markets, LLC (FXCM), its parent company, FXCM Holdings, LLC and FXCM's founding partners, Dror Niv and William Ahdout, to pay a $7 million penalty for FXCM's defrauding of retail forex customers* (Release 7528-17) [Press release].

## What it argues

The release says the CFTC's Order "finds that, between September 4, 2009 though at least 2014 (the Relevant Period), FXCM engaged in false and misleading solicitations of FXCM's retail foreign exchange (forex) customers by concealing its relationship with its most important market maker and by misrepresenting that its 'No Dealing Desk' platform had no conflicts of interest with its customers." FXCM and two founders pay a $7 million penalty jointly and severally and agree to withdraw from registration and never to seek it again. FXCM, its parent and the CEO are also held responsible for false statements to the National Futures Association (NFA).

The mechanism, as the release gives it: FXCM told customers that on its No Dealing Desk platform it was "merely that of a credit intermediary," that profits or losses of customers would have no effect on its bottom line, and that risk was borne by independent market makers. In fact FXCM "had an undisclosed interest in the market maker that consistently 'won' the largest share of FXCM's trading volume – and thus was taking positions opposite FXCM's retail customers." The market maker came from a 2009 plan to build an algorithmic system; it was spun off but stayed "closely aligned," got "special trading privileges," a no-interest loan, space in FXCM's offices and FXCM employees. It rebated "approximately 70 percent of its revenue" to FXCM, about $77 million from 2010 through 2014. The release adds that FXCM omitted these details in a meeting with the NFA. The two founders are held liable as "controlling persons."

## Relation to the argument

This is a case of an intermediary that presented itself as neutral ("credit intermediary," "no conflicts") while holding an interest in the counterparty to its customers' trades. It supports premise P10 and ruling D8's comparison: in retail FX, as on a platform, the middle party's neutrality was a claim, and what corrected it was a regulator's order, not any right the customer held. It also marks a limit on disclosure as the remedy (ruling D3): the CFTC's quoted statement is that "full and truthful disclosure to customers" is "vital to the integrity" of markets, but here nondisclosure was the offence and customers could not have read what was withheld. The release does not say customers lost money in a measured amount, and it does not say how the customers' fills compared with others'.

Facet check: the J-facet read only the lead paragraph and quoted it accurately ("concealing its relationship," "$7 million," withdraw from registration). The full release adds the 70% rebate and $77 million figure, which the facet does not use.

## Caution

A press release, not the Order. The findings are those of a settled order, and the release does not say the respondents admitted or denied them. It gives no customer loss figure. The release names retail forex customers, not institutional dealers, so it should not be generalised to the wholesale market the FCA and NYDFS documents cover.
