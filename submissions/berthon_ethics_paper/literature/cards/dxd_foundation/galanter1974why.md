**Identifier:** doi:10.2307/3053023 · **Read depth:** partial (publisher PDF from Cambridge Core, journal pagination 95–160, 66 PDF pages; main text pp. 95–112, 113–125, 129–137 and 141–151 read closely; the long footnotes read selectively; pp. 126–128, 138–141 and 145–148 read in part; the reference list skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none of its own; a conjectural essay built on secondary studies, with figures cited from others in footnotes · **Parties modeled:** two parties plus a third-party forum (repeat player against one-shotter before a court-like body), with lawyers as intermediaries · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Galanter asks when litigation can redistribute power, and answers that the "architecture" of the legal system gives the "haves" interlocking advantages. He admits the essay is conjecture: "The speculative and tentative nature of the assertions here will be apparent and is acknowledged here wholesale" (p. 95).

He starts from the parties, not the rules (p. 97). A repeat player (RP) is "a unit which has had and anticipates repeated litigation, which has low stakes in the outcome of any one case, and which has the resources to pursue its long-run interests" (p. 98). A one-shotter (OS) is the opposite case. He lists nine RP advantages (pp. 98–103): advance intelligence ("It is the RP who writes the form contract", p. 98), expertise, informal ties with the forum, a reputation for commitment, the freedom to play the odds (p. 99), and the freedom to "play for rules as well as immediate gains" (p. 100), with further advantages in spotting which rules will "penetrate" (items 8-9, p. 103). RPs settle cases likely to make bad precedent, so the stock of precedent skews their way (pp. 101–102). Figure 1 sorts litigation into four boxes by RP or OS on each side (p. 107). Most litigation sits in RP-against-OS (Box II). He cites Wanner's sample of 7,900 civil cases in three cities, where business and government units were plaintiffs in almost six cases in ten (n. 29, p. 108).

Three more layers add to the first. Lawyers: specialists who serve OSs lack the RP's repeat position, and lawyers whose clients come through a broker may serve the broker, because "The source of business generally counts for more than the client" (Carlin, quoted p. 118). Institutions are passive and overloaded, which favours the possessor and pushes claimants to settle (pp. 119–122). Rules "tend to favor older, culturally dominant interests" (p. 123). Figure 3 shows the layers reinforcing each other (p. 125).

Parties in a dense, continuing relationship avoid courts and build private remedies, because each can withhold future dealing. Government and monopolies are different: "Informal controls depend upon the ultimate sanction of withdrawal and refusal to continue beneficial relations" (p. 111), and those parties lack it, so more disputes reach the forum (pp. 111–112).

On reform he concludes that "change at the level of substantive rules is not likely in itself to be determinative of redistributive outcomes" (p. 149). Rules are abundant and organized parties are scarce: "If rules are the most abundant resource for reformers, parties capable of pursuing long-range strategies are the rarest" (p. 150). His priority is to aggregate OSs into something like RPs: membership associations, assignees of fragmentary rights, interest-group sponsors, clearing-houses, government prosecution (pp. 141–142).

## Relation to the argument
Ruling D8 (compare courts, FX markets and platforms; "look for where the convention bends towards"). Galanter gives the mechanism for the tilt: a neutral rule still favours the party that writes the form contract and plays repeatedly. That supports premise P10 (the interface owner's interest differs from the user's), and the form-contract line (p. 98) anticipates platform terms of service.

It also cuts against any hope that a convention alone protects the weaker party. On his account "changes in the rules can be filtered out unless accompanied by changes at other levels" (p. 149). For the paper, a spacing-style convention would need organized parties and facilities behind it. His scheme for turning OSs into RPs is a closer analogue to counter-delegation (Part 1, line 144) than to a rule; the match is the card's reading, not Galanter's.

Facet check: the facet maps the platform to the extreme RP and the driver or seller to a one-shotter. Galanter's own note 4 (p. 97) says welfare clients and taxpayers "are not pure OSs, since there is next year's tax bill and next month's welfare check", and pp. 129–133 predict private ordering where relationships are dense and enduring. A driver is in a continuing, unequal relationship, so the mapping needs that qualification. The facet's p. 111 quotation (arbitration "detached from official sanctions") describes RP-against-RP relations of comparable size, not the platform case.

## Caution
A 1974 essay on the American system, tentative by its author's own account; the figures in it come from secondary studies of small-claims, tenant and injury litigation, and none concerns online platforms. Applying "repeat player" to platforms is the project's step. I skimmed parts of pp. 126–128, 138–141 and 145–148 and most footnotes, so claims about those passages need a second read.
