## What it argues
What I could see. The article follows Gavrilov's "Techniques of reading in classical antiquity" in the same issue, which Burnyeat translated. Its public extract opens on a passage "available to classical scholars since the editio princeps of 1663" and contrasts the dramatic scenes of reading in Gavrilov's paper (Theseus, Augustine, Demosthenes) with "a calm, straightforward statement about reading in general. No problems about the inference from art to life; this is a statement about life" (Cambridge Core extract, p. 74). The public page also shows the article's 14 footnotes (main text not shown). They name the target: Burnyeat quotes Stanford (1967, p. 2), "There is no clear reference at all to silent reading by any Greek, named or unnamed, until after the fourth century A.D." (n. 2); calls Knox's case "unassailable" where humour is involved (n. 3); says the tone and context of Ptolemy's remark mean scholars "should treat it as the plainest of plain statements of fact" (n. 10); and cites Gilliard (1993) as having "to rebut the myth because Balogh has had more effect than Knox" (n. 12). Note 1 says the Greek text quoted follows Lammert with a change from the Liverpool–Manchester seminar edition in Huby & Neal (eds.), *The Criterion of Truth* (Liverpool, 1989), ch. 11. Footnote numbers only; the page each falls on (74–76) is not shown. Johnson (2000, p. 593) credits the note with "focusing on neglected but important evidence (Burnyeat on Ptolemy)" and, with Gavrilov, with improving "the evidential base."

The passage is Ptolemy's (second century CE). A blog quotation of Burnyeat's translation has Ptolemy say that "we keep quiet when engaged in the readings themselves if we are concentrating hard on the texts before us." I have not seen this sentence in the article and give it only as a lead to check.

## Relation to the argument
**Premise P1 and the dropped claim that spacing created silent reading.** The note belongs to the case, led by Knox (1968) and Gavrilov (1997), that skilled ancient readers could and did read silently long before word separation. If the Ptolemy rendering is accurate, it is the clearest single statement of that: a general remark, not a story, tying silent reading to hard concentration. That supports the argument map's decision to drop "spacing created silent reading" and its use of Saenger's history as a guide (D10) rather than a cause (D1, D2).

It does not bear on whether unspaced script burdened trained readers, which is the live dispute behind P1 (Vatri, via Heilmann 2020). A reader can read silently and still pay a cost for parsing unspaced text.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 85 cites Burnyeat (1997), with Knox, Gavrilov and Heilmann, for "classical accounts show that skilled readers could and did read silently long before word spacing appeared." What I could check supports the direction: the extract presents a general statement about reading, Johnson and Heilmann place the note on the silent-reading side, and the footnotes (nn. 2, 3, 10, 12) show Burnyeat arguing against the view that the ancients did not read silently. I could not check the claim against the article's own words. "Skilled readers" is not visible in anything I saw; the Ptolemy rendering speaks of readers "concentrating hard," which fits but is second-hand.
