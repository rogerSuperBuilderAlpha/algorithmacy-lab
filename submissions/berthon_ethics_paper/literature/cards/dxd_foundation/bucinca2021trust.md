## What it argues
People helped by AI tend to "overrely": they accept a wrong suggestion they would have beaten alone. Explanations have not cured this. The authors argue from dual-process theory that people rarely examine each explanation and instead form a general heuristic about the AI (pp. 188:1–188:2). They borrow "cognitive forcing functions" from clinical medicine: interventions applied at decision time that interrupt heuristic reasoning (p. 188:4).

The task is a nutrition one chosen so laypeople could do it: swap the highest-carbohydrate ingredient in a meal photo for a low-carbohydrate one of similar flavour (§3.1). Six conditions: no AI; two simple explainable-AI (SXAI) baselines, where explanation or explanation plus uncertainty appear at once; and three forcing functions (CFF): the suggestion shown only on request (on demand), shown after the person's own first decision (update), or shown after a 30-second "AI is processing" wait (§3.2). The simulated AI misidentified the main carbohydrate source on a fixed set of six of the 24 scored questions (§3.3–3.4).

The headline result is narrow. Over all questions, both AI types beat no AI and did not differ from each other (overall correct: no AI .17, SXAI .35, CFF .33; Table 1). On the six wrong-AI questions, CFF did better than SXAI, but still worse than no AI. Marginal means for picking the right carbohydrate source on those questions: no AI .49, SXAI .08, CFF .27 (d = .66 for CFF against SXAI); overall correct: .18, .03, .09 (d = .37; Table 1, p. 188:10). Overreliance on the carbohydrate source was .64 for SXAI and .48 for CFF (p = .003); on the full two-part decision the difference (.30 against .26) was not significant (Table 2, p. 188:11). The designs within each category did not differ. The authors state: "Our results did not provide support for the hypothesis (H1b) that cognitive forcing functions will improve the performance of human+AI teams" (p. 188:16), and the human–AI teams still did worse than the AI alone.

There was a price. Trust was nominally higher for SXAI (3.91 against 3.72 on a 5-point scale, not significant), and CFF was rated more complex (2.95 against 2.64; Table 3). On wrong-AI questions, people "performed best in conditions that they preferred and trusted the least, and that they rated as the most difficult" (p. 188:16). Splitting at the median of Need for Cognition (NFC), forcing functions improved overall and carbohydrate-source accuracy for high-NFC people, but only carbohydrate-source accuracy for low-NFC people (pp. 188:14–188:16, Tables 4–5). High-NFC people also trusted and preferred CFF less than SXAI. The authors name the risk "intervention-generated inequalities" (p. 188:12).

## Relation to the argument
ARGUMENT_MAP, "Where the argument is exposed" item 4: lowering a cost has costs. The facet and the map both use this study as showing that the more demanding design worked best. Facet check: the full text gives a narrower result. Forcing functions beat simple explanations only on the questions where the AI was wrong, did not raise overall performance, did not differ from each other, and never reached the no-AI level on those questions. "Worked best" holds among the AI conditions on wrong-AI questions only.

It does support premise P9 (aids that hand the person more to read show weak effects on what people do): the explanation conditions raised reliance on wrong advice relative to working alone. The forcing functions that helped add friction or reorder the sequence, not more to read, so they are not clear instances of "more demanding" in the sense the argument uses. The NFC split bears on the open question of novices and experts (ruling D10): the benefit went mostly to people who like effortful thinking. It tests no triad and no interested mediator.

## Caution
One low-stakes task in nutrition, done by online workers, against a simulated AI whose errors were of one kind (misreading the image). Forcing functions were short; no one practised them over time. The NFC analysis is a median split on a four-item scale, described by the authors as "a limited internal audit" (p. 188:12). Preprint v1; check against the published article before quoting numbers.
