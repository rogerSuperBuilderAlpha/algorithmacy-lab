## What it argues
Degno and colleagues ask when, during a fixation, the loss of word spaces first shows up in the brain, and how it combines with the loss of a valid preview of the next word. The "unspaced" manipulation is narrow. Before the eyes cross an invisible boundary, the space after the upcoming word is filled by a random letter; once they cross it, the normal text appears (§2.2, p. 5). Participants read silently, with comprehension questions on 25 percent of trials, and answered 96 percent of questions correctly (§3.1, p. 8). All 39 noticed something unusual on the display, and 18 said they noticed spaces removed (§3.2, p. 8).

Eye movements: reading was slower when the preview lacked spaces, by 30 ms on first fixation, 35 ms on single fixation and 98 ms on gaze duration; an invalid preview cost 29, 35 and 37 ms. The preview effect was larger with spaces intact (first fixation 46 ms against 12 ms; gaze 56 against 17 ms; §3.3, p. 8). Brain potentials: spaced and unspaced previews differed from 0 to 300 ms after fixation onset (visual and orthographic processing), but not in the 300–500 ms window usually tied to lexical processing (§3.4, pp. 9–10; §4, p. 16). They note that "the effect size of inter-word spacing was larger compared to the size of the parafoveal preview effect" (p. 17), suggest that spaces are processed faster than letters (p. 18), and conclude that "spatial selection precedes lexical processing" (abstract, p. 1).

## Relation to the argument
It supplies the mechanism side of premise P3 (`ARGUMENT_MAP.md`): word-boundary information is used early in each fixation to pick the next target, and removing it disrupts reading before lexical access is under way. That matches the account Saenger's critics grant for modern readers (Heilmann, p. 180: moderns read unspaced English more slowly). It is also the basis for the project's "interface turns an inferential task into a perceptual one" step (P5), in the narrow sense that the spaces act on the first, visual stage of reading.

The effect sizes are tens of milliseconds per word for habituated English readers who lost spaces for the upcoming word only. They are not the "third to a half" slowing of whole unspaced text in P3, and they say nothing about ancient readers, learners or practised readers of an unspaced script. Facet D's note on this source (no reliable spacing effect in the 300–500 ms window) matches the paper.

Facet check: no contradiction. One reminder from the full text: "unspaced" means unspaced in the parafoveal preview, so the study does not show how an entire unspaced page reads.

## Caution
English readers only, one sentence per trial, random letters as the replacement (more disruptive than the numbers or shapes used elsewhere, by the authors' own comment on p. 4). The authors had weak expectations for the late EEG window and offer a cancellation explanation after the fact (p. 17). Do not cite it for a general figure on how much spacing speeds reading.
