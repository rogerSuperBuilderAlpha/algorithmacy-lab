## What it argues
Jordan and colleagues explain what the Reicher-Wheeler paradigm (R-WP) does and then list recent studies that claim to use it but do not. The setting is the word superiority effect: under brief display, "words are easier to identify than nonwords, and even single letters, and reports of this phenomenon date from some of the earliest experiments in the history of psychology (Cattell, 1886; Erdmann and Dodge, 1898; see also Huey, 1908)" (Introduction). Those early results were confounded, because participants could use "non-perceptual guesswork" that helps words and not nonwords (Introduction).

The R-WP fixes this with a forced choice. After a brief display, two letters appear, "both letters are consistent with the rest of the stimulus", so that "selecting the correct letter cannot be assisted non-perceptually" (section "Actual contributions of the Reicher-Wheeler paradigm"). The paradigm "became known as the Reicher-Wheeler Paradigm (hereafter, R-WP) after it was first reported by Reicher (1969) and Wheeler (1970)." They cite Johnston (1978) for the result that free report, without forced choice, produced larger word advantages than the R-WP did, which is what guessing would predict.

The authors then name studies that departed from the design: Marzouki et al. (2022) used a bar-marker probe on Arabic words; Mok (2009) used an underline probe with written reports; Chen et al. (2018) used different probe characters for word and nonword conditions, so probe identity is confounded with stimulus type. They name further cases with mismatched forced-choice alternatives (Casaponsa & Duñabeitia, 2016; Laszlo & Federmeier, 2007; Chen et al., 2017; Matić et al., 2018) (section "Misconceptions of the Reicher-Wheeler paradigm in the literature"). They say the findings of these studies "remain to be determined under true R-WP conditions" (Discussion). They offer no data of their own.

## Caution
A short corrective review by researchers who have used the paradigm themselves (Jordan is a co-author of several of the R-WP studies it cites as exemplary). It does not re-analyse any of the criticised studies. Its claim that those studies' results are "unclear" is a methodological argument, not a replication. Nothing in it concerns reading of connected text.
