## What it argues

Ryan gives "a broad historical setting" for Yamey's criticisms of Sombart and others, who held double-entry bookkeeping to be central to the rise of capitalism (p. 85). He quotes the claims first: Spengler's "decisive event," Nussbaum's remark that its importance "can hardly be exaggerated," Mattessich's ranking of Pacioli's chapter with "the achievements attained by Columbus, Copernicus, Galileo, Descartes and Newton" (pp. 85–86; Spengler's own comparison names Columbus and Copernicus only). A footnote concedes that Pacioli's 1494 *Summa* was the first printed account, and that "it is generally agreed that he described the system as it had been practiced for about 200 years" (p. 86, n. 2).

He then summarises Yamey. In 1949 Yamey looked for regular balancing and found "little evidence of a careful calculation and analysis of profit" (p. 87, quoting Yamey 1949, p. 110). In 1959 he examined the double-entry accounts of six individuals and one partnership; Ryan says the diversity "virtually precludes generalising": four of seven had assets or liabilities missing, three balanced annually, three irregularly and one never in eleven years, and three were out of balance (pp. 90–91). By Yamey's own account the seven were not "a representative selection" (p. 90). Cost accounting joined double entry only in the last quarter of the nineteenth century (p. 95). Yamey "saw no reason to modify his initial view" that the link between double-entry output and economic decisions was not established (p. 91).

Ryan's contribution is a reconciliation. Yamey worked at the micro level of individual merchants' decisions; Sombart and the others wrote about society. Using Williamson's four levels of social analysis, Ryan places the historians' claim at the first level, informal institutions, where change takes centuries (pp. 92–94). He judges Yamey's scepticism "soundly based" for the period Yamey examined (p. 95), but holds that the question "transcends microeconomic decision making" (p. 96). His verdict: "double-entry bookkeeping, as an essential aspect of capitalism, emerged with it at about the same time, and with it, contributed to economic development" (p. 96).

## Caution

A thirteen-page note with no new data or archive work. It reports Yamey second-hand, and Sangster (2025, pp. 372–374) argues that Yamey's conclusions mix two definitions, bookkeeping and financial accounting, so Ryan's "soundly based" stands on the same ground. His own thesis joins a macro claim that cannot be tested here to a metaphor. It gives no date for the emergence of double entry beyond Pacioli's "about 200 years" of earlier practice.
