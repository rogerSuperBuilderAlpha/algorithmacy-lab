# *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868 (2009).

**Identifier:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/08-22.ZS.html (syllabus) with the opinion and dissents at `.../08-22.ZO.html`, `.ZD.html`, `.ZD1.html` · **Read depth:** full_text (the syllabus, Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, Chief Justice Roberts's dissent and Justice Scalia's dissent, from Cornell LII HTML; LII prints no U.S. Reports pagination, so locators are the opinion's Parts I–IV and the slip-opinion pages the syllabus cites, "Pp. 6–20") · **Source-tier:** court opinion (U.S. Supreme Court, 5–4) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none; a due-process ruling on judicial recusal · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (litigants and the adjudicator; a donor who helped choose the adjudicator) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

A West Virginia jury awarded Caperton $50 million against Massey for fraudulent misrepresentation, concealment and tortious interference (Part I). Massey's chairman, Don Blankenship, then spent "$3 million" to help elect Brent Benjamin to the state's high court, "more than the total amount spent by all other Benjamin supporters and three times the amount spent by Benjamin's own committee" (Part I; the opinion's own sums are "almost $2.5 million" to a §527 group, "just over $500,000" in independent expenditures, and $1,000 direct). Benjamin won 382,036 to 334,301 votes (53.3% to 46.7%). He refused to recuse three times, and the court reversed the verdict 3–2, twice.

The Court holds: "in all the circumstances of this case, due process requires recusal" (opening). Its reasoning is that "objective standards" implement due process because the judge's own search for actual bias "is not one that the law can easily superintend or review" (Part III). It draws on *Tumey v. Ohio* (a mayor-judge paid only on conviction), *Ward v. Monroeville*, *Aetna v. Lavoie*, *In re Murchison* ("no man can be a judge in his own case") and *Mayberry*. The test is whether, "under a realistic appraisal of psychological tendencies and human weakness," the interest "poses such a risk of actual bias or prejudgment that the practice must be forbidden" (Part III, quoting *Withrow*). The Court does "not question his subjective findings of impartiality" and does not decide that Benjamin was biased (Part III). The rule: "a serious risk of actual bias—based on objective and reasonable perceptions—when a person with a personal stake in a particular case had a significant and disproportionate influence in placing the judge on the case by raising funds or directing the judge's election campaign when the case was pending or imminent" (Part III). The inquiry looks at the contribution's size against the total contributed and spent and "the apparent effect" on the outcome, not whether it was "a necessary and sufficient cause" (Part III). Part IV says the case is "extreme," that most disputes will be settled by judicial codes (ABA Canon 2, 28 U.S.C. §455(a), state canons), and that the constitutional standard "will thus be confined to rare instances."

Roberts (joined by Scalia, Thomas, Alito) dissents: until now due process required recusal only for a financial interest and for certain criminal contempts, and the new "probability of bias" standard "provides no guidance"; he lists 40 questions it leaves open, including "How much money is too much money?" He also argues the spending was independent and the margin comfortable (7 points). Scalia's separate dissent predicts "vast uncertainty" and a new "Caperton claim."

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D8 asks how courts hold their conventions, to compare with FX markets and platforms. *Caperton* supplies a precise court answer to premise P10's concern about an interested party: when a third party who decides a dispute can be tempted by an interest in the outcome, the rule does not wait for proof that the interest worked. It prohibits the arrangement on the risk. The test is deliberately objective and does not rely on the decider's own account of his motives, which the Court calls hard for anyone to review. A platform's reviewer asked to judge its owner's own rule is in the structurally analogous position, and the paper can say so as an analogy, not as a holding. The case also gives a floor and a ceiling: due process sets the "outer boundaries," and codes of conduct do the daily work (Part IV), which fits the paper's interest in who writes the convention. The dissents show the cost: an objective but vague standard invites strategic claims (Roberts, Part III.A).

## Caution

A constitutional holding about elected state judges and campaign spending, 5–4, on "extreme" facts the majority says are rare. It should not be cited for a general right to a neutral decider outside courts, or for platform moderation.
