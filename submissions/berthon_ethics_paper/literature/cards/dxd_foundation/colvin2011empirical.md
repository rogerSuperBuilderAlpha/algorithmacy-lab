# Colvin, A. J. S. (2011). An empirical study of employment arbitration: Case outcomes and processes. *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies, 8*(1), 1–23.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/j.1740-1461.2010.01200.x · **Read depth:** full_text (the "Forthcoming" author manuscript from Cornell eCommons, 43 PDF pages; locators are the manuscript's own page numbers 1–30, not the journal's 1–23; the tables are at the end of the manuscript; numbers cited here come from the prose; the reference list was not read in full) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (accepted manuscript) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** American Arbitration Association (AAA) filings under the California Code, Jan 2003–Dec 2007: 5,592 cases, of which 1,647 were mediations; 3,945 employment arbitrations; 1,213 ended in an award; all under employer-promulgated procedures; descriptive statistics, bivariate tests and logit/OLS models with year dummies and employer-clustered errors · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (employee, employer, arbitrator chosen from a pool the employer may use repeatedly) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Colvin uses state-mandated public filings to describe outcomes of mandatory employment arbitration. His abstract lists the findings (p. 1): employee win rate 21.4%, lower than in employment litigation trials; median award $36,500 and mean $109,858 where employees won; mean time to disposition 284.4 days for settled cases and 361.5 days for cases decided after a hearing; mean fees $6,340 per case ($11,070 for cases decided after a hearing), with employers paying all fees beyond a small filing fee in 97% of such cases; 82.4% of employees earning under $100,000 a year.

Details with locators. A "win" is "any case in which some award of damages, however small, is made in favor of the employee" (broad, "arbitration-favorable"), giving 260 of 1,213 award cases, 21.4% (p. 7). Earlier convenience-sample studies found employee win rates of 40–75% (p. 6); litigation trials 33–36% in federal discrimination trials and 50–60% in state trials (p. 6). Colvin flags "apples and oranges": 59.1% of cases settled (2,328), and litigation settles in "upwards of 70 percent" (p. 7). He names a "arbitration-litigation gap" (p. 8). Median arbitration awards are "around 5–10 times" smaller than litigation medians (pp. 10–11). Hearing-to-award is "about half as long" as litigation, though "a year to resolve cases is still a relatively long period" (p. 12). Win rates rise with salary: 22.7% under $100,000, 31.4% for $100,001–250,000, 42.9% above (the top cell has 14 observations; p. 16); salary is reported in only 37% of award cases (p. 16).

Repeat players are the core. 2,613 of 3,941 cases (66.3%) involved a repeat employer (p. 19). Employees won 31.6% against one-shot employers and 16.9% against repeat employers (p < .01); mean awards were $40,546 and $16,134 (p. 19–20). 624 of 3,934 cases (15.9%) had a repeat employer–arbitrator pairing; the employee win rate was 23.4% without a pairing and 12.0% with one (p < .01) (p. 21). Inside the repeat-employer subsample the gap is 12.0% against 18.6% (p < .05) (p. 21). Self-represented employees were 24.9% of cases (980 of 3,940) and won 18.3% against 22.9% with counsel (p < .10) (p. 24); with a repeat pairing the rates were 14.7% (counsel) and 8.0% (self-represented) (p. 25). In the logit model the odds ratio is 0.514 for repeat employers and 0.598 for repeat pairings (pp. 27–28). Colvin offers six explanations, four about size and expertise (resources, experience, fairer HR policies, internal grievance procedures) and two specific to pairings (arbitrator bias to win future business; employers learning to pick favourable arbitrators) (pp. 17–19). The pairing result persists after controlling for repeat employers, which "indicates that the employer is receiving an advantage from choosing the same arbitrator in multiple cases" (p. 29).

## Relation to the argument

For ruling D8 and premise P10, this is quantitative evidence on one private third-party forum designed and supplied by the party with the repeat interest. It shows what the J-facet needs: when one party plays the forum repeatedly and picks or funds the decider, outcomes differ for the one-shot party, and a pairing effect appears even beyond size advantages. It sharpens the comparison with platforms: platforms are a stronger case than arbitration since the owner writes the rule and runs the forum; arbitration at least has a named neutral. Colvin himself separates what the data cannot show: bias versus expertise. The gap he finds is not proven to be bias, since explanations 1–4 predict the repeat-employer effect without bias (p. 19).

Facet check: the J-facet had only the abstract and its summary agrees with the abstract. The full text adds the repeat-player numbers (the abstract only says "significantly lower") and the caveat that the arbitration–litigation comparison is not matched. Nothing in the abstract is contradicted.

## Caution

Manuscript version, not the journal pagination; tables unread. AAA data only, which Colvin says "may be somewhat unrepresentative" (p. 2); California Code filings omit the employee's name, claim basis and decision text; salary is missing for most cases. The litigation comparisons use other studies' samples and are not matched. No causal claim about arbitrator bias is made or supported. The period ends in 2007; the AAA's policies have changed since.
