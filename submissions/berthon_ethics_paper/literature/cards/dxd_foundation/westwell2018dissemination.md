## What it argues

Westwell studies the *ordines romani*, Frankish texts describing Roman liturgical ceremonies, as whole manuscripts rather than as witnesses to an original text. His thesis is that their reception was local and creative. The summary puts it directly: "what occurred under the Carolingians in the liturgy did not involve the imposition of the Roman rite from above. What was 'Roman' and 'correct' was decided by individuals, each in their own case" (thesis summary, front matter).

The Introduction sets this against Michel Andrieu, the texts' editor, for whom Carolingian reform "was a uniform endeavour with a single goal" imposed from the centre (p. 4). Westwell follows McKitterick, who found interventions to be "local initiatives" that "differed in nature from one place to another", and Hen, who found no evidence of "centralised imposition" of particular liturgical texts (pp. 4–5). He keeps the term *correctio* but questions it: it "suggest[s] a teleological movement" or "dictates handed uncomplicatedly down from above, from Emperor or Pope" (p. 255), whereas the manuscripts show "constant dialogue and adaptation" (p. 255).

The Conclusion generalises. "Closeness to the centre of power, even to the shaping of the very decrees and edicts traditionally seen as demanding uniformity, has not made that supposed uniformity any more real" (p. 288). Even the most Roman texts "were never received as the final word, the trumpet call to uniformity" (p. 289). The network of bishops and monasteries, "highly linked, constantly communicating, and revolving around the imperial centre, never sought a single answer" (pp. 289–290). The *ordines* belonged to a "'higher' liturgical discussion" of "a court-favoured few" (p. 287), not to local priests' handbooks.

## Relation to the argument

**Supports the caution the draft draws.** The draft cites Westwell for one point: that "uniformity imposed from above can be overstated". The thesis makes exactly that argument for liturgy (summary; pp. 4–5, 255, 288–289).

**Cuts against the sentence that follows.** The draft concludes the paragraph: "What they show is who stood behind the page: an authority with standing over the scribe." Westwell's picture complicates that. In the liturgical manuscripts he studied, what counted as correct was settled by bishops, liturgists and communities in a network, and the centre's decrees did not make it uniform (pp. 255, 288). That is still an institution with standing over its scribes, so the draft's claim survives, but the authority was distributed and negotiated, not a single ruler's order. Facet O reached the same reading.

**Out of scope.** Westwell is about liturgical texts, not script, layout or word separation; I found no discussion of word division in the parts read or by keyword search. He cannot be cited for any claim about the page's conventions, only about the limits of Carolingian imposition in one domain.
