Eye movements during silent and oral reading in a regular orthography: Basic characteristics and correlations with childhood cognitive abilities and adolescent reading skills. *PLOS ONE, 12*(2), e0170986. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0170986

**Identifier:** doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0170986; PMC5289712 (open access) · **Read depth:** full_text (PMC XML: introduction, methods, Results 3.1–3.5 with Tables 1–4, discussion, limitations, conclusion; locators are section numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** 23 German-speaking adolescents aged 13;6 from a Graz birth cohort volunteered; one was excluded for esotropia, leaving 22, and one whose oral gaze duration (565 ms) lay more than three SD above the mean was removed, so n = 21 analysed (§§2.1, 2.6); they read a 150-word school-book passage first silently then aloud on an iView X Hi-Speed tracker at 500 Hz; reading skills by the LGVT silent-reading test; preschool K-ABC scores at 5;7 (§2) · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and page) · **Facets:** W · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues

A within-subject comparison of reading modes in a regular orthography. Reading aloud took 73,791 ms for the text against 56,481 ms silently (Table 1), 31% longer (my arithmetic); fixations per word rose from 1.24 to 1.62, average fixation from 207 to 225 ms, gaze duration from 323 to 389 ms, saccadic amplitude fell from 3.30° to 2.99°, and regressions rose from 18% to 21% of saccades (p = .054) (Table 1). Five of eleven measures correlated across modes (first fixation r = .83), six did not (§3.2). Reading skill predicted silent-reading eye movements (R² = .29–.60 for seven measures) but, in oral reading, only saccadic amplitude (R² = .36) (§3.3), and the gap between modes in gaze duration, refixation time, saccade count and amplitude grew with skill (R² = .37–.64) (§3.4). Preschool cognitive scores predicted later reading skill (R² = .27–.32) but not eye movements (§3.5). The authors read the oral cost as the dual demand of articulation and of keeping the eye-voice span, citing Vorstius et al. (2014) and Laubrock and Kliegl (§4).

## Relation to the argument

A small datum for the premise behind the abstract's offloading claim: voicing the text is a cost, measured here at about a third more time with more and longer fixations, and the cost is larger for better readers. But the text was spaced. The oral cost that spacing is said to remove persists on a spaced page, so the sentence "word separation freed literacy by moving boundary detection from the voice to the page" cannot mean that spacing removed the voice's cost; at most it removed one job the voice may have done. Supports facet W's narrower reading; adds nothing on unspaced text.

## Caution

n = 21, one order only (silent then aloud, with the LGVT and neuromotor tests between), no comprehension check, a hard text with compounds; the authors list these limits themselves (§4.1). The percentage above is the card's arithmetic on Table 1 means. A minor card: useful for one number on the cost of voicing, not for anything about spacing.
