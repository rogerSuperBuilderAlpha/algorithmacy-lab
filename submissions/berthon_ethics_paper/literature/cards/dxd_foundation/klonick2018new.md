## What it argues

Klonick argues that Facebook, Twitter and YouTube are best understood as private governors of online speech, not as state actors, broadcasters or editors. She asks why platforms moderate at all, given the immunity of section 230, and answers from interviews: "American free speech norms", corporate responsibility, and the economic need to give users an environment that matches their expectations (Part II, pp. 1616–30). She describes a system that moved from vague standards ("Feel bad? Take it down", p. 1631) to detailed rules, because content volume, user diversity and outsourced moderators demanded consistency (pp. 1631–35). Willner called the shift "ultimately a form of technical writing" (p. 1634).

Enforcement runs through tiers of human moderators, trained "to exercise professional judgment" under internal rules; the moderators "act in a capacity very similar to that of judges" (pp. 1642, 1647; the "judges" sentence is at p. 1647). Numbers she reports: YouTube had about sixty staff reviewing flagged video in mid-2006 (p. 1632); Facebook's first internal rulebook ran to roughly 15,000 words and the later version over eighty pages (p. 1634); users flag over one million items a day (p. 1638); after one incident Zuckerberg announced 3,000 more moderators, a rise of two-thirds (p. 1641). Appeals differ by platform: "Facebook allows an appeal of the removal of only a profile or page — not individual posts or content", while at YouTube suspensions, strikes and content removal are appealable, and at Twitter "any form of action related to the Twitter Rules can be appealed" (p. 1648).

Who changes the rules? The platforms do, in response to "a diverse pluralistic group of forces" (p. 1664): government requests, media coverage, civil-society groups and the process of moderation itself (pp. 1649–58). She credits a media outcry with reversing the removal of the "Napalm Girl" photograph, and notes that the photo had probably been removed thousands of times before without effect because earlier posters were not famous (pp. 1654–55). She also reports that the rulebook is private: "no major platform has made such guidelines public" (p. 1639, quoting Buni and Chemaly), and the internal rules change more often than the public ones (pp. 1639, 1648).

Part IV names two problems. Equal access: "the lack of an appeals system for individual users" and "different treatment and rule sets for powerful users" show that fair participation "is not currently a prioritized part of platform moderation systems" (p. 1665). Accountability: the system "leaves users essentially powerless"; only responsiveness to user norms gives them any power, "in an indirect and amorphous way" (p. 1666); and "Besides exit or leveraging of government, media, or third-party lobbying groups, users are simply dependent on the whims of these corporations" (p. 1668). She proposes voluntary "technological due process" after Citron (p. 1668) and warns that regulation should follow an accurate picture of how moderation works (pp. 1666, 1670).

## Relation to the argument

For D8, Klonick supplies the platform side of the comparison in 2016–17: a private rulebook, written by the owner, changed by the owner, enforced by trained staff in tiers, with escalation but a thin appeal. In form the system resembles a court (rules, examples, training, escalation) but lacks what the court comparison needs. The rulebook is not public; moderators do not "reason directly from prior content decisions" (p. 1646), so there is no precedent; and the appeal is partial. P10 gets a qualification. Klonick finds the owner's interest partly aligned with users' collective norms, because engagement drives revenue (pp. 1627–30). Yet she finds the alignment indirect and no protection for an individual user. A convention that the owner can change alone is what her account shows. She also names the same triad shape the paper uses, "a new triadic model of speech that sits between the state and speakers-publishers" (p. 1603; again p. 1664), but her third party is the platform between state and speaker, not an algorithm between two people; do not borrow her triad as support for the paper's.

## Caution

The data are 2016 interviews and 2012 leaked documents about Facebook, YouTube and Twitter. Footnote 319 says the leaked oDesk "Abuse Standards" "are not the actual rules of Facebook" but "oDesk's approximation" (p. 1644). The article never mentions the Oversight Board, so its account of appeals describes Facebook as of 2017 and is no guide to the platform after the Board began deciding cases. Her interviewees are present or former company policy staff, who describe their own work. A law review article offers no sampling or outcome data and no count of how often a moderator's decision is appealed or reversed. Do not cite it for how any platform moderates today.
