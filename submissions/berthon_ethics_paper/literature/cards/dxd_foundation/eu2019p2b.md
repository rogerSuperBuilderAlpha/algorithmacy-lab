## What it argues

The Regulation sets "rules to ensure that business users of online intermediation services and corporate website users in relation to online search engines are granted appropriate transparency, fairness and effective redress possibilities" (Art. 1(1)). The reason is dependence: because of "strong data-driven indirect network effects," small businesses depend on the services, "the providers of those services often have superior bargaining power, which enables them to, in effect, behave unilaterally in a way that can be unfair" (recital 2). Redress is poor: providers often lack "accessible and effective internal complaint-handling systems" and business users fear retaliation (recital 5).

The main duties:
- **Terms (Art. 3).** Plain and intelligible language; available at all stages; terms must "set out the grounds for decisions to suspend or terminate or impose any other kind of restriction" (3(1)(c)). Changes need notice "at least 15 days" (3(2)). Non-compliant terms and changes "shall be null and void" (3(3)).
- **Restriction, suspension, termination (Art. 4).** A "statement of reasons" on a durable medium, before or when a restriction or suspension takes effect, and at least 30 days before a termination (4(1)–(2)). The user must be able to "clarify the facts and circumstances" through the internal complaint system, and the provider must reinstate if it revokes (4(3)). The statement must refer to "the specific facts or circumstances, including contents of third party notifications," and the grounds (4(5)). Exceptions: legal obligation, repeated infringement (4(4)–(5)).
- **Ranking (Art. 5).** Providers set out "the main parameters determining ranking and the reasons for the relative importance of those main parameters" (5(1)); the description must let users understand whether and how ranking reflects the characteristics of the goods (5(5)). But providers are "not required to disclose algorithms or any information that, with reasonable certainty, would result in the enabling of deception of consumers or consumer harm through the manipulation of search results" (5(6)); recital 27 repeats that detailed functioning, including algorithms, need not be disclosed, and that commercial interest "should . . . never lead to a refusal to disclose the main parameters."
- **Differentiated treatment (Art. 7).** A description of any differentiated treatment of the provider's own goods or those of businesses it controls (data access, ranking, remuneration). **Data (Art. 9)**, **specific terms (Art. 8)** including no retroactive changes, and **restrictions on offering different conditions elsewhere (Art. 10)**.
- **Redress.** An internal complaint-handling system, "easily accessible and free of charge," based on "transparency and equal treatment," with annual public statistics: total complaints, main types, average processing time, aggregate outcomes (Art. 11(1), (4)); two or more named mediators who are "impartial and independent," affordable and quick, with the provider bearing "a reasonable proportion" of costs (Art. 12); representative organisations and public bodies may sue to stop non-compliance (Art. 14). Small enterprises are exempt from Arts. 11 and 12 (11(5), 12(7)).
- **Soft law and review.** The Commission encourages codes of conduct (Art. 17) and must evaluate the Regulation by 13 January 2022 and every three years (Art. 18), including whether competition between a provider's own offers and business users' offers is fair and whether providers misuse privileged data (18(2)(d)). Member States set penalties that are "effective, proportionate and dissuasive" (Art. 15).

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D8 asks where the convention bends on platforms. The Regulation is the strongest legal counter-example to the J-facet's "highly draconian" reading: it forces notice, reasons, a complaint route and mediation on platforms, as to business users. It also bears on ruling D3 (disclosure is a literacy intervention, not an algorithmacy one) in a way the paper can use: Article 5 is a disclosure rule built to give users "an adequate understanding" of ranking (5(5)) while expressly protecting the algorithm (5(6), recital 27). The law therefore ratifies the author's distinction, a statement of main parameters without the algorithm. The scope is a limit: only business users and search-engine website owners are covered, not the consumers or workers the paper's triad centres on, and small enterprises are exempt from the complaint and mediation duties. Article 4 gives no right to a decision by anyone but the provider, and the final say stays with it (the user may complain, mediate or sue). For premise P10, Art. 7 and recitals 30–31 state the conflict directly: a provider that also sells on its own platform has "an economic incentive and the ability" to favour itself.

Facet check: the J-facet read the UK-retained version on legislation.gov.uk and noted that Art. 5(6)–(7) were omitted there. The official EU text contains both: 5(6) is the no-algorithm-disclosure clause above. Facet statements about Arts. 3, 4, 11 and 12 should be rechecked against the pinpoints above (e.g. 15 days' notice, 30 days' notice before termination), since the UK text varied in jurisdictional references.

## Caution

A statute is not evidence of practice: nothing here shows platforms complying or users benefiting. The Regulation covers business users, not consumers, and not all intermediaries. Article 18 requires periodic Commission reviews; check for amendments before citing it as the law in force. Recitals were read selectively.
