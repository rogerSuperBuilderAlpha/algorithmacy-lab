# Financial Stability Board. (2015). *Foreign exchange benchmarks: Report on progress in implementing the September 2014 recommendations* (1 October 2015).

**Identifier:** https://fsb.org/uploads/FX-Benchmarks-progress-report.pdf · **Read depth:** full_text (21-page PDF; the printed page numbers 1–13 of the body are used as locators; Appendix B, extra currency charts for six pairs, was not read beyond its title and the body's statement that the patterns are similar) · **Source-tier:** report (international standard-setter) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** a survey of progress as at 30 June 2015 gathered through foreign exchange committees and central banks (Appendix A lists them), plus a data analysis by the Bank of Canada and the Federal Reserve Board of trading around the London 4pm fix, Nov 2014–May 2015, for six currencies (EUR and JPY from EBS; AUD, GBP, CAD and MXN from Thomson Reuters Matching), with the pre-change comparison being April–September 2013 data from the 2014 report (fn. 15) · **Parties modeled:** other (market participants, a benchmark administrator, and official-sector overseers) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

The report says "there has been good progress in implementing many of the September 2014 recommendations, although on some, the progress has been mixed" (p. 1). It reviews the fifteen recommendations in four groups.

**Methodology (recs. 1–4).** "On 15 February 2015, WM widened the window used to calculate benchmark rates from 1 minute for 'traded' currencies and 2 minutes for 'non-traded' currencies, to 5 minutes for all currency pairs" (p. 2). WM added data feeds (Thomson Reuters Matching for four more pairs, approved 15 February 2015), and planned a user group that "does not yet exist" but should convene by the end of 2015 (pp. 2–3). IOSCO would follow up its review of WM. In April 2015 the WM/Reuters 4pm rate and six other benchmarks became subject to FCA standards on governance, conflicts and record keeping (p. 3).

**Execution (recs. 6–8).** Independent netting facilities are growing but some banks are reluctant (pp. 3–4). Most sell-side respondents had largely implemented charging for fixing transactions, and "the widening of the fix window by WM in February 2015 was a focal point for a number of banks to begin charging" (p. 4). Some clients complained about lack of transition periods and about first-mover disadvantage (p. 4). Many larger banks moved fixing orders from the voice desk to electronic desks, some by physical separation, others only by separate order-collection processes (p. 5).

**Conduct (recs. 9–13).** The global committees issued a joint statement in January 2015 and "Shared Global Principles" in March 2015 (Tokyo); the BIS Markets Committee was to complete a single global code by May 2017 (pp. 6–7).

**Data (§3).** Dealer-driven algorithmic trading grew from about Q3 2014 and jumped when the window widened, and is now the largest share of volume in the window for EUR and JPY (p. 8). Volume remains highest in the window, but is distributed more evenly and is less front-loaded than under the one-minute window (pp. 8–10). Bid-offer spreads are lowest in the window and market depth rises, as in 2013 (p. 11). Prices begin to move only once the window opens; high-frequency volatility stays near 2013 levels except for EUR; five-minute volatility rose relative to the rest of the day (p. 12). Order flow has "a slightly higher influence on price behaviour," and the report cannot say whether this comes from algorithms or the window (p. 13). The authors infer from the lack of pre-window volume that participants may no longer trade ahead using knowledge of aggregate fixing flow, and add that this "does not mean, however, that prices will not move, even temporarily, when there are sizeable one-way flows" (p. 13). The conclusion: the implementation of the recommendations, together with enhanced scrutiny, "appears to have moved the market in a favourable direction" (p. 13), with implementation for non-London fixes "less advanced."

## Relation to the argument

For ruling D8 (where the convention bends) the report is the dated evidence that an FX convention changed: the fix window went from one or two minutes to five on a stated date, five months after the FSB's 2014 recommendations, by the administrator's action, with the official sector recommending and then reporting. The pattern fits the J-facet's reading of FX as a market whose conventions are set by a private administrator (WM) and industry committees under official-sector pressure. It also records that the administrator's user group had not yet been formed, which is the voice of users still pending. For the paper's claim about conventions that an owner can withdraw alone, the window change shows a convention changing by the benchmark owner after outside pressure, not by the clients who relied on the fix. Treat the effect claims with care: the report reads the data as "may indicate" and compares different periods.

Facet check: the J-facet quoted the 15 February 2015 sentence accurately and read only §2.1. The rest of the report adds the charging and separation findings, the algorithmic shift, and the report's own caution that causes are unclear (p. 13); none of it contradicts the facet.

## Caution

A progress report assembled from self-reported survey answers and an official-sector analysis of a seven-month sample, with no control group and a pre-change sample from a different year. It describes the market's own response and does not test whether manipulation fell. Charts were read as described in the text; the underlying numbers are not given. The report does not mention last look.
