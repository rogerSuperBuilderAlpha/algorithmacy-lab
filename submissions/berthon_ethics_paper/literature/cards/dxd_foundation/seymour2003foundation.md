# Seymour, P. H. K., Aro, M., & Erskine, J. M. (2003). Foundation literacy acquisition in European orthographies. *British Journal of Psychology, 94*(2), 143–174.

## What it argues

The COST A8 network tests whether orthographic complexity slows the first phase of learning to read, or only shapes later phases (pp. 144–145). It classifies the languages on two dimensions drawn from the members' own estimates: syllabic complexity and orthographic depth (Table 1, p. 146). French, Portuguese, Danish and English count as deep. Children across the countries read lists of nine letters, familiar words and simple nonwords.

Letter knowledge does not separate the languages: every group reached about 90% or better, and the Scottish Primary 1 group (age 5) scored 94% (Study 1, p. 151). Word reading does. By accuracy on familiar words: Finnish 98.3%, German 97.7%, Spanish 94.7%, Portuguese 73.5%, French P1 79.1%, Danish P1 71.1%, Scottish P1 33.9% (Table 5, p. 153). The Scottish P2 group (about age 6½) reached 76.4%; French P2 reached 99.2% and Danish P2 92.6%. Nonword accuracy runs the same way: Scottish P1 29.3%, Danish P1 53.7%, Portuguese 76.9%, against 82–95% in the other Grade 1 groups (Table 6, p. 156). For English, the authors regress accuracy on British Ability Scales reading age: the Scottish group crosses 90% on words at a reading age of 7 years 4 months (p. 152), and passes the monosyllable nonword threshold above 7.5 years (p. 157). They conclude that English readers need "2½ or more years" to reach what most European children reach in the first year, "a ratio of about 2.5:1" (pp. 167–168). The abstract says "more than twice as slow."

Syllabic complexity selectively slows nonword decoding and enlarges the word advantage (word minus nonword accuracy: 8.63 points in complex-syllable languages against 1.97 in simple ones; p. 160). Age of starting does not explain the gap: age correlated weakly with performance (Table 3, p. 149), and Danish children, who start at 7, still lag (pp. 165–166). The authors reject a teaching-method explanation, since Scottish schools use a mixed sight-word and phonics method fitted to a deep orthography (p. 166). Their mechanism: a deep orthography forces a "dual" logographic plus alphabetic foundation, and "there will always be a cost" that makes "irreducible differences in rates of progress" (p. 169). Variation between children is also wider in deep orthographies; normal scores in English, Danish, French and Portuguese fall in the disability range of the shallow ones (p. 165).

## Relation to the argument

Facet C reads this as support for premise P6 in the narrow sense: a script's design changes the rate at which children in school acquire it. The full text confirms that, and gives the size: roughly 2.5 to 1 for English against most European scripts. It also fits the ruling that the paper offers history as a guide, not a mechanism (D10): the cost sits in the script's design, and the script change that would remove it is not on offer. Seymour et al. do not test an intervention. They treat the cost as built into the orthography ("irreducible"), which cuts against any reading of the paper that expects design alone to remove a learning cost, and it concerns letter-sound mapping, not spaces between words, so the word-separation precedent (P2, P5) gets no direct support. It says nothing about who attends school (D1, D2).

Facet check: the facet quotes the abstract's "more than twice as slow." The body gives a ratio of about 2.5:1 and also two further points the facet does not record: letter-sound knowledge does not differ between languages, and the gap sits in word and nonword reading.

## Caution

The English sample is two Dundee groups; the comparison uses the high-socioeconomic-status group only, tested at about 5½ and 6½ years, younger than every other group, so the authors admit that immaturity may add to the English gap (p. 167). Group sizes are small and the French data have missing cases (Table 2 note). The depth classification rests on committee estimates, not a computed measure (p. 146 note 1). The tests are short lists of very simple items, built as a "primitive" decoding model, not text reading. The 2.5:1 ratio is a regression extrapolation on reading age, not an observed time-to-mastery.
