## What it argues

Kellogg, Valentine and Christin read the research on algorithms at work through labour process theory, in which control is "contested terrain" between employers and workers (p. 366). They define algorithmic technologies as procedures that are "more encompassing, instantaneous, interactive, and opaque" than earlier workplace technologies (p. 366) and name four affordances: comprehensive, instantaneous, interactive, opaque (Table 1, p. 371). Employers, they find, control workers through "6 Rs": they direct by restricting and recommending, evaluate by recording and rating, and discipline by replacing and rewarding (abstract, p. 366; Tables 2–4, pp. 373, 377, 380).

Several findings bear on this paper. They describe opacity as partly deliberate: "information asymmetries are not random: instead, they are deliberately created by employers to constrain workers' choices" (p. 384). They cite Lee et al. (2015) for surge pricing that was "often erratic and unreliable" (p. 374) and for platforms that "narrow shift choices, ride choices, or delivery choices" (p. 375). They say workers "often do not fully grasp how algorithms are being used to direct, evaluate, and discipline them" and that "there is frequently no procedure in place for workers to get access to, contest, or challenge algorithmic decisions" (p. 387). Individualised, real-time control, they argue, makes collective resistance harder: "Whereas previous systems of control allowed collectives of workers to organize and share resistance tactics over time … algorithmic control can make such initiatives and contestations harder to achieve" (p. 386).

The last section names worker resistance "algoactivism" (pp. 391–395). Individual tactics include non-cooperation, "leveraging algorithms" and negotiating with clients (p. 391). Workers "reverse engineer the algorithm that produced the rating" (p. 391); Airbnb hosts "participated in online forums, read the company's technical documentation, and monitored competitors' profiles" to work out what drives ratings (p. 391); Lee et al.'s drivers logged off and avoided areas (p. 391). Collectively, workers organise "using online forums and platforms" in which they "help each other learn new systems and practices, anticipate or avoid disciplinary processes" (p. 392). The authors add that forums are one route among platform cooperatives, discursive framing about fairness and legal mobilisation (pp. 392–395).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D7 and premise P9 (learning by deciding).** The review supports the idea that workers learn a mediator from what it does, not from documentation, but only in part. Its examples of learning mix the two. The Airbnb hosts read "the company's technical documentation" *as well as* forums and competitors' profiles (p. 391). So the review does not license the claim that workers learn *without* the company's account. It does license the weaker claim that workers infer the rules by acting on the system and pooling what they observe.

**Ruling D8 and premise P12 (who holds the convention).** This is the review's strongest use for the paper. Opacity is described as a choice the employer makes (p. 384), and the employer can recalibrate "architecture, nudges, and sanctions" while claiming full automation (p. 384). That fits P12: the owner of the interface is an interested party and can change a convention alone. The due-process passage (p. 387) gives the paper an older vocabulary (notice and a hearing) for what the weaker party lacks.

**What it cuts.** The review is about employer–worker control, a dyad with an instrument. Customers appear as raters, not as a second party whose moves the worker must predict. It does not support the paper's triadic claim (D9) and should not be cited for it. It also reports variation: some drivers experienced autonomy (Cameron 2018, p. 385), and some workers see the platform as an ally (p. 385).

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 266: "Ride-hail drivers do not learn dispatch and surge behavior from company documentation. They form working theories from what the system does in response to their own moves, and they trade those theories in online forums (Lee et al., 2015; Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020)." Partly supported. Kellogg et al. report reverse engineering, practical workarounds and forum knowledge-sharing (pp. 391–392), and they cite Lee et al. for drivers' tactics (p. 391). They are a secondary review here: they do not observe ride-hail drivers themselves. Two cautions: (1) the one ride-hail forum example they give is a blog, "The Rideshare Guy" (p. 392), and their Airbnb example includes reading the company's documentation (p. 391), so "do not learn … from company documentation" goes further than this source; (2) they say nothing specific about drivers forming theories of *dispatch*. Citing Kellogg et al. for the general pattern (workers infer and pool) is safe; for the ride-hail specifics, Lee et al. (2015) is the primary source.

## Caution

A narrative review without a stated search method, so it cannot be cited for how common any tactic is. Much of its evidence is secondary and pre-2019. It treats control between employer and worker; its "platform" examples include crowd work and Airbnb, not only ride-hail. The bib entry spells the third author "Angéle"; the article prints "Angèle". OpenAlex dates the work 2019 (online first); the volume year is 2020, as the draft has it.

## Also used by facet T (2026-10-15)

Facet T asks which literatures model the platform as a third party with its own objective. This review is the cautionary case: it treats the algorithm as the employer's instrument in an employer–worker dyad, with customers as raters (see "What it cuts" above; the phrasing is this card's, not the review's). Set against `stark2020algorithmic` (p. 54: "this triangular geometry … [is] key") and `vallas2020what` (16.10: the platform as *tertius gaudens*), it marks the dividing line in the literature. The abstract's "irreducible algorithmic triad" sides with Stark and Pais and Vallas and Schor; a reader schooled in Kellogg, Valentine and Christin will ask why the customer is a party rather than an input, and the paper should answer with the alliance evidence (`vallas2020what` 16.7; `cruz2023digital` p. 3) and with the ride-hail cases in which the customer's decision enters the mediator's rule (`cohen2016using`, `castillo2025matching`).
