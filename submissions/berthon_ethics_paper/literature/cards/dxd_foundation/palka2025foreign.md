# Pałka, P., Porębski, A., Pałosz, R., & Wiśniewska, K. (2025). Do foreign service providers simply ignore the EU consumer law? An empirical study of 100 online terms of service. *Journal of Consumer Policy, 48*(3), 233–268.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s10603-024-09580-z · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access publisher PDF, journal pagination 233–268; the two appendix tables of regression output skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** coding of 100 online terms of service (ToS) obtained 22 February 2022 from platforms operating in Poland, sixteen market sectors, coded by two annotators on nine variables against the EU Unfair Contract Terms Directive (UCTD); logistic regression and contingency tests on three predictors; purposive sample, exploratory · **Parties modeled:** two parties (provider and consumer) with public enforcers as a third party · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
The authors ask how many ToS offered in the EU contain clauses that could be unfair under the UCTD, and whether the pattern follows the provider's headquarters, listing status or price. Their count is blunt: "100% of contracts contained clauses that could be deemed unfair" (p. 234). Of the 100, 76% had a potentially unfair limit on liability, 85% had clauses limiting consumers' rights in dispute resolution, and 99% had at least one clause letting the provider alter the economic relationship unilaterally (p. 234, p. 247). Seventy-four percent breached five or more of the nine variables, and 41% breached seven, eight or nine (p. 246).

Unfairness here is "potential": a judgment from the text and the law, not a court or agency finding (p. 239). Each variable took the value −1 (clear violation), 0 or 1; they also built binary versions. The sample held 15 providers headquartered in Poland, 23 elsewhere in the EU, 44 in the US and 18 in other jurisdictions (p. 239); 28 services were free, 8 paid, 64 optionally paid; 38 were publicly listed, 39 private and 23 indirectly public (p. 244).

Headquarters was the only predictor with a pattern. For six of nine variables the best model contained headquarters alone (p. 248). In logistic models on the binary variables, seven of nine variables related to headquarters at p < .035, and pseudo-R² (McFadden) exceeded .25 for unilateral change of contract and for account deletion (p. 248). No non-EU group ever scored significantly better than the EU. Grouped as EU (n = 38) against non-EU (n = 62), full compliance was more common in the EU group, but incompliance stayed "high or very high" in the EU on four variables (pp. 249–250). Payment status and public listing showed no link, apart from one variable (arb) where the best model also contained payment status (p. 248).

The authors offer untested explanations: providers weigh the cost of fines against the gain from deterring suits (an "in terrorem" effect, p. 234, p. 252); US firms treat penalties as business costs; ToS are copied between firms; national agencies police home firms first; and "diffusion of responsibility" among national agencies that are each competent but none responsible (p. 257). They also raise an uncomfortable reading, that under-enforcement may suit a system sceptical of mandatory terms (pp. 253–255), and ask lawmakers to say so openly. Their proposals: target non-EU providers, use automated clause detection, centralise enforcement for very large platforms under the Digital Services Act, and standardise the format and placement of risky clauses (pp. 257–258).

## Relation to the argument
Ruling D8 (platforms "take a highly draconian position"). The study gives a measured form of that claim for one convention, the platform's contract. It supports premise P10, that the interface owner's convention is not one the user can hold: the contract is written by the provider, and the clause type most often present (99%) is the right to alter the relationship unilaterally, with 81 of 100 coded −1 on unilateral change of contract, i.e. reserving a right to change the contract without a valid reason (Table 3, p. 247; definition in Table 1, p. 241).

It also complicates D8. The law has bent toward the consumer: the UCTD makes unfair terms non-binding (p. 236), and EU providers score better. What fails is enforcement, spread across agencies, not the existence of a convention. The proposal to put risky clauses "always in the same part of the contract" (p. 258) is a regulator-imposed layout convention, which echoes the spacing precedent; the match is the card's reading, not the authors'.

Facet check: the facet reads the paper as "terms still contain unfair clauses after the EU consumer-law regime". The text agrees but adds two limits. The comparison is within the EU market, so it says nothing about platform terms elsewhere. EU-headquartered firms were also affected (23 non-Polish EU firms plus 15 Polish).

## Caution
Potential unfairness from text alone; no court has held these clauses unfair, and the UCTD makes them non-binding when contested. The sample is purposive and built around the Polish market in 2022; the authors say it cannot be deemed representative of the entire population of online services (p. 244); the "other" group is heterogeneous; subgroup counts are small; the headquarters variable is the top parent's jurisdiction. The main text reports no inter-annotator statistic, only consistency checks and collaborative resolution of ambiguities by the principal investigator and the annotators (p. 239). The causal explanations are speculation, as the authors say (p. 255).
