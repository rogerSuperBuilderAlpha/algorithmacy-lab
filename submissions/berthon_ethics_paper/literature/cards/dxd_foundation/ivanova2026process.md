## What it argues
Ivanova traces how Hangul went from a royal invention (1443) to the national script. The abstract says that despite its design the script "faced strong resistance from the Confucian elite" and "for centuries the Korean alphabet was marginalized and associated with women, children, and the lower social classes" (p. 136). The body names the sources of the stigma: later rulers called it *onmun* ("plain script", my translation) and used other names tied to women, the middle class and children (p. 137). Hangul stayed mostly in fiction and, apart from one 1447 poem, was not used officially until the late nineteenth century (p. 138). The turn came with the 1894 Kabo reforms, which she says ordered official documents in Hangul, and with nationalism after the opening of the country (pp. 138–139). Under Japanese rule the Korean Language Society (from 1921; renamed 1931) worked on a standard spelling, a dictionary and a loanword code; Japan broke the society in the 1942 incident, and 31 members were arrested (pp. 143–149).

Two passages bear on word separation. First, she says the new script was to be written in syllable blocks, top to bottom and right to left, "as in China, without division between words" (my translation of "без разделение между думите", p. 137). Second, the Unified Orthography of 1933 (the plan was promulgated 29 October 1933) set three principles; the third is "всяка дума трябва да се пише отделно, а не слято с другите думи", that each word must be written separately and not run together with other words (p. 144). Chapter 7 of the plan covers spaces ("Интервали"), with rules that repeat that principle, write numbers in decimal form, and put spaces within compound proper names (p. 145). The plan came out of 125 meetings over three years and went to some 500 localities and public figures for comment (pp. 144–145).

## Relation to the argument
This is the best Korean-side evidence in the set, because it gives a word-separation convention an author, a date and a committee. On Ivanova's account Hangul ran without word division from 1443, and the separate-writing rule entered the standard orthography only in 1933, about 490 years later. That fits the facet C and F point that the script alone did not carry literacy (`T_adjudication.md`: "Hangul stayed marginal for four centuries"), and it gives the paper a second, later case of a spacing convention set by a body of practitioners (compare D8, who holds the convention). The Korean case therefore does not fit a story in which spacing comes first and enables mass reading. Ivanova gives no literacy data, so the case shows the order of events and nothing about cause.

Limits on what it shows: Ivanova does not say when Koreans first put spaces in Hangul texts, and she does not link the 1933 rule to any change in the number of readers. Her only literacy claim is the stigma and the late state adoption. The paper also cannot be used for P4 or P6 without further sources.

Facet check: facets C and F cite only the abstract, so the statements about spacing in the body are new to the project. The facet's "marginalized" quote is accurate.
