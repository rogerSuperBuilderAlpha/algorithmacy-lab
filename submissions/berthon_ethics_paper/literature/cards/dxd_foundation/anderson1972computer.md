## What it argues

The Air Force wanted several users with different clearances to share one computer. Anderson's panel states the problem as "the threat from a malicious user" (Preface, p. iv) and asks for a research and development programme to meet it. It argues that the usual defences, which wall off the machine from outsiders, do not work once the user population is mixed, because a malicious user "has legitimate access to a system" (§3.1, p. 8). The defence therefore "must reside in the process that controls the operation and execution of arbitrary programs", and its principal requirement is to "control and limit the references a program can make to other programs and data to just those authorized (by an external authority) for the user on whose behalf the program is executed" (p. 8).

The panel's answer is the reference monitor, a mechanism "which enforces the authorized access relationships between subjects and objects of a system" (§3.2.1, p. 9). Its implementation, the reference validation mechanism, checks each reference against a list of authorised types of reference, pictured as an access matrix (Figures 1–2, pp. 9–10). Three design requirements follow: the mechanism "must be tamper proof", it "must always be invoked", and it "must be small enough to be subject to analysis and tests, the completeness of which can be assured" (pp. 9–10). The panel explains why the third matters: since the mechanism "is the security mechanism in the system, it must be possible to ascertain that it works correctly in all cases" (p. 10). Section 3.5 applies the test to a commercial system and finds it fails two requirements: the supervisor runs with bounds checking suspended, so validation is not always invoked, and the supervisor with its support software, at 200,000 to 300,000 instructions, is too large to analyse (p. 12).

The report's diagnosis of why systems fail is a claim about design intent. Their designers "never considered other than a benign environment where seeming violations of the system are presumed to be accidental", so existing controls "are there to contain accidents or errors" (pp. 2–3). Add-on security and patching are "futile" (§4.3.2, p. 21); security "must be designed in at its inception" (Conclusions, p. 32). Tiger-team penetration tests prove nothing about security: they "can only reveal system flaws" (p. 4). Cost estimates support the point: a flaw in a contemporary system costs a person about one month, under $3,000, to find and exploit; a system "repaired" by over 250 changes (10–15 person-years over six months) fell to a second penetration in under one person-week (§5.2, p. 30). The report also warns that installations accept operating-system updates "without question", which leaves the door open to a trap door planted by the supplier (§4.3.1, p. 19).

The monitor has a stated edge. It "is predicated upon positive identification (authentication) of all users at all times", physical security and protected communications, and "these areas are not included" (§3.7, p. 13). Accidental spillage and similar threats are called serious but solvable "by well understood techniques" (p. 2). The report does not mention audit, logging, separation of duties or least privilege by those names. The nearest it comes is the access matrix, in which "privilege" is READ, WRITE or EXECUTE (p. 9), and "security surveillance techniques" as one line in the exploratory plan (p. 24).

## Relation to the argument

Facet G places this report first in the security lineage behind the Nadella insider post (premise P11): authority and load-bearing controls sit outside the thing being controlled. Anderson gives the clean statement of that idea. The controls must live in a mechanism the controlled program cannot subvert, because the program is assumed able to try.

Three limits on that use, all from the text. First, Anderson assumes a monitor that is small enough to verify (p. 10). A harness around a frontier model is not small, and the report treats size as the thing that makes certification possible. Second, the monitor mediates references against a list written "by an external authority" (p. 8); it does not read evidence or exercise judgment, and no human reader is part of the design. Third, "tamper proof" attaches to the enforcement mechanism (p. 9), and §3.3 softens it to "tamper resistant" in the certification step (p. 11).

On the paper's two tests (ARGUMENT_MAP, "Where the argument is exposed", item 1: what task the person keeps, whom the move serves): the reference monitor moves work from a person to an artefact, as ARGUMENT_MAP notes in the dropped-claims table. In Anderson's design the person who loses work is the system's operator or the cleared user, and the party the monitor serves is the owner of the classified data, who also writes the authorisation list. That is the owner-serves-owner arrangement the paper contrasts with the scribes' case under P10. The report does not touch a mediator with its own objective, or a counterparty that is not the controlling party.

## Caution

A planning document by a panel, written to secure funding for a prototype programme; it argues for a research agenda and gives cost figures (for example, an $8.0 million prototype programme, p. 24) rather than test results. The penetration costs are estimates "based on current experience", not measurements (pp. 29–30). Volume I is a summary: the arguments for the reference-monitor requirements, the penetration appendix and the rationale are in Volume II, which this card does not cover. Section numbering in the NIST copy is inconsistent: the table of contents lists §1.3 as "Penetration of Systems" and §1.4 as "Inadequacy of Contemporary System Designs", but the body prints them the other way; the contents list §3.1 as "Emerging Design Principles" and §3.2.2 as "Operating Requirements", while the body headings read "Requirements For Defense Against a Malicious User" and "Design Requirements". Cite by page. The scan has OCR noise, and the quotations here were checked against the text layer by eye. The report predates networked, adaptive, learning systems; it models a program that is hostile, not one that is merely opaque or misaligned.
