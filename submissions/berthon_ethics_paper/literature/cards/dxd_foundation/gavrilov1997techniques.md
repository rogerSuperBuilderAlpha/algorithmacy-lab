## What it argues

What can be seen from the opening paragraph: Gavrilov sets out the view he will attack, that "the Greeks and Romans always, or nearly always, read aloud. They did not read to themselves, silently, save in rare and special cases" (p. 56, Crossref abstract). The body is not available here, so his conclusions are known only at second hand. Johnson (2000, p. 593) says that after "the recent work of Gavrilov and Burnyeat" one can "assert that there was no cognitive difficulty when fully literate ancient readers wished to read silently" (as quoted in facet B). Heilmann (2020, p. 179, n. 13) lists Gavrilov among "well-argued counterproposals" not yet accepted.

The endnotes, read in full on the publisher page, show the structure and some claims directly.
- He targets the standard view in its named holders: Kenney, Rosalind Thomas, Lamer, Eisenhut, Rohde, Norden, Balogh's "Voces paginarum" (nn. 1–8).
- He grounds his argument in twentieth-century reading psychology (Gray 1957; Gibson & Levin 1975; Soviet work on reading pathology; the eye–voice span) (nn. 15–29).
- He reads Augustine *Confessions* VI 3 on Ambrose and VIII 12 together (nn. 31–43) and Euripides' *Hippolytus*, where Theseus reads Phaedra's letter (nn. 44–51).
- The paper was translated from Russian by M. F. Burnyeat. An earlier version appeared in 1989 in Leningrad (n. 52).
- On word separation, n. 28 states his view outright: "scriptio continua would be bound to affect the speed and accuracy of ancient reading to some degree, but (pace Raible) that quantitative effect should not impair anyone's ability to practise both of the two kinds of reading discussed in this paper". He adds that "the correlation between them must have been much the same as it is today" (n. 28, on the endnotes page; I cannot place it on a printed page).

## Relation to the argument

**Premises P1–P2 and the historiographical boundary.** Gavrilov is a classicist who holds that ancient readers could read silently and that unspaced script slowed reading without changing what kind of reading was possible (n. 28). That supports the draft's boundary sentence. It also cuts against the strongest form of the word-separation precedent. If scriptio continua did not stop silent reading, word separation did not create silent reading. At most it made reading faster and easier, which is the draft's narrower claim (P5, a re-representation). Facet B's open question ("I cannot say whether Gavrilov addresses word separation or Saenger") is now partly answered by n. 28. He addresses scriptio continua, against Raible. Whether he addresses Saenger cannot be seen: Saenger's book (1997) appeared in the same year and does not occur in the notes.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 85: "Nor was silent reading impossible in the ancient world: classical accounts show that skilled readers could and did read silently long before word spacing appeared (Knox, 1968; Gavrilov, 1997; Burnyeat, 1997; Heilmann, 2020)."
