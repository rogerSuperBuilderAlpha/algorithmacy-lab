# Suzor, N. (2018). Digital constitutionalism: Using the rule of law to evaluate the legitimacy of governance by platforms. *Social Media + Society, 4*(3), 1–11.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/2056305118787812 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher-formatted open-access PDF, CC BY-NC 4.0, from the QUT repository eprints.qut.edu.au/120050; journal pagination 1–11; reference list read only to the end of the "B" entries in the extracted text) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** doctrinal and document analysis of the terms of service of 14 English-language social media platforms, collected June 2015; no coding scheme reported beyond the three rule-of-law headings · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (platform owner governs users; courts and states sit outside) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Suzor proposes that the legitimacy of platform governance be judged by rule-of-law values: "consensual, transparent, equally applied and relatively stable, and fairly enforced" (abstract, p. 1). He opens with Facebook's 2009 promise of binding user votes on its terms: users opposed a privacy-policy change by 88% of 668,872 votes, under 1% of over a billion registered users, short of the 30% turnout needed, and by 2012 Facebook had dropped the commitment (p. 1–2). The legal frame is that terms of service are consumer contracts, "firm to consumer, not sovereign to citizen" (p. 3), and U.S. constitutional rights bind only the state, so "if you don't like it, leave" is the legal answer (p. 3).

He sorts the values into three headings and applies each to the 14 platforms (Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Pinterest, Google+, Instagram, Tumblr, Flickr, Vine, Meetup, Tagged, MeetMe, ClassMates, Ask.fm; VK excluded; note 3, p. 9):
- **Consent: governance limited by law (pp. 5–6).** Facebook is the only platform to limit termination to violations of its terms; the other 13 reserve termination "for any reason (or no reason)" (table, p. 5–6). "In all cases examined, the Terms of Service provided broad, unfettered discretion to platform owners," and "Like constitutional documents, Terms of Service grant powers, but unlike constitutions, they rarely limit those powers" (p. 6).
- **Equality and predictability (pp. 6–7).** Terms can all be changed by the platform alone. The text says "Approximately half of the platforms committed to some responsibility to inform users" of changes; only Facebook commits to an opportunity to review and comment, "but its commitment to a democratic voting mechanism has been removed"; some give no notice at all, so users must bear "responsibility for continuously checking the legal terms" (p. 7). The table's column grouping did not survive text extraction, so I give no per-platform counts for notice. Only Facebook, Google+ and Instagram limit content removal to unlawful or rule-breaking content; the other 11 reserve wide discretion (table, p. 7); LinkedIn: "We are not obligated to publish any information or content on our Service and can remove it in our sole discretion, with or without notice" (p. 7).
- **Due process (pp. 7–8).** No terms set up an internal dispute process; "The criteria that platform moderation teams use to evaluate decisions are secret"; six of the terms require binding arbitration (the platform pays costs in three), almost all fix the platform's home jurisdiction, most of those requiring arbitration also bar class actions, and all cap liability, often at USD 100 (LinkedIn USD 1,000 for paying users). "Users have no realistic chance of challenging decisions made by platforms in any formal legal process" (p. 8).

He is explicit about limits: he does not want platforms held to the standards of a constitutional democracy, favours "thin constitutionalism," and notes the method reads only formal documents, not community guidelines, moderator training documents or enforcement practice, so it shows "the outer legal bounds only" (pp. 5, 8–9).

## Relation to the argument
Supports the author's ruling D8, that platforms "take a highly draconian position," on the contract text, and gives premise P10 a named mechanism: the owner writes the rules, changes them alone and cannot be reviewed. The change-of-terms finding (p. 7) speaks to "a convention that the owner can withdraw alone is no convention" almost directly. It also hands the paper a vocabulary for what conventions would need to carry (notice, reasons, stability, appeal), which the Seattle form (`seattle2024deactivation`) and the EU instruments in facet J then meet.

Facet check: the facet had this article by abstract only and recorded 14 platforms; the full text confirms 14 and the quoted values. It also shows the sample is 2015 and U.S./common-law centred (p. 4–5), a limit the facet did not carry.

## Caution
Collected June 2015; terms have changed (the article notes LinkedIn's rewrite). Formal contracts only, no enforcement data, no count of how often clauses were used. Common-law and U.S. focus. A framework paper: the finding that platforms "perform" poorly (abstract) is a classification of clauses, with no effect on users measured.
