# *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927).

**Identifier:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/273/510 · **Read depth:** full_text (Cornell LII reproduction of the opinion of the Court by Chief Justice Taft; paragraph-numbered, no U.S. Reports page breaks, so locators are paragraph numbers, "¶", which are my count: ¶1 is the first paragraph after "delivered the opinion of the Court", and the block-quoted statute and ordinance paragraphs each count as one; counsel's argument is omitted in the source, "intentionally omitted" at pp. 511–513) · **Source-tier:** court opinion (U.S. Supreme Court) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none (legal holding on one village mayor's court) · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (accused, the State, and a judge paid from the outcome) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Taft states the question: whether Ohio's Prohibition Act, in letting a village mayor try the accused, denies due process under the Fourteenth Amendment "because of the pecuniary and other interest which those statutes give the mayor in the result of the trial" (¶1). The facts: Tumey was fined $100 for possessing liquor; the mayor received $12 in costs from a convicted defendant and nothing from an acquitted one (¶¶2, 39); fines were split half to the State and half to the village (¶14) and the ordinance sent half the village's share into a "secret service fund" paying detectives and marshals 15% of fines and the prosecutor 10% (¶¶19–22). Over May–December 1923 the court collected over $20,000, of which the mayor took $696.35 on top of his salary (¶36).

The holding: "it certainly violates the Fourteenth Amendment and deprives a defendant in a criminal case of due process of law to subject his liberty or property to the judgment of a court, the judge of which has a direct, personal, substantial pecuniary interest in reaching a conclusion against him in his case" (¶38). Taft draws a line short of this: "matters of kinship, personal bias, state policy, remoteness of interest would seem generally to be matters merely of legislative discretion" (¶38). He reviews English common-law practice and finds "the greatest sensitiveness over the existence of any pecuniary interest however small" (¶44), and finds the pay-only-on-conviction practice not embedded in custom (¶64). A de minimis exception is allowed, but about $100 a month in costs was not "minute, remote, trifling, or insignificant" (¶65).

Two points matter beyond money. First, the test is structural, not about the particular man: "There are doubtless mayors who would not allow such a consideration as $12 costs in each case to affect their judgment," yet "Every procedure which would offer a possible temptation to the average man as a judge to forget the burden of proof required to convict the defendant, or which might lead him not to hold the balance nice, clear, and true between the state and the accused denies the latter due process" (¶66). Second, a separate ground: the mayor was also the village's chief executive responsible for its finances, so "an official perforce occupies two practically and seriously inconsistent positions, one partisan and the other judicial" (¶67), while the mere union of executive and judicial duties in a small-village mayor is not itself a violation (¶67). Actual guilt does not cure it: "he had the right to have an impartial judge" (¶69). The judgment was reversed (¶70).

## Relation to the argument
This is the court-side case for the author's ruling D8 and premise P10. A third party that gains from one side's loss is disqualified as a matter of constitutional design, and the disqualifying fact is the interest, not proof of bias. A platform that decides disputes between itself and its users, and keeps the fees, fines or account value, is in the mayor's position by this test; Suzor's finding (`suzor2018digital`) that terms give the owner unreviewable discretion is the other half. It also supports the author's reading of convention as something held outside the interested party.

It cuts the other way in two places the paper should keep. The rule binds the State only (Fourteenth Amendment), and Taft expressly leaves "personal bias" and "remoteness of interest" to legislatures (¶38), so even courts police only the clearest interest by constitutional law. And the remedy is after the fact, at the accused's own initiative.

## Caution
A criminal due-process holding about a paid judge. It does not decide platforms, private arbitrators or civil disputes. LII text has no U.S. Reports pagination (cite as 273 U.S. 510 with ¶ for now, and take page pins from the official reporter before print). Counsel's arguments are omitted in the source. The Court says it was "referred to no cases at common law in England" showing judges paid only on conviction and reasons from analogous cases (¶41).
