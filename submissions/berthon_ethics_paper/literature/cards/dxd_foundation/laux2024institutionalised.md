## What it argues
Laux asks how to make human oversight of AI effective when people are unreliable overseers. He starts from the claim in the abstract that "Empirical research suggests, however, that humans are not reliable in fulfilling their oversight tasks" (p. 2853). In the body he is more careful: "The empirical record of such biases is still subject to debate" (p. 2854), and "A general assessment (or prediction) of the reliability of human overseers is currently unavailable" (p. 2858).

Contribution 1 (§2, pp. 2854–2856): Article 14 of the draft AI Act demands effective oversight for high-risk systems but gives little guidance on how, mentions "AI literacy" only through a Parliament amendment (p. 2856), and leaves developers' oversight duties underdefined (abstract, p. 2853). Contribution 2 (§3, pp. 2857–2858): a taxonomy by counterfactual influence. First-degree overseers (a doctor using an AI score, a moderator reviewing flagged posts) shape the decision. Second-degree overseers are "one step removed from the local decision in question, fulfilling auditing or review roles"; they "have no counterfactual influence on the initial decisional output but instead are corrective of it" (p. 2854). Crossed with two challenges, lack of competence and wrong incentives, this gives four areas of concern, A to D (Table 2, p. 2860).

Contribution 3 (§5, pp. 2859–2863): six principles taken from Sztompka's and Braithwaite's democratic theory of "institutionalised distrust": justification; periodical mandates; collective decisions; limited competence of institutions; justiciability and accountability; transparency (p. 2854). They anticipate that overseers will fail and design around it, for example by rotating auditors, requiring two or more reviewers, offering appeals and making oversight performance public. Laux says the principles are aimed "first and foremost" at public institutions (p. 2854) and notes trade-offs among them: rotating auditors protects against capture but undermines competence (Table 3, p. 2863). He warns that deployers "are incentivised to create the appearance of a human-in-the-loop" and that transparency "requires more than the mere provision of information": it must be "absorbable by laypeople citizens" (p. 2862).

## Relation to the argument
It is the best fit in my batch for ruling D8, "we just dont know yet... look at how courts and forex markets work". Laux draws on liberal-democratic institutions, courts and appeals among them, for who may check the checker, and his principles name the roles (rotating auditors, appeals bodies, publicity of oversight performance) that a convention would need. But he writes for public bodies and says so, so the move to platforms is the paper's to make, not his.

It bears on premise P10 (the owner of an interface cannot be the holder of the convention): his incentive challenge, with auditors captured by those they audit through repeated commissions, is a close analogue (p. 2859).

On the post's P19 and P15: the second-degree role, reviewing a record after the fact, is the role the post gives a person. Laux's remedies there are institutional (rotation, collective review, appeals), not an interface that makes the reading easy.

It touches D3: his transparency principle asks for information that laypeople can absorb, not just publish. That supports the claim that disclosure is a literacy matter, but he does not use the word as the author does.

Facet check: Facet G quotes only the abstract sentence about unreliable overseers. The body qualifies it (the empirical record is "subject to debate"), and the paper is a proposal that offers no new empirical test.

## Caution
Written about the draft AI Act in 2023, before the final text. It is a normative proposal; the principles are not tested, and the author expects uncertainty in how they affect competence. He applies them to public institutions first. Do not cite its article numbers against the 2024 Regulation without checking.
