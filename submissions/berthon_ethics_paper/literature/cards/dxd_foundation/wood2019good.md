## What it argues

The authors ask what job quality remote gig work has and why. Their answer places control at the end of the labour process rather than inside it. Direct monitoring (keystroke counts, screenshots): "these monitoring mechanisms were easily circumvented" (Findings, "Autonomy in the shadow of algorithmic management"); "A far more effective means of control was the 'algorithmic management' enabled by platform-based rating and reputation systems." Workers "with the best scores and the most experience tended to receive more work due to clients' preferences and the platforms' algorithmic ranking of workers within search results." Where Uber deactivates, "online labour platforms, instead, use algorithms to filter work away from those with low ratings." Hence "control operated at the end of the labour process rather than during it," which "afforded workers freedom to work however they wished as long as the end product was accurate or satisfactory to the client."

Survey figures: 72% could choose the order of tasks and 74% their methods; 62% said the job involved unforeseen problem solving and 57% complex problems; but 54% worked at very high speed, 60% to tight deadlines, 22% experienced pain, 80% said the pace was set "by direct demands from clients," 54% lost sleep, 54% said there was not enough work, 44% felt easily replaceable. Mean weekly income among those paid in USD was $165 (σ = $209, N = 610; SEA $181, N = 304; SSA $150, N = 307); among low-skilled workers the 10th–90th percentile income ratio was 1:19 (N = 328); the average respondent spent 16 hours a week looking for work, "39% of the total time spent on remote gig work." The explanation is power: an "open, market mediated employment relationship" with "a global oversupply of labour relative to demand," in which the resources that matter are skills and "platform reputation," which the authors call an emerging "symbolic power" (Conclusions).

## Relation to the argument

**P11 and ruling D9.** The paper describes the triad in operation: the client rates, the platform ranks and filters, and the worker's access to the next client depends on both. Algorithmic management is "an extension of 'customer management' strategies" in which customers are "agents in the management circuit" (Digital control, quoting Fuller and Smith 1991). That supports the abstract's picture of counterparts coordinating through a mediator that binds (ranking decides visibility) more than it supports "optimizes," which the paper does not address.

**What it adds to "gaming the mediator."** The gaming here is modest and on the other party: workers time their activity around screenshots and use second monitors; they do not report gaming the ranking. The pressure that drives intensity comes from clients and from other workers, not from reading the algorithm. This narrows the abstract's claim: on these platforms the person's strategic burden is competition and client management, with reputation as the currency.

**Where it cuts against the author.** The workers *welcomed* platform monitoring because it guaranteed payment; the mediator here protects one party from the other's non-payment. And worker–worker competition is a fourth relation the triad omits.

## Caution

Non-probability samples on two unnamed platforms; the authors say "Probability sampling is not possible through an online labour platform" and describe their purposive sample as "likely to be more representative of the population than samples recruited simply by posting an open task" (Methods); they do not claim generalisability. Fieldwork 2014–2015. Remote freelance work, not ride-hail or delivery, so the mediator ranks and filters rather than prices or matches. The PMC text has no page numbers; cite by section until the journal PDF is opened.
