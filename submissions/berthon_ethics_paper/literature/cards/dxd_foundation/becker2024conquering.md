## What it argues
Becker and Won ask whether Protestantism raised literacy in Korean in colonial Korea. Their outcome is the share of residents who could read and write Korean per 1,000, from the 1930 census (§3.1). The census counted a person as literate only if he or she could read and answer the questionnaire without help (n. 9, p. 6). The town mean is 209 per 1,000 (male 344, female 70; §3.2, p. 7).

The OLS result: each additional Methodist or Presbyterian church goes with 6.1 more literate residents per 1,000 (Table 1; §4, p. 7). The IV result, using person-years of foreign missionary residence as the instrument for church count: one more church raises literacy by 21.8 per 1,000 (SE 8.7) at an 80 km spread rule, 22.5 at 65 km, and 31.6 (SE 11.1) at 50 km, against a dependent-variable mean of 206.1 (Table 2, p. 11; first-stage F 35.9, 31.6, 21.8). The authors write the effect as "between 2.2% to 3.2%" (p. 10). The effect of public schools is "very small and statistically insignificant" (p. 11). The spatial RD, limited to the Comity border in Hwanghae Province, finds literacy "about 4.1%–5.3% higher" in Presbyterian than in Methodist territory (p. 13); the text gives 5.6–7.0 for males and 3.0–3.7 for females. They credit the Presbyterian "Nevius plan" of self-governing, self-supporting native churches that ran their own schools and night classes (§2.2–2.3, pp. 4–6).

For this project, the passages on script matter more than the headline. Before the missions, "the Joseon regime did not teach Hangul" (p. 6). Mission and church schools "taught the Korean alphabet, basic math, and the Bible" (p. 6). The authors explain their exclusion restriction thus: foreign missionaries could not teach in Korean, so "literacy was the result of Koreans teaching Koreans" (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument
The facet C finding stands and gets stronger: at population scale, Hangul's effect on literacy cannot be separated from schooling, missions and campaigns, and this paper is the best-identified case in the set. It contradicts the idea that a well-designed script spreads by itself. The script was available from 1443, and the 1930 census literacy gain is attributed to organised teaching by churches. This is the evidence behind the "no single move" line (`T_adjudication.md`; P6, the number of readers did not rise through the interface change alone).

It does not test any property of the script itself, and says nothing on word spacing. Its identification is a causal claim about church presence, not about Hangul. The IV exclusion restriction also leans on the authors' historical judgment that missionaries affected literacy only through native churches (p. 9), which a sceptic could contest.

Facet check: the facet's quotation "Protestantism played a crucial role in the accumulation of human capital" does not appear in the open PDF I read (abstract or text); the printed abstract says Protestant church density predicts 1930 literacy and that Presbyterians did better than Methodists. The facet probably quoted an earlier or database abstract. Quote the printed abstract instead. A second flag: Becker and Won put the start of government efforts to teach Hangul at "the Gabo Reform in 1886" (p. 6), while Ivanova (`ivanova2026process`, p. 138) dates the Kabo reforms to 1894 and the first joint hangul-and-hanja newspaper to 1886. One of them has the date wrong or they mean different things.

## Caution
It measures *Korean-language* literacy, not literacy in Hangul as opposed to mixed script, and the census definition is a self-report-plus-questionnaire test. It covers 1930 only. The percentage figures in the text are percentage points on a per-1,000 scale; check the table before quoting. The RD uses a border that mostly coincides with administrative lines, which the authors flag (p. 11). It says nothing about the three centuries before the missions.
