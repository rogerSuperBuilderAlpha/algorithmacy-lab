## What it argues
The paper's "key concern ... is multiple use": systems where "all users should not have identical authority" need the machine to enforce an authority structure (I.A.1). It defines protection narrowly, as control of executing programs' access to stored information, and warns that "a narrow concentration on protection mechanisms ... may lead to false confidence in the system as a whole" (I.A.1). The "intruder" "may be an otherwise legitimate user" (I.A.1).

It sorts systems into functional levels (unprotected, all-or-nothing, controlled sharing, user-programmed sharing, "putting strings on information"; I.A.2), then gives eight design principles because "no one knows how to build a system without flaws" (I.A.3–4): economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation ("Every access to every object must be checked for authority"), open design, separation of privilege, least privilege ("limits the damage that can result from an accident or error"), least common mechanism, and psychological acceptability ("the human interface be designed for ease of use, so that users routinely and automatically apply the protection mechanisms correctly"). Two more, work factor and compromise recording, "apply only imperfectly to computer systems"; of the second: "it is difficult to guarantee discovery once security is broken" because internal records "can be undone by a clever attacker" (I.A.3). The principles "serve best as warnings", not "absolute rules" (I.A.3).

Section I.B builds the guard-and-wall model, with a supervisor protecting its own implementation. Section II develops capabilities (tickets) and access control lists, and finds that revocation, propagation and review of access are hard in capability systems (II.B.3). Section II.C.4 treats who may change the lists. Hierarchical control "provides for exercise of authority but ... has no provision for preventing abuse of that authority", so the paper proposes a "prescript" that triggers logging, delay or a second principal's assent, to "ensure that some independent judgment moderates otherwise unfettered use of authority" (II.C.4). Section II.C.5 separates discretionary from nondiscretionary controls and introduces confinement of borrowed programs. Section II.E introduces protected subsystems. Section III.C (Concluding Remarks) says users of the first protection mechanisms "have found them relatively clumsy to program and not especially well matched to the users' image of the problem"; Section III.B.c says "User interfaces that more closely match the mental models people have of information protection are needed."

## Relation to the argument
It supports P11's lineage claim, as facet G argues: a small enforcing component outside the thing it governs, mediating every access, with privilege cut to the task. It also bears on P10 and the author's Part-1 reply (facet X, objection 6). Separation of privilege names a structure in which "no single accident, deception, or breach of trust is sufficient" because different organizations hold the keys; least common mechanism lets dissatisfied users "provide a substitute or not use it at all" (I.A.3). Both are designs where one party cannot withdraw a protection alone, though they are built inside one system, not among parties whose interests diverge. The prescript passage is the closest this source comes to P10's worry about unchecked authority at the top of a hierarchy.

It cuts against the author in two places. The reference-monitor idea moves work to an artefact, so it cannot mark the paper off from Nadella (ARGUMENT_MAP, "What the argument no longer claims"). And I.A.3(h) puts the human interface inside security engineering, which strengthens the "category error" objection less than facet X's reply (3) suggests: the interface in question is the one by which a person states protection policy over their own objects, not an interface to an interested third party's mediation. On ruling D3: open design lets a "skeptical user" check the mechanism, but its safety rests on keys, so disclosure there does not substitute for control.

## Caution
A 1975 tutorial on single-machine time-sharing systems. Its "users" are people sharing files; none is a customer of an adaptive platform. It supplies principles, not evidence on human performance, and its one empirical figure (more than 50 percent of about 300 self-chosen passwords guessable, I.B.4) is uncited there. Cite section numbers, not pages, and say the edition is the authors' web edition (a note on the references page, signed Norm Hardy, 1997, says the OCR of that material performs poorly); the journal text may differ in wording and layout.
