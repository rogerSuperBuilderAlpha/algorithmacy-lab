## What it argues
Torrens-Álvarez and Tuten ask why early-thirteenth-century Castilian legal documents mix Latin and Romance. Earlier scholars, they say, mostly blame "some kind of scribal incompetence" (p. 700). The authors argue the opposite. Nicholaus could write continuous Latin, continuous Romance and a conventional hybrid, and he chose among them by genre and by what each register signalled (pp. 701, 718–722). They tie the hybrid register to urbanisation, a lay property market, and the rise of autonomous lay scribes who name themselves (*Nicholaus Martini scripsit*) and who answer for the documents as individuals (pp. 709–717). They describe the later steps as the royal chancery adopting the vernacular: by their cited figure, 34 percent of Fernando III's chancery documents in the 1230s were in nearly or fully continuous Romance (n. 4, p. 699, citing Ostos Salcedo), and all of Alfonso X's internal chancery documents (n. 4).

Their second target is the assumption that these documents had to be easy to understand. The abstract says they "argue against an overemphasis on ease of comprehension as a factor shaping the design and reception of written texts and oral performances of them" (p. 698). In section 6 they argue that stressing a "need" for comprehensibility "may reflect a modern, utilitarian conception of discourse" (p. 731). Latin in the Mass and in formulae signalled institutional authority whether or not listeners understood it. The public reading aloud of a deed "had authorization and authentication as primary goals, not comprehension of documentary content" (p. 732). The conclusion follows: the texts "were not always fully comprehensible to all readers and listeners" (p. 735), yet they carried authority, and that is part of why Romance later acquired authority too.

## Relation to the argument
The paper is not about word separation, and "continuous Romance" in it means a text wholly in one register, not unspaced script. Nothing in the text bears on P1–P4 directly. The facet B note was right that it is a lead only; the full text confirms that and also shows where the lead goes.

Two uses are open, both analogical. First, it is a published case of historians contesting an ease-of-reading explanation of a change in how texts look and are read, and replacing it with social function. That is the same shape as the Saenger/Vatri/Heilmann dispute over cost, so it can sit beside ruling D2 as a reminder that "the new form was easier to read" needs its own evidence. Second, it speaks to ruling D8 (who holds the convention). The scribes form a "community of practice" (p. 714); the convention later gains the backing of the town council and then the crown, with the named scribe as "guarantor" (p. 717). A convention held by working practitioners and then underwritten by public authority is a possible comparison for the "bends towards" question D8 leaves open. The scribes served the parties to a transaction, not a platform's owner, so it does not bear on the P10 contrast.

## Caution
Do not cite it for anything about word separation, silent reading or the Insular scribes. Its evidence is one scribe in one city over 17 years, and its claim about comprehension rests on analogy to the Mass and on the authors' reading of the hybrid forms, not on any measure of what listeners understood. The page for the 34 percent figure is a note that cites another author.
