# Kohsom, C., & Gobet, F. (1997). Adding spaces to Thai and English: Effects on reading. *Proceedings of the 19th Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society* (pp. 388–393). Erlbaum.

**Identifier:** Brunel repository copy, https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/2122/1/Cogsci%2797-thai.pdf · **Read depth:** full_text (author's copy headed "To appear in the Proceedings"; six pages, no printed page numbers, so locators are section names) · **Source-tier:** proceedings paper · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** within-subject 2 (language: Thai, English) x 2 (spacing) x 2 (coherence) reading-aloud experiment, 8 native Thai speakers at the University of Pittsburgh (5 women, 3 men; mean age 26; mean TOEFL 566); 4 paragraphs of about 10 lines per cell; outcome is time per paragraph and errors · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and text) · **Facets:** D, E · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Kohsom and Gobet ask what happens when spaces are added to a language written without them. They set two predictions against each other. Spaces add information, so readers should speed up and err less; or adult Thai readers have tuned their reading to unspaced text, so spaces should slow them (Introduction). They test Thai readers on Thai and on English, with spaces present or removed and with coherent or word-scrambled text.

Method (Methods, Material): Thai spaced text has one space added after each word; unspaced English has every space removed. Incoherent paragraphs replace words at random with words of the same size. Participants read aloud, pressing the space key at the end of each paragraph, and were recorded.

Results (Results; analyses by language, df = 1, 7):
- Thai: incoherent text was slower, F = 38.68, p < .001. Spacing sped reading, but only "marginally significant" (F = 4.21, p < .08). Six of eight participants were faster with spaces on coherent text, seven of eight on incoherent text. No coherence-by-spacing interaction.
- English: spaced text was faster, F = 50.75, p < .001; coherent text was faster, F = 14.65, p < .01; no interaction.
- Size of the spacing effect: about 33.72 s per paragraph in English against 1.37 s in Thai (t = 6.76, p < .001). Thai/coherent/unspaced took 0.47 s per word, 46.2 s per paragraph; English/coherent/spaced 0.66 s per word.
- Errors (Table 1): Thai total errors per paragraph were 1.4 (coherent, spaced) against 1.7 (coherent, unspaced), and 2.7 against 3.4 for incoherent text. English totals were 2.4 against 5.6 and 3.2 against 8.0. In Thai only "word added" errors showed a reliable spacing effect (F = 6.44, p < .05).
- Retrospective reports: the Thai readers "thought that spaces were making reading harder" (Results, Reading Time), though they read faster.
- No transfer: the unspaced English cost for the Thai speakers (55% more time on coherent text) was higher than the 30% to 48% Kohsom and Gobet cite for English natives from Rayner and Pollatsek (1996) (Discussion).

The authors conclude that spacing "seems then to be a cue that is universal across languages" (Discussion), and they raise the question whether Thai should follow Latin, which they date, citing Boorstin (1983), to "late in the eighth century" (Introduction). Their closing line calls the decision "a trade-off between the cognitive and educational gain of adding spaces and its cost—cultural, social, and economic."

## Relation to the argument
This is the source behind P4 ("readers fluent in unspaced scripts gain nothing from added spaces"). It cuts against the wording of P4. Adult Thai readers did gain, in time (marginally) and in errors, even though they reported the opposite preference. Facet and ARGUMENT_MAP mark Kohsom `[ok]`; the card supports the hedge "on mixed evidence" and not "gain nothing".

Facet check: I find nothing in the paper that matches "gain nothing". The paper's own claim is a small gain for natives of an unspaced script and a large one for the second-language text. The large effect belongs to the English text, where readers have no practice without spaces, which fits P3 and the learners clause of P4.

It bears on D2 (the debate over whether unspaced script burdened trained readers): the paper sides with those who say spaces help even trained readers, and it names the opponent, Epelboim and colleagues (1994), who argued words rather than spaces guide the eye. Kohsom and Gobet reject that reading and add that the missing coherence-by-spacing interaction, which Booth, Epelboim and Steinman (1995) used to put meaning ahead of spaces, does not replicate. It also bears on D6 and D10: the authors treat spacing as one cue among language-specific ones and frame its adoption as a cost-benefit choice, close to the paper's "guide, not mechanism" stance.

## Caution
Eight participants, one Thai passage and one English passage, reading aloud rather than silently, no eye tracking, Macintosh screen with 18-point fonts. The Thai spacing effect did not reach p < .05. Language is not randomised, so the Thai-versus-English contrast is confounded, as the authors say. Spaced Thai text had extra spaces at sentence ends in one condition and not in another, handled by removing them in the unspaced condition. The "eighth century" date for Latin spacing comes from Boorstin, a popular history, and should not be cited from this paper; use Saenger.
