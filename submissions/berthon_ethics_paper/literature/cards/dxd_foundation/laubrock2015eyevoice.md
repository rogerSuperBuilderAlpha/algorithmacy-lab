## What it argues

The eye-voice span (EVS) is the distance by which the eye leads the voice in reading aloud. Measured on-line, "the distribution of the EVS in milliseconds from the beginning of the first fixation on a word to the onset of its pronunciation was nearly symmetric, with a mean of 561 ms and a standard deviation of 230 ms"; from the end of the last fixation on a word it was 254 ms (Temporal EVS). In letters, the eye was 16.2 letters ahead at fixation onset and "still 9.7 letters ahead of the voice" at fixation offset (Spatial EVS). Readers regulate this distance: a large EVS at fixation onset predicts longer fixations and a large EVS at offset predicts regressions and refixations, and "effects of the EVS were among the strongest effects observed in the LMM analyses" (Discussion). The authors' account is working memory: "the eyes need to wait for the voice because the size of the working memory buffer is limited" (Discussion); the target is "a constant EVS at fixation offset of about 10 letters". Oral against silent reading (Table 1): mean fixation 253 against 209 ms, gaze duration 334 against 247 ms, saccade length 5.9 against 7.0 letters, skipping .14 against .21, regressions .06 against .10; comprehension 97.7% and 97.4%. The canonical word-frequency, length and predictability effects, and the effects of neighbouring words, "looked rather similar for oral and silent reading" (Evidence for Differences). Buswell's 1920 estimate of the EVS, about 15 letters, is confirmed; the off-line "lights-off" method (6–10 words) is "widely off-track" (Discussion).

The introduction states as background: "Silent reading is a fairly recent cultural invention, at least in the West, where it was introduced only around the 8th century, following the introduction of word spaces" (Introduction, ¶2). The citation attached to that sentence is Manguel (1996), *A History of Reading*, not Saenger; the PMC XML carries it.

## Relation to the argument

The abstract has the unspaced reader "sound out the line just to hear where each word ended". This paper measures what the voice does when a skilled reader reads a spaced page aloud, and the order of events is the reverse: the eye finds and identifies words two to three words (about ten to sixteen letters) before the voice reaches them, and the voice is the slower follower that the eyes wait for.

- **Cuts against** the voice as the organ of boundary detection in any fluent reader, spaced or not: identification precedes articulation by roughly half a second. The claim can survive only for a reader whose span collapses to the word in hand, which is the beginner (the authors cite 8.4 letters for dyslexic against 13.8 for normal readers, Introduction).
- **Supports** the abstract's cost premise indirectly: oral reading is slower on every measure in Table 1, and the authors locate the cost in buffering for articulation, not in segmentation.
- **Reception datum.** A 2015 reading-science paper restates the spacing-made-silent-reading story as settled fact with an "8th century" date, on the authority of Manguel's popular history (1996), not of Saenger. That is the kind of circulation the project's ruling D2 asks the author to review rather than repeat; the sentence is an aside, with no evidence of its own.
- For the paper's human-factors frame, the EVS is a worked example of "ecological feedback immediacy" (Shneiderman; Kahneman & Klein, in the abstract's last sentence): the voice gives the eye a running signal of how far ahead it may go.

## Caution

Skilled adolescent and young adult readers of spaced German text; the silent group is a different sample; no unspaced condition, no learners, no Latin. The historical sentence is not a finding and should not be cited as one. The EVS figures are means over a corpus with large within-reader variance (SD 230 ms, 5.2 letters).
