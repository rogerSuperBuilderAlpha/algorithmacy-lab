# Casner, S. M., Geven, R. W., Recker, M. P., & Schooler, J. W. (2014). The retention of manual flying skills in the automated cockpit. *Human Factors, 56*(8), 1506–1516. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628

## What it argues
Casner and colleagues test whether long use of cockpit automation erodes the skills of flying by hand. Following Mengelkoch et al. (1971), they separate "hand-eye" skills (instrument scanning and control) from cognitive skills (keeping track of position, deciding the next navigational step, recognising instrument failures) (pp. 1506–1507).

Hand-eye skills held up. Pilots' "instrument scanning and manual control skills to be mostly intact, even when pilots reported that they were infrequently practiced" (abstract, p. 1506); the one significant effect was more speed deviations in the missed approach under flight-director manual control (χ² = 9.94, p < .01, OR = 9.0; p. 1510). Cognitive skills did not. Flying without the FMC, 6 of 16 pilots failed to fly the published missed-approach heading, 4 failed to set the final approach course, and "Only 1 pilot was able to complete the arrival, approach, and missed approach without committing any of the errors described" (p. 1512, Table 5). In the failure events, 75% followed a faulty altimeter past a 300-foot deviation, and in the failed pitot-static system event "all but 1 pilot followed the erroneous airspeed indicator to the point at which the stall warning system was triggered"; at least half had trouble resolving it (pp. 1513–1514).

Mind-wandering tracked these failures. Task-unrelated thought was 20.0% under autoflight, 11.7% under flight-director manual control and 6.9% under raw-data flying (F(2) = 4.09, p < .05; p. 1510). Navigation errors correlated with task-unrelated thought, r(14) = .56, p < .05, about 30% of the variance (p. 1512); stick-shaker counts in the airspeed failure correlated with it, r(14) = .76, p < .001 (p. 1514). The authors conclude that cognitive skill retention "may depend on the degree to which pilots remain actively engaged in supervising the automation" (abstract) and that "Pilots who kept their minds on task more performed better" (p. 1514). They recommend practice and "more active automation monitoring practices," noting evidence of "the difficulty in maintaining one's thoughts focused on the activities of an automated system that seldom fails" (p. 1515).

## Relation to the argument
**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The paper sits with facet I's finding that pilots read the situation. The skills that decayed are the ones of following the flight, keeping track of position and cross-checking instruments, which is reading and monitoring. The skills that survived are the act-and-adjust loop of hand-flying, which matches the author's picture of acting and gauging "in the moment." So the paper supports D7 for the control loop and contradicts "pilots don't spend time reading the situation" for the rest: the pilots who stayed engaged in following the flight kept their skills.

**Premise P13 (Nadella).** The post leaves a person to read records and halt the model. This paper is evidence that people asked to supervise an automated system that "seldom fails" drift off task, and that the drift predicts failure when something goes wrong (pp. 1512, 1514–1515). The argument map says the critique of the post should not rest on "people review badly"; this paper is aviation evidence that bears on that question without settling it for software agents.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 33 cites the paper, with Metzger and Parasuraman (2001), for "Prolonged reliance on automated aids shifts the human operator from active control toward passive monitoring, with costs to vigilance and to manual skill." Partly supported. The shift toward supervision is the paper's premise (p. 1507), and "costs to vigilance" fits the mind-wandering results (pp. 1510, 1512, 1514). "Costs to... manual skill" needs qualifying: the paper found hand-eye manual control "mostly intact" and found the costs in the cognitive skills of manual flight (navigation, failure recognition). A faithful wording would be "with costs to vigilance and to the cognitive skills of flying by hand." Lines 771–772 list the source in Claude's notes, which are not part of the paper.

## Caution
Sixteen pilots, one aircraft type, one simulator session each; correlations with N = 16 (df = 14). Pilots knew they were in a study; the confederate copilot withheld the help a real copilot gives, which may inflate errors. Skills were not measured before automation use, so "retention" is inferred from self-reported practice, not tracked over time. The authors warn that a change in initial training "may invalidate the findings" on hand-eye skills (p. 1515).
