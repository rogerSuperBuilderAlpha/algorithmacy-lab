## What it argues
Chapter XXV takes up how unmetrical prose can be made to resemble a beautiful poem or lyric (p. 251). Dionysius argues from Demosthenes, claiming that his speeches show close attention to rhythm and the order of words. He imagines a critic who says that so great a man would not have worked "in metres and rhythms after the fashion of clay-modellers... shifting the words to and fro" and fretting over the cases of nouns and moods of verbs (p. 265), and answers that Isocrates spent ten years on his *Panegyric* "according to the lowest recorded estimate" and Plato kept combing his dialogues at eighty (p. 265). A story of Plato's tablet with the opening words of the *Republic* in different orders closes the point (pp. 265–267).

At the end of the chapter he replies to the sceptic with "a fact familiar to all of us": learning to read. Roberts's translation (p. 269): "When we are taught to read, first we learn off the names of the letters, then their forms and their values, then in due course syllables and their modifications, and finally words and their properties ... After acquiring the knowledge of these things, we begin to write and read, syllable by syllable and slowly at first." Once "the lapse of a considerable time" has fixed the forms of words in the mind, he adds, we deal with them "without the least difficulty," and any book put in our hands we read "without stumbling." The same holds for the trained writer's feel for word order and clause sounds, and for lyre and flute players who run over a new tune "with little trouble" after "much toiling and moiling" (p. 269).

Roberts's note (p. 269) quotes Wilkins on whether the method begins with letter names or letter powers; Wilkins rejects the powers-first reading as "hardly consistent with his language" and cites Athenaeus on a school chant of syllables and an Attic terracotta school plate that "contains a number of syllables."

## Relation to the argument
The passage is a witness to how Greek children were taught to read: letters, then forms and values, then syllables, then words, slowly at first, with fluency after "a considerable time." It does not mention word division or any property of the page. So it is neither evidence for nor against the claim that separating words lowered the cost of learning to read (rulings D1, D2; premise P6). What it gives the paper is the shape of the cost an ancient reader paid and what it bought: the training is long and the result is fluent reading of whatever book is handed over, which fits the view of facet B's critics that trained readers handled unspaced text easily (P4, P1). It also places the learning of reading inside an argument about craft: Dionysius uses reading as the example of a skill that becomes effortless through practice, a human-factors point (practice converts effort into habit) the paper can set beside Quintilian (`quintilian1920institutio`, 1.1.32–33).
