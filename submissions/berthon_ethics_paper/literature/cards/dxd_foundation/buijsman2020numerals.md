# Buijsman, S. (2020). How numerals support new cognitive capacities. *Synthese, 197*, 3779–3796.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11229-018-01989-7 · **Read depth:** full_text (published open-access PDF; journal pagination; reference list skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (philosophy of cognitive science, special issue MATHCOGENCUL) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none of its own; a philosophical argument built on a review of other authors' experiments · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (a reader or calculator and a numeral system) · **Facets:** F · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Buijsman tests Menary's (2015) argument that arithmetic proves cognition is extended into the environment (the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition, HEC) and not merely embedded in it (HEMC). He splits Menary's Discrete Number System in two. Small-number concepts rest on innate systems and on telling one object from more than one; numerals only prompt their learning and need not enter the explanation (§2, pp. 3781–3784). So small numbers give no support for externalism.

Multi-digit numerals differ. The brain processes them in parts: "the evidence now strongly favours the hypothesis that numerals are processed in a decomposed fashion" (§3, pp. 3785–3786). In the unit-decade compatibility effect, people compare 42 and 57 faster than 47 and 62, because the digits agree with the whole only in the first pair (p. 3786). Carries and borrows add time and errors. Buijsman adds that multiplication algorithms which split the problem by digit "fit in perfectly" with this processing, which may be why those algorithms won out (p. 3787).

Numeral systems differ in cost. Krajcsi and Szabó (2012), as reported, taught participants a new sign-value system and a new place-value system, and "performance on simple addition and comparison tasks is better for the sign-value system," so sign-value systems "are easier to learn and work with for those kinds of tasks" (pp. 3787–3788). Schlimm and Neth (2008) find that Roman numerals need fewer remembered addition facts but "more basic perceptual-motor interactions" than Hindu-Arabic numerals (p. 3788). Little is known about processing in other systems (p. 3788).

His verdict: the large-number case refutes internalism, but "the case study does not decide between these two alternatives" (§4, p. 3790), because causal explanations fit the facts as well as constitutive ones. People may often use public symbols because it is easier, but that does not show they cannot do without them once the capacity is acquired (p. 3793).

## Relation to the argument

The use is narrow. A philosopher who read the experiments restates that the cost of a notation depends on task and user: sign-value systems are easier to learn for simple addition and comparison, and Roman numerals trade memory for perceptual-motor work (p. 3788). That bears on facet F's lesson 1 ("the cost saving is task- and user-specific") and on premise P6 (the move lowered a cost, most for readers still learning). It also fits ruling D6: the numeral story is a bundle of notation, algorithm and layout.

It does not bear on word separation. It does not say who Krajcsi and Szabó's participants were or how long they practised, so it cannot back a claim about novices against experts.

Facet check: facet F wrote "The cost saving of place value shows up with practice and with column layout." Buijsman's text contains neither point. The practice claim has no support here, and the column point comes from Menary's multiplication layouts (pp. 3786–3787) without a cost figure. The facet's quotation of Krajcsi and Szabó is accurate; the sentence runs from the foot of p. 3787 to the top of p. 3788.

## Caution

A philosophy paper about extended cognition, not notation design. The experiments are second-hand: participants, N and training in Krajcsi and Szabó are not given, and the sign-value result holds for "those kinds of tasks" only. Buijsman restricts his case to Western, mostly Hindu-Arabic numerals. Not a source for the history of numerals, for adoption, or for place value lowering the cost of arithmetic.
