## What it argues

From the snippets and the subject headings alone: the book "describes the growth of a literate mentality", tracing "the halting acceptance of literate" ways in England, and argues that "literate habits and assumptions, comprising a literate mentality, had to take root in diverse social groups". The growth of records is tied to government: the country "was dominated by a centralizing royal bureaucracy", and "the formation of the royal bureaucracy was thus a complex process of experiment and" (snippet ends). The closing chapter, "Practical Literacy", opens: "The theme of this book has been the growth of literacy for practical purposes"; a snippet carrying the running head "Practical Literacy 329" reads "extended to nearly every sphere of government, … both central and local".

On printing and mass literacy, the snippets show Clanchy placing the medieval change before print: "Certainly the printing press produced many more copies of texts. Whether" (snippet ends); he describes a seal that "does exactly the same as the printing press: it reliably copies and multiplies information" by mechanical means. One further snippet names movable type only in a typology of reproduction ("one single sign (movable type)"), not as a cause of literacy. On mass literacy he writes: "Teaching in vernaculars was the foundation of modern European mass literacy, not the humanists' curriculum of dead languages", and, of someone in his period whose identity the snippet cuts off, that they "would not have understood the modern demand for mass literacy".

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D1, premise P6, and the historiographical boundary in §3.** Clanchy is the right authority for the claim that literate practice in England grew with government records and administrative centralisation, well before print. That supports the draft's list of "legal documentation, administrative centralization". It also cuts against reading print (Eisenstein) as the start: Clanchy's book is the standard argument that a literate mentality formed in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. It is not a history of mass literacy; the period he studies had a small literate elite, and his remark on vernacular teaching places the foundation of mass literacy later.
