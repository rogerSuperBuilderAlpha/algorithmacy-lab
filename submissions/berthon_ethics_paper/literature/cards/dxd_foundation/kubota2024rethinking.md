## What it argues
Kubota, Grill-Spector and Nordt review how word-selective cortex arises in ventral temporal cortex (VTC) and what it displaces. They set out four accounts (section "Theories on the development of word-selective regions"): classical cultural recycling (Dehaene and Cohen), where reading takes over cortex tuned to something else, first taken to be faces; graded hemispheric specialization (Behrmann and Plaut), where words and faces compete and faces end up right-lateralized; a theory of emergence, where words appear in unspecified cortex without displacing anything; and a revised recycling theory (Dehaene-Lambertz et al. 2018), where competition need not destroy prior representations.

Their argument rests on longitudinal evidence, since a cross-sectional comparison cannot tell these accounts apart (section "Theories", last paragraph):
- Right lateralization of face responses appears in infants aged 4 to 6 months, before any reading instruction (section "The sequence of lateralization over development", citing de Heering and Rossion 2015). In a 10-child longitudinal study the face-selective volume was larger in the right hemisphere before and after instruction, and a longitudinal study from age 5 to 17 found no change in lateralization of word- or face-selective cortex (same section). The authors flag that "the study measuring children before and after reading instruction consisted of a small sample (10 participants)."
- Lateralization of word and face regions was not related over development (section on "relationship between ... lateralization", citing Nordt et al. 2021, Fig. 3E).
- Cortex that became word-selective was earlier "weakly specified" for bodies and tools in first-graders (Dehaene-Lambertz et al. 2018) and, in children aged 5 to 12, selective for limbs and not faces (Nordt et al. 2021) (section "Looking back in time").
- Hypothesis (section "Re-thinking cortical recycling"): cortex is recycled according to the visual diet and behavioural relevance of a category at each age. Infants look at faces, then at hands, and adults' first fixations in a scene go to text.

Conclusion (Concluding remarks): competition between words and faces is called "into question", and the data fit recycling of limb- or tool-selective cortex into word-selective cortex.

## Caution
A review, not an experiment; its core claims rest on a handful of longitudinal studies with small samples. It covers children learning to read alphabetic or similar scripts in literate societies, and says nothing about historical shifts in script. The authors say further work should compare more stimulus categories before settling between the accounts, and they propose the visual-diet hypothesis as untested. This is the PMC author manuscript, not the typeset article.
