## What it argues

Tunbridge holds that Insular scribes began to separate the words of Latin text because they were reading and copying Latin as a foreign language, and that the separation shows a linguistic analysis learned from the grammarians. The Long Abstract states the case: "Thus Irish scribes when copying Latin began to abandon scriptio continua in favour of a layout which clarified the syntax and morphology of the language and hence met the needs of the non-native Latin speaker" (Long Abstract p. 4). Chapter 2 supplies the motive. Old Irish scribes wrote their own language in syntactic word-groups (pp. 89-92), so an Irish student met Latin's narrower word boundaries as a difficulty (pp. 93-97), and the Continental exemplars "were in a sense part of the problem" because their unbroken lines "made no concessions to foreigners" (p. 97). Chapter 3 supplies the method: the eight parts of speech taught by the grammarians became "the principle underlying the separation of words" (Long Abstract p. 4), and gaps in the teaching on prepositions explain the typical Insular "errors" (pp. 124-150).

Chapter 4 describes the practice. Scribes marked words by space, points, tags on final letters and variant letter forms (pp. 165-175). Tunbridge counts groupings in three manuscripts. In one column of the St Paul Pompeius, prepositions occur ten times in groups and separately only at a line end, and *et* is joined all six times (p. 183). In Hatton 93 (early ninth century), 38 of 42 *et* are joined, and of 65 chances to separate a preposition 49 are clearly attached, 5 slightly separated and 11 clearly separated (pp. 185-186). Her table shows prepositions and *et* joined in all three manuscripts, a pattern "common to most Insular scribes from the late seventh through to the ninth centuries" (p. 190). She also holds that "there is no question of their consciously following a rigid set of 'rules'" (p. 190). Fully consistent separation into parts of speech does not appear before the twelfth century (p. 125).

Chapter 5 traces the early stages. All the earliest Irish manuscripts "already contain some kind of separation" (p. 193). Her table (pp. 214-215) runs from erratic wax tablets and syntactic-unit spacing (Ussher I, Milan Orosius) through word-based separation (St Gall Isidore, second quarter of the 7th century) to the Bangor Antiphonary (c. 680-691) and the Schaffhausen Adomnan (before 713). She warns against reading this as a straight line (pp. 214-216), and notes that the earliest full word separation turns up "not in formal liturgical books but in the more informally written copy of the *Etymologiae*" (p. 216). The Anglo-Saxons took the habit from the Irish, and Wearmouth-Jarrow scribes later compressed the spacing under scriptorium discipline (pp. 217-227). Scribes copy as readers, so the practice "fundamentally represents a kind of linguistic analysis" (pp. 228-229). Chapter 6 links separation to abbreviation: both show that Latin was seen "primarily as a written or 'visible' language" (p. 289, quoting Parkes), and both mean readers took in "word by word" rather than "letter by letter" (p. 297).

## Relation to the argument

Facet check: the facet (A6) read only the abstract and called the thesis "independent of Saenger" and hedged ("may have"). The full text confirms a different method (grammar teaching and manuscript counts). It is stronger than the abstract on motive: "must be seen as a practical outcome" of the grammarians' study (p. 98) and "primarily a linguistic phenomenon" (Long Abstract p. 7). It is not independent of Parkes, who supervised it (Acknowledgements), and its dating of the earliest manuscripts rests on Brown and Lowe (p. 194, n. 3), so it adds no independent test of Saenger's chronology (premise P2).

- **P1 and P2.** It supports the foreign-learner motive and the late seventh-century Irish date (Bangor, Adomnan) from the manuscripts themselves. It says nothing about whether *trained* readers of continuous script were burdened; its readers are learners.
- **P5 (the scribe carries the boundary work).** Supported, and complicated. The scribe did the analysis, but with errors and with his own grouping habits. Hatton 93 renders *catholicon latine* as "catholi conlatine" and shows that a new Continental text composed no later than 822 could still circulate in continuous script (pp. 191-192). Inference mine: a reader who receives pre-analysed text also receives the analyst's mistakes.
- **P6 and ruling D1/D2.** The "no training needed" line is not supported here. The thesis concerns what scribes had to know (grammar), and it reports no change in how many people read.
- **D6 (a bundle, but is one move the critical one?).** Tunbridge treats separation with abbreviation, punctuation and page layout (pp. 34-38). She argues, conditionally on Parkes, that heavy abbreviation "was dependent on a degree of word separation" (p. 295), but allows that abbreviation was possible in continuous script (p. 294). That is partial support for separation as an enabling move.
- **P10 and D8 (who holds the convention).** The grammarians "on the whole ignored the specific needs of scribes as copyists" (p. 150) and were silent on separation (p. 156). The convention lived in scribal practice. A scriptorium could suppress it locally: Wearmouth-Jarrow discipline "sought to suppress" ample spacing (p. 225), and Hatton 48 avoids it to imitate Continental models (p. 295, n. 155). The scribes also shared their readers' position as learners of Latin (p. 234). The sources do not show a single party able to withdraw the convention for everyone.
- **Silent reading.** She cites Parkes against Saenger on the date of silent reading (p. 235, n.), which fits the dropped claim that spacing created silent reading.

## Caution

A thesis, unrefereed and unpublished; I did not check whether it later appeared as articles. The motive rests on inference from grammar teaching and the pattern of groupings, not on any record of why a scribe spaced a word. The dates come from Lowe and Brown. The counts are small samples (one column, a few folios) and she says so (p. 184). Nothing measures reading cost, speed or training time. Her speed claim (p. 298) leans on Saenger. The Oxford scan's OCR is rough in places. I read in full: the abstracts, Preface, Chapter 1 pp. 34-41, Chapter 2 pp. 89-98, Chapter 3 pp. 124-126 and 148-150, Chapter 4 (all), Chapter 5 (all), Chapter 6 pp. 237-238 and 279-299, the Conclusion. I skimmed or did not read: Chapter 1 pp. 2-33 (Patrick's alphabets, *Ad Cuimnanum*), Chapter 2 pp. 43-88 (the *Auraicept*), Chapter 3 pp. 99-123 and 126-147 (the grammarians), Chapter 6 pp. 239-278 (manuscript-by-manuscript abbreviation studies), the Appendix (pp. 301-317, first page only), the plates and manuscript lists. The bibliography pp. 333-366 was not read through; references come from footnotes.
