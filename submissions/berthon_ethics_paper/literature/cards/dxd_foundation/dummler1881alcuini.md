## What it argues

The poem addresses those who sit and copy sacred law and the sayings of the Fathers ("Hic sedeant sacrae scribentes famina legis", l. 1). They are not to add their own trifles (ll. 3–4), and are to seek corrected exemplars: "Correctosque sibi quaerant studiose libellos" (l. 5). Lines 7–10 give the instruction the draft uses: "Per cola distinguant proprios et commata sensus, / Et punctos ponant ordine quosque suo, / Ne vel falsa legat, taceat vel forte repente / Ante pios fratres lector in ecclesia." The scribes are to mark the sense units by cola and commata and to place the points in order, so that the lector in church before the brethren neither reads wrongly nor falls suddenly silent. The poem goes on to the worth of the work: "Fodere quam vites melius est scribere libros" (l. 13), and closes (ll. 15–16) on the master who reads the Fathers.

The text has a thin basis. Dümmler prints it from Duchesne's 1617 edition of Alcuin ("Q", no. CXXVI), made from a St-Bertin manuscript long lost, and from Brouwer's *Antiquitates Fuldenses* (p. 46), "ubi desunt v. 7–10, 13–16" (p. 320, apparatus). The one other witness he cites therefore lacks the lines on cola, commata and points.

The instruction is about punctuation (cola, commata, points) for reading aloud. It does not mention spaces between words.

## Relation to the argument

**P10, ruling D8, and D6 (a complex of conventions).** The poem, which names no speaker and is Alcuin's by the edition's attribution, tells scribes how to lay out text for a reader, and names the harm (the lector errs or stalls). That supports the draft's picture of an authority standing behind the page, and it shows punctuation for the reader as part of what that authority cared about. The reader here reads aloud, in church; the poem gives no support to any link between the reform and silent reading.

## Caution

Read in the 1881 MGH OCR only. The poem's opening "Hic sedeant" marks it as a place inscription, and Dümmler prints it in a group whose running head reads "Inscriptiones in quodam monasterio forte Turonensi" (p. 321; index "Turonensis (?) 319–23"), so his own placement at Tours is hedged ("forte", "?"). Dümmler's preface says Alcuin already held Ferrières, Troyes and Flavigny and became abbot of St-Martin at Tours in 796; the poem is undated. Alcuin wrote as an abbot and teacher, not as a ruler, so he fits the draft's "church authorities" as the head of houses, not as a bishop.
