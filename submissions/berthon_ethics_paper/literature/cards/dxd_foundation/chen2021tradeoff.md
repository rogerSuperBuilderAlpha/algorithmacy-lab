# Chen, M., Wang, Y., Zhao, B., Li, X., & Bai, X. (2021). The trade-off between format familiarity and word-segmentation facilitation in Chinese reading. *Frontiers in Psychology, 12*, Article 602931.

**Identifier:** doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.602931 · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access publisher PDF, 16 pages; I read all the text, tables and discussion; the reference list was partly garbled in extraction, so I could not check it entry by entry) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** two eye-tracking experiments, 40 Chinese undergraduates each (different people; mean ages about 20.5–20.8), 60 sentences of 15–17 characters in four spacing formats, read right to left; Experiment 2 follows 10 days of 30-minute right-to-left reading training · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Bai et al. (2008) found that native Chinese readers gain nothing from spaces between words. A later explanation said the gain exists but is cancelled by the unfamiliar look of spaced text. Chen and colleagues test that "trade-off" by making the unspaced format unfamiliar too (p. 1). Experiment 1: 40 students read Chinese from right to left, a direction they had not practised, in four formats (unspaced, inter-character spaced, inter-word spaced, non-word spaced). Inter-word spacing gave the shortest total fixation time (3,993 ms against 4,385 ms unspaced) and the fastest reading speed (4.60 against 4.31 characters per second; Table 2A, p. 4; Table 3, p. 4; text, pp. 4–5). Experiment 2: another 40 students first trained 10 days on right-to-left essays, then read the same sentences. Total fixation time was 3,043 ms inter-word against 3,116 ms unspaced, and speed was 5.65 against 5.55 characters per second, with no significant difference between those two formats (Table 4A, p. 7; text, pp. 6–8). Mean fixation duration still favoured both spaced formats over unspaced (p. 7). The authors conclude "there is indeed a trade-off between format familiarity and the facilitation of word segmentation" (abstract, p. 1) and read the shrinking benefit as familiarity offsetting segmentation help.

They state a weakness themselves: a "floor effect" could explain Experiment 2, since trained readers may already read as fast as possible (p. 8). They reply that this cannot explain the Experiment 1 benefit in the same population (p. 8), and they do not test it. They also say training used only unspaced reversed text, so the spaced format's own familiarity was not trained (p. 11–12).

## Relation to the argument
Direct support for the mechanism behind P4: the benefit of spacing shrinks as the reader's practice with the format grows, and a script that readers know well gains little from added spaces. Read with Heilmann (`heilmann2020reading`), it gives an experimental analogue of "habit decides, so do not project our difficulty onto ancient readers": readers of an unfamiliar format profit from word segmentation, and ten days of practice cut the profit. It fits the project's cost-for-novices reading (P6).

The limits are large. The manipulation is reading direction, not spacing history, and the "unfamiliar format" is a Chinese text mirrored right to left, which has little to do with a Latin-script reader meeting scriptio continua. The two experiments use different participants and no untrained control group, so the contrast between them is between samples, not within persons. The authors' wording "changed smaller" means a non-significant difference in two measures, not a measured size change.

Facet check: facet D (row "Practice") reads the abstract correctly, and I confirm the Experiment 2 null on total time and speed. The facet notes a corrigendum; the open copy I read carries none, so I cannot confirm that point.

## Caution
A single lab, university students, one script, mirrored text. It cannot be cited for the size of any spacing effect on native readers in ordinary text, or for the claim that practice removes the benefit in normal reading. Its Table 3 reports η² of 0.77 and 0.68 for saccade length where the text gives 0.83 and 0.72; treat the exact statistics as unverified.
