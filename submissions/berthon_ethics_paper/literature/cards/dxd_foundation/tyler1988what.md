# Tyler, T. R. (1988). What is procedural justice?: Criteria used by citizens to assess the fairness of legal procedures. *Law & Society Review, 22*(1), 103–135.

**Identifier:** doi:10.2307/3053563 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher PDF from Cambridge Core, journal pagination; the even-numbered pages 118–126 lost their running heads in the scan, so locators for pp. 118–127 are given as ranges; reference list skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** telephone survey of Chicago residents, spring 1984; 1,575 interviewed (63% of those contacted), 733 reported personal contact with the police or courts in the past year, 652 with usable experiences were analysed; cross-sectional, correlational · **Parties modeled:** person against a legal authority (police or court) acting as decider; disputes between two parties are the minority (18%) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Tyler first replicates the finding that how people judge the fairness of a procedure shapes their satisfaction and their view of the authorities, apart from the outcome they got (pp. 103–105, 119–120). He then asks what makes a procedure seem fair. He combines two lists: Thibaut and Walker's process and decision control, and Leventhal's six criteria (consistency, bias suppression, decision quality, correctability, representation, ethicality; pp. 104–105).

The respondents were callers to the police (47%), people stopped by the police (31%) and court users (23%) (p. 110). He measured each criterion with a few survey items and regressed a rating of the fairness of the procedure on all of them at once. The criteria explain 69% of the variance (adjusted R²; pp. 121–122, Table 3). Seven make independent contributions, with standardized coefficients: the authorities' effort to be fair .30, honesty .23, ethicality (politeness and respect for rights) .21, quality of decisions .17, representation .17, correctability .14, and bias .07. Consistency with past experience or with other people's treatment does not matter (β = .04, not significant), though earlier work predicted it would. Fairness judgments explain about 24% of the variance in satisfaction and evaluation that nonfairness factors leave unexplained, against about 1% the other way (p. 117).

Two analyses of variation follow. He tests eight criteria against six features of the situation and eight personal traits. Sixteen of 48 situation comparisons were significant (33%), against 5 of 64 trait comparisons (8%), which is about chance (pp. 125–126). So "the meaning of procedural justice varied according to the nature of the situation, not the characteristics of the people involved" (abstract, p. 103). In disputes, representation and consistency count for more; in non-disputes, the officer's effort to be fair (pp. 125–127). He concludes there are "no universally fair procedures" (p. 131), but that people share a common conception within a setting, which helps officials, because all parties will "focus on similar issues" (p. 132).

## Relation to the argument
Ruling D8 supplies the yardstick question: how to compare courts, FX markets and platforms. Tyler gives a list of criteria that people use, and evidence that voice (representation) and a way to correct errors each add to perceived fairness. The ruling's open question about where conventions bend can use his list as a comparison grid.

Facet check: the facet says the study "supports the use of the Q2 list as a yardstick for all three settings". The study cuts the other way on one point. Its main finding is that the weights on criteria change with the situation, and its stated conclusion is that no procedure is universally fair (p. 131). The grid needs weights per setting. The facet's list also names "notice", which Tyler did not measure; his items cover representation (process and decision control, p. 111), correctability and the others above. Correctability was measured only as whether the respondent knew of an agency to which they could have complained, and 33% did (p. 113).

The card's reading, not Tyler's: his strongest predictor, the effort to be fair, is an inference about an official's motive (pp. 128–129), and the respondent had a person to read. A platform's mediator offers no such person, which bears on premise P7 (inferring what the mediator will do). Treat that as a question for the paper, not a result.

## Caution
Self-reports by Chicago residents in 1984 about police and court contacts, mostly not disputes (the 18% figure applies to cases with a dispute). Footnote 5 (p. 128) notes that the data are correlational, so the causal order cannot be tested. The fairness rating and the criteria come from the same respondent. It says nothing about financial markets, platforms or automated mediators.
