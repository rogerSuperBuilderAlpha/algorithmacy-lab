## What it argues
Garrett, a reviewer from Northwestern University writing for librarians, presents Saenger's book as proof that medieval scribes pursued "readability" centuries before print. He states the thesis in Saenger's strong form. Word space "was introduced into medieval manuscripts to serve as a co-constituent of meaning, a visual cue allowing the rapid parsing of units of meaning at the sentence, word, and morphemic level" (p. 487). Without it the reader must separate the text "at considerable cognitive expense", by repeated passes (p. 487). He dates the change from "the seventh century on", beginning in the scriptoria of the British Isles and moving east and south (p. 487), and he adds the other cues Saenger treats: capitals, line-end hyphens, ligatures, the ampersand (p. 487). He notes that the scholarly apparatus takes 200 of the book's nearly 500 pages (p. 487).

Garrett then sets Saenger against Ong. Ong gives white space to typography; Garrett says Ong "overlooks the microtextual origins of white space as a coformative of meaning" (p. 488). He describes scribes who tried and dropped devices, such as spaces between syllables ("aerated text"), as a "hit-and-miss, trial-and-error, by no means linear" path (p. 488). He closes by extending the claim to screens: "Many of the issues we face today (e.g., the readability of extended texts on scrollable computer screens) had their roots in the ruminations of Irish scribes of the seventh century" (p. 488).

## Relation to the argument
This is reception data, not evidence. It shows that a 1998 reader outside the classicists' dispute took the efficiency-and-cost framing as the book's message and carried it straight to interface design. That is the nearest published precedent for the author's own move (spacing as an interface change), and it dates the move to 1998. Garrett argues the cost claim (P1, P2 in `ARGUMENT_MAP.md`) only by restating Saenger. He does not test the physiology, he does not mention Knox, Vatri or Heilmann, and he says nothing about whether spacing raised the number of readers (P6). So it supports no premise as evidence.

It bears on rulings D1 and D10 in one way. The review credits Saenger with correcting Ong on *dating* (space is older than print), not with a mechanism that restructures thought. Garrett's own phrase for the result is a "continuum of technological innovation" (p. 488), which fits the author's "guide, not mechanism" reading (D10) and fits the earlier ruling against an Ong-style restructuring. It cuts against any use of the review to show that spacing "ignited" anything.
