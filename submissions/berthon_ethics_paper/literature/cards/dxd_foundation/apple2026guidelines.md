## What it argues

The page sets the terms on which an app reaches the App Store and stays there: five parts (Safety, Performance, Business, Design, Legal), pre-submission checks and a short account of what follows submission. Three features matter for who holds a convention.

First, the author is rule-writer, enforcer and judge of the line. The introduction says "We will reject apps for any content or behavior that we believe is over the line." It answers the question of what line with "I'll know it when I see it," and calls the text "a living document; new apps presenting new questions may result in new rules at any time." The Business section adds that its lists "are not exhaustive, and your submission may trigger a change or update to our policies" (3.2).

Second, procedure for the person on the receiving end is thin and runs through Apple. "Rejections: Our goal is to apply these guidelines fairly and consistently, but nobody's perfect." Developers may write to the review team, "submit an appeal," and "suggest changes to the guidelines themselves." The page names no independent reviewer, deadline, standard of review or notice period for rule changes. A Code of Conduct breach ends the account, and restoration needs "a written statement detailing the improvements you plan to make" that Apple approves (5.6). Bug fixes to live apps are not held up over guideline violations "except for those related to legal or safety issues" (After You Submit).

Third, the page hands upkeep of the convention to the developer. Apps with user-generated content need filtering, a reporting mechanism with "timely responses to concerns," a way to block abusive users and published contact details (1.2), and "It is your responsibility to remove content that violates this guideline, your terms of service, or your community standards." Apps hosting chatbots and mini-apps carry the same duties (4.7.1). The rules also bend by place: apps on the US storefront need no entitlement to link to outside purchase methods (3.1.1(a)), browser-engine entitlements exist for the EU and Japan (2.5.6), and alternative marketplaces exist "in some markets and on certain platforms" (Introduction). The page names no law as the reason. Rule 5.1.2(i) separately requires explicit permission before personal data are shared with "third parties, including with third-party AI."

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D8 and premise P10, in the platform column of facet J. The page is a clean instance of an owner who writes the convention, rewrites it at will, interprets it by discretion and runs the only appeal. That supports P10: a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention. It also holds an appeal and an invitation to propose rule changes, which contradicts a flat "no appeal" reading. What it lacks is what the EU texts require: a notice period, reasons and independent review (`eu2019p2b`, `eu2022dsa`). The users' side of the triad, the person who downloads the app, has no channel to Apple in this text.

Facet check: facet J's quotations and its "no notice period for changes" hold as far as I can see, including the 5.6 restoration clause. Add the regional exceptions and the delegation of duties in 1.2 and 4.7, which facet J did not use.

## Caution

A living page: quote it only with the retrieval date and the "Last Updated" line. It states rules, not practice: no counts of rejections, appeals or restorations, no timelines, and nothing on whether an appeal is heard by anyone other than Apple. One platform's rulebook cannot stand for all platforms.
