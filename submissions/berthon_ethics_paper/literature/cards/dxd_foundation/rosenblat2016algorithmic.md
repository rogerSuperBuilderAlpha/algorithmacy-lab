# Rosenblat, A., & Stark, L. (2016). Algorithmic labor and information asymmetries: A case study of Uber's drivers. *International Journal of Communication, 10*, 3758–3784.

**Identifier:** https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/view/4892 · **Read depth:** full_text (journal PDF, IJoC pagination 3758–3784, CC BY-NC-ND) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (qualitative case study) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** nine-month study, Dec 2014–Sep 2015 (p. 3759); about 1,350 archival items from five driver forums (three large: UberDrive, 700–1,000 daily visitors; UberOps, about 5,100 members; UberCool), plus semi-structured interviews with seven drivers (p. 3760); a design critique of the app and a discursive critique of Uber's communications; forum and driver names changed · **Parties modeled:** two parties + a platform as intermediary (driver, passenger and Uber; Uber is also the employer-like party) · **Facets:** J, X · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

The authors find that "Uber does leverage significant indirect control over how drivers do their jobs," and that "the information and power asymmetries produced by the Uber application are fundamental to its ability to structure control over its workers" (abstract, p. 3758). Uber also uses "rhetorical invocations of digital technology and algorithms" to frame the relation as a neutral one (p. 3758).

The evidence comes in four groups. (1) **Blind acceptance.** Drivers have "about 15 seconds" to accept a request and see no destination or fare; refusing risks "deactivation" (p. 3762). In Savannah the $5 uberX minimum fare, less a $1 safe-rides fee and at least 20% of the remaining $4, leaves the driver $3.20 before costs (p. 3762). (2) **Unilateral terms.** Uber's agreement lets drivers "negotiate a lower fare, but not a higher one" (p. 3763). A driver reports a commission rising from 20% to 28% on the XL platform after a forced click-through of new terms (p. 3764). Rate cuts come with Uber graphs promising a 25% rise in hourly earnings, which drivers call "Uber math" (p. 3764). (3) **Adjudication by Uber's data.** Uber "claims that it has the data to adjudicate disputes," treating its data as "an objective, third-party witness" (p. 3765). One driver used dash-cam footage to reverse a halved fare; the authors conclude, "Uber can hold drivers accountable to the 'most efficient' routes, but drivers need their own data to hold Uber accountable for the wages they are owed" (p. 3765). (4) **Surge and nudging.** Surge is priced on the passenger's location, not the driver's (p. 3766). Messages blur predictions with measurements; when a CSR is pressed, the reply is that prediction "is never going to be 100% accurate" (pp. 3768–3771). Drivers' only channel is email to support staff whose answers resemble templates, with "no managerial correspondent empowered with the role of 'foreman'" (p. 3771). Passengers act as "middle managers" through ratings, with about 4.6 of 5 needed to stay active, averaged over the last 500 rated trips (pp. 3772–3775).

They state the limits of the sample: forum users "may be more strongly opinionated," and the accounts "are not necessarily representative" (p. 3760). They defend the structural point, that these features "could potentially affect any driver using the application" (p. 3760).

## Relation to the argument

This is the driver-side evidence for premise P10 (the scribes shared their readers' interest; a platform owner does not share its users'). The interface is the instrument of the party with an interest: Uber sets rates, shows only part of a request, and describes its role as "a neutral intermediary" (p. 3761) while shaping behaviour through "soft control" (p. 3761). It also bears on ruling D3 (disclosure is a literacy intervention, not an algorithmacy one) in an indirect way: the drivers do read and interpret the app, and they build their own literacy (logs, dash-cams, forum advice such as "Don't chase the surge," p. 3766), yet they cannot change the rule they face. The authors' own remedy, "A contextually aware manager able to explain what is happening to the software-based infrastructure," is a human route of explanation, not a disclosure of the algorithm (p. 3771). On ruling D8 (where the convention bends), the case shows the direction on a platform: toward the owner, with the driver's redress limited to an email queue and self-collected evidence.

## Caution

Forum-based and non-representative by the authors' own statement; seven interviews. The study is from 2014–2015 and fares, rating thresholds and policies have since changed, so the figures (15 seconds, 4.6, 20–28%) are dated. It describes Uber's design and drivers' reports of it; it does not measure effects on earnings or test any intervention. Uber's side is represented only through public statements, legal briefs and support emails drivers forwarded.
