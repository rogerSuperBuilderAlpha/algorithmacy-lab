## What it argues

The premise is the speech segmentation problem: "continuous speech lacks directly accessible cues to word boundaries. Prominent acoustic cues, such as pauses, are rare and occur only inconsistently"; the task "is particularly apparent when [adults] try to identify discrete words in an unfamiliar foreign language" (The Speech Segmentation Problem). Listeners segment by transitional probabilities between syllables and by prosody. The experiments ask which prosodic cues help. "only in Experiment 1 did we replicate the finding that statistical cues alone suffice for successful speech segmentation" (Robustness of the Results and Sensitivity to the Testing Environment). "Overall, pauses and final lengthening were perceived as converging with the statistical cues and facilitated speech segmentation, with pauses helping most. Final-syllable shortening hindered baseline speech segmentation, indicating that when cues conflict, prosodic cues can override statistical cues" (abstract); pitch changes had "little effect". In Experiment 3, the laboratory design with auditory test items and "a strong auditory memory component", only pauses raised performance above baseline (Table 3; D′ Analysis and Results, Exp. 3). The authors' explanation for the primacy of the pause: "in real speech, pauses almost exclusively occur at word boundaries and are not as flexibly distributed as changes in duration or pitch" (The Relative Strengths of Different Prosodic Cues). They record that the effects were clearest in the informal pilot and weakest in the most conventional laboratory design (Robustness of the Results and Sensitivity to the Testing Environment).

## Relation to the argument

The abstract says an unspaced line forced a reader "to sound out the line just to hear where each word ended". That assumes hearing delivers word ends. Speech-segmentation research begins from the opposite fact: the signal does not mark word boundaries, and a listener recovers them from statistics, prosody and, above all, the lexicon (Mattys, White & Melhorn 2005, abstract only). The one acoustic cue that reliably marks a boundary in these experiments is a silence between words, the auditory analogue of the space.

- **Cuts against** the mechanism as stated: voicing an unspaced string does not, by itself, make the boundaries audible. A reader who sounds out *scriptio continua* hears a continuous stream, which is what a listener to a foreign language hears.
- **Supports a narrower claim.** For a reader who already holds the words by ear, pronouncing the string lets the auditory lexicon do the segmenting; the Insular learner of Latin who had the liturgy by heart is such a reader. Busch (2002, p. 20, after Raible) describes exactly this second channel. The experiments here do not test it.
- **Supports the representational-effect reading** from the speech side: the cue that works is a gap; the Insular scribes put a gap on the page. That is the analogy the author wants, with the caveat that a pause in speech and a space on a page are different media and the transfer is the author's, not the authors'.

## In conversation with

## Caution

Nonsense trisyllabic languages, adult German listeners, minutes of exposure; nothing here is about reading or Latin, and the transfer to a reader sounding out text is the card's, not the paper's. The authors stress that effects were sensitive to setting and that Experiment 3, the most standard design, showed the weakest effects. Pause length was fixed at 250 ms; stimuli were synthesised from isolated syllables without coarticulation.
