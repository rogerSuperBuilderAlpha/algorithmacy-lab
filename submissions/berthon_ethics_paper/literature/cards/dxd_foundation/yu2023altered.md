**Identifier:** doi:10.1167/iovs.64.13.21 · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access article text, PMC10584020, Europe PMC XML; no printed page numbers, so locators are section names; figures and the Table were read as text only) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** within-subject gaze-contingent experiment, 24 normally sighted young adults (18 to 23 years, mean 19.62; nine men) at Northeastern University, reading five-sentence pages aloud in Courier New with a simulated peripheral aperture or central scotoma of 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 degrees, plus no-scotoma baseline; EyeLink 1000 Plus at 500 Hz; 28 text pages per person; three people dropped from central-scotoma analysis, leaving 21 · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and degraded text) · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Yu and Kwon ask how much of the visual field a reader needs, and where, before eye movements depart from normal. They find a critical size, not a gradual decline.

Results (Results; Table; Figs. 2 and 4):
- Reading speed fell 54% under the smallest aperture (226.44 ± 5.35 wpm intact against 104.73 ± 6.70 wpm with a 2-degree aperture, p < .001), and 51% under the largest central scotoma (214.08 ± 4.48 against 104.09 ± 5.64 wpm). The effect of peripheral scotoma size on speed was F(5,115) = 117.24, p < .001; the effect of central scotoma size was F(5,100) = 123.77, p < .001.
- Peripheral aperture: below 6 degrees (about 11 visible letters), fixation duration and fixation count rose sharply. At the 2-degree aperture, fixation duration rose 21% (214 to 256 ms), fixations per line doubled (12.45 to 24.83), and saccade amplitude fell 30% (3.27 to 2.29 degrees). Saccade amplitude and velocity began to fall below an 8-degree aperture (14 letters).
- Central scotoma: a scotoma larger than 2.5 degrees (about 4.4 letters) hurt speed. At 10 degrees, saccade amplitude rose 98%, fixation duration 13% (217 to 245 ms), fixations per line 89%, and the share of regressive saccades 86% (24% to 45%). Microsaccade rate fell nearly 70%.
- The authors read the 6-degree figure as matching the visual-span hypothesis, with the visual span at roughly 9 to 14 letters for healthy adults (Discussion, take-home message 5).
- In the Introduction they state the standard perceptual span as "three to four letters to the left of fixation and 14 to 15 letters to the right of fixation," citing McConkie and Rayner, Rayner (1986), and others.

The Methods give letter size as 0.5 degrees with 0.065-degree interletter spacing, so one letter occupies 0.565 degrees; this is how they convert degrees into letters.

## Relation to the argument
The paper does not examine word spacing, and no premise in the argument map depends on its data. The project uses it as an open source that restates the perceptual-span figures. The Introduction attributes them to Rayner and McConkie and others, so the card supports only that Yu and Kwon assert them. The Discussion's interpretive line, that visibility and eye movements are tightly coupled and that longer, more frequent fixations may be "optimal reading strategies to compensate," is relevant to D6 only by analogy: a degraded input changes where and how long the eyes rest, not just how fast the reader goes.

Facet check: the facet describes the paper as one of three open papers that restate the perceptual-span figures. That is accurate. Yu and Kwon contribute a different number too: the span they measure in this task is about 11 to 14 letters, which they call "slightly" smaller than the perceptual span of 14 to 19 letters, and they give reasons (Discussion).

## Caution
Young adults with normal vision, simulated and not clinical scotomas, no adaptation period (the authors say people with real central loss settle on a preferred retinal locus and show smaller saccades, which this sample did not), simple second- to fourth-grade sentences read aloud, 56 characters per line. The span figures it quotes are restated from other work; cite the original studies. The Table's microsaccade-amplitude result was null (p = .78 peripheral, p = .31 central).
