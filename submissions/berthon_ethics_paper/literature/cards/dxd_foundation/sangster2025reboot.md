## What it argues

Sangster charges that the accounting-history literature on double entry before 1800 is "antiquarian": it asks "what" and "how" and never "why," reads the past through present practice, and trusts its predecessors instead of revisiting sources. The abstract says the result is that "unsupportable assertions are accepted as axiomatic fact" (p. 368).

He traces the line of descent from Besta (pp. 370–372). Yamey, who dominates the English literature, uses two definitions without saying so: De Roover's bookkeeping, and a "Sombart-inspired" one that needs modern accounting features such as closed ledgers and profit-and-loss accounts. In Sangster's words, Yamey "was not assessing the bookkeeping. He was assessing the accounting" (p. 373). Yet "Neither Yamey nor De Roover ever wrote that double entry bookkeeping was rarely used before 1800" (p. 374). Hoskin and Macve's 1986 statement that it was rare is an "unfounded and misleading assertion" that discouraged further work (p. 375). Yamey generalised from a few ledgers and "less than 4 per cent" of the bookkeeping manuals published before 1800 (p. 374); a footnote counts 882 editions and says he would have needed six times more manuals for 95 per cent confidence about Great Britain (n. 8, p. 387).

Two recent articles serve as examples of a literature "increasingly corrupted by misunderstanding" (abstract, p. 368; pp. 378–380). One places features of the 1211 Florentine bank ledger centuries too late in a table; the other redefines single-entry bookkeeping so that it contains double entries. Sangster's counter-evidence is that surviving Tuscan books show "no uniformity in practice beyond a superficial level, even in individual firms; no Darwinian progression" (p. 378). He recommends a "critical traditional" history that returns to the account books and draws on other disciplines, whose historians "dismiss the medieval accounting history literature with contempt" (p. 376).

## Relation to the argument

Facet F uses this article as a case where a one-agent origin story (Pacioli, 1494) and a one-cause story (double entry made capitalism) fall apart (case 4). The body supports part of that.

It supports this much. Pacioli's treatise describes the Venetian paired journal-and-ledger method, which was not how double entry was done everywhere (p. 375 and n. 11), and he describes an "elementary" system (p. 384). Double entry is attested well before him: the first confirmed instances Sangster names are 1296 and 1299 (p. 379, citing De Roover 1963 and Lee 1977), about 195 to 198 years before 1494 by this card's subtraction. Economic historians "might recognise" it in the 1211 ledger (p. 378). That fits the lesson that credit for a convention goes to a later, visible description (D6).

It does not support a settled account of why double entry was used. The abstract says economic historians found "two primary purposes before 1800: control over debt and distant agents, factors, and partners," and that "few used it to identify overall profit or wealth." The body does not set that claim out; it argues that nobody has asked "why." Cite the abstract's sentence as Sangster's summary of economic historians, not as a finding he demonstrates.

Facet check: (1) Facet F attaches "a ledger of 1211 … and entity-wide double entry … by the end of the 13th century" to "Sangster 2025 [online 2024], abstract." Those details belong to `sangster2024emergence` (*Economic History Review*). Here 1211 is the earliest paragraph-format ledger (p. 378), and the confirmed double-entry dates are 1296 and 1299. (2) The facet lists Yamey's contest against Sombart as support for deflating the causal claim. Sangster does not overturn Yamey's finding that few identified profit before 1800; the abstract concurs. He argues that Yamey's definitions and synonyms invite readers to take it as a finding about bookkeeping (pp. 373–374). So Sangster qualifies how Yamey can be cited, and does not reject the Sombart criticism.

## Caution

A polemic by a specialist, not a neutral survey; it faults named scholars and reports no systematic review of the literature it condemns. Its positive claims about use before 1800 appear in the abstract and in scattered evidence in the body, so take them as Sangster's view; the 2024 *Economic History Review* paper (`sangster2024emergence`, not read here) is the likelier source for the origin story. It concerns business and bank ledgers, not reading or notation.
