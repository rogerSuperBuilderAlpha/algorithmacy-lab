# Bassetti, B., & Masterson, J. (2012). Effects of removing morphemic information and adding interword spacing on reading in Chinese experienced and inexperienced readers. *Reading and Writing, 25*(9), 2291–2314.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11145-012-9358-7 · **Read depth:** full_text (White Rose Research Online *submitted version*, 24 pages, with the authors' names masked as "Author" in some self-citations; it is not the published article, so wording and numbers may differ from the version of record, and no journal pagination applies, so locators are sections and tables) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (read as submitted manuscript) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** two 2 × 2 repeated-measures sentence–picture verification experiments; Exp. 1, university students in China (N = 24 stated; the text counts 13 women and 10 men); Exp. 2, 22 primary-school children in Nanjing (mean age 8;7); 42 trials of eight-syllable sentences · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and script) · **Facets:** C, D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

The paper has two aims. First, it asks why Chinese readers read pinyin (romanised, word-spaced) slower than hanzi, and tests whether the cause is missing morphemic information rather than unfamiliarity or homophony. Children serve as a control because they get heavy pinyin exposure but little hanzi practice. Second, it asks whether interword spacing helps Chinese readers, who are experienced or inexperienced. Each sentence appears in hanzi or pinyin, with spaces either between words or between every morpheme (syllable); the two spacings occupy the same screen width.

Script results. Adults read hanzi at 5.34 syllables per second (SD 1.65) and pinyin at 1.88 (SD 0.33), "almost three times more slowly" (Exp. 1, Results). Children read hanzi at 2.85 and 2.64 sps (morpheme and word spacing) and pinyin at 0.96 and 0.83 (Table 2), and the authors note that the 3:1 ratio holds for both groups (Exp. 2, Discussion). They conclude that pinyin is slow because it "does not provide morphemic information."

Spacing results. In Exp. 1, interword spacing slowed adult hanzi reading from 5.54 to 5.14 sps (Table 1; t = 2.48, p = .021); pinyin did not change (1.89 vs 1.87). The slowdown came almost wholly from the second block: 0.77 sps (SD 0.82) for readers who met hanzi after pinyin, 0.03 sps for those who read hanzi first, so the authors suggest readers primed to notice spaces are disrupted. In Exp. 2, children were slower with word spacing for both scripts (F(1, 21) = 11.80, p = .002) and less accurate (hanzi 95.67% vs 90.91%; Table 2; F(1, 21) = 11.96, p = .002). Spacing effect did not correlate with pinyin proficiency in adults (r = 0.34, p = .107), so the study finds no sign that weaker readers gain more.

The Conclusions state it flatly: "interword spacing does not facilitate the reading of either experienced or inexperienced Chinese readers," and the study "fail[s] to support the universality of the facilitative effects of interword spacing" (Abstract). The authors also read the literature: spacing helps Thai only for scrambled text (Kohsom & Gobet), helps Japanese only when kanji are replaced by kana (Sainio et al.), and helps English-speaking learners of Chinese, which they put down to the learners' word-spaced first language rather than to low proficiency (Exp. 2, Discussion).

## Relation to the argument

Premise P4: the facet records that this study finds no gain for children. The full text confirms and sharpens it: spaced words made children slower and less accurate. That cuts against any claim that spaces lower the cost of reading for beginners as a general rule. The paper's own explanation carries the author's point, though: the benefit appears for readers whose first writing system is already word-spaced (L1 English learners of Chinese, Exp. 2 Discussion). It treats spacing as a convention a reader holds, not a property of the page. That fits the paper's guide-not-mechanism rulings (D1, D10): a convention helps readers who already hold it. The authors also state the English-centric bias in reading research with a quotation from Coulmas (2003, p. 212).

Facet check: facet D marked this "read in part" and lists "no gain for children" correctly. The full text adds that the child effect is negative, not null, and that the adult hanzi slowdown depends on block order. The submitted version says of Bai et al. 2008 that they "found faster reading with interword spacing", which "probably happened because hanzi-spaced sentences occupied twice the width on screen compared with word-spaced sentences". That holds only against character spacing: in Bai's Table 1, word-spaced text took 5,239 ms and character-spaced text 5,686 ms, while normal unspaced text took 5,221 ms, so Bai found no gain over unspaced text. The width explanation is Bassetti and Masterson's, not a finding of Bai et al.

## Caution

A submitted manuscript; check the published article before quoting numbers. The task is sentence–picture verification of short, simple sentences, not text reading. Sample sizes are small (24 and 22) and the order effect was a post-hoc check requested by a reviewer. The authors' priming explanation is a hypothesis, not tested. The "faster pinyin for English learners" comparison rests on the authors' own earlier study ("Author, 2009"), which I did not read.
