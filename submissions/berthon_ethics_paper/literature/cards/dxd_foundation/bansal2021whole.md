## What it argues
Bansal and colleagues ask whether explanations help a human–AI team beat both the human and the AI working alone ("complementary performance"). They note that earlier studies showing gains from explanations used AIs far more accurate than people, so "one would achieve an even better result by stripping humans from the loop" (§1; Table 1). They therefore matched AI accuracy to human accuracy.

Main results. Showing the AI's recommendation and confidence alone produced complementary performance in all three tasks: for Beer, team accuracy 0.89 against AI 0.84 and humans alone 0.82 (§5.1). No explanation condition beat that confidence-only baseline: for Beer, 0.89 ± 0.05 (confidence) against 0.88 ± 0.06 (Explain-Top-1), z = −1.18, p = .24, with the same null pattern for Amzbook and LSAT (§5.1). Adaptive and expert explanations also failed to improve accuracy significantly (§5.1). Split by whether the AI was right, explaining the top prediction "lead to better accuracy when the AI recommendation was correct but worse when the AI was incorrect" (§5.1; Fig. 4B). The abstract states the headline: explanations "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness."

Subjective ratings diverged from performance. More participants rated expert adaptive explanations useful than AI ones (Beer 53% vs 38%), but "this increased preference did not translate to an improvement in team performance" (§5.2). In the qualitative coding of 409 responses, 47% used the AI "as a prior guide" and 25% as a post-check; some participants built confidence thresholds (27% of the 90 who mentioned confidence) and learned from feedback whether to trust the AI (§5.3, Table 3). Participants got immediate correctness feedback after every item (§4.3).

The discussion calls for explanations that "accurately inform the user" rather than persuade (§6.2), and notes that showing the answer first "makes it almost impossible for the human to reason independently" (§6.3, anchoring).

## Relation to the argument
**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** This is one of the cleaner experiments behind P8's claim that handing the person a text (here, an explanation) does not improve decisions: no explanation beat a bare confidence number, and explanations raised acceptance of wrong advice. It supports D3's direction for this task type. It does not show disclosure "doesn't affect" navigation: explanations changed behaviour (more agreement), in the wrong direction when the AI erred.

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The qualitative section cuts in the author's favour on one point. With immediate feedback after each item, some workers learned when to rely on the AI ("stopped after I got 2 incorrect answers", §5.3). That is understanding through deciding and seeing the result, under the "rapid and unequivocal" feedback that P9 requires. It is a dyad, not a triad.

**Premise P10 (second candidate).** The confidence number, a single signal, did as well as any explanation. That fits the paper's interest in conventions that return a short signal rather than a text, though Bansal et al. did not test a convention of that kind.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 33 cites Bansal et al. (2021), with Chen et al. (2023), for "the explanations can raise acceptance of the recommendation whether or not it is correct." Supported: abstract; §5.1 and Fig. 4B (explanations improved accuracy when the AI was right and lowered it when the AI was wrong). The surrounding sentence's claim that exposing mechanics does not improve decision quality is attributed to Parasuraman and Manzey, but Bansal et al. support it too for this setting (§5.1: no explanation condition beat confidence alone). ## Caution
Crowd workers on low-stakes tasks with ground truth; the authors say results "may not generalize to high-stakes domains with expert users" (§6.1). Accuracy was the only outcome. Two explanation formats only (highlights and short natural-language arguments). The AI was a classifier advising one person, so nothing here bears directly on a mediator with interests of its own. The 1,626 figure in §1 and the "more than 1,500 participants" in §7 are the authors' counts; I did not re-add the per-condition numbers.
