## What it argues

The IAB states that "when there is a conflict between the interests of end users of the Internet and other parties, IETF decisions should favor end users" (Abstract). The document defines end users broadly, including people affected by a system they are not using (§2), and grounds the priority in the IETF mission statement (RFC 3935) and in the warning that measurable success "ignores how technology is so often used as a lever to assert power over users, rather than empower them" (§3).

Section 4 lists ways to act on the priority. §4.1 concedes that "The IETF community does not have any unique insight into what is 'good for end users'" and that, even when the IETF reaches out, end users' "participation is minimal -- until a change affects someone in a way they don't like." It recommends going to affected communities and treating civil-society organisations as "a primary channel". §4.2 praises the standardised browser "user agent" as an intermediary that lets users switch implementations, and contrasts IoT, where "opaque, vendor-specific software" prevails. §4.3 says that "inappropriate concentration of power on the Internet has become a concerning phenomenon -- one that protocol design might have some influence upon." The document is explicit that it "is not an Internet Standards Track specification" (Status of This Memo).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The RFC is a standards body's own admission that end users are mostly absent from its process (§4.1), which supports the draft's claim that a standards body is an unreliable holder of a user-protecting convention. It also cuts slightly the other way: the IAB commits the IETF to favour end users in conflicts and names the user agent as a structural safeguard (§4.2). A user agent that represents the person against a service is close to what the draft wants a convention-holder to do. The draft could use §4.2 as a positive example rather than only as a gap.

**What it does not say.** Nothing in RFC 8890 says that adoption of standards is voluntary or that standards do not bind platforms. That claim belongs to RFC 2026 (`rfc2026`), whose §1.1 describes the Internet as supporting communication "through voluntary adherence to open protocols and procedures defined by Internet Standards" (checked in S2 against the RFC Editor text).

**Use in DRAFT_v2:**
- Line 428 ("Technical standards bodies cannot fill the gap: adoption of their standards is voluntary, and end users take little part in writing them (Nottingham, 2020)"): half supported. "End users take little part" matches §4.1 ("their participation is minimal"). "Adoption of their standards is voluntary" is not in RFC 8890; cite RFC 2026 (Bradner, 1996) for it. "Cannot fill the gap" is the draft's inference; the RFC itself argues the IETF should and can do more for end users (§4).
- Line 641 (reference entry): correct in substance; it lacks the month (August) and the DOI 10.17487/RFC8890.
- Line 750 (notes table): the earlier draft's "explicitly declare[s]" claim is unsupported by the text, as the note implies.

## Caution

An informational IAB statement of intent with no evidence base and no enforcement; it binds nobody, including the IETF's working groups. It speaks for the IETF of 2020 and says nothing about W3C or other bodies.
