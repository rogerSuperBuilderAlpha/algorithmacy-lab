## What it argues
Reis summarizes Johnson in one paragraph. Johnson "sees [reading] more broadly" than as "a strictly cognitive act," and defines it as "the negotiated construction of meaning within a particular sociocultural context." He studies "reading communities" and how their attitudes to books "were both informed by and shaped their worldviews."

The one sentence on script: "Johnson shows that the scriptio continua of the bookroll and its costly aesthetic features ensured that the consumers of texts were limited." Restricting access "was precisely the point for elites who used reading to create and reaffirm the values of high society." The chapters on Pliny, Tacitus, Galen, Gellius, Fronto, Lucian and the Egyptian papyri show aristocrats fitting reading into daily routines. Such reading "separated them from the hoi polloi." Reis ends by calling the work "an important contribution to discussions of elite culture."

## Relation to the argument
Reis states the sharpest version of the status reading: unspaced script, together with the bookroll's cost, "ensured" a limited readership. Ker and Mannering say the script is consistent with an elite culture; Reis says it limited the readership, and attributes the aim to the elites. That wording is Reis's compression and the paper should not treat it as Johnson's. It touches P6 (the move lowered a cost of reading, did not by itself raise the number of readers) and the dropped claim "Spacing 'ignited literacy'": on this account the Roman page excluded by design, so spacing did not cure an accident.

The review does not mention word separation as a change, Saenger, silent reading or training for novices, so it adds nothing to P1–P4.
