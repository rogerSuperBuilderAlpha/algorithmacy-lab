## What it argues

From the snippets: the evidence "shows that England was massively illiterate despite an epoch of educational expansion"; "husbandmen, labourers and women were massively illiterate", with literacy ranked by social status and occupation; and literacy "was responsive to changes in the economic, political and religious history of the Tudor" period. Promoters of literacy were often unheeded, "while the pull of economic and social utility seems to have been felt". He places England within "a general transition from restricted to mass literacy" in the western world and contrasts it with places where "mass literacy was achieved at the behest of, and through the agency of, an evangelical" authority (New England, Sweden, Scotland). He cites Eisenstein's *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* for "the modern view of the impact of printing".

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P6 and the historiographical boundary in §3.** Cressy is good support for the draft's point that literacy spread slowly and through social and institutional forces, not from a single device: by his measure most of the English population was still illiterate in the mid-seventeenth century, two centuries after print. It cuts against any reading in which a technique of the page, or print, produces mass literacy on its own. It also shows where mass literacy did arrive early: through church and state campaigns, which fits ruling D8's interest in who enforces a convention.
