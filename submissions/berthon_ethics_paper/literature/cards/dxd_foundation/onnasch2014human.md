## What it argues
Degree of automation (DOA) is defined on the Parasuraman–Sheridan–Wickens stages and levels: a later stage or a higher level is "more automation" (pp. 477–478). The hypothesis is the "lumberjack effect": "the higher they are, the farther they fall" (p. 477), that is, routine performance rises with DOA and failure performance falls.

Results (Table 1, p. 481): routine primary-task performance, 16 studies, overall tau = .51, p < .001; return-to-manual primary-task performance after an automation breakdown, 9 studies, tau = −.34, p = .03; routine secondary-task performance, 8 studies, tau = .29, p = .04, but five of eight studies showed zero correlation; subjective workload, 12 studies, tau = −.24, p = .056; situation awareness, 11 studies, tau = −.29, p = .04. Eight of the nine studies with both routine and failure data showed a more positive correlation for routine than for failure performance (p. 483). The two studies with the strongest trade-off were also among those with the strongest loss of situation awareness (tau = −.71 and −.63, p. 484).

The moderator analysis is the paper's main finding. For the six studies that moved DOA across the boundary from information analysis (stage 2) to action selection (stage 3), the routine tau rose to +.68 and the return-to-manual tau fell to −.90 (p. 484). The authors explain it by the generation effect: "Actively choosing actions manually ... supports SA in a way that supports the manual performance in case of automation breakdown. When that choice is removed, the automation failure response suffers" (p. 485). Hence "there is no specific DOA below which automation-induced performance benefits can be increased without any performance costs", and if return-to-manual matters, "human operators should be kept involved at least to some extent in decision and action selection" (p. 486). Prior failure training, expertise and number of concurrent tasks did not moderate the trade-off; a post hoc look for an effect of "ecological" displays found none, which the authors attribute to low power (p. 485).

## Relation to the argument
This is the quantitative form of Bainbridge's irony and the best dyadic evidence for the abstract's claim that reading the machine's output is a weak way to stay able to act. Where automation takes over the choosing and leaves the person to accept or veto, failure performance collapses (tau −.90). That supports the abstract's turn to "epistemic action" and the author's D7: the person who decides, learns; the person who reads a decision does not. It also supports Onnasch et al.'s own prescription, which is the engagement remedy inside the dyad.

It does not support anything beyond the dyad. Every study has one operator and one aid; the automation's "failure" is a breakdown, not a divergent objective. The effect sizes are rank correlations from significance coding, so no magnitudes transfer to a platform setting. For P10, the finding sits badly with the first candidate convention (a rule that removes a strategic task): removing choice is exactly what raised the cost of failure here, though the failure in question is manual take-over, not coordination.

## Caution
Eighteen studies, mostly students and simulations; effects coded dichotomously and ranked, so the taus are ordinal summaries, not effect sizes, and the authors call the SA result in need of caution (p. 485). The boundary analysis rests on six studies. The repository copy carries the journal layout and pagination. Nothing here concerns platforms, mediators or audit logs.
