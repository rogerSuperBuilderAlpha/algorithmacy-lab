# Galliussi, J., Perondi, L., Chia, G., Gerbino, W., & Bernardis, P. (2020). Inter-letter spacing, inter-word spacing, and font with dyslexia-friendly features: Testing text readability in people with and without dyslexia. *Annals of Dyslexia, 70*, 141–152.

## What it argues
Galliussi and colleagues ask whether "dyslexia-friendly" fonts help because of their letterforms or because of their wider spacing, and they split spacing into its letter and word components for the first time among the studies they review (p. 143). Each child read a baseline text and eight matched test texts, one per condition, in 14-pt type with a Latin-square assignment of texts to conditions (p. 144–145). Increased spacing was +70/1000 em between letters and +270/1000 em between words (p. 144). Speed was syllables per second; accuracy was a log ratio of words correct to errors (p. 145).

The letterform had no main effect for either group (speed, children with dyslexia, F(1,434) = 0.006, p = .936; controls, F(1,434) = 0.843, p = .359; pp. 147, 149). Spacing mattered through an interaction. Widening letter spacing without widening word spacing slowed both groups. For children with dyslexia, the default condition ran at 3.22 syll/s and the wide-letter, default-word condition at 3.04 syll/s (t(63) = −4.258, p < .001); controls ran at 4.99 against 4.88 syll/s (t(63) = −2.577, p = .036; pp. 147, 149). Widening both was no better than widening neither (3.23 against 3.22 syll/s for children with dyslexia). One letterform interaction did appear for the dyslexic group alone: with the dyslexia-friendly letterform, widening word spacing raised speed from 3.10 to 3.26 syll/s (letterform x inter-word spacing, F(1,434) = 4.471, p = .035; t(63) = 3.33, p < .001), and not with the standard letterform (p. 147). Accuracy showed no reliable effect of any factor (pp. 147–149). The authors read the slowdown as a segmentation failure: "in this unusual spacing condition, it is difficult to segment sentences into words because the sizes of the (increased) inter-letter and the (default) inter-word spacing appeared similar" (p. 150).

They also say the spacing effects were small: "not practically meaningful (e.g., the difference in reading speed between the two conditions did not exceed 0.20 syll/s)" (p. 150). The groups differed enormously at baseline (dyslexic 2.91 against control 4.49 syll/s, about 35% lower; p. 145), so the spacing changes were minor beside the group gap. They found no general speed gain from wider spacing, unlike Zorzi et al. (2012), and offer line length, amount of widening and age as explanations (p. 150). They did not measure comprehension.

## Relation to the argument
This is the one paper in the batch whose result turns on the relation between letter gaps and word gaps. It supports the claim that a space works as a boundary cue only when it is wider than the gaps inside words (premise P5: re-representing the task so the page marks what the reader would otherwise infer). Widen the letters and the boundary vanishes, and reading slows. That fits the author's wish to find the move that "does most of the work" among a complex of conventions (ruling D6): relative gap size, not whitespace as such, is the candidate.

It cuts against any wider claim that more space helps the struggling reader (premise P6, "most for readers still learning"): the dyslexic and control groups responded alike, and the effects were small. Its subjects are 12-year-olds who already read Italian, a transparent orthography, so it says nothing about how long a learner takes to acquire reading (ruling D2). Under D10 (history as a guide) it is background for the analogy only.

Facet check: the facet's one-line summary, "reading speed is impaired by an increase in inter-letter spacing not combined with an adequate increase in inter-word spacing," matches the abstract. It omits that the authors call the effect practically negligible (under 0.20 syll/s) and found no letterform effect.

## In conversation with
- `zorzi2012extra` — the seminal extra-large-spacing study; Galliussi et al. fail to reproduce its general gain and note Zorzi did not match sentence length (p. 142, p. 150).
- `hakvoort2017improvements` — "partly replicated" Zorzi with equal-length sentences, finding fewer errors but no speed gain (p. 142).
- Leads not in the bib: Marinus et al. (2016, *Dyslexia*), which separated Dyslexie letterform from spacing; Bachmann (2013), the EasyReading study; Kuster et al. (2017) and Wery & Diliberto (2017), no-effect studies of dyslexia fonts; Schneps et al. (2013), shorter lines.

## Caution
One Italian-language sample of 12-year-olds, read aloud, with no comprehension measure. Accuracy sits near ceiling in a transparent orthography (the authors say so, p. 150–151). The tested widening is modest and the effect sizes are tiny (partial η² around .01 to .04). It concerns letter-versus-word spacing in an alphabetic script, not the removal of word spaces, so it cannot be cited as evidence on scriptio continua. The ESM appendices (text norms, font design) were not read.
