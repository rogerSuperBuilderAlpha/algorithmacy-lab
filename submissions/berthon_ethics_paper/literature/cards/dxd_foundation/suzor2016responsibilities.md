## What it argues
Suzor opens with the claim that "The ways in which platforms are governed matters" (para. 1) and that platforms are not neutral: their architecture and algorithms shape what people say and see, and "the powers they provide are carefully wielded and selectively enforced" (para. 1). States now enlist platforms in regulation (he cites "over 65 Million takedown notices" received by Google for its search results, as of 2016), and civil society presses the language of corporate "responsibility" (para. 2–3).

The legal gap is the centre: terms of service are "formalised as legal contracts that most commonly allocate a great deal of power to the operators," so "In legal terms, the discretion of the platform owner is practically absolute," and constitutional language — "freedom of speech and association, requirements of due process and natural justice" — "has almost no application in the 'private' sphere" (para. 4). He proposes judging legitimacy against the rule of law: private governance should be "consensual, predictable, equal, and fair," with the "primary requirement" that "power is not exercised arbitrarily" (para. 5).

The only evidence is one summary: he examined "the legal terms and conditions of fifteen of the largest English-language social media platforms," each "analyzed to identify the extent to which they provided protections for due process interests of users," and "In all cases examined, the terms of service provided broad, unfettered discretion to platform owners." The line the facet leans on follows: "Like constitutional documents, terms of service grant powers; but unlike constitutions, they rarely limit those powers or regulate the ways they are exercised" (para. 6). He ends by arguing that some decentralisation of governance is "both inevitable and desirable" but that enforcement must be "regular, transparent, equally and proportionately applied, and fair" (para. 7).
