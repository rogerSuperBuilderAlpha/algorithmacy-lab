## What it argues

Seven vision scientists take stock of Gestalt psychology on the centenary of Wertheimer's 1912 paper. They say the field recovered from its mid-century fall by turning demonstrations into measured laws, tying them to natural-image statistics and finding neural correlates, while "modern vision science cannot be considered some kind of contemporary Gestalt approach either" (§7.2).

The part that matters here is grouping by proximity (§3). Wertheimer "first posed the problem of perceptual grouping in his ground-breaking 1923 paper by asking what stimulus factors influence the perceived grouping of discrete elements" (§3.1). Equally spaced dots form one line; when some sit closer than others, "the closer ones grouped together strongly into pairs". That "factor of relative distance, which Wertheimer called proximity, was the first of his famous laws or (more accurately) principles of grouping" (§3.1).

Later work made it a law. Oyama (1961) found the ratio of times observers saw horizontal and vertical grouping follows a power of the distance ratio, with exponent α ≈ 2.89 (§3.3.3). Kubovy and colleagues, with 300-ms dot-lattice displays, showed that grouping by proximity "depends only on the relative distance between dots in competing organizations, not on the overall configuration" (§3.3.3); once the longer distance exceeds the shorter by about 1.5, grouping along it "is almost never seen." Proximity and similarity combine additively (§3.3.3). In labelled natural contours the power-law exponent is 2.92, close to Oyama's perceptual value, and proximity cuts the entropy of a grouping decision by roughly 75%, against about 10% each for good continuation and similarity (§§4.2.1, 4.4.2). The authors read this as a visual system tuned to the statistics of its scenes.

They also complicate the classical picture: "grouping principles operate at multiple levels," and the conscious result depends on a representation built after depth and lightness constancy (§3.5), so grouping is not simply early and automatic (§7.2). Infants as young as 3 to 4 months group by proximity, yet contour grouping keeps developing into the late teens and experience shapes adult grouping (§4.4.1). Direct neural correlates "are relatively rare" (§7.3).
