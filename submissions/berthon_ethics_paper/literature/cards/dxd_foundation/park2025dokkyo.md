## What it argues
The presenter divides late Joseon society into five groups: yangban, chungin, commoners, cheonmin and slaves, and women. Most yangban and chungin had "dual literacy in both Hangul and Classical Chinese", and learned both from childhood because "bilingual texts (Hangul-Chinese), which used both scripts side by side, had become widespread from the elementary education stage onward". Commoners and cheonmin "were often excluded from the long-term, systematic education required to acquire literacy in Classical Chinese", so few could read it and "they primarily conducted their literate activities using Hangul". Most women, whatever their class, found learning to read harder than men did; upper-class women learned by "learning by observing over the shoulder" or from fathers and brothers, and those who read conduct manuals such as the *Yeosaseo* often gained dual literacy. The abstract closes by expecting that class ratios from household registers could be matched to these patterns to estimate literate-population size and rates in Hangul and Chinese. It gives no numbers.

## Relation to the argument
Supports, as a claim and not a measurement, the facet-F point that a script made for easy learning (Hangul) was read first by those shut out of the elite script: commoners and women. That fits a cost-lowering reading of a notation (P6) and the premise that learners gain most from a re-representation (P4). It gives no evidence on how many people learned or how fast, and the speaker names the estimate as future work.
