## What it argues
Davidson's headnote calls the work "the first attempt at a systematic grammar made in the Western World, and for many generations a text-book in the schools of the Roman Empire," and gives what Suidas says of Dionysios, a pupil of Aristarchos who taught in Rome in Pompey's time. On authorship he writes that the work's "genuineness and authenticity... have been impugned, but have been defended by Lersch, and are now generally admitted" (p. 3, 1874). The pamphlet is a numbered list of 25 definitions.

§1 defines grammar as "an experimental knowledge of the usages of language as generally current among poets and prose writers" in six parts, the first of which is "Trained reading with due regard to Prosody" (p. 3). Davidson's note: prosody "includes everything designated by diacritical marks — aspiration, accentuation, quantity, and sometimes pauses" (p. 3 n.). §2, "On Reading": "Reading is the rendering of poetic or prose productions without stumbling or hesitancy. It must be done with due regard to expression, prosody, and pauses. Through the expression we learn the merit of the piece; from the prosody, the art of the reader; and from the pauses, the meaning intended to be conveyed" (p. 4). It adds that tragedy is to be read heroically, comedy conversationally, elegiacs thrillingly and so on, and that reading that ignores the rules "degrades the merits of the poets."

§4 on punctuation names three marks and ties them to breath and sense: "the full stop denotes that the sense is complete; the semicolon is a sign of where to take breath; the comma shows that the sense is not yet complete" (p. 4); §5 adds that at the full stop "the pause is long, at the comma, very short" (p. 4). §7 gives the 24 letters, seven vowels and 17 consonants; §8 defines a syllable; §12: "A Word is the smallest part of an ordered sentence" (p. 8); §13: a sentence is a combination of words "making complete sense," and there are eight parts of speech (p. 8). Nothing in §§1–14 or the closing section mentions how words are written or divided on the page.

## Relation to the argument
Source for facet K on what an ancient school taught as "reading." Two points bear on rulings D1–D2 and premise P6. First, reading is the first part of grammar and is defined as delivery with attention to prosody and pauses, so phrasing was a trained skill of the reader. Second, the definition of punctuation puts the breath and the sense at the reader's, not the page's, discretion: stops are marks of "where to take breath." That fits the reading that readers of unspaced scripts supplied structure themselves (P1), and it adds to the evidence in `quintilian1920institutio` (1.8.1) that pausing and phrasing were learned. It does not say that separating words reduced any of this, nor that it did not.
