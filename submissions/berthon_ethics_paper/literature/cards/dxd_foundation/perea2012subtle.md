# Perea, M., & Gomez, P. (2012). Subtle increases in interletter spacing facilitate the encoding of words during normal reading. *PLoS ONE, 7*(10), e47568.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0047568 · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access published PDF, seven pages; PLoS ONE prints no page range, so locators are section names and PDF page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** within-subject eye-tracking experiment, 24 University of València students (mean age 20, native Spanish), 120 target sentences (40 per spacing condition; Times New Roman 14 pt, one line per sentence); interletter spacing 0.0 (default), +1.0 and +1.5 points, applied to all characters including spaces; target word high or low frequency · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (skilled reader and text) · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Earlier lexical-decision studies by Perea and colleagues found that single words are identified faster with a small spacing increase (about +1.2 points; "w a t e r" faster than "water"). Perea and Gomez ask whether this carries over to normal sentence reading. They offer two rival predictions: wider letters ease encoding (less crowding) or push neighbouring words further into the periphery and raise the number of fixations (Introduction, PDF pp. 1–2).

Results (Results, PDF pp. 3–5; Table 2–3):
- Average fixation durations after forward saccades shrank in order: 228 ms (default), 221 ms (+1.0), 216 ms (+1.5); F1(2,42) = 31.05, p < .001. First fixations on the target word: 227, 225 and 215 ms. 22 of 24 participants showed the advantage (Discussion, PDF p. 4).
- No spacing effect on gaze duration or total time on the target word, and no spacing-by-frequency interaction on first fixation (there was one on total time, significant only by items, p = .04, a reading cost for high-frequency words at +1.5), which the authors take as evidence of early encoding (Results; Discussion).
- Offsetting costs: more forward fixations and shorter saccades (7.58, 7.52, 7.27 characters), and landing positions closer to the word's start (2.1 against 1.8 and 1.7 on a five-area scale).
- Total sentence reading time: 2,119 ms (default), 2,068 ms (+1.0), 2,103 ms (+1.5). The +1.0 gain was significant by participants (F1(1,21) = 6.31, p = .02) and not by items (F2(1,117) = 2.32, p = .13). The authors write that they are "cautious in making strong inferences from the total reading data" because the effect is small, only two-thirds of participants showed it, and it missed significance by items (Discussion, PDF p. 5).

They conclude that "interletter spacing is a relevant parameter when processing text" and that e-book software should let readers adjust it (closing paragraph of the Discussion, PDF p. 6; the paper has no separate Conclusion heading). They note the benefit for dyslexic readers shown by Perea et al. (2012) in total reading time, which control children did not show (Discussion).

## Relation to the argument
Letter spacing, not word spacing, so the premises P1 to P5 are untouched. The paper matters to facet D as the positive case for subtle spacing among skilled adults: a real gain in fixation duration, cancelled in total reading time by extra fixations. That pattern shows the limit of reading "page-level" cost reductions from a single measure, the same caution that applies to ruling D6 (look for the move that does most of the work, with the cost measured end to end). It also shows that a spacing change helps at one stage and costs at another, which the argument should say if it ever lists spacing among the "interface" fixes. The authors note that their manipulation widened every character, including the word spaces, "so that between-word boundaries were well-defined" (Discussion); the finding does not transfer to cases where letter gaps approach word gaps, the case the authors discuss from Paterson and Jordan (2010), where large letter gaps without clear word boundaries hurt reading (Discussion, PDF p. 5).

Facet check: the facet's one-line summary, "Fixations briefest at +1.5 spacing in skilled readers," is correct and omits that total reading time did not follow.

## Caution
Twenty-four adults, Spanish, single-line sentences, one font (Times New Roman, narrowly spaced by default), a CRT screen. The authors warn the result may not carry to e-readers or other fonts. The sentence-level speed gain is small and not reliable by items, so the paper cannot be cited for faster reading, only for shorter fixations. The dyslexia point is reported from another study and was not tested here.
