# Price, C. J., & Devlin, J. T. (2011). The interactive account of ventral occipitotemporal contributions to reading. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 15*(6), 246–253.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.tics.2011.04.001 · **Read depth:** full_text (PMC author manuscript, PMC3223525, Europe PMC XML, including Boxes 1 to 4 and the Glossary; no journal page numbers, so locators are section and box names) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed theory article (the XML types it "Opinion") · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none (theoretical account that reviews fMRI, priming and lesion findings) · **Parties modeled:** other (a brain region mediating between visual input and language) · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Price and Devlin put forward the Interactive Account of the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (vOT). vOT, they argue, is not tuned to orthography. It combines bottom-up visuospatial input with top-down predictions from phonological and semantic areas, in a predictive-coding framework. In the Abstract: "specialization for orthography emerges from regional interactions without assuming that vOT is selectively tuned to orthographic features."

The main steps, with locators:
- Opening section: vOT responds to many non-orthographic stimuli and even to touch, so no single functional label fits all its responses.
- Section "The Interactive Account": activation reflects forward input plus backward prediction plus prediction error; fMRI cannot separate them. Without learned links between orthography and phonology, vOT activation to words is low because "phonological areas do not send backward predictions to vOT."
- Section on evidence for automatic top-down effects: a masked picture prime of the same object reduced vOT activation to a named word, though participants did not see the prime.
- Sections on selectivity: activation is higher for pseudowords than for words and than for consonant strings, and higher for low- than for high-frequency words. The authors explain these as prediction error and reject explanations from bigram frequency or familiarity.
- Box 1: activation is low in pre-literates, high in early learners, lower again in skilled readers as predictions improve; in developmental dyslexia vOT activation is low, which they attribute to a failure to establish top-down predictions.
- Box 2: damage to vOT disconnects forward and backward links and hurts reading more than object naming because words share repeating parts in similar combinations (attitude, altitude, aptitude).
- Box 3: vOT neurons are "general-purpose analyzers of visual forms," with no one-to-one mapping to letters or bigrams.
- Concluding remarks: the account "obviates the need to postulate a novel form of learning-related plasticity (e.g. 'neuronal recycling') [7] or reading-specific neuronal responses (e.g. 'bigram detectors') [8]."

## Relation to the argument
The paper bears on facet D's claim that the neuroscience of reading offers no clean evidence that a written convention reshaped a dedicated brain region. Price and Devlin reject a reading-specific region and recycling, and Kubota et al. (2024) reject a different part of the recycling story. The paper never discusses word spacing, unspaced script or reading acquisition in the historical sense; it says nothing on P1 to P6 or on rulings D1 to D10.

Facet check: the facet quotes the Abstract line on orthography emerging "from regional interactions", and the quotation is exact. The facet records the paper as read in part (abstract and opening); the full reading adds that the account is a framework argued from existing studies, not a new experiment, and that it explicitly sets itself against Dehaene's recycling and bigram-detector proposals. It also makes the point that task and attention reverse selectivity, which weakens any claim that vOT activation shows "word-specific" tuning.

## Caution
A 2011 theoretical position paper; later longitudinal work (see `kubota2024rethinking`) tests recycling directly and the account's own predictions about dyslexia rest on a small set of cited studies. The paper is about alphabetic orthographies in adults, and its Box 3 neuronal properties are extrapolated from monkey data because the authors know of no human single-cell data. The Europe PMC XML (CC BY 3.0, Elsevier Sponsored Documents) flags itself "pmc-prop-manuscript no", so it may be the publisher text rather than an author manuscript; no journal pagination is present, so page numbers could not be checked.
