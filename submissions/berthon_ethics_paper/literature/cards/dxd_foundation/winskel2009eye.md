# Winskel, H., Radach, R., & Luksaneeyanawin, S. (2009). Eye movements when reading spaced and unspaced Thai and English: A comparison of Thai–English bilinguals and English monolinguals. *Journal of Memory and Language, 61*(3), 339–351.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.jml.2009.07.002 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher-formatted PDF in the author copy kept locally, 13 printed pages, 339–351 with the journal's pagination; the Method and Results were read in full, and the reference list was scanned) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** eye-tracking (EyeLink II, 250 Hz) of single-line sentence reading, 2 (spacing) x 2 (target word frequency); 36 Thai–English bilinguals from Chulalongkorn University reading Thai and English (two sessions on separate days), 36 English monolinguals from the University of Western Sydney reading English; 72 sentences per language, 144 trials per list, Latin square · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and text) · **Facets:** D, E · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Winskel, Radach and Luksaneeyanawin run the first controlled eye-movement study of Thai, an alphabetic script written without spaces between words. They test whether spaces help eye guidance, word identification, both, or neither, in a script whose readers have never needed them (pp. 341–342). The spaces in the Thai sentences were inserted by two Thai linguists who agreed on every segmentation (p. 343).

Results, with the paper's locators:
- Thai sentence level (p. 344, Table 1, p. 344): spaced sentences took longer, 1,834 ms against 1,746 ms unspaced (F1(1,35) = 4.03, p = .05; minF' not significant, p = .13), with more fixations, 9.27 against 8.62. The authors report a "5% decrement in reading rate" when spaces were added and note that the spaced sentences were 12.8% longer on screen.
- Thai target words (p. 345): no spacing effect on first fixation duration; gaze duration and total fixation duration were shorter with spaces (gaze duration F1(1,35) = 18.41, p < .001). Reading times differed between people: 15 of 36 read spaced sentences significantly slower, 6 read them faster, 15 showed no difference (p. 345); the Discussion restates this as 42% hurt, 16% helped, 42% unaffected (p. 349).
- Thai landing positions: unchanged by spacing, "just left of center of the word (approximately 0.3 of a letter)" (p. 346). From this the authors conclude spaces help later word processing and not eye guidance or early segmentation (p. 348).
- English, bilinguals (p. 344): a 45% decrement in reading rate without spaces; sentence reading time 2,133 ms spaced against 3,887 ms unspaced; fixations 10.25 against 15.05.
- English, monolinguals (p. 344): a 33% decrement; 1,827 ms against 2,730 ms; fixations 9.00 against 11.66. The language-group-by-spacing interaction was reliable (F1(1,70) = 23.81, p < .001). Bilinguals' landing positions fell about 0.9 of a letter from mid-word without spaces against about 0.5 for monolinguals (p. 346).
- Transfer: the authors expected Thai readers' lifelong practice with unspaced text to help with unspaced English, and "the prediction ... was not supported" (p. 349). Skill in the language being read mattered more than practice with an unspaced script.

The authors cite the standard figure that removing spaces from English "typically slows reading by 30–50%" (p. 340), and they treat unspaced reading in Thai as fast enough to mediate saccade targeting (p. 349).

## Relation to the argument
P3 (removing boundaries slows practised readers by a third to a half) rests on this paper at p. 344 and it holds: 33% for monolinguals and 45% for bilinguals. P4 (readers fluent in unspaced scripts gain nothing from added spaces) is only partly right. The Thai readers gained at the word level (shorter gaze and total fixation times) but lost at the sentence level, and the paper's explanation for the sentence-level loss is mixed: extra fixations on newly visible words, and 12.8% longer lines. For D2 the paper supports the view that fluent readers of an unspaced script cope well. the authors judge that experienced Thai readers solve the problem of finding word boundaries without spaces far better in Thai than either group does in English (p. 349). For D6, the paper separates two jobs that spaces might do, guiding the eye and speeding word recognition, and finds only the second in Thai.

Facet check: the facet cites the paper for the cost of removing spaces. That is correct. The facet does not mention the Thai result, which is the more informative one for the unspaced-script debate: a 5% slowdown at sentence level with a word-level gain, and large differences between readers.

## In conversation with
- `rayner1998unspaced` — the design template (high- and low-frequency targets in one frame) and the English baseline; this study replicates its pattern.

- `sainio2007role` — Japanese hiragana, where spaces helped by about 12% against 30–50% in English; mixed Kanji–Hiragana text did not gain.

## Caution
Single-line sentences read for comprehension with a yes/no check on 10% to 15% of trials; Courier font; target words were nominals of five or six letters, and 13% of the Thai data, 8% of the bilinguals' English data and 13% of the monolinguals' data were dropped for short first fixations, skipped targets or tracking loss (p. 345). The bilinguals scored 39% on the English vocabulary test against the monolinguals' 90% (p. 342), so the group comparison mixes proficiency with script experience. The sentence-level Thai effect is at p = .05 by participants and not reliable in the combined test. The authors' own sentence-length confound limits any claim about global speed.

## Also used by facet W (2026-10-15)

Facet W uses the Thai result as the test case for "freed literacy by moving boundary detection from the voice to the page": readers who never had spaces detect boundaries silently, and when spaces are added they gain at the word (shorter gaze, p. 345) and lose at the sentence (5% slower, p. 344). Boundary detection without spaces is therefore eye-and-lexicon work, not voice work, in a fluent reader; the abstract's "from the voice" holds, if at all, for learners (`kasisopa2016child` shows Thai schooling spaces Grade 1 text and then withdraws the spaces). The English result (33% and 45% slower without spaces, p. 344) is the cost side of the representational effect.
