## What it argues

Parasuraman and Manzey review two literatures that had been kept apart, "automation complacency" in supervisory control and "automation bias" with decision aids, and argue they are "different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role" (p. 381). Complacency they define by three features: someone monitors an automated system, monitors less often than some standard, and a failure is missed as a result (p. 382).

On complacency, the foundation is Parasuraman, Molloy and Singh (1993). Operators who also ran two manual tasks detected automation failures 82% of the time when reliability varied and 33% when it stayed constant; single-task detection was about 97% (p. 384). The authors read this as "an active reallocation of attention," not a passive state (p. 384). The effect persists with a single failure (about half of multitask participants detected it; in a driving simulator half of drivers missed it and 14% failed to avoid a collision, p. 385), among pilots and air-traffic controllers (pp. 386-387), and after up to 60 minutes of practice (p. 387).

On automation bias, the review collects omission and commission errors. In one simulated engine fire, 75% of pilots followed an automated checklist that named the wrong engine, against 25% with a paper checklist (p. 392). In another study every pilot shut down an engine on a false warning, a 100% commission rate (p. 393). Radiologists' detection of unmarked cancers fell from 46% to 21% with a prompting aid (p. 394), and decision accuracy in a simulated process-control fault-diagnosis task fell from 89% unaided to 70% with an aid giving wrong diagnoses (p. 395). Training and instructions to verify did not prevent it, and "the presence of a second crewmember did not affect the strength of automation bias" (p. 397). Instructed accountability for overall performance or accuracy reduced omission and commission errors (n = 181, p. 396), though the authors call the result "not fully conclusive" because it may reflect higher motivation.

The integrated model (Fig. 6, p. 404) has "complacency potential" fed by system, person and task context; an attentional bias that causes a loss of situation awareness; omission or commission errors only when the automation fails; and two feedback loops, a positive one ("learned carelessness") that raises complacency when nothing goes wrong, and a negative one after failures (p. 405). They answer Moray's objection that low monitoring can be rational: in AutoCAMS operators sampled less than a normative model required (pp. 399-402). Their design suggestions are modest: aid information analysis rather than recommend decisions, raise accountability, allocate functions flexibly (p. 406).

## Relation to the argument

Supports premise P12 (people perform sustained monitoring badly) and the claim in the post's P19 that a person holding a halt right is the weak link. It also bears on P9, the argument map's claim that aids handing a person something to read give weaker effects: the review reports evidence that aids supporting only information analysis lead to less commission-error bias than aids that recommend actions (p. 396), though the authors call this preliminary (p. 406). It says nothing about triads, platforms or the affected, and every operator in it is the operator of the automation.

Facet check: facet G says the studies are "lab and simulator work with one operator and one automation." Mostly, but the review also draws on accident reports (Royal Majesty, pp. 382-383), field trials of physicians (p. 394) and two team studies (p. 397). Facet G also says the brake-holder in P19 "is the person these studies describe." The review is narrower. Complacency is "typically found under conditions of multiple-task load" (p. 387; also the Abstract, p. 381). When participants only backed up the automation, detection was "near perfect" (p. 384), and in the single-failure study most single-task participants detected it (p. 385). The brake-holder who has no competing task is the weaker case for complacency. Against that, the authors expect worse monitoring at the very low failure rates of real systems, citing an estimate of one critical signal every two weeks (p. 385), so a dedicated watcher does not escape the problem. Automation bias, which needs no multitask load, is closer to the P15 reader.

## In conversation with

- `bainbridge1983ironies`: the first work cited, among the foundations of research on human interaction with automation (p. 381).
- `parasuraman2000types`: levels of automation; the review cites it for the finding that level and type of processing change costs (p. 406).
- `dekker2002maba`: Dekker and Woods call complacency and situation awareness "folk models"; the authors rebut this (p. 383). That is the same Dekker and Woods the argument map flags as unread.
- Leads, not in the bib: Mosier and Skitka (1996) on automation bias; Lee and See (2004) on trust, cited as a review that found no convincing link between poor monitoring and high trust (p. 389); Bahner, Hüper and Manzey (2008) on complacency and training experience (pp. 400-402); Moray and Inagaki (2000) on monitoring as a rational strategy (p. 398).

## Caution

The evidence is experiments on small samples in simulated tasks, with many students, and the authors call parts of it thin: evidence on the first-failure effect is "equivocal" (p. 386), the personality evidence is "very small" (p. 390), and they concede Moray's point that complacency should be inferred from monitoring behaviour, not missed failures alone (p. 399). Fatigued operators in one study were more careful than alert ones (p. 404), which cuts against a model in which fatigue only worsens review. Published 2010; nothing on language-model agents, logs, or sustained audit of recorded actions.
