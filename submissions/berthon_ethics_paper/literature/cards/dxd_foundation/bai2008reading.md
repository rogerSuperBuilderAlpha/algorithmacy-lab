## What it argues

The paper asks two questions: does inserting spaces between words in unspaced Chinese help skilled readers, and is the word or the character the primary reading unit (Introduction). Exp. 1 crosses four layouts: normal unspaced text, spaces between every character, spaces between words, and spaces that group characters into nonwords. A prescreen of 12 non-participating Chinese readers agreed on word boundaries 95% of the time (Exp. 1, Method). Exp. 2 repeats the design with grey highlighting in place of spaces, so the text keeps one spatial layout across conditions.

Results, Exp. 1 (Table 1, means): total sentence reading time was 5,221 ms for normal text and 5,239 ms for word-spaced text, against 5,686 ms for character-spaced and 5,867 ms for nonword-spaced text; normal and word-spaced did not differ (p > .05), and nonword spacing was reliably slower than both (p < .01). Mean fixation duration fell from 246 ms (normal) to 227 ms (word-spaced), and fixations rose from 17.7 to 19.3 per sentence, so word-spaced readers made "more but shorter fixations" (Exp. 1, local analyses). In the local analyses, gaze duration on two-character words was 297 ms unspaced and 272 ms word-spaced; total fixation time was 440 ms and 379 ms.

Exp. 2 (Table 2): total sentence time was 3,745 ms normal, 3,808 ms word-highlighted, 4,006 ms character-highlighted and 4,083 ms nonword-highlighted. Normal and word conditions did not differ; both were easier than the other two.

The authors' summary: inserting spaces or highlighting at word boundaries "did not facilitate reading Chinese, at least beyond the level observed for normal unspaced text," and "demarcating word boundaries ... did not interfere with reading" (General Discussion). They explain the tie as two opposed factors: unspaced text is familiar but hinders word identification, spaced text is unfamiliar but eases it (Exp. 1, Discussion). Their conclusion on the second question is that words, not characters, are the primary unit.

Their review of earlier work (Introduction) matters for the paper's evidence base. They report that Kohsom and Gobet (1997) found spaced Thai faster to read, that Sainio et al. (2007) found spacing helped syllabic Hiragana but not mixed Kanji–Hiragana text, whose Kanji already segment the line, and that Inhoff et al. (1997) found no effect in Chinese, which the authors attribute to a coarse 50 Hz tracker and small spaces. They add that without spaces English reading speed "typically decreases by up to 50%."

## Relation to the argument

Premise P4: skilled readers of an unspaced script gain nothing from added spaces. This is the card's main use, and the full text confirms the facet's Table 1 reading. The pair of results also bears on P3: familiarity cost and segmentation benefit cancel in practiced readers, so a null total time does not mean spaces do nothing. The fixation pattern (more, shorter fixations; shorter gaze on words) shows the spaces did change processing. The Kanji point from Sainio et al. supports the paper's refinement that spaces help most where the script gives readers no other boundary cue.

Facet check: facet D marks this source "read in part." The full text adds no contradiction. It does add two things the facet might want: the null result is on N = 16 and N = 24 skilled adults only, and the "words are the unit" claim rests on character-spacing and nonword-spacing costs. The source says nothing about learners, so it cannot carry the learner half of P4.

## Caution

Skilled adult readers only; nothing here speaks to children, learners or second-language readers. Sentences were short and presented one at a time. The spacing conditions are confounded with line length in Exp. 1, which Exp. 2 answers with highlighting, itself an unfamiliar format. Local-analysis effects are often reliable by participants but not by items. The manuscript's reading-speed row in Table 1 is garbled in the text I had, so the card cites reading times instead.
