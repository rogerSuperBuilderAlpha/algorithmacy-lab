# *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976).

## What it argues

George Eldridge, a disability beneficiary since June 1968, got a questionnaire from a state agency in March 1972, answered it, and received a letter saying the agency had tentatively found his disability ended in May 1972. He replied in writing, the agency finalised the finding, and benefits ended after July. He sued rather than seek reconsideration, claiming a right to an evidentiary hearing before termination, as in *Goldberg v. Kelly* (Part I). The Court first holds the district court had jurisdiction under 42 U.S.C. §405(g), because the presentation of a claim is the non-waivable element and exhaustion is waivable here (Part II; syllabus pp. 326–332).

On the merits the Court holds that "an evidentiary hearing is not required prior to the termination of disability benefits and that the present administrative procedures fully comport with due process" (end of Part III). It first states the principle: "The fundamental requirement of due process is the opportunity to be heard 'at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner'" (Part III.A, quoting *Armstrong v. Manzo*), and that due process "is flexible and calls for such procedural protections as the particular situation demands" (Part III.A, quoting *Morrissey*). It then sets out the three factors: "First, the private interest that will be affected by the official action; second, the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards; and finally, the Government's interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens . . ." (Part III.A; syllabus pp. 332–335).

Applied: the private interest is weaker than the welfare recipient's in *Goldberg* because disability benefits are "not based upon financial need" and other aid exists (Part III.C), though the Court concedes the delay from cutoff to a decision after a hearing "exceeds one year" (10 to 11 months for the hearing alone). The risk of error is lower because the inquiry is "a more sharply focused and easily documented decision" resting on "routine, standard, and unbiased medical reports" (Part III.D). Written submissions suit the case: the recipient gets a detailed questionnaire, a summary of the evidence, the agency's reasons and a chance to "mold" his argument. The Court addresses reversal rates, which ran "from a high of 58.6% for appealed reconsideration decisions to an overall reversal rate of only 3.3%," and says "Bare statistics rarely provide a satisfactory measure" because the system is open-file and fresh examinations follow new evidence (Part III.D). The government's interest is the cost of hearings on demand (Part III.E). The Court warns against "wholesale transplantation of the rules of procedure, trial and review" from courts to agencies, and says "the judicial model of an evidentiary hearing is neither a required, nor even the most effective, method of decisionmaking in all circumstances" (Part III.E; syllabus pp. 347–349). Brennan, joined by Marshall, would have required the *Goldberg* hearing.

## Relation to the argument

For ruling D8 this is the court-side statement of what procedure a person owes to a party whose benefit a third party can end: notice of the tentative decision and its reasons, access to the evidence, a chance to respond, and a later hearing, scaled to the interest, the risk of error and cost. That is the notice-reasons-redress list the J-facet uses as a yardstick (Q2). Two lessons cut in different directions. First, it supports comparing platforms with a standard: the Court treats written notice and reasons before the cutoff as enough where error risk is low, which is stronger than what platforms commonly give. Second, it complicates any claim that a court model is the benchmark: the Court explicitly refuses to impose the judicial model on administrative decisions and weighs the decider's costs. It governs only governmental action, so it applies to platforms by analogy, not by law.

## Caution

A 1976 ruling on one federal benefit program. It decides pre-termination procedure when later review and back pay exist; it does not address private actors, automated decisions or opaque rules. The three-factor test is a balancing test whose outcomes vary. Page-level pin cites need the U.S. Reports before print.
