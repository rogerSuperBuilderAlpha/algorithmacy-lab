# Barton, D., & Hamilton, M. E. (1980). *Awareness of the segmental structure of English in adults of various literacy levels* (ERIC Document ED222858). Stanford University, Department of Linguistics; National Institute of Education grant NIE-G-80-0040.

## What it argues

The premise is that "Speech consists of a continuous stream of sound" and that "our writing system provides an explicit segmentation of the language into word length units" (§1.1, p. 1). The question is which parts of segmental awareness depend on literacy. Mean errors were 13.40 (basic), 10.35 (medium) and 7.40 (high), p < .001 (Table 1). The components that separated the groups were counting the words in short phrases ("number of words": 52.5%, 41.3%, 17.5% errors, p < .001), identifying a specified word in a sentence (32.0%, 12.0%, 16.0%, p < .001), segmentation errors when saying a sentence one word at a time (22.0%, 19.3%, 12.0%, p < .005), breaking words into syllables (30.0%, 18.0%, 8.0%, p < .025) and forgetting part of a sentence (Table 2). Slowing down, counting words in a sentence and giving a first sound did not differ by group (Table 2). Of 250 segmentation errors, 80% were either "errors of convention" (*myself*, *everything*, *into*, *around*, *enough* given as two words; 14, 14, 12, 10, 12 basic-group subjects against 7, 6, 7, 2, 0 in the high group, Table 3) or failures to analyse a unitary phrase (*more or less*, *rock and roll*). The authors' reading: "our subjects are grappling with the same problems of segmentation which linguists typically find difficult" (§5); literates treated contractions as one word and syllabified *family* and *different* "as written" more often (Tables 4–5); and "It is not that literate adults have a different way of segmenting, but rather they have an additional way of segmenting ... Literacy gives an additional way of operating on language" (§5, pp. 14–15; the p. 15 number is illegible in the scan). Following Cole (1980) they hold that "literacy gives very specific skills ... rather than a general change in people's mode of thinking" (§5, p. 15), and that awareness and reading "interact" (§5, p. 15).

## Relation to the argument

The abstract says an unspaced reader sounded out the line "to hear where each word ended". That presumes the word is a unit a hearer finds in speech. Barton and Hamilton's adults, all speakers of English, disagreed with the dictionary about where words end exactly where English writing settles the matter by convention, and the least literate disagreed most. The written word boundary is in part what literacy teaches.

- **Cuts against** treating "oracy" as the source of word boundaries: speech gave these adults units, but not the units writing marks. For a Latin learner the gap would be wider, not narrower.
- **Supports** the "representational effect" half of the sentence more than the "voice" half: the space makes the word an object one can count and point to, which is what the high-literacy group could do. This is Olson's claim that writing is a model for speech, tested on adults (Olson 1996 and Homer & Olson 1999 are abstract-only leads in the facet file).
- **For ruling D10** (guide, not mechanism): the authors' own conclusion is specific skills, not a restructured mind, which is the reading the project already takes of Scribner and Cole.

## Caution

An unpublished 1980 report on 60 adults, with OCR errors in the ERIC scan; the "non-literates" could read to grade 3.9; statistics are group error rates without effect sizes. The tasks are metalinguistic judgements in English, not reading, and say nothing about Latin or manuscripts. Use it for the general point that word segmentation of speech is partly a literate convention, not for any number.
