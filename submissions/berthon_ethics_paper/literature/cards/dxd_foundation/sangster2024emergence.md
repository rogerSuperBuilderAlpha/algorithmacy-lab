## What it argues
Sangster asks why double entry bookkeeping emerged, a question the article opens by calling unanswered: "There is still not a satisfactory explanation for the emergence of double-entry bookkeeping in Italy in the thirteenth century" (p. 500, quoting Dobie and Oldroyd 2020). He blames twentieth-century historians who defined double entry by its outputs (profit-and-loss accounts, balance sheets) and so recognised almost no medieval example, the earliest being a Genoese government ledger of 1340 (pp. 500–501). Yamey's repeated conclusion, which they accepted, was that double entry became useful only with nineteenth-century joint-stock companies (p. 500).

He instead adopts Lane's input definition: "any accounts with duality of entry" count as "an elementary form of double entry" (p. 501). Every entry then needs an amount, a debit or credit sign, and the identity of the contra account (p. 501). Sangster reads the 1211 entries against that test (pp. 504–507, Figures 1–3). He finds it met, and he withdraws his 2016 label "dual entry" for one pair of entries after correcting a mistranslated semicolon ("posted to debit above", p. 506). The same entries resemble the ledgers of 1296 and 1299, which differ mainly by page numbers and a separate money column (p. 507).

His causal account runs from coins to credit to evidence. Coinage was scarce, of variable quality and costly to assay (an £8 payment meant more than 1,920 silver denari checked one by one, p. 508). Moneychangers took deposits and paid by "book transfer" between customers' accounts (p. 509). Fairs grew and trade moved among strangers, so default and dispute rose (p. 511). Roman and Lombard law fitted commerce poorly, and courts of customary law presided over by merchants took over (pp. 511–514). A banker who made a book transfer needed the contra entry to prove it, so duality followed from the evidential demand (p. 518). The ledger also served as "an aide-mémoire as well as a document of record" (p. 513). Custom came first and law followed: "commercial law was derived from banking and mercantile customary practice, not the other way round" (p. 517). A Bologna statute of 1245 compelled book transfers at the fair, and Sangster reads it as a response to clearing the bankers had already invented (p. 517). Genoa's 1327 legislation prescribed accounts kept by "the method of the bankers" (p. 519). Sangster dates the method to the twelfth century, with bankers evolving it "for at least 47 years" by 1211, an inference from Pisa's court of customary law in 1164 (p. 520). Figure 4 (p. 523) draws the causal chain.

## Relation to the argument
Facet F (parallel moves) uses it as the Pacioli-and-double-entry case: a convention with a long, collective, practice-first origin, not one inventor. The text supports that, though not in the form the facet states (see Facet check).

For the word-separation precedent, the useful finding is the order of events: practice, then recognition by a body that adjudicates, then codification. That order bears on ruling D8 (the holder of the convention is unknown; compare courts and forex markets; look for where the convention bends) and on the open question in P10. The convention here was held by the interested party, since the bankers' method protected creditors and the banker's own risk (pp. 516, 522). It still became binding because courts of customary law, staffed by merchants who "did understand the needs of business" (p. 513), accepted it as evidence. That cuts against the premise as the map words it (a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention): the bankers could not withdraw it, because the courts and the guild-licensed books (p. 517) held it. That is my reading; Sangster does not state it. It also fits P7 poorly, since the bankers' ledger records what already happened between two parties and gives each of them no help inferring what the intermediary will do.

One passage touches reading directly. Sangster reports Santini's view that entries in the spoken language, not Latin, meant "all merchants could read, understand, and thus genuinely approve the entries made in their names" (p. 519), and he files this under "pragmatic literacy" (n. 134). Sangster offers it as a hypothesis, not a result. It is the only line here that resembles the spacing story of a convention lowering a reading cost for the parties, and the paper does not test it. Ruling D10 (history as a guide, not mechanism) and ruling D1 (review the causal analysis, do not assert it) both apply: this is a causal argument by context, with no counterfactual.

**Facet check:** The facet had only the abstract. Reading the whole text, I find the abstract-level claims intact, with five qualifications. (1) The facet says the 1211 ledger "has" double entries and so double entries are "nearly 280 years older" than Pacioli (1494 minus 1211 is 283). That holds only under Sangster's input definition; under the output definition earlier historians used, the earliest case is 1340 (p. 501). Contra entries almost never survive, and double entry is confirmed mainly from cross-references and offset (p. 506). (2) The facet says Sangster takes apart the "Pacioli" one-agent story. The text never discusses Pacioli's *Summa*; the name appears only in the title of a cited article (n. 48, Ciambotti 2016). Sangster targets the Yamey–Besta–Sombart definitional tradition, not Pacioli. The Pacioli comparison is the facet's, so the table row and the "Pacioli introduced double entry" item (F, lines 291 and 324) should not attribute it to Sangster. (3) He dates the origin to the twelfth century, earlier than 1211; the ledger is evidence of a method already running. (4) On "double entry produced capitalism," Sangster does not rebut it. He reports Yamey's view and cites Yamey 1949, p. 103, for the claim that credit dealings led to systematic accounting (n. 154, p. 523). (5) The facet gives the sentence "the reason for such a detailed bookkeeping method is unknown" in quotation marks; the abstract reads "The reason for the emergence of such a detailed bookkeeping method is unknown." No contradiction of substance.

## Caution
A single-case historical argument built on four surviving pages, about 160 entries, and an inferred chain of causes. Sangster states that the contra account "if it existed, has not survived" in virtually all cases (p. 506), and the dating of the twelfth-century origin rests on inference from Pisan custom, not on a surviving twelfth-century ledger. The finding that bankers' ledgers were accepted as evidence in court rests on secondary sources (Lattes, Santini, Chiaudano) and on a negative: no notarised account book is known among more than 3,000 Florentine books, based on the author's decade in the archives and the recall of colleagues (n. 118, p. 517). The Innes credit theory of money (pp. 521–522) and the trust argument are interpretive. Citable as a case of practice preceding codification, and as the claim that the method's detail answered evidential demands. Not citable for the origin of double entry as a settled fact, for Pacioli, or for the spacing history. No difference was found between the open-access version and the version of record, since this is the version of record.
