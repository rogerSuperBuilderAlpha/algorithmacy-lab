## What it argues

Da Costa follows how English printers presented and described tables of contents and indices, using their prefaces and headings as evidence. She starts from Parkes: "the late medieval book differs more from its early medieval predecessors than it does from the printed books of our own day" (p. 1). The finding aids began in thirteenth-century scholarly manuscripts and were "initially directed at a scholarly readership," though they also reached vernacular manuscripts (pp. 3–4).

Caxton was "innovative in exploring their potential for appealing to English readers" (p. 5). His tables were simple lists of chapter headings in book order. They "did have the advantage of not requiring any instruction in how to use them" (p. 8) but located material only roughly because few folios were numbered. Fuller aids, with alphabetical order, folio numbers or signatures, needed explanation and labour. Only 33 of the 225 editions with a table or index used alphabetical order (p. 13, n. 24). Printers who tried signature references (the St Albans printer in 1485, de Worde, Berthelet in 1528) had to teach readers to use them, and Berthelet gave his up after one further edition; her reading is that "printers readily perceived that it was easier to use folio numbers" (pp. 10–12). By the 1510s printers increasingly expected readers to know tables and advertised them as ways to find material "lightly" or quickly (p. 17). Treveris built tables into three medical works for less learned buyers such as barbers and young surgeons (pp. 19–21).

The second half turns to control. Tables "could be used polemically as well as to market books, controlling how large numbers of readers read rather than simply guiding them to material they desired" (p. 22). The 1535 Marsilius translation, More's *Dialogue Concerning Heresies* (1530) and the "principall notes" of Tyndale and Barnes tell the reader what to conclude, and the evangelical writers claim to free the reader while the tables steer (pp. 22–29). Her last sentence: "we also take it for granted that tables of content and indices are neutral additions to texts, rather than highly crafted and potentially polemical works" (p. 30).

## Relation to the argument

This is facet F's case 3 (finding devices), and the one source in the batch where the aid's maker is an interested party.

**P6 and D6.** The devices grow over three centuries, in several places, in competing orders, with no single inventor. That fits the retreat from "the" move to "a complex of conventions."

**P10.** A finding aid can serve its maker's interest in steering the reading. That supports the claim that who owns the aid matters. It does not show the owner can withdraw it alone; her cases are single printings.

**Who the aid served.** Facet F says the devices served specialists first and credits none with widening the base of readers. The article agrees that the first audience was scholarly (p. 4). It adds that some printers aimed tables at less learned buyers, and that tables may have served "mixed literacy groups" and listeners as well as private readers (p. 19). It gives no data on whether the push worked, so it cannot support "ignited" in either direction.

Facet check: facet F rests on the published abstract, which this manuscript lacks, so I could not match its phrase "marketed these features as time-saving innovations." The body does show printers selling tables as quick finding aids (p. 17). It adds what the abstract lacks: the 11% and 33-of-225 figures, and printers from 1485 to 1528 explaining reference schemes to readers at length (pp. 10–12).

## Caution

An accepted manuscript, so pagination differs from the journal. Corpus coverage is rough: EEBO holds about 88% of Caxton's ESTC entries and about 98% of de Worde's (p. 2; she calls the percentages rough, n. 5). Her evidence is what printers said about tables, not what readers did with them, and she shows no change in the number of readers. The chronology begins in 1476 and does not reach the word-separation period.
