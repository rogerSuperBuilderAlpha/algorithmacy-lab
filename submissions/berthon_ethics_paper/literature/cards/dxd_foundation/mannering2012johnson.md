## What it argues
Mannering reads Johnson's book as "an illuminating series of case studies which form a sociological account of the structure and dynamics of literary and scholarly culture in the Roman empire." He walks it chapter by chapter.

On chapter 1, Johnson moves "beyond speculation on the neurophysiology of reading, the cognitive act having been settled by scholarly consensus as essentially the same for the ancients as for us today," and takes his terms ("reading," "reading events," "reading culture") from anthropology and sociolinguistics (pp. 11–12 of the book, as Mannering cites them).

On chapter 2, the part that bears on word separation: "The considerable effort in apprehending scriptio continua with only minimal punctuation is interpreted by Johnson as a hallmark of the exclusivity of literary culture as social institution." The chapter draws on Quintilian (book pp. 26–31) for the mechanics of reading, including mastery of vowels and memorized syllables, and Mannering notes "the years of training required" for the bookroll. The layout of the roll "instantiates what it means to be educated in the high Roman empire."

Chapters 3 to 9 move through Pliny, Tacitus, Galen, Gellius, Fronto, Lucian and the Oxyrhynchus papyri. Mannering's one disagreement is a misreading of Pliny, *Ep.* 3.1, where "challenging" should be deleted from a table entry on p. 38. Of the writing he says the claims "can lose their position of priority amid the elaborateness of his writing style." Two items stand out for this paper. Galen "invites a careful, possibly novice reader" (chapter 5), and Gellius's community "makes no accommodation for novices" (chapter 6). The conclusion calls the book a reimagining of the ancient scholar as part of "a collective, at times performative endeavor, one also by definition elitist and exclusive."

## Relation to the argument
Mannering gives the same reading of *scriptio continua* as Ker: a status marker, with training as the price of entry. That matters in three places.

- Ruling D2 (review the debate): Mannering supplies the second reception of Johnson's sociological view. He states as settled that the cognitive act is the same for ancients and moderns, a claim the paper should not repeat as fact, since it is Johnson's premise as the reviewer reports it and Saenger and Heilmann dispute it from opposite sides.
- P1 and P6: the "years of training" and "novice" lines are the closest this batch comes to evidence that readers of unspaced script were a trained, selected group. They cut against any version of the argument in which spacing is what made reading widely teachable, because on Johnson's account the Roman elite kept the page hard to keep readers few.
- Ruling D6 and P10: the review reports a case where the convention serves its holders, a useful contrast with the Insular scribes who spaced for learners.

The bib lists the 2012 paperback date; the reviewed work first appeared in 2010.
