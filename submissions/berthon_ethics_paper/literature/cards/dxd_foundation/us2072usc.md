# 28 U.S.C. § 2072 — Rules of procedure and evidence; power to prescribe.

**Identifier:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2072 · **Read depth:** full_text (Cornell LII reproduction of the current Code text, with editorial notes; whole section read) · **Source-tier:** statute · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none · **Parties modeled:** other (the Supreme Court as rule-maker for lower federal courts and the litigants who live under the rules) · **Facets:** J · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
The section, three subsections long, gives the rule-writing power over federal court procedure to the Supreme Court and fences it. (a): "The Supreme Court shall have the power to prescribe general rules of practice and procedure and rules of evidence for cases in the United States district courts (including proceedings before magistrate judges thereof) and courts of appeals." (b): "Such rules shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right. All laws in conflict with such rules shall be of no further force or effect after such rules have taken effect." (c): the rules "may define when a ruling of a district court is final for the purposes of appeal under section 1291."

The editorial notes show the history: the section was added by Pub. L. 100–702 (19 November 1988), amended by Pub. L. 101–650 (1 December 1990) to add subsection (c), and replaces an earlier § 2072 (1948) that covered only civil procedure and was repealed with effect from 1 December 1988. The notes also record that the Supreme Court's admiralty rules (1920) were rescinded in 1966 when civil and admiralty procedure were unified.

## Relation to the argument
This is the textual anchor for the courts leg of facet J and the author's ruling D8. Procedure in federal courts is a written convention with a named author (the Supreme Court, under a delegation from Congress), and the statute itself limits what that convention may do: it may govern how rights are enforced but not change the rights. For the paper's question about who holds the convention, it gives a clear model of a holder bound by a higher text, which a platform's terms of service lack (see `suzor2018digital`). Subsection (b) is also the court-side version of the distinction between procedure and substance that the paper needs for the claim that conventions about process can change while the underlying entitlement stays.

It does not show how the Court actually writes rules; that is § 2073 (`us2073usc`) and the committee process. This section alone leaves the reader with a Court that writes rules by itself.
