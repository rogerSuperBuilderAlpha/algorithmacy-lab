# Ziegler, J. C., Bertrand, D., Tóth, D., Csépe, V., Reis, A., Faísca, L., Saine, N., Lyytinen, H., Vaessen, A., & Blomert, L. (2010). Orthographic depth and its impact on universal predictors of reading: A cross-language investigation. *Psychological Science, 21*(4), 551–559.

## What it argues

English is the outlier among alphabetic orthographies, and some researchers fear English-based work overstates the role of phonological awareness (p. 551–552, citing Share 2008 and Landerl & Wimmer 2000). The authors rank five languages by onset entropy, an index of how unpredictable letter-to-sound mappings are: Finnish 0.0, Hungarian 0.17, Dutch 0.23, Portuguese 0.42, French 0.46, with English 0.83 for comparison (Table 1, p. 552). They test three claims: phonological awareness is the main factor in each language (strong), it matters everywhere but with varying impact (weak), or it matters equally everywhere (p. 553).

Results. In the per-country regressions, phonological awareness was the strongest correlate of reading speed and accuracy in every country but Finland (standardized betas for reading speed from 0.31 in Finland to 0.54 in Portugal; Table 2, p. 555), and the strongest correlate of decoding in all five. Rapid automatized naming (RAN) was a weaker correlate, limited to speed. Nonverbal IQ had no significant influence. In the multilevel model, entropy raised the weight of phonological awareness for reading speed (γ = 0.445, p ≤ .05), reading accuracy (0.689, p ≤ .05) and decoding speed (0.687, p ≤ .001; Table 3, p. 556). The authors conclude that the data "strongly support the weak claim," that phonological awareness "is important in all languages but that its impact is modulated by script entropy" (p. 556). A vocabulary effect that looked stronger in transparent scripts vanished once Finland was dropped (p. 555–556). The authors read the entropy effect as reciprocal: a transparent script "promotes access to phonemes," which in turn raises phonological awareness (p. 556).

The introduction restates Seymour et al. (2003): at the end of Grade 1, reading accuracy reached ceiling in Italian, German, Greek, Spanish and Finnish; it was around 80% in Portuguese, French and Danish and "only 34%" in English (p. 551–552).

## Relation to the argument

Facet C uses this paper, with Seymour et al., as modern cross-script evidence for premise P6 (a script's design changes the rate at which children learn). The full text keeps that role but narrows it. The paper measures which cognitive skills predict reading in children already in school, not how fast a script is learned; the rate claim belongs to Seymour et al. as reported here. What the paper adds is the shape of the dependence: how much a skill matters varies with the script, and the better the script maps onto sounds, the less a child needs the separate skill of manipulating sounds. That supports the paper's guide-not-mechanism ruling (D10) in a weak form: the interface changes what the reader must bring to it. It says nothing about spaces between words or about who gets to school (D1, D2).

Facet check: the facet says the paper has "the same bearing as Seymour." It does not. It reports predictors of reading in Grade 2, not acquisition rates, and the study itself restricts its claim to alphabetic scripts and flags Chinese as unexplored (p. 557).

## Caution

Correlational, one time point, Grade 2 only; the authors say the age may favor finding strong phonological-awareness effects. Tests differ by country, so only standardized weights compare across countries; five languages is a small Level 2 sample, and the entropy effect is clearest for decoding speed (p ≤ .001) while the reading effects are p ≤ .05. Finland departs from the pattern on several measures. Alphabetic scripts only. I did not read the Supplemental Material.
