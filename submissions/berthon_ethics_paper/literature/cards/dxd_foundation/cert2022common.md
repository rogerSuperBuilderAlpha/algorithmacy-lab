## What it argues

The guide offers 22 "best practices" for an insider risk management program (IRMP). It defines an insider threat as the potential for a person with current or former authorised access to critical assets to use that access, "either maliciously or unintentionally", to harm the organisation (p. 3), and it says insider threats come from "a combination of technical, behavioral, and organizational factors" (p. iv). Its primary scenarios are IP theft, IT sabotage, fraud, misuse of authorised access, unintentional incidents, espionage and workplace violence (p. 3). It also concedes limits: "not all insiders can be stopped", so the organisation must "sustain critical operations during and recover after" an event (p. 6).

The parts that bear on this project run as follows.

*Privileged users and the audit trail (Best Practice 11).* Privileged users can conceal their actions by logging in as other users, modifying system logs and falsifying "audit logs and monitoring reports" (p. 78). The remedies are non-repudiation (each action attributable to one person), having several privileged users build the security design, regular review of privileged activity by the information-security team, and two-person release rules for production changes (pp. 78–79). A small firm with one administrator should take the audit function away from that person: "any method used must assure that the system administrator has no control over the auditing function" (p. 79). Best Practice 17 adds that change logs and backups must be protected, since insiders "modified change logs to conceal their activity or implicate someone else" (p. 115), and that roles in change management be split "so that no one person can make a change unnoticed by others" (p. 115).

*Monitoring (Best Practice 12).* "Simply logging all network activity is not sufficient"; the data volume "drives the need for tools that can fuse data", and monitoring should use "a risk-based approach" focused first on critical assets (p. 83). A SIEM (security information and event management) system gives a "centralized view" and filters "background noise", and with a rich data set it lets an analyst analyse past data "even when an alert was not generated", which matters most for people leaving the organisation (pp. 83–84). The guide recommends review of at least the previous 90 days when a person resigns (p. 84). Table 4 lists 25 technical and 15 non-technical data sources (pp. 84–87) and says the data sources "are not comprehensive enough to completely prevent or detect all insider threats in all organizations" (p. 87). Challenges are false positives, baselines at several levels, and getting enough context to analysts before alerts reach them (p. 88). Large organisations should have someone "hunt for critical incidents" below the top-priority alerts, which may take several staff monitoring workforce activity full time (p. 89). The case "An Ugly Picture" shows three alerts that no one correlated (p. 88).

*Baselines (Best Practice 14).* A baseline of normal behaviour is the reference for anomaly detection, but "baselining current activities might incorporate ongoing and unrecognized malicious activity", and a single all-encompassing baseline "can conceal concerning behavior" (pp. 99–100).

*Separation of duties and least privilege (Best Practice 15).* Separation of duties means dividing functions among several people so that no one can steal, defraud or sabotage without the others; the guide holds that people are "less likely to engage in malicious acts" if that means collaborating with someone else (p. 103). Least privilege means authorising people to use only the resources their jobs need, and it must be managed as moves and promotions occur (pp. 103–104). The guide adds that separation of duties is easily circumvented "if it is enforced by policy rather than by technical controls" (p. 104). Backup and restore, source-code release and auditing are the examples (pp. 103, 105; Best Practice 18, same logic). Every account must "be attributable to an individual" (Best Practice 10, p. 74). The guide maps these to NIST SP 800-53 controls AC-5, AC-6, AU-2, AU-6 and AU-9 (pp. 81, 89, 106).

*The human side.* Best Practices 5, 8 and 21 treat insider risk as partly a product of how the organisation treats people: inconsistent enforcement breeds disgruntlement (p. 45), rushing in a high-stress environment causes mistakes (p. 61), and "positive incentives" (job engagement, perceived organisational support, connectedness) should sit beside "negative incentives that attempt to force compliance through constraints, monitoring, and punishment" (pp. 136–138). The aim is to move an IRMP "from a 'big brother' program to a 'good employer' program" (p. 138).

## Relation to the argument

Facet G cites this guide for insider-threat controls behind the argument map's P11 (the structure of Nadella's insider post) and P12 (people monitor badly). It supports the post's pairing of least privilege, separation of duties and logging with a human reader, and it is useful on four counts.

It names the tampering problem that the post's "tamper-proof evidence" (its principle 15) answers. The people with the most access are the people best placed to falsify the record (p. 78), so the guide separates the auditor from the audited (p. 79). This is the closest security-practice analogue to the argument's claim that the party under oversight should not hold the controls, and it is stated for human administrators, not models.

It shows the human review burden as an explicit part of the control design, and with more nuance than the facet gives. The guide does not ask for review of everything; it asks for risk-based monitoring around critical assets, correlation tools and baselines (p. 83). It also asks for after-the-fact review of the previous 90 days for leavers (p. 84) and for staff who "hunt" past the highest-priority alerts, in some settings full time (p. 89). That bears on the gap that the argument map leaves under P12 (reconstruction by trained auditors): the guide describes exactly such work as normal practice, but gives no evidence on how well people do it.

It cuts against a strong reading of "observe everything". The guide says full logging alone does not work (p. 83) and lists its own cost, privacy and false-positive limits (p. 88). It also counts "unintentional" insiders in its definition (p. 3), which fits the project's treatment of models as risks without assuming malice.

The victim in the definition is "the organization" (p. 3), and the program's stakeholders are management, HR, legal, security, IT, data owners and engineers (p. iv). Its definition and stakeholder list name no outside party affected by the insider's actions as a party to the program; customers appear only as victims in case studies (for example p. 100). That is the extension the paper needs.

Facet check: the facet's quotations from the definition, the 3,000-incident base, and Best Practice 12 are accurate. The facet says the practice 12 passages "cut against reading observe everything (P15) as literal full-volume human review"; that is right for the opening, but the same practice supports retrospective human review and full-time monitors (pp. 84, 89), so the guide supports the post's principle 15 (after-the-fact reconstruction) more than the facet suggests. The facet read only the titles of practices 5, 8 and 21; I read the text of 21 and only the opening pages of 5 and 8, and they confirm the facet's description (stress, negative environments, positive incentives to align the workforce) without changing it. I found no direct contradiction.

## Caution

Practitioner guidance, not a study. The "evidence-based" claim rests on an incident repository of publicly disclosed cases in which the person was "charged and convicted or found liable" (p. 4), a sample that selects for detected and prosecuted insiders, and the guide admits that reliable data are hard to get (p. 4). It gives no effect sizes for any control. Its examples concern human employees; it says nothing about AI models or agents. The figures on p. 5 (n = 1,314 incident types, n = 1,179 financial impacts, n = 1,515 victim organisations) are read from a graphic, and I cite none of them here. Dating is inconsistent: the cover notice says 2022, the history figure labels this edition 2021 and retitles it "Common Sense Guide to Managing Insider Risk" (p. 1), and the front matter calls it the seventh edition. Cite the printed pages, which differ from the PDF's by an offset.
