## What it argues

**Structure (Crossref, Routledge 2016 reprint, which keeps the 1992 pagination as far as the page ranges show).** Introduction pp. 1–6; Antiquity: Aids for Inexperienced Readers and the Prehistory of Punctuation pp. 9–19; Changing Attitudes to the Written Word: Components in a "Grammar of Legibility" pp. 20–29; Carolingian *Renovatio*: Augmenting Old Notation with New Symbols pp. 30–34; The Requirements of Public Worship pp. 35–40; The Development of the General Repertory of Punctuation pp. 41–49; The Technology of Printing and the Stabilization of the Symbols pp. 50–61; Influences on the Application of Punctuation pp. 65–96; The Layout and Punctuation of Verse pp. 97–114. O'Donnell gives the same divisions for the 1993 US edition (narrative pp. 9–61, influences pp. 65–96, plates pp. 160–297).

**Content (second-hand).** O'Donnell (1994) reports that the book distinguishes punctuation for one who reads aloud from punctuation that marks "logical structures in a text for the eye of a silent reader," shows that liturgical practice encouraged punctuation, and names non-alphabetic marks a "grammar of legibility." He criticises errors on late antiquity, a "designedly narrow" scope that leaves out *mise en page*, and plate transcriptions; he misses Saenger's work. The review "never mentions word separation, spacing or *scriptio continua*" (card `odonnell1994parkes`). The facets carry more than one snippet (none seen by me or by the S2 verifier; all second-hand). Facet A, from facet E: p. 1, "word separation, features of layout and punctuation, were developed to make it easier for readers to extract the information conveyed in the written medium," and where Latin was "a foreign language, there was a need for conventions which made it easier to read"; ch. 3 (pp. 30–34), "Irish and Anglo-Saxon missionaries taught their scribal practices to pupils in new foundations," and Carolingian scribes "developed a complex of new graphic conventions" to improve intelligibility; p. 12, marks "linking or separating words (hyphen or diastole)". Facet K §K14, verbatim but unpaged: "The Anglo-Saxons learned the practices of word-separation, layout and punctuation from Irish teachers"; "Because many had to learn Latin as a foreign language, there was a need for conventions which made it easier to read"; and zero hits for "Saenger" in the book.

## Relation to the argument

**P2, P6 and ruling D6.** Parkes's framing, a "grammar of legibility" with spacing among other conventions, supports D6's complex-of-conventions reading. The chapter titles place Insular and Carolingian developments in the narrative (pp. 20–34). The second-hand snippets in facets A and K (above) show Parkes naming word separation among the new conventions, tying it to learners of Latin as a foreign language, and saying the Anglo-Saxons learned it from Irish teachers. None of them dates standardisation on the Continent. O'Donnell's review is silent on spacing. So the draft's pairing "Saenger, 1997; Parkes, 1992" rests, for Parkes, on chapter titles and second-hand snippets; for the Insular half the snippets are on point, for the eleventh-century half nothing was found.
