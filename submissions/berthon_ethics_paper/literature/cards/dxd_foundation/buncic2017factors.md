# Bunčić, D. (2017). Factors influencing the success and failure of writing reforms. *Studi Slavistici, 14*, 21–46.

**Identifier:** doi:10.13128/studi_slavis-21937 · **Read depth:** full_text (published open-access PDF, journal pagination; the English abstract sits at p. 46, after the references) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (comparative case survey, linguistics) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** a qualitative comparison of script, glyphic and spelling reforms in Slavic and former Soviet languages, "adding a few prominent cases from other languages" (p. 22); no case count, coding scheme or statistics · **Parties modeled:** other (a state or reform commission, a literate public, and the script itself) · **Facets:** F · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Bunčić asks why some reforms of writing take hold. His opening contrast is the Russian spelling reform of 1917–18, "very successful," against the 1930 plan to Latinise Russian, the Chinese Second Simplification Scheme of 1977 (withdrawn in 1986 after "continuing public resistance"), the French "Rectifications" of 1990 and the Uzbek Latinisation of 1993 (p. 21). He quotes Coulmas that "practical criteria of efficiency have never been the sole determining factor" (p. 21) and works through four groups of factors.

Linguistic factors come first (§2). Whether a reform moves toward phonology or morphology "does not seem to be decisive for its success" (p. 26). Sound proposals can wait a very long time: a German rule for ss after short vowels, proposed in 1827, became standard through the 1996 reform, "more than 170 years" later (p. 39); the Russian letter ё, invented in 1783, was made compulsory in schools in 1942, "159 years" on (p. 38).

Economy comes second (§3). He checks the 1929 Soviet estimate that Latin letters would shorten Cyrillic text by 11–13%, finds about 12% on a sample (23 Cyrillic lines against 20½ Latin lines, p. 30), and argues that "information that has to be taken in visually cannot be compressed without decreasing the ease and speed of reading" (p. 32). Such "scientific" claims "are usually void because orthography does not change the information density needed in a given situation" (p. 41).

Third are the semiotic values a script carries, such as Arabic for Islam (§4). Fourth are sociological factors (§5). Dictatorship does not guarantee success, and decrees without legitimacy "can be short-lived" (p. 35). "Doubtless the main obstacle for writing reforms are the people who can write" (p. 36). Success tracks political turning points: "there seems to be a certain kairos, a short time span immediately after a political change, during which a writing reform can be accomplished more easily than later" (p. 39). The abstract states: "linguistic factors turn out to play a very minor role compared to extralinguistic factors" and "the most decisive factor seems to be timing" (p. 46).

## Relation to the argument

Bunčić does not discuss word separation, and a scribal habit is not a state reform, so he cannot speak to P2 or the chronology. He supplies a check on the design-precedent framing (ruling D1). A writing convention with good linguistic design does not win on that ground alone; adoption turns on timing, the installed base of writers, and what the new form signals. That cuts against any account in which spacing spread because it was the better interface, and it supports the retreat to "one of a complex of conventions" (P6; D6; facet F, lesson 5, "users must adopt together").

It also bears on D8 (who holds the convention). In his cases the holders are states, commissions and the writing public, and the public can sink a decree, as with the Chinese Second Scheme.

Facet check: facet F quotes the abstract accurately, and the body makes the same two points (pp. 26, 39–41). Its counter-case row names "installed base" and "the revolutionary bundle" as Bunčić's causes of failure. The installed-base point is there (p. 36). The revolutionary bundle is not; Bunčić does not treat reforms as bundles, and the phrase belongs to the facet.

## Caution

A survey of cases chosen by a Slavist, with no sampling frame or count. "Timing" is built from those cases and is not a tested model. It covers state-sponsored changes to letters, spelling and script from the 18th to the 21st century; nothing in it concerns manuscript conventions or the early Middle Ages. Cite it for the claim that linguistic merit does not decide adoption, and for the timing and installed-base points, as stated for these cases.
