# Biggs, N. (2020). Decoding chancery records from the 1240s. *BSHM Bulletin: Journal of the British Society for the History of Mathematics, 36*(1), 1–12.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/26375451.2020.1834796 · **Read depth:** full_text (LSE Research Online accepted manuscript, not the published article; the manuscript's own pages 1–14 are used; references skimmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (history of mathematics) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** reverse arithmetic on individual entries of the Calendar of the Liberate Rolls, Henry III, 1240–45 (cited as C 22 to C 297); no count of entries and no sampling · **Parties modeled:** none (a royal chancery and its suppliers, read through their accounts) · **Facets:** F · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Biggs reads the king's payments for gold as ciphertext. A payment of 12 pounds 5 shillings 3 pence for brooches weighing 27 shillings 3 pence becomes 2,943 pence for 327 pennyweights, and "the key fact is that 2943 is equal to 9 times 327" (p. 3). Price divided by weight yields a "fineness factor" (8, 8½, 9, 10, 11) that stood for the touchstone grade of the gold. A scale of up to 24 grades may be the earliest English evidence of the carat system (§3, p. 7). The same method lets him revisit Grierson's identification of the *oboli de musc'* as Almohad gold coins: an obol weighed just over 1½ pennyweights at factor 10, and the "pieces of ten pennyweights" were "not coins at all, but simply pieces of gold bullion" (pp. 11–12) (§5, pp. 9–12).

The wider aim is to find "Fibonacci's fingerprints" in English documents, that is, whether Hindu-Arabic algorithms had reached the king's officers by the 1240s. Here Biggs is cautious. He cites the "lack of evidence about the methods used to perform the calculations" (p. 2), says "very few people knew how to do such calculations" and that the procedures "were known only to a select few," probably passed on by word of mouth (p. 8), and allows that "some form of abacus was used" (p. 8). He concludes that "although we cannot give a firm answer to our question," the evidence "puts the focus" on the 1240s (p. 9). The closing view is only that the evidence is "consistent with the view that there were significant links between the practical problems of medieval finance and the introduction of the Hindu-Arabic algorithms in Western Europe" (p. 12).

## Relation to the argument

Biggs supplies one case in the bundle table of facet F (row 1, Hindu-Arabic numerals). It supports the retreat from "the" move (ruling D6; `ARGUMENT_MAP.md`, "What the argument no longer claims"). The introduction says: "The use of Roman numerals in official records continued long after the underlying calculations were being done with the Hindu-Arabic numerals and algorithms," and that "the people who did the calculations tended to keep their methods secret" (p. 3). If so, a notation can work while the recorded notation stays old, and a small group can hold a practice without publishing it. That is a thin analogy for D8 and P10, no more.

## Caution

The accepted manuscript, so pagination differs from the journal. A short inference from individual entries about gold prices; nothing on reading or uptake. The Roman-numeral lag is asserted, not demonstrated, and no data say how many officers used which method. The Frederick II and Fibonacci link in the conclusion rests on what Biggs himself marks as having "no documentary evidence" (p. 12).
