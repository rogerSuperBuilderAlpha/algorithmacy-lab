## What it argues
Thai is an alphabetic script written without spaces between words. Chantavarin and Leung test whether adding spaces helps adult native readers, and whether it helps more on compound nouns, where the unspaced boundary is hardest to find. They insert two consecutive spaces between words, with word boundaries set by one native-speaker researcher on a conservative criterion (§2.2, p. 8). The design is 2 × 2 and every participant read 76 sentences.

Results: spacing made the sentence slower and the words faster. Sentence reading time was longer with spaces for compounds (3,804 ms against 3,676 unspaced) and for simple nouns (3,675 against 3,546), with more fixations and shorter average fixations (Tables 2–3, pp. 11–12; the spacing estimate for sentence time is 132 ms, SE 35). On the target noun, first fixation duration fell from 330 to 297 ms for compounds and from 219 to 210 ms for simple nouns; gaze duration fell from 352 to 315 and from 272 to 260 ms (Table 2, p. 11). The spacing × noun-type interaction was 24 ms on first fixation and 25 ms on gaze duration (p. 17), and the authors report 93.2% power for it (n. 1, p. 13). Spacing moved the landing position toward the word centre only on compounds (§3.4, pp. 16–17). They conclude that spaces give "salient word boundary information" that helps early and late word processing, most for compounds (p. 20). Readers are not just tolerating spaces: "Since Thai script's orthography is 'unspaced', the observed facilitative effect of space information is not due to familiarity with spaced text" (p. 17).

The introduction summarises the field. Removing spaces from spaced scripts disrupts eye movements in English (p. 2) and, in the cited studies, German, Spanish and French (p. 3). Baek et al. (2022) found the same for Korean, "though not to the same extent as in English" (pp. 2–3). Leung et al. (2021) found no significant loss of readability for Arabic, which the authors tie to cursive letter forms that already mark words (p. 3). In unspaced Chinese, Japanese and Thai, adding spaces shortens fixation durations on words but lengthens total reading time (p. 3).

## Relation to the argument
It qualifies premise P4 (`ARGUMENT_MAP.md`: "Readers fluent in unspaced scripts gain nothing from added spaces"). Adult native Thai readers do gain at the level of the word, on fixation and gaze time, and lose at the level of the sentence. "Gain nothing" holds only for whole-sentence speed. The result also tells against the familiarity account in Chen et al. (`chen2021tradeoff`), at least for Thai: the authors say the benefit cannot be due to familiarity with spaced text.

For P3 and P1 it adds a modern case in which a native reader of an unspaced alphabetic script uses spaces at the word level, which cuts a little against Heilmann's and Vatri's reading of Thai (`heilmann2020reading`, p. 181: Thai eye movements the same as English). Heilmann takes the Thai landing-position result from Winskel et al. (2009); Chantavarin and Leung find that landing position does shift with spacing for compounds, and they put the mismatch down partly to earlier studies choosing target words without regard to morphological structure (p. 19). It shows what spacing does for habituated readers of one script; it cannot show what it did for ancient readers or for learners.

## Caution
One lab, one sample of university students, one-line sentences, and bimorphemic transparent compounds only. The "spaced" condition used double spaces, which is wider than the single space in English. The authors say word segmentation involved subjectivity and previous Thai studies likely used different cut-offs (p. 18–19). The effects are tens of milliseconds on words, with a sentence-level cost in the opposite direction, so they cannot be cited as showing that spacing speeds Thai reading.
