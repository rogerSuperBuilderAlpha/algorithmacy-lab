# Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. *Automatica, 19*(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 · **Read depth:** full_text (the Pergamon journal PDF, 5 pages in the journal's layout and pagination 775–779, from an open mirror at ckrybus.com; local copy dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/S/bainbridge1983.pdf; I read the whole paper including the reference list) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed brief paper (revised from an IFAC/IFIP/IFORS/IEA conference paper, Baden-Baden, September 1982) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** none; a position paper on process-control automation that draws on cited experiments (Ephrath 1980; Ephrath & Young 1981; Ekkers et al. 1979; Mackworth 1950) and flight-deck examples · **Parties modeled:** person–system (one operator and the automatic control system; a supervisor who can be called; no third party) · **Facets:** S · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
The paper's claim is a paradox: "the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator" (p. 775). Two ironies follow from the designer's wish to eliminate the operator. Designer errors are a major source of operating problems, and "the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate" (p. 775). After automation the operator is left with two kinds of task: "He may be expected to monitor that the automatic system is operating correctly, and if it is not he may be expected to call a more experienced operator or to take-over himself" (p. 775, §1.1).

On take-over, manual skill decays without use, so "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one" (p. 775). Knowledge of the plant "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness" (p. 775). Working storage of the current plant state "takes time to build up"; manual operators come in a quarter to half an hour before taking over, so an operator who must act quickly "can only do so on the basis of minimum information" (p. 776).

On monitoring, the paper cites Mackworth's vigilance work: "it is impossible for even a highly motivated human being to maintain effective visual attention towards a source of information on which very little happens, for more than about half an hour" (p. 776). Logging as a remedy fails: "people can write down numbers without noticing what they are" (p. 776). The deeper irony is that "the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively" (p. 776). If the computer decides faster and on more dimensions than a person, "There is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly"; the operator can only judge the decisions "at some meta-level"; "The human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776). If the operator must follow the computer's decisions, the computer must decide "using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (p. 777). Ephrath (1980) found "system performance was worse with computer aiding, because the operator made the decisions anyway, and checking the computer added to his workload" (p. 777). "Automatic systems should fail obviously" (p. 777), because graceful degradation camouflages failure.

Section 3 turns to human–computer collaboration. Under manual control operators "obtain enough feedback about the results of their actions within a few seconds to correct their own errors", whereas Wiener and Curry (1980) show the same errors persisting in setting up and monitoring automatic equipment "when they do not get adequate feedback" (p. 777, §3.2). On displays: "It certainly would be ironic if we find that the most compatible display is not the best display to give to the operator after all!", since data processed for meaning is better remembered (p. 778). The section ends with a proposal: software displays could enrich the task "by allowing him to design his own interface" (p. 778). The conclusion: people without time pressure "can be impressive problem solvers. The difficulty remains that they are less effective when under time pressure" (p. 778). "Perhaps the final irony is that it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training" (p. 777).

## Relation to the argument
This is the founding statement of the problem the abstract's second sentence names: a system that does the job and then hands a person the job of checking it. Bainbridge says that check is impossible in real time and degrades to a meta-level judgment of acceptability (p. 776). It supports the abstract's claim that read-to-check is the standing model, and it supports the dyad claim: every sentence has one operator facing one automatic system; the only other human is a supervisor called in.

It also supplies the author's own alternative in embryo and so complicates D7 and P9 (`../../SOURCE.md`, `../../ARGUMENT_MAP.md`). Feedback "within a few seconds" lets operators correct themselves under manual control (p. 777); that is the feedback immediacy the abstract asks for, stated in 1983 inside the dyad. Knowledge "develops only through use and feedback" (p. 775) is the abstract's "epistemic action" and Kahneman–Klein condition. But Bainbridge cuts against the author's pilot claim: take-over needs working storage that takes a quarter to half an hour to build (p. 776), so a person who has not been reading the situation cannot act well on it. Her remedies are training, simulators, displays and self-designed interfaces, all inside the operator–system pair; nothing here reaches a mediator with its own objective between two counterparts.

## Caution
A five-page position paper from process control, 1983, with no data of its own; the numbers it gives (half an hour of vigilance; quarter to half an hour to build working storage) are reported from other work and practice, not measured here. The copy read is a third-party mirror of the Pergamon PDF, not downloaded from Elsevier; text and pagination are the journal's. Do not cite it for platforms, two-sided coordination or audit logs, none of which it discusses; its "log" (p. 776) is an operator's written record, not a machine record.
