## What it argues

Chinese text has no spaces, so "Chinese readers thus have to depend on lexical knowledge to segment characters into words" (Introduction), and some linguists hold that the word is a concept "mostly borrowed from Indo-European languages" (Introduction). The authors test whether eye movements in Chinese nonetheless show the word-based signature known from spaced scripts. They do. Mean fixation was 244 ms and mean forward saccade 3.15 characters (Overall analyses). Gaze duration on word n fell with the frequency and predictability of word n, rose with its length, fell with the frequency, predictability and length of word n−1 (spillover) and fell with the predictability of word n+1 (a parafoveal-on-foveal effect); "the effects of word properties on gaze durations in Chinese reading appear to be completely analogous to those found in alphabetic languages", and removing them worsened the model fit, χ²(9) = 208.74, p < .001 (Gaze duration on words). The pattern held when the dependent measure was defined on characters rather than words (Fixation durations on characters) and in fixation probabilities and saccade length. There was no preferred viewing location within words, as earlier Chinese studies had found (Discussion). Conclusion: "Despite the fact that words are not spatially segmented in Chinese ... reading appears just as reliant on words in Chinese as in other languages" (Conclusion), and any model of Chinese reading "must include a model of word segmentation" that runs in parallel with identification (Implications for modeling).

## Relation to the argument

The abstract treats boundary detection as something the voice did before spaces and the page did after. Here are 46 fluent readers detecting boundaries in silence with neither, by lexical knowledge applied as the eye moves.

- **Cuts against** "unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line": in a living unspaced script, skilled readers segment silently and their eye movements look like those of spaced-script readers.
- **Supports** the project's narrower premise (P5 in the argument map) that spacing moves an inferential task to a perceptual one: the authors' models need a segmentation component precisely because the page does not supply it, and the absence of a preferred viewing location shows the eye lacks the target that spaces give.
- **Bears on "representational effect".** Li et al. note that the word is a disputed unit in Chinese and that native speakers disagree about boundaries (Introduction); writing that separates words makes the word a visible object, which is Olson's "model" thesis in another form (see `barton1980awareness`). The abstract's phrase could be grounded here with care.
- Inference mine: what makes silent unspaced reading cheap for these readers is a lifetime of lexical knowledge in their first language, which the Insular learners of Latin did not have. That is the learner's condition the Insular story turns on (`tunbridge1992study`).

## Caution

Skilled adult readers only; corpus regression rather than controlled manipulation, and the authors warn that some parafoveal-on-foveal effects "appear to only have robust support from statistical corpus analyses" (n. 1). Chinese characters are already visually discrete boxes, unlike the letters of Latin *scriptio continua*, and the authors compare them with letters only as "salient units" (n. 2). Pagination is the manuscript's.
