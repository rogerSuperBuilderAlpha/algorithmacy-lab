## What it argues
The paper takes "dark patterns," a term coined by the practitioner Harry Brignull in 2010, and asks what ethical concerns practitioners pack into it. The authors define the phenomenon as "instances where designers use their knowledge of human behavior (e.g., psychology) and the desires of end users to implement deceptive functionality that is not in the user's best interest" (Introduction, p. 1). Two researchers searched for two months (search engines, social media, practitioner blogs, news sites, and a bot collecting the #darkpatterns hashtag) and built a corpus of 118 artifacts: 45 from social media, 40 from practitioner blogs, 19 from news outlets, 10 from the researchers' own use, and 4 from darkpatterns.org (p. 4). They coded the corpus against Brignull's taxonomy (Table 1, p. 4), found it mixed context, strategy and outcome, and re-coded by open coding. The result is five strategies, nested over Brignull's types (Figure 1, p. 5): nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference (hidden information, preselection, aesthetic manipulation) and forced action.

Illustrations from the corpus include Instagram's notification prompt with only "Not Now" and "OK" (p. 5); an Uber prompt that urges a driver who tries to stop to reach "an arbitrary goal for the day," which "takes advantage of a person's natural tendency to 'income target'" (p. 5); Salesforce requiring consent to a privacy statement before a user can unsubscribe (p. 6); and a Windows 10 update that blocks shutdown (p. 8). The Discussion places the five strategies on Norman's gulfs of execution and evaluation: some leave the user "flying blind" (interference, obstruction), some foreground only the actions "desired by the shareholder" (nagging, forced action), and sneaking removes the identification of action possibilities entirely (p. 8). They add that some dark patterns "test well from a usability perspective (e.g., forced action, nagging)" while costing user choice (pp. 8–9). The closing sections turn to design responsibility and "designer's character," call for ethics education in UX, and note that UX has no licensure, unlike medicine or architecture (pp. 9–10). They state the limit that "our current corpus is limited by artifacts shared by UX practitioners" (p. 10).

## Relation to the argument
It serves premise P10 and the objection on the interested designer. The paper's own words, "user value is supplanted in favor of shareholder value" (Abstract), say that in this corpus the interface is built by or for a party whose interest diverges from the user's. That gives the paper an empirical-qualitative anchor for the claim that a convention at the interface can be turned against the people it serves, and for the point that the owner of an interface is not like the scribes. Two limits. The corpus is of examples practitioners chose to share, so it shows what kinds of dark patterns exist, not how common they are; frequency is the job of `mathur2019dark`. And the strategies are aimed at a single user's choices, not at coordination between two users through a mediator, so they bear on ruling D8 only as a warning about who holds the convention.
