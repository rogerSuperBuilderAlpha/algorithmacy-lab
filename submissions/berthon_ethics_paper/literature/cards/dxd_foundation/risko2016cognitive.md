## What it argues

Risko and Gilbert define cognitive offloading as "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task in order to reduce cognitive demand" (ms. p. 2, abstract; repeated p. 3 and in the glossary, p. 25). They name two questions: what triggers offloading, and what it costs and gains (p. 4).

Triggers. In the external-normalization studies, people tilt their heads to read rotated text more often as rotation costs rise (pp. 4–5). The same link between internal demand and spontaneous offloading appears for reminders and for writing items down (p. 5; Fig. 2 caption, p. 28). Demand alone does not explain it. People offload more when they wrongly judge the task effortful: rotating both words and frame draws more tilting than rotating words alone, though the two conditions cost the same (pp. 5–6). Lower confidence in one's memory, including confidence set by a perceptual task, predicts more reminder-setting (pp. 7–8). In a short-term memory task participants wrote items down "about 40% of the time" with two items, where unaided accuracy was already at ceiling (p. 8).

Consequences. People who expected a typed trivia statement to be erased remembered it better than people who expected it saved (p. 9). Saving also shifts memory from "what" to "where" (pp. 9–10). Searching online raised the belief that one has knowledge "in-the-head", and recent Google use raised reported "cognitive self-esteem" and predicted quiz performance (p. 10), and knowing that Internet access was coming made people more likely to answer "don't know" (pp. 10–11).

The framework (Fig. 3, p. 11) has three arrows: metacognitive evaluation selects a strategy (a); using it changes later metacognition (b) and lower-level processes (c). It predicts a self-reinforcing "drift away from reliance on internal capabilities" in a setting with effective technology (p. 13).

Box 3 (pp. 33–34) lists costs: worse spatial memory after turn-by-turn GPS driving; worse memory for photographed objects; and automation complacency, bias and "skill decay." Its sentence on design reads: "In many cases the decision to offload is not made by the individual. Rather, offloading is 'built-in' to the task environment by design" (p. 33). Practical implications (p. 14): a benefit holds only if the offloaded demand is "unnecessary with respect to the learning goal," and what is saved must be "redistributed productively." Box 2 (p. 32) sets cognitive impartiality against the cognitive-miser view and leaves it open.

## Relation to the argument

Premise P5 (the scribes' move re-represented the task) and the engagement-layer ruling D7. Vocabulary: the authors treat "computational offloading" (Scaife and Rogers) and "epistemic actions" (Kirsh and Maglio) as neighbours of their term (p. 3). Their term is narrower: the person acts. A scribe's spacing is another person's act on the page, not the reader's. Box 3 stretches the term, because it counts offloading that a designer builds in as an instance. That lets the paper say a convention built into the artefact is offloading by design, but the text never mentions writing, reading or conventions, so the extension is the paper's own.

The costs side bears on "Where the argument is exposed" item 4, "lowering a cost has costs." Box 3 names complacency, bias and skill decay, all from safety-critical automation. The drift prediction (p. 13) suggests a reader of spaced text may rely on the artefact and lose the unspaced skill. That is a prediction from the framework; no study of reading is cited. P4 (fluent readers of unspaced script gain nothing) fits the "unnecessary demand" condition on p. 14, but the authors do not discuss it.

Facet check: the facet says the definition "requires the person's own physical action, so it does not cover a scribe's spacing." The definition does say physical action, but Box 3 includes built-in offloading, so the facet should say the review also covers designer-built cases. Its other quotations and the Box 3 cost list match the text. The facet cites the definition at ms. p. 2; it is also on p. 3.

## Caution

A review of mostly laboratory studies with recall, rotation and reminder tasks, all in adults with modern tools. It has no study of reading, writing or any historical convention. The framework is proposed, not tested (the authors call for research, p. 12). Read from the author manuscript; page numbers, figures and wording may differ in the journal version, so cite by section or check against the journal PDF before giving journal pages. Several cited studies were "in press" at the time of writing (refs 32, 33, 99).
