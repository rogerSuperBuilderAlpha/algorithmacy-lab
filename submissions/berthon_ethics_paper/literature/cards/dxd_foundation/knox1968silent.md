## What it argues
Knox answers Josef Balogh's 1927 claim that the ancient world "always read out loud" what moderns read silently (Balogh's own question, p. 87 of his article, as Knox quotes it at p. 435). He grants the half that holds: Balogh's evidence "does indeed make it perfectly clear that the normal way to read a literary text … was out loud" (p. 421). He rejects the other half, that silent reading was almost unheard of, because "common sense rebels against the idea that scholarly readers … did not develop a technique of silent, faster reading" (p. 421), and he asks whether Aristarchus, Callimachus or Didymus pronounced every syllable of the books they used (pp. 421-422).

He works through Balogh's cases one by one.
- Augustine on Ambrose (*Conf.* 6.3): Augustine was "an African provincial from a poor family" and Ambrose had been a consular prefect educated at Rome, so "there is no reason to suppose that Ambrose's silent reading would have excited such comment in the Italian imperial circles" (p. 422). Augustine himself reads Romans "in silentio" at *Conf.* 8.12 (p. 422).
- Horace *Sat.* 1.6.122–3 and Lucian *Adv. ind.* 2 ("your eye keeping ahead of your mouth"): both are ambiguous, and Knox reads Lucian's man as reading aloud and reading correctly, using Quintilian *Inst.* 1.1.32 on looking ahead while pronouncing (pp. 423–426).
- Cicero *Tusc.* 5.116 (found by W. P. Clark): the deaf can take "much greater pleasure" in reading songs than in hearing them, a sentence Cicero "could not possibly have written … if silent reading of poetic texts had been impossible or even a nine-day wonder" (p. 427).
- Letters and wills: Knox takes apart Balogh's readings of Suetonius *Aug.* 39, Horace *Sat.* 2.5, the Cydippe story and Plutarch's Caesar-and-Cato letter (pp. 428–432). He adds E. G. Turner's find from Antiphanes' *Sappho*, a riddle whose answer, a letter, has children that "for anyone who happens to be standing near the man who is reading … are inaudible" (p. 432–433), and two scenes where a character reads silently on stage: Euripides *Hipp.* 856ff and Aristophanes *Knights* 115–128 (p. 433–434).
- Balogh's "acoustic" evidence (*pagina loquitur* and kin) is metaphor. Phaedra's letter "sings" and "shouts" in Euripides, but "the audience has watched Theseus read the letter, and he did so in silence" (pp. 434–435).

Conclusion (p. 435): "Ancient books were normally read aloud, but there is nothing to show that silent reading of books was anything extraordinary except the famous passage from Augustine's *Confessions*," and that is countered by Cicero. Silent reading of letters was "taken completely for granted" in fifth- and fourth-century Athens (p. 434).

## Caution
A 1968 philological argument with no data. It tests Balogh's evidence and finds it insufficient; it does not show how common silent reading was. Knox notes there are "many gradations between vocal and silent reading" (n. 8, quoting Hendrickson), so the article cannot settle the later subvocalization dispute. Most of the evidence is of short texts (letters, oracles, wills), and Knox says himself that literary books were normally read aloud. Greek and Latin passages in the scan are OCR-garbled in places; quotations here are from the English prose.

## Also used by facet W (2026-10-15)

Facet W (word spacing, oracy and literacy) takes from Knox the point that his dispute with Balogh is over whether silent reading was *extraordinary*, not over the page: Knox never names word separation, and his own evidence of silent reading (letters, wills, the Antiphanes riddle, pp. 432–434) is all unspaced text read without voice. Against the abstract's "unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line", Knox is a direct counter-witness: fifth- and fourth-century Athenians read unspaced letters silently as a matter of course.
