## What it argues

The Code offers "a common set of guidelines" for the wholesale foreign exchange market (p. 1). It binds nobody: it "does not impose legal or regulatory obligations on Market Participants nor does it substitute for regulation" (p. 1). The Global Foreign Exchange Committee (GFXC) "was established in May 2017 as a forum bringing together central banks and private sector participants" and maintains the text (p. 1). Participation is optional through a Statement of Commitment, which "like the Code, is voluntary" (p. 5; form and explanatory note pp. 78–81). The Foreword adds that "the decision of what steps should be undertaken, and in what manner, resides with each Market Participant" (p. 5). I found no complaint body, sanction, monitoring of adherence or register of breaches anywhere in the 82 pages.

Scope matters for the comparison. The Code covers wholesale participants; it does not expect to apply to the general retail public or to private-banking customers (p. 5). Its clients are banks, funds and corporate treasuries.

The six headings are Ethics, Governance, Execution, Information Sharing, Risk Management and Compliance, and Confirmation and Settlement. Four sets of principles bear on a third party that sits between a client and a price:

- **Discretion is allowed but bounded.** A Principal has "no obligation to execute the order until both parties are in agreement", but where acceptance gives it discretion it must use it reasonably and fairly, and not so as to disadvantage the Client (Principle 8, pp. 12–13). Principle 9 asks firms to tell clients "where discretion may exist or may be expected, and how it may be exercised" and "the basis on which trade requests and/or orders might be rejected" (p. 14), and to disclose how conflicts of interest in principal dealing are identified and addressed (p. 14).
- **Last look (Principle 17, pp. 22–23).** The dealer "has sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes, over whether the Client's trade request is accepted or not". The Code limits the purpose: last look "should not be used for purposes of information gathering", and the dealer should not trade on the request's information during the window. Disclosure is the instrument: the dealer must explain, "at a minimum", whether price changes may affect acceptance, the typical decision time and the purpose. A carve-out box and "good practice" to be available for dialogue with clients follow on p. 23. Annex 1 gives a prohibited case (pp. 62–63).
- **Records and requests.** Principle 36 asks for a record of the reasons behind electronic order rejections, to give clients transparency "where appropriate", and says information "should be made available to Clients upon request" (p. 40). Principle 39: "At the request of a Client, Market Participants should be able to provide information regarding the actions taken in handling a specific transaction" (p. 42).
- **Terms.** Principle 40 asks participants to "make clear if standard terms are used, and if changes are proposed. Where changes are substantial, these should be agreed before any transaction" (p. 42). Principle 7 asks firms to give "personnel or external parties" confidential channels to raise concerns, investigated by independent functions (p. 11).

Principle 18 (algorithmic and aggregation services, pp. 23–24) makes disclosure of the strategy, fees, conflicts and routing preferences the convention; clients are encouraged to use standard disclosure templates and transaction-cost data to evaluate the service.

## Relation to the argument

D8 asks where the convention bends in forex markets. This text answers: toward the dealers' own committee, in the form of voluntary disclosure and process duties, with discretion left in the dealer's hands. The mechanism for an interested counterparty is disclosure plus internal controls plus a client's right to ask; the Code gives the client no remedy. That is the pattern D3 calls a literacy-based intervention, and the FX Code applies it to an algorithmic counterpart (Principle 18). It speaks to P10 in a direct way: the Code assumes the dealer is interested, names the conflict (Principle 9) and asks only for disclosure and restraint.

The comparison with platforms mixes populations. The Code protects professional counterparties; Annex 1 even treats a bank that exploits a corporate client's lack of sophistication on mark-up as a breach (p. 61), while platform users are mostly retail.

**Facet check:** (1) J:234 and the audit say the Code requires no notice of change to clients. Principle 40 (p. 42) asks that substantial changes to terms be "agreed before any transaction", and Principle 8 (p. 12) asks that a changed capacity be "agreed by both parties". Both are voluntary, but both are stronger than DSA Art. 14(2), which only requires that recipients be informed. ## Caution

A voluntary code is evidence of what the industry says good practice is, not of what dealers do. The text has no enforcement data. The printed pages 22–24 and 82 have Latin filler text in the text layer (template residue); I read around it, and the page-82 text is a blank template. The Code covers wholesale FX only. The December 2024 edition postdates the facet's other FX sources (2014–2017), and I did not read earlier editions, so I cannot say how its last-look wording differs from them.
