## What it argues
Opening: punctuation is "ubiquitous and invisible," and editors impose their own systems on ancient texts. O'Donnell sets Parkes after the earlier work of Wingo (1972) and Mueller (1964), both of which "concentrate on the fragmentary manuscript and epigraphic evidence from antiquity." The next paragraph gives the book's plan: a narrative of Western punctuation to the early modern period (book pp. 9–61), a discussion of "influences on the application of punctuation" (pp. 65–96) with a chapter on verse, and 74 plates (pp. 160–297). Greek "is emphatically not discussed." What non-alphabetic marks do for reading is create "a grammar of legibility."

Then: Parkes distinguishes logical from rhetorical analysis. "Roughly (this is no novel idea), earlier practice emphasized punctuation in the service of one who would read aloud … while our modern practice is to regard punctuation as a way of marking logical structures in a text for the eye of a silent reader." Three cautions follow. Parkes relies on secondary literature outside the later Middle Ages and makes factual errors on late antiquity, including, in O'Donnell's account, a pope who never existed. The topic is "designedly narrow, perhaps too narrow," and leaves out *mise en page* and the question of "how reading actually works." The plates have transcription errors, and later additions to the manuscripts go unreported. The closing section praises "the demonstration that liturgical practice in the middle ages had a strong influence on encouraging the insertion of punctuation in manuscripts, for the public performer needed a manuscript that was easily read at sight." Then: "I miss mention of Paul Saenger's work on the growth of silent reading the later middle ages, and here the work of Richard and Mary Rouse offer the necessary bridge between punctuation studies and a wider consideration of how books were being used in the later middle ages to transmit information."
