## What it argues

Delacroix and Lawrence argue that individual consent, data ownership and top-down rules such as the GDPR leave people vulnerable. The harm they have in mind is cumulative: "death by a thousand cuts" from many small data disclosures that together define a person (p. 3). Ownership is "both unlikely and inadequate" as a remedy (p. 1), because data resembles a river's non-exclusive rights more than a castle, and because few people have the time or knowledge to monitor their data (pp. 3–4). They also argue that contract-based approaches cannot reverse the power asymmetry (p. 4).

Their remedy is a "bottom-up" data trust: a legal trust in which data subjects pool their data rights and a trustee exercises those rights on their behalf, "bound by a fiduciary obligation of undivided loyalty" (p. 1). Key Points call this "an independent intermediary between data subjects and data collectors" (p. 1). They want "a plurality of Trusts," so that people can choose among them and switch (pp. 1, 8). Loyalty is the core requirement: "trustees cannot profit from a Trust, and more generally cannot allow their own interests to conflict with those of the beneficiaries" (p. 6). On that ground they reject Balkin's "information fiduciary" (a platform with fiduciary duties), because "a fiduciary obligation towards data subjects is incompatible with the data controllers' responsibility towards shareholders" (p. 6).

The scheme needs four conditions: low entry costs, secure data, portability and erasure (p. 8). A trust has power only if it can both release its members' data on its terms and "withdraw data from a particular controller" (p. 8). Today those rights are limited, and the GDPR does not let a subject mandate a third party to exercise them, so the authors call for regulatory change (pp. 8–13). The case studies show where a trust could negotiate: research consent, platform terms and conditions, credit-reference agencies (pp. 13–15). On implementation they name low uptake, default publicly funded trusts, exit from a trust, accountability and liability as open problems (pp. 16–17).

## Relation to the argument

This is the only source in the batch that puts an independent third party on the user's side, and it states the interest condition that P10 needs: a fiduciary must be free of the other side's interests, which a platform's owner is not. The conflict argument is the authors' own; in my reading it runs on the same logic as Jensen and Meckling's agency costs (P10 cites 1976, p. 308), whom the authors do not cite. It offers one candidate answer to ruling D8, which leaves the holder of the convention open: a convention held by a trustee under an equity court's supervision, not by the platform. The paper does not compare courts with forex markets or platforms, so it is a single case for facet J, not the comparison. The court's power to "supervise and, if necessary, to intervene on behalf of the beneficiaries" is part of the case for Trusts (p. 6).

It differs from the foundation paper's central move. Word separation changes the artefact so that readers do less. Here a trustee takes over a task, the exercise of seldom-used rights, and the person keeps the task of choosing a trust (pp. 8–9, 16). That is closer to P8's removal of a task than to P5's re-representation, with the task relocated to a person. It also makes exit a design requirement: portability and erasure are conditions 3 and 4, and the authors note they are only partly backed by law (p. 8).

The paper does not model coordination or measure irreducibility (D9). The reading of subject, controller and trustee as a triad is mine.

Facet check: the facet's "legal proposal with no measured effects" is confirmed. The facet lists only title and two authors; the full text shows the interest-conflict argument and the four conditions, which are what make it relevant.

## Caution

A proposal, not a test. The case studies are illustrations; the authors say so (p. 13). It is written for English and EU law and for the GDPR as of 2019; the CCPA is mentioned only in footnotes. Whether data rights can be held in trust, and whether the GDPR rights can be mandated to a trustee, are argued, not settled (pp. 10–13). Uptake, funding, liability and security are listed as unresolved (pp. 16–17). It cannot be cited for the claim that users would use trusts, or that trustees would act as the model says.
