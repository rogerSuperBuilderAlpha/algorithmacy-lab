## What it argues

Kahneman (heuristics and biases, HB) and Klein (naturalistic decision making, NDM) report that they agreed on more than they expected. Their question: "Under what conditions are the intuitions of professionals worthy of trust?" (p. 524). Their answer has two necessary conditions: "an environment of sufficiently high validity and adequate opportunity to practice the skill" (p. 520). Validity "describes the causal and statistical structure of the relevant environment" (p. 520); it can hold under uncertainty, as in poker. Skilled intuition is recognition in Simon's sense (p. 520). Their examples: fireground commanders and neonatal nurses (high validity) against stock picking and long-term political forecasting (zero validity; Tetlock's experts did no better than newspaper readers, p. 520). They borrow Hogarth's "wicked environments" (p. 520).

Four further claims. (1) Intuitions have no subjective marker: "high subjective confidence is not a good indication of validity" (p. 522). (2) Expertise is "fractionated": nurses, physicians and auditors appear on both Shanteau's lists of good and poor performers (p. 522). Feedback in long-term judgments is "delayed, sparse, and ambiguous" (p. 523). (3) Algorithms beat people when validity is low (consistency is the advantage; Grove et al.'s meta-analysis of 136 studies: mechanical judgment better in 63, equal in 65, worse in 8, p. 523) and when validity is very high and lapses of attention cause failure. (4) Algorithms that replace judgment "must remain under human supervision," yet "human operators become more passive and less vigilant when algorithms are in charge" (automation bias; p. 524). Their summary list says high validity is necessary and that learning needs "prolonged practice and feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (p. 524).

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D7 holds that "we understand THROUGH deciding and quickly ingesting the results." The source supports a conditional version. Learning by acting yields skill only where the environment has stable cues and feedback is rapid and unequivocal; where it does not, practice yields confidence without skill. This matches Part 1's line 66, which says an adaptive intermediary disrupts that feedback. It also bears on premise P7: a counterpart's behaviour through an adaptive third party is, on this account, a candidate low-validity or wicked environment, but the paper does not say so and the step is the project's inference `[inf]`.

On the Nadella contrast, section "Augmenting Professional Judgment" is useful. It says algorithms should replace judgment in some tasks, must remain under supervision, and supervision decays through passivity (p. 524). That is the same residual-human-task problem premise P12 raises about the post's human reviewers, from a source that is not about AI models.

## Caution

A consensus statement by two authors, not a test; the claims are summaries of earlier studies and examples (the officer-assessment anecdote, the typhoid physician). The authors say their "hunches about the frequency of exceptions" differ (p. 521) and call attitudes to the Meehl paradigm "a sore point" (p. 523). "Feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" is their wording for part of a necessary condition (adequate opportunity to learn), not a measured threshold (p. 524). The article does not address interfaces, platforms or counterparts.

## Also used by facet AA (2026-10-15)

Facet AA takes from this article the two necessary conditions for trustworthy intuition, "an environment of sufficiently high validity and adequate opportunity to practice the skill" (p. 520), and the summary that learning needs "prolonged practice and feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (p. 524). The abstract pairs this article with Shneiderman (1983) under "ecological feedback immediacy." The facet's reading is that the article carries the *ecological* half (validity, the "causal and statistical structure of the relevant environment," p. 520) and only a clause of the *immediacy* half ("rapid"), and that the clause is conjoined with "unequivocal." The experiments read for the facet separate the two: with feedback rapid in every condition, noise in outcomes alone stopped pairs from learning to cooperate (`berebymeyer2006speed`, pp. 1035–1038); in public-goods games people learned from their own payoffs only when their own action moved the payoff enough to be seen through others' behaviour (`burtonchellew2021payoff`, ms pp. 5–7). The fuller classification of what makes an environment "wicked," which this article borrows from Hogarth (2001), is now carded as `hogarth2015two`, where a nonstationary environment, and one that reacts to the person's own actions, is "a likely Case E" unless the dynamics can be inferred (p. 382). The facet also notes that Kahneman is a co-author of Thaler et al. (1997), where more frequent feedback lowered risk taking and earnings; the two lines of his work do not agree that faster feedback is better.
