## What it argues

Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff argue that behavioural science supports two different non-coercive policies, not one. A nudge targets behaviour. A boost targets competence. They adopt Thaler and Sunstein's definition of a nudge as "any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives" (p. 976). A nudge co-opts cognitive and motivational "deficiencies" through the external choice architecture (Table 1, p. 974; p. 976). Boosts, by contrast, "target competences rather than immediate behavior" and "may enlist human cognition ..., the environment (e.g., information representation or physical environment), or both" (p. 977). Table 1 (p. 974) sets the two apart on seven dimensions, though strictly it contrasts *non-educative* nudges with *long-term* boosts (its title): target, research roots, causal pathway, assumed cognitive architecture, reversibility, programmatic ambition and normative implications.

Their worked contrast is representation. Changing statistics from probabilities to natural frequencies improved Bayesian inference "not by explicit instruction, but by changing the information format". They class that as a *short-term boost*. Teaching people to make the translation themselves is a long-term boost (p. 977). "Educative nudges", such as reminders, warnings and labels, "largely overlap" with short-term boosts, and "The mere provision of information is often not enough" (p. 977). Boosts "adapt the external world (e.g., representation of information)" as well as training the mind (p. 979).

They offer an empirical test: if behaviour reverts when the intervention is removed, it was likely a nudge; if behaviour persists, more likely a boost (p. 981). Boosts must be "explicit, visible, and transparent" because they need the person's cooperation, and are therefore more respectful of autonomy (p. 982). The boost vision includes equipping people for "commercially constructed choice architectures and industry nudges" that mandated disclosure will not reach (p. 981). They do not claim boosts are more effective. That "is an empirical question" (p. 978), "an empirical issue" (p. 983).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and Claim 2 (§9).** The paper is the obvious neighbour the draft must place itself against, and it cuts closer than the draft allows. A convention that changes what a mechanism asks a person to calculate, and shows that it has, would fall in Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff's scheme either among nudges (it changes the choice architecture and leaves competence alone) or among *short-term boosts* (it changes the representation so that the task becomes easier, like natural frequencies, p. 977). The word-separation precedent (P5, "re-represented the page") is a representation change of exactly the natural-frequency kind. On their criteria it is a short-term boost. The draft's claim to be neither nudge nor boost has to answer that.

**Where it helps.** Their reversibility test (p. 981) gives the draft a way to state Claim 2 as a prediction: a DXD convention should change behaviour while in place and leave no competence behind when removed, like a nudge. Unlike a nudge, it does not exploit a bias and it is transparent about what it changed. That matches their "boosts must be transparent" rule (p. 982) on one dimension and the nudge pattern on another. Their remark that boosts aim to equip people against "industry nudges" (p. 981) is near the draft's two-party worry (P12).

## Caution

A conceptual article, with no new data. The examples are drawn from cited studies that were not read here. The authors make no claim that boosts work better than nudges. The scheme concerns a policymaker and one individual. A platform as an interested choice architect appears only in the passing remark about "industry nudges" (p. 981).
