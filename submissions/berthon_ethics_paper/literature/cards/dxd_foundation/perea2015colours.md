# Perea, M., Tejero, P., & Winskel, H. (2015). Can colours be used to segment words when reading? *Acta Psychologica, 159*, 8–13. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2015.05.005

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2015.05.005 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher-formatted PDF from the first author's University of Valencia page, uv.es/mperea/coloursPTW.pdf; journal pagination 8–13; read whole, including the two footnotes reporting extra experiments) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** eye-tracking (EyeLink II, 500 Hz) of 24 University of Valencia students, native Spanish speakers, none colour-blind, reading 120 single-line Spanish sentences (the Perea & Acha 2009 set) in three formats: spaced with alternating red/green words, unspaced with alternating colours, unspaced in one colour; 60 high- and 60 low-frequency target words (pp. 9–10) · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and typography) · **Facets:** W · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues

The question is whether a visual cue other than space can do the space's job without pushing neighbouring words out of high-acuity vision (p. 8). Alternating colour nearly does. Total sentence reading time was 3,701 ms for unspaced one-colour text, 2,293 ms for unspaced alternating-colour text and 2,188 ms for spaced text (p. 10, Table 2), "i.e., a 4.7% reading cost" for colour against spaces (p. 12), where the one-colour unspaced text cost 69% (my arithmetic, 3,701/2,188). Progressive saccades were 9.8, 7.3 and 7.3 per sentence; mean fixation 289, 247 and 234 ms (Table 2). At the word, the frequency effect on gaze duration was 85 ms in one-colour unspaced text against 45 ms with colour and 27 ms with spaces, and the colour and spaced conditions did not differ reliably (p. 11), so "word identification processes were not severely hindered by lack of spaces between words" once colour marked them (p. 12). Landing positions still moved toward word beginnings without spaces (1.7, 1.9, 2.2 on a five-zone scale, p. 11), which the authors read as the low-spatial-frequency role of the space in saccade targeting (p. 12). Comprehension was above 93% in every condition (p. 10). Footnote 1 reports a further 24 readers trained on a 3,000-word unspaced alternating-colour story; spaced text kept "a small (but significant) advantage ... (around 9 words per minute)" (p. 13). Footnote 2 reports 33 skilled Thai readers for whom spaces in one colour imposed a cost (as in Winskel et al. 2009) while alternating colour had no effect (p. 13).

The paper restates its predecessors with numbers. Perea and Acha (2009): spaced 1,856 ms, alternating bold 2,708 ms, unspaced 3,343 ms, "a 31% reading cost" for bold against spaces (p. 9). Rayner, Fischer and Pollatsek (1998): "the lack of spaces between words in English hinders the process of word identification (e.g., lexical effects such as word-frequency are magnified in unspaced sentences relative to spaced sentences) as well as eye movement guidance (e.g., landing sites are closer to the beginning of the word ...)" (p. 8). Its one historical sentence: "Around 1000 A.D., spaces were added between words to help the process of word segmentation (see Saenger, 1997 ...)" (p. 8).

## Relation to the argument

This is the cleanest experimental support for the abstract's phrase "exploiting a representational effect": what the reader needs is a boundary the eye can see, and space is one of several marks that supply it. Colour recovers almost everything; bold recovers less; nothing recovers all of it at the saccade-targeting level. Three consequences for the sentence under test.

- **Supports** "moving boundary detection ... to the page" in the sense that a visual mark relieves the reader of segmentation at a measured cost: 69% longer without any mark in this sample of readers trained on spaces.
- **Narrows** "to the page" to "to any salient visual mark", which matters for the paper's design argument: the Insular innovation is one instance of a class (Zhang & Norman's representational effect), not a unique discovery.
- **Says nothing about the voice.** No condition involved reading aloud; the cost is measured on silent readers.
- **A date to flag.** The authors date word spaces to "around 1000 A.D." on Saenger's authority. That is the Continental adoption, not the seventh-century Insular origin the abstract names; both are in Saenger, and the paper should not borrow this sentence as corroboration.
- Footnote 2 repeats the Thai finding (`winskel2009eye`): readers of an unspaced script are slowed by added spaces, which P4 and D2 already carry.

## Caution

Twenty-four skilled adult readers of one spaced language, single sentences, laboratory conditions; the unspaced cost mixes unfamiliarity with the loss of the cue. The footnoted Thai experiment recorded no eye movements. No child, learner or oral-reading condition. The historical dating is an aside borrowed from Saenger and is not evidence.
