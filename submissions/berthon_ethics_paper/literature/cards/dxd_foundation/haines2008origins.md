# Haines, J. (2008). The origins of the musical staff. *The Musical Quarterly*, 91(3–4), 327–378.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1093/musqtl/gdp002 · **Read depth:** full_text (journal pagination 327–378, from a PDF of the Oxford Advance Access version posted 4 May 2009; figures are images and were not examined, and the 100-plus endnotes were skimmed, not read in full) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** codicological survey; the author reports personally inspecting over one hundred manuscripts, seventh to fifteenth century, and reads pricking and ruling patterns for dates and origins; no counts beyond one table of 13 Carthusian books · **Parties modeled:** other (scribes, orders, readers of notated books) · **Facets:** F · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Haines rejects the claim that Guido of Arezzo invented the staff. He opens with the historians who credit him (Hoppin, Apel, Palisca, Lang, the *Oxford History of Western Music*, pp. 327–328), notes that Burney, Hawkins and Reese had already seen earlier precedent (p. 328), and says "a prototype of the staff existed a century and a half before Guido" in the ninth-century *Musica enchiriadis* (p. 329). Guido's lines, colours and clef letters were all in the *Enchiriadis* tradition. The verdict: "the claim implied by Guido's status as 'the one who invented the staff' is untenable"; his role was "a clever pedagogy of synthesis backed up by a fair bit of self-promotion" (p. 331). Haines also queries the usual reading of Guido's *spisse linee* as "closer together": "spisse" more often means "thick" (p. 331).

The positive account comes from page layout. Parchment was pricked and ruled for text; the staff "arose, in an unspectacular and natural way, from the ruling of the medieval page" (p. 336). Three phases follow.
- Phase one, ninth to eleventh century: scribes add notes within the existing ruling, with no layout designed for music (p. 340). Aquitanian books widen the ruling (up to 20 mm) and shrink the text; Guido's books use several ruled lines for pitch, and "this popularization, then, rather than an invention of the staff, was Guido's historical contribution" (p. 344).
- Phase two, twelfth century: Guido's layout gave music "five or more times" the space of the text, which wasted parchment (p. 346). Scribes in Italy, France and England responded with a set-off or double prick for the text line, and the Carthusians adopted it consistently: of 13 Carthusian music books listed in Table 1 (12th to 13th century), 10 show a 4+2 pricking pattern and three are marked unknown (pp. 353–355).
- Phase three, thirteenth century: an all-red staff drawn independently of the page ruling, two or three rulings for music against one for text (p. 359). Dominican master book BL Add. 23935 (c. 1260) has about 120,000 red staff lines, which Haines shows were drawn one at a time. He argues from five paleographic tests that the multi-nib rake appears only around 1300 (pp. 363–365).

He concludes that the staff was "the product of the anonymous ingenuity of many monks" (p. 368), that Guido "did not invent the musical staff" and introduced nothing substantially new, and that the break from dependence on the text page was the work of "hundreds if not thousands of nameless monks" (p. 369).

## Relation to the argument
This is the closest published parallel to the paper's own correction of a single-inventor story. Haines replaces "Guido invented the staff" with a bundle of layout conventions built by many hands over four centuries, which matches D6 and the argument map's "no longer claims 'the' move". It also reads as a precedent for D1 (design precedent): the staff is a re-representation made through page design, where space for pitch is allotted by ruling. Two cautions for the paper. First, Haines treats the changes as driven by proportion, cost and space on parchment, not by training readers; he never says the staff shortened the learning of chant. Second, he does not mention word separation or Saenger.

Facet check: the facet's two quotations (p. 331 "untenable … self-promotion"; the "popularization" sentence) are exact; the second falls on p. 344. The facet records a partial read; the full text adds that Guido's own contribution is dated only through manuscripts from the late 11th or early 12th century, since no copy of the prologue survives from his time (p. 345), so any claim about what Guido proposed rests on later copies.

## Caution
Figures were not examined. Dating of many books is "within a half century" (p. 347) and the pricking patterns survive in a minority of books because pages were trimmed (p. 337). The count of books is not given beyond Table 1. Haines argues about page layout, not about reading ability; it does not support a claim that the staff taught more people to sing.
