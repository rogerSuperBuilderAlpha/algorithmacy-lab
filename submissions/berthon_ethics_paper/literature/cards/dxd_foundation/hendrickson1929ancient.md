## What it argues

Hendrickson, writing from Yale two years after Balogh, argues that antiquity read aloud because its theory of language made it so: "To the theorists of antiquity ... there was no doubt that a word was a sound, and that sound was necessary to convey sense" (p. 185), citing Augustine's *De magistro* (iv, 8) and *omne verbum sonat*. He opens with a comparison the abstract might use: early readers stood to the page "as most of us stand toward a page of music", which "conveyed meaning only as it was sounded and heard" (p. 184). The evidence is the familiar set. Augustine on Ambrose (*Conf.* 6.3): "for Augustine the manner of reading was only strange, not necessarily difficult or incredible" (p. 186). Acts 8:30, where Philip "heard him reading the prophet Esaias" (p. 186). Horace's *legentem aut tacitum*, which contrasts "sound (legentem) with silence (tacitum)" (p. 187). The Greek habit of using "to hear" for "to read", from Croesus and the Delphic oracle through Plato, Polybius and Plutarch (pp. 188–190), which he calls a sign of the "ear-mindedness" of antiquity against modern "eye-mindedness" (p. 191). Cydippe reading *sine murmure*, Nasica *tacitus leget*, Augustus's knights *taciti* (p. 192).

His verdict is more hedged than Balogh's. Silent reading "was not, therefore, impossible (though the degree of silence is still open to debate); but it not only was unusual, it was accounted an imperfect and defective method of reading" (p. 192), citing Lucian's collector, "your eye outstripping your voice", and Wieland's 1789 note (pp. 192–193). He insists on "many gradations between vocal and silent reading, descending from distinct oral utterance to indistinct murmurs, to whispers, to mere lip motions, and so on through unconscious muscular movements ... to pure eye-reading" (p. 193). The conclusion dates the change: "Throughout antiquity and the Middle Ages reading aloud was the general habit of the learned as well as of the unlearned. Silent reading was unusual, but in what degree exceptional or possible the evidence as yet collected does not permit us to say. With the renewed literary activity of the Renaissance and the invention of printing the educated world went over by degrees to the now universal habit of silent reading" (pp. 193–194). He closes with Egger's *parole intérieure* and laboratory evidence of throat-muscle activity in silent readers (p. 194), and with "phenomenal devourers of books" who seem to skip inner articulation (pp. 194–195).

The article never mentions word separation, *scriptio continua*, spacing or punctuation.

## Relation to the argument

- **Supports** the abstract's premise that ancient reading was normally voiced, from the second founding text of that consensus.
- **Cuts against "forced".** Hendrickson's own explanation is a theory of the word as sound and a culture of the ear, not the layout of the page; he reports Augustine finding silence strange "not necessarily difficult"; and he treats vocal and silent reading as a continuum. None of this is the physiological necessity the abstract's "forced ... to sound out" implies.
- **Cuts against the chronology and the agent.** He places the move to silent reading at the Renaissance and the press, a thousand years after the Irish scribes, and names no change in the page. Facet B's classicists (Knox 1968 onward) later rejected even his "unusual"; the abstract inherits the Balogh–Hendrickson consensus through Saenger without its own dating.
- **Useful to the author** for the music-score analogy (p. 184) and the "gradations" sentence (p. 193), both of which fit a human-factors description of a display that must be sounded to be parsed, if the author wants to keep that frame as a learner's condition rather than a universal one.

## Caution

A 1929 essay by a Latinist that reads as a lecture (n. 1 says it was read to the New York Latin Club in 1921 and to the New Jersey section of the Classical Association of the Middle States "in November last"); the evidence is anecdotal and the author says so ("the evidence as yet collected does not permit us to say", p. 193). It predates Knox, Gavrilov, Burnyeat, Johnson and all eye-movement work on unspaced text. The remarks on the Orient (n. 2, pp. 186–187) and on American farmers (p. 193) are period colour, not evidence. Do not cite it for word separation; it does not discuss it.
