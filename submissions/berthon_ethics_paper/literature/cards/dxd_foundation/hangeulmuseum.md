# National Hangeul Museum. (2015). Read society through its Hangeul letters [Exhibition page, 21 April to 7 June 2015].

## What it argues
The page presents handwritten Hangeul letters as "the dominant form of communication since the invention of Hangeul", written by "all people of Joseon regardless of the social standings from kings to commoners", and says the letters "played an important role in disseminating Hangeul". Sections give examples. A letter from a landlord to his slave is read as implying "the slave could read Hangeul". The earliest surviving Hangeul letter is dated to the 1490s, from Na Sin-geol (1461–1524) to his wife; the Annals of the Joseon Dynasty record letters exchanged "from 15th century". "Most people of the ruling class used Chinese characters while commoners used Hangeul when writing letters." After the 1894 Gabo Reform and the start of postal service, Hangeul letters "became to be the most common way of communication" and schools taught letter writing. The court style of calligraphy (Gungche) developed from the 17th to the late 19th century; some court ladies acted as secretaries and specialised in transcribing Hangeul letters for queens, and the page says the court style "could be further developed by them" (other court ladies were servants or slaves).

On later literacy, the page states: "The illiteracy rate of Korean people was 78% in 1945. It went down to 4.1% in 1958 as the government-led measures to eradicate illiteracy had been implemented." It adds: "it's not easy to figure out the rate accurately because proper surveys had not been taken place until 1990", and reports a 2001 Korean Educational Development Institute study finding 24.8% of adults had difficulty reading, writing and using arithmetic.

## Relation to the argument
Offers a museum's account that a script's spread ran through ordinary use (letters) by non-elites and women. This is one of the "bundle" strands for facet F's claim that a cost-lowering writing system spread through a complex of practices (D6). It does not give a mechanism and cannot show that the script's design caused the spread.
