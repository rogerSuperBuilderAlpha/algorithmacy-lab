## What it argues
Ker reports Johnson's book and endorses it. Johnson objects to "scholars' typically narrow focus on the question of whether the Greeks and Romans were technologically and/or cognitively inhibited from reading silently" (¶1). Johnson's interest in Augustine's account of Ambrose (*Conf.* 6.3.3) lies less in proving that silent reading was known earlier "than to draw attention to Augustine's expectations and the reasons for his surprise" (¶1). Ker quotes Johnson's definition: "reading is not simply the cognitive processing by the individual of the technology of writing, but rather the negotiated construction of meaning within a particular sociocultural context" (¶1, citing Johnson p. 12).

On the point that matters here, Ker says that chapter 2 shows how "certain general characteristics of ancient reading and ancient bookrolls, such as the absence of spaces between words (scriptio continua), are not signs of a technological lack, but are consistent with an elite culture in which reading from a bookroll with full articulation and comprehension is a marker of education and status, and often an act of connoisseurship" (¶2). The case studies (Pliny, Tacitus's *Dialogus*, Galen, Gellius, Fronto, Lucian, the Oxyrhynchus papyri) show "an emphatically social reading culture" in which an elite group shares texts and excludes others (¶3). Ker adds that the model "will no doubt need to be expanded and/or refined," as Johnson concedes at p. 206 (¶4). He closes by comparing the Roman reading circle to a college humanities class (¶5).

## Relation to the argument
Ker is a witness to what Johnson says, not to the evidence behind it. Two uses.

- P1 and its weakest point (whether unspaced script burdened trained readers): the review reports Johnson reading *scriptio continua* as a mark of status, not of a technological deficit. This sits beside Heilmann 2020 and Vatri 2012, who deny a cognitive burden for trained readers. It does not touch the cost for learners (P6), which Johnson's elite-reader frame sets aside.
- Rulings D1, D2 and D6: Johnson gives a social cause for the persistence of unspaced script in antiquity, where Saenger gives a cognitive one. On Ker's account the page was kept hard on purpose, so the Roman case fits the paper's point that a convention serves the people who hold it (P10), though Ker says nothing of Saenger or of any medieval change.
