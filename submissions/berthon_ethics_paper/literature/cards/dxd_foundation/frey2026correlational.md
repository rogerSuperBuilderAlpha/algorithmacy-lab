## What it argues
Frey and colleagues compare three "spans" that the literature often runs together, measuring all three in the same adults under the same letter size and font. The visual span is how many letters a reader can identify in one glance, measured with letter trigrams. The visual attention span is how many letters a reader can report from a brief array. The perceptual span is how much text around a fixation helps reading, measured by moving a window of readable text and masking the rest.

Their own values: visual span 7.83 letters (SD 1.71); visual attention span 5.09 letters (SD 0.42); perceptual span 8.87 letters (SD 3.54), or 4.79 degrees of visual angle (PDF p. 5). Visual span correlated with both others (r = .46 with each); the other two did not correlate with each other (Table 1, PDF p. 5–6). All three correlated with reading time, but in a regression only perceptual span explained variance in reading time beyond the others (ΔR² = .126; Table 4, PDF p. 6–7). The three together explained about one third of the variance in reading time and one quarter of the eye-movement measures (PDF p. 6, 8). The authors warn that this rests on 30 people, that reading speed came from the same task as the perceptual span, and that linguistic ability was not measured, so the link to language "should be considered a hypothesis" (PDF p. 8).

The text the project cares about is a restatement. In the introduction the authors write: "the PS size is estimated to extend about 14-15 characters to the right of fixation and 3-4 characters to the left of fixation, in English" (PDF p. 4). They cite McConkie and Rayner (1975), Rayner and McConkie (1976), Rayner, Well and Pollatsek (1980) and Underwood and McConkie (1985) for it.

## Relation to the argument
The paper is not about word spacing, and it sits on no premise in `ARGUMENT_MAP.md`. It serves facet D only as an open, secondhand restatement of the classical English figures; it adds no evidence for them. The 14–15 and 3–4 figures matter indirectly to P3 (word-boundary cues act in the parafovea), but the primary studies are the ones to cite.

Facet check: facet D uses Frey 2026 as the open source for 14–15 characters right and 3–4 left. That is an accurate quotation. The facet does not mention that the article's own measured perceptual span was 8.87 letters, well below the classical figure. The authors attribute the gap to wider letters (0.54° against 0.46° in earlier work) and suggest the span is more stable in degrees than in letters (PDF p. 8). Anyone quoting "14–15 characters" from Frey should know the same paper finds less in its own sample.

## Caution
An unedited accepted manuscript. Small, homogeneous sample; correlational; adult readers of French, with the English figures only restated. It cannot be cited as a primary source for the size of the English perceptual span, and it should not be used for anything about word spacing.
