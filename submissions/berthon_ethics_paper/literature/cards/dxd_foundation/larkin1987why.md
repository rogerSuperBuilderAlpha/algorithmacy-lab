## What it argues

Larkin and Simon ask when an external diagram beats a sentence-like description of the same problem. They separate two ideas. Two representations are "informationally equivalent" if each can be inferred from the other; they are computationally equivalent only if, in addition, "any inference that can be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the one can also be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the other" (p. 67). A representation is data structures plus the programs that act on them, plus attention management (pp. 67–68). A "sentential" structure is a single sequence; a "diagrammatic" one indexes information by two-dimensional location (p. 68).

Three costs differ between the two: search, recognition, inference. In the pulley problem, the sentential form takes 138 element-considerations across seven inferences (Table 2, p. 77); the diagrammatic form needs "essentially all of the work" to be the seven inferences themselves, because attention moves only to adjacent locations (pp. 79–80). In the geometry proof the larger gap is recognition: the sentence form needs 40 added "perceptual" elements against 5 added by the geometry rules (p. 90), and an average of 24 tests per element searched (p. 90). With a diagram, "the perceptual work of recognition is explicit and extensive" in one form and "automatic and easy" in the other (p. 91). Inference differs little (p. 71). Three further diagrams (supply and demand, free-body, energy-level, pp. 93–97) are used to test that the same properties hold when the diagram is not of a spatial arrangement.

The authors close by hedging the proverb: diagrams "can be better representations not because they contain more information, but because the indexing of this information can support extremely useful and efficient computational processes. But this means that diagrams are useful only to those who know the appropriate computational processes" and who know how to construct a good diagram (p. 99). No arbitrary diagram is worth ten thousand words (p. 98).

## Relation to the argument

Premise P5 (re-representation turns an inferential task into a perceptual one, with the work carried by whoever builds the artefact). The paper supports this in the cleanest formal terms available. The informational/computational pair is on p. 67 (the page ARGUMENT_MAP cites), and the recognition passage is on p. 91 (the page facet E cites); both quotations are accurate. Two further passages help the scribe analogy. Drawing the diagram "makes these new inferences which are then displayed explicitly" (p. 70), so the drawer does the computing once and every later reader gets it free. And the solver "often also needs the knowledge of how to construct a 'good' diagram" (p. 99), which places the skilled work on the maker.

It also bears on P4 (fluent readers gain nothing from added spaces; learners gain most). On pp. 70–71 the authors write that recognition is tied to the exact form: Serbian and Croatian are written in Cyrillic and Roman letters, and readers of one "can read each other's newspapers only with the greatest difficulty"; a representation "is useful only if one has the productions that can use it", which is why physics students may find diagrams "largely useless". This is a mechanism for why a re-representation helps only readers who hold the matching routines, and why switching forms costs a trained reader (p. 71: the specificity "severely limit[s] the immediate substitutability of one representation for another"). It sits close to the Bai et al. result in P4 but is the authors' argument, not a test.

Where it does not help. The paper treats diagrams against sentences, not word division in a script; it says nothing on spacing, reading instruction or history. Applying it to a page is the project's move (ARGUMENT_MAP P5, weakest point). The paper limits itself to "a form of external memory" (p. 97), a solver who reads the artefact; there is no action loop, so it gives no support to D7 (understanding through deciding and ingesting results). It says nothing on D6 (one move or a complex), D3, D4 or D8.

## Caution

Cite it for the vocabulary (informational against computational equivalence; search, recognition, inference as the places a representation can differ) and for the claim that benefit depends on the reader's stored routines. Do not cite it as evidence about writing, spacing or readers: it contains no human data, and the efficiency numbers (138, 40 against 5, 24) are counts from the authors' own hand-run model with assumptions they call "extremely conservative" (p. 92), not measurements. The authors flag mental imagery as speculation (p. 98).
