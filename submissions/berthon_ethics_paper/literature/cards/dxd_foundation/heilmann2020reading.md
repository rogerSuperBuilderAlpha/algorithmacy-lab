## What it argues
Heilmann's aim is narrower than the dispute he enters. Charlesworth treats lectional signs as marks of manuscripts made for public reading aloud; Heilmann says the signs do not show that (pp. 177–178). To get there he first attacks the premise that unspaced script forced vocalised reading. He calls Saenger's book "the most elaborated defense" of that view and says its arguments "cannot carry the burden of proof" and "presuppose precisely what must be proved" (p. 178). He calls the reading-aloud consensus a "quasi-dogma" (p. 179) and replaces the aloud/silent binary with a scale from vocalised to non-vocalised reading (p. 179).

On mechanism (pp. 180–181) he concedes that modern readers of English read unspaced text more slowly, then says the data come "exclusively" from subjects "socialized in modern 'western' scripts and untrained in reading scriptio continua" (p. 180). He cites Epelboim's training studies, then Vatri 2012: "there is no reason to assume that reading unspaced text is a particularly demanding cognitive task in itself, and Saenger's model must be rejected" (p. 181, quoting Vatri 2012, 639). He adds Thai as a nearer analogue than Saenger's Vai syllabary (p. 181). On the ancient sources he reads the passages Saenger-side scholars cite as about learners: they "should rather be interpreted within the context of the process of learning to read" (p. 181), and Greek papyri that separate words or syllables "are school exercises" (n. 28, p. 181). On Rome (pp. 182–183) he follows Wingo and the Vindolanda tablets to show that Latin writers used word dividers into the second century CE, so unspaced script "is not a characteristic of deficient writing systems" (p. 182). His conclusion: "We should be careful not to project our difficulties with reading scriptio continua onto ancient readers" (p. 183). The rest of the chapter (pp. 183–190) argues that diacritics, apostrophes, paragraphoi and nomina sacra served visual recognition and disambiguation, not performance.

## Relation to the argument
This is the strongest published dissent from premise P1 (`ARGUMENT_MAP.md`: trained readers supplied the boundaries, with Vatri via Heilmann at p. 181 as its weakest point). It does not decide ruling D2. Heilmann does not argue that spacing *shortened the training* of readers, which is the claim D2 waits on scholars to supply. His own note 28 points the other way: the ancient complaints are about learners, which is the cost-for-novices reading that P4 and P6 need. He also concedes the slowing for untrained moderns (p. 180). So the chapter weakens "unspaced script burdened trained readers" and leaves "spacing eased learners" untouched.

It also bears on P2/P3. The Latin evidence (interpuncts, then their decline) shows that spacing was not a one-time invention in the seventh century. The paper should not write "the scribes' move" as if no earlier practice existed.

## Caution
Heilmann argues a thesis about New Testament manuscripts, and the Saenger critique is a few pages in service of it. He summarises the eye-movement work through Vatri and does not run or reanalyse any of it. His inference from the Roman return to unspaced script rebuts a physiological deficiency, not Saenger's social account (a community can drop an aid for reasons of status). The note on Oliver gives no full reference. Do not cite him for the claim that spacing made no difference to anyone.

## Also used by facet W (2026-10-15)

Facet W takes two points. First, Heilmann's distinction between reading "with vocalization, subvocalization, or without vocalization", the first two lying "on a scale from loud to quiet" (p. 179), replaces the aloud/silent binary the abstract's sentence assumes (S2 correction: the earlier wording was not a quotation, 2026-10-15); Hendrickson (1929, p. 193) had already described "many gradations". Second, his n. 28 (p. 181), that the ancient passages about laboured reading of unspaced text "should rather be interpreted within the context of the process of learning to read", is the reading facet W recommends for the abstract: unspaced text taxed learners, and the Insular scribes were learners of Latin. His concession that moderns untrained in *scriptio continua* read it slowly (p. 180) is what the Perea and Rayner experiments measure.
