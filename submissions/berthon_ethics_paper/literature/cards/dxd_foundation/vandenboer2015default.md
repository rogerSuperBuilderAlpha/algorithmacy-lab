# van den Boer, M., & Hakvoort, B. E. (2015). Default spacing is the optimal spacing for word reading. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 68*(4), 697–709.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/17470218.2014.964272 · **Read depth:** full_text (published version, UvA-DARE repository copy; journal pagination 697–709, PDF page N = journal page 695 + N; the Table 1 overview of earlier spacing studies was skimmed, not read cell by cell) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** word-naming experiment, Dutch children, 105 in Grade 2 and 92 in Grade 4, each naming 144 words (monosyllabic or bisyllabic) in six interletter-spacing conditions (−0.5, default, +0.5, +1, +1.5, +2 points), 24 words per condition, voice-key timing; a subsample of 29 poor readers (15 in Grade 2, 14 in Grade 4) analysed separately · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and single word) · **Facets:** D · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Van den Boer and Hakvoort test the idea that more space between letters helps children read, especially weak readers, by crowding reduction. They point out that findings are split: lexical-decision studies show gains for children and adults, while word-naming and sentence studies of typical readers mostly show none, and adults sometimes slow down (Introduction, pp. 697–699). Their own experiment covers a range of spacings on both sides of the default.

Results (Results, pp. 704–705):
- Accuracy fell only at −0.5: χ²(1) = 12.174, p < .001, with no difference by grade or word length; the other spacings did not differ from default.
- Naming rate was lower at −0.5 (χ²(1) = 6.308, p = .012) and slightly higher at +0.5 (χ²(1) = 6.257, p = .012) than at default, for both grades and both word lengths.
- Combined fluency (items per second times proportion correct): only −0.5 differed from default (F(1,193) = 23.835, p < .001). The +0.5 speed gain did not show up in this combined measure; the authors say the faster rate "goes hand in hand with a small decrease in accuracy" (p. 706).
- The 29 poor readers behaved the same way: fluency lower at −0.5 (F(1,28) = 5.500, p = .026), no gain at any widening.

The authors read the −0.5 deficit in all groups as a crowding effect, and the absence of gains up to +2 as evidence that "default LS is the optimal LS for children's word reading fluency" (Discussion, p. 706). The abstract states: "We conclude that an increase in interletter spacing has no effect on word naming fluency" (p. 697). They offer reasons the lexical-decision gains may not transfer to naming, and suggest spacing might act at sentence or text level, where crowding from neighbouring words matters more (Discussion, p. 707).

## Relation to the argument
It concerns letter spacing in single words. It enters facet D as the null-or-negative result against "more space, easier reading", and it supplies the useful asymmetry: tightening hurts all readers alike, loosening within a modest range helps none. That bears on ruling D6 and on premise P6 only by analogy: a spacing intervention can lower a cost only where the default is already tight enough to cost something, and the groups least able to read did not gain more. Nothing here concerns word boundaries, so it gives no support to P1 to P5. Under ruling D10 it is a reason not to cite "spacing helps struggling readers" as a general fact without naming the unit (letter, word, line).

## In conversation with
- `perea2012effects` — Perea, Panadero, Moret-Tatay & Gómez (2012), the lexical-decision gains for skilled adults, young readers and dyslexic readers that this study fails to extend to naming (Introduction).
- `perea2012subtle` — cited among sentence-reading studies where typical adults show no gain (Introduction).
- `zorzi2012extra` — the extra-large-spacing finding for dyslexia (Introduction and Discussion).
- `cohen2008reading` — fMRI evidence that large spacing shifts word reading to a serial dorsal route at +2.25 and +3 (Introduction).
- `hakvoort2017improvements` — the same authors' later sentence-level study, which finds an accuracy gain; the two papers agree that speed does not change.
- Leads not in the bib: Risko, Lanthier & Besner (2011) and Vinckier et al. (2011), who report slowing from wide spacing in adults; Tai et al. (2006), fewer but longer fixations; Legge & Bigelow (2011) on the range of print sizes; Spinelli et al. (2002) on crowding in dyslexic children.

## Caution
Single-word naming, not sentences or text. Grade 2 and Grade 4 children, Dutch, one proportional font. Six pre-set spacings in one font (14-point Times New Roman); the passages I read do not state the unit of the spacing steps, so the results do not map onto em fractions. The poor-reader group is small (N = 29, spread across two grades and two word lengths). The authors note that effects of spacing may differ at sentence level and that crowding was never measured directly (Discussion).
