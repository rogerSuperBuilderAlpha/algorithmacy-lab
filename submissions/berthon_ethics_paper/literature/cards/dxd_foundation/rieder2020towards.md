## What it argues

The authors start from a regulatory habit, "opening the black box" through disclosure, explanation and audit, and say it fails for two reasons. First, transparency as information disclosure carries untested steps: from disclosure to visibility, to insight, to "effective regulatory judgement" (p. 5). It also does not simply reveal: in Flyverbom's words, quoted by the authors, "transparency reconfigures - rather than reproduces its objects and subjects" (p. 5). Second, platforms resist the image. Machine-learning decisions cannot "be easily traced back to singular factors" (p. 7), the algorithm is spread across many components and keeps changing, so audits face a "snapshot logic" (p. 7), and "platform companies are therefore neither fully in control of actual outcomes, nor fully aware what is happening within their systems" (p. 8).

They propose observability: "Unlike transparency, which nominally describes a state that may exist or not, observability emphasises the conditions for the practice of observing in a given domain" (p. 3). They set out three principles: widen the normative and analytical horizon, with "public interest" as the reference norm (pp. 10-13); observe platform behaviour continuously, through data-access agreements, accountability interfaces, developer APIs and scraping or volunteer plug-ins (pp. 13-18); and build the capacity for collaborative knowledge creation, with qualitative methods, journalists and NGOs, public funding, "centres of expertise" and a "European Platform Observatory" (section 3.3 and the Conclusion, pp. 18-24; the "centres of expertise" and Observatory passages are on p. 23). The article is explicit that observability is "not meant to be a radical departure from the call for transparency" (p. 21).

One point the authors press repeatedly concerns who controls the access. Of the data-sharing initiatives they discuss (Facebook, Social Science One), the authors say they "have been plagued by delays and restrictions," and report that scholars "have rightfully criticised that the scope and modalities for access remain in the hands of platforms themselves" (p. 15; the second clause is attributed to Hegelich 2020 and Suzor et al. 2019). Without a legal framework, "companies can not only start and stop such initiatives at will but are also able to control parameters" such as scope and granularity (p. 15). In the conclusion they say of Twitter's new academic API that "these efforts are still voluntary and can be revoked at any time" (p. 22).

## Relation to the argument

The article gives the academic meaning of "observability", the word Nadella's post uses for its seven principles. It bears on the argument at two points.

First, premise P10 (a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention). Rieder and Hofmann give a documented case in a neighbouring domain: access that the platform grants, sets the terms of and can end at will is not accountability. They treat the fix as law, not goodwill. This supports the step from conflict of interest to "cannot withdraw alone" that the argument map marks `[inf]`, though their example is data access for researchers, not a reading convention for users.

Second, the seat of the observer. In their usage the observers are outsiders to the platform and the platform holds the power. In the post the deployer observes a model that serves it. The word is the same and the seat is opposite, which bears on ruling D4 (the author's contrast with the post) and D5.

Facet check: facet G says Rieder and Hofmann "agree with Nadella that the black box will not be opened; both choose observation of behaviour." Half right. They agree the box will not simply open, and they favour observing behaviour over time (pp. 13-14). But they do not choose observation in place of transparency; they say it is not "a radical departure" from it (p. 21) and that traditional disclosure, reports and whistleblowing can remain "part of an observability framework" (p. 13). The facet read the article in part; the full text adds the access-control argument above, which the facet did not record.

## Caution

A policy-oriented conceptual article from 2020. It concerns outside scrutiny of platforms (EU Digital Services Act debate), not people using a platform to coordinate with each other, and it predates language-model agents. Its many examples are named projects described in a sentence or two, not evaluated. It makes no empirical claim that observability works.
