## What it argues
The authors make four contributions (pp. 429–30, 434–39). Law already shapes whether humans stay in or leave algorithmic decisions, by requiring, encouraging, discouraging or (so far, to their knowledge) never prohibiting them (Part II, pp. 446–59). Regulators fall into the "MABA-MABA trap" (Part III, pp. 460–73), which they describe as "an assumption that is intuitive, wrong, and dangerous: that human-machine systems represent the best of both worlds and don't introduce new issues of their own" (p. 438). Policymakers put humans in the loop for nine different reasons, which the authors sort into corrective, resilience, justificatory, dignitary, accountability, stand-in, friction, "warm body" and interface roles (Part IV, pp. 473–87). And regulators should state the role, weigh context and "regulate the human-in-the-loop system" (abstract, p. 430; Part V, pp. 487–509).

They define the human in the loop narrowly, as "an individual who is involved in a single, particular decision made in conjunction with an algorithm" (p. 440), and say the definition does not require the human to be effective (p. 442). They then argue that the narrow frame misleads, because humans also design, train, deploy and evaluate the system (pp. 443–44).

The evidence for failure is borrowed. The authors list rubber-stamping, automation bias, "skill fade," biased use of advice and "the basic tautological challenge of relying on humans to monitor the performance of systems designed to improve on human performance" (p. 469), citing Green, Parasuraman and Manzey, and Bainbridge. They add new roles that "tax humans with tasks that run into known human weaknesses, such as sustaining vigilance" (p. 472). Case figures: Autopilot aborted control less than one second before impact in sixteen known instances (p. 438); Michigan's MIDAS showed a ninety-three percent error rate with no human, against forty-four percent when a human reviewed (p. 475). Their positive models are U.S. railroad, nuclear and medical-device rules that require human-factors design, training and organisational planning (pp. 492–97). The 2021 draft EU AI Act is their anti-example: users carry no direct oversight duties, and the Act may overload or "set up" the overseer (pp. 503–05).

## Relation to the argument
**P12 (people perform sustained monitoring badly).** The paper restates the case through Bainbridge and Green and adds a legal mechanism: liability rules push a human into the loop without power to affect the outcome (pp. 452–53, 483). It reports no new measurement, so it does not add independent evidence to P12. It does give P12 a cleaner target than "people are bad at monitoring": the failure lies in the system design that leaves the person the residue.

**P11 (the Nadella post leaves a person to read evidence and to halt).** Crootof et al. supply the test the argument lacks. A halt right is a resilience role (p. 478); a person who answers for the system is an accountability role, and the person may be held responsible "legitimately" only "if the human in the loop has the power, information, judgment, and time to make the final decision" (pp. 482–83). Otherwise the person is a "liability sponge" or "moral crumple zone" (p. 483). Whether the post's authorised person has those four things is for the post to settle; I did not re-read it here.

**The paper's two tests (what task the person keeps; whom the move serves).** The "what task does the person keep" test finds second-hand support: the authors quote Dekker and Woods (nn. 200–205) that automation "creates new human strengths and weaknesses" and that the question is "How do we get along together?" (p. 472). The bib's `dekker2002maba` is not accessible, so these are the only sentences from it on the project's shelf. The "whom does it serve" test finds a legal parallel in Part II.B.2: designers keep a human nominally in the loop to absorb liability.

**D7 (understanding through deciding).** The paper cuts against any easy reading. Handoff in a split-second emergency "may cause deadly delay or errors" (p. 437), and their own advice is to design for alerts, enough time to assess the situation, and smooth transfer of control (pp. 439, 499).

## Caution
A legal-scholarship synthesis; every empirical claim about human performance is cited to another author. It examines the 2021 draft EU AI Act, not the adopted Regulation. Its safety-critical examples come from heavy regulatory regimes, which the authors say may not suit other settings (p. 492). The "human in the loop" is the deployer's overseer of one decision, not the person acted on or a counterpart in a three-party exchange; it says little about platforms that mediate between users. The copy read is a layout proof; wording may differ slightly from the final volume.
