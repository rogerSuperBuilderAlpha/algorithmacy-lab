## What it argues

Douek says the Board won acceptance and failed at the tasks that justified it. It has "not explained the normative framework" behind its speech decisions, measures impact with easy proxies, and avoids the hardest cases (pp. 379–380). Her explanation is institutional. Meta built the Board mainly to buy legitimacy: it was "intended to outsource the decision-making, not necessarily to make it better or more speech protective" (p. 385), and its decisions "have force only insofar and for as long as Meta ... agrees they do" (p. 375).

She credits the Board with real independence. Funding sits in an irrevocable trust ($130 million, then $150 million) and independent trustees alone can remove members (pp. 383–384, 397). The Board refused Meta's argument that fixing an error moots a case (p. 398). Meta declined 50 of 251 recommendations (pp. 401–402; 201 accepted, by my subtraction).

The numbers cut the other way. In 2022 the Board received about 3,537 appeals a day against 35,608,178 pieces of content Meta actioned daily in the last quarter and 140,598 internal appeals a day (p. 393); 2.5% of internal appellants went on to the Board. It issued 45 decisions in three years, where the Supreme Court issued 60 full opinions in its 2021 term (p. 394). Meta judged its own original decision wrong in 32 of 50 shortlisted cases in 2022 (n. 139, p. 396), so many "overturns" are corrections Meta agrees with. The Board tracks whether Meta implements recommendations, not what the changes do (p. 426). It has said a post should come down eight times (p. 429).

Part IV gives a realist reading. The Board is "a servant of two masters" (p. 437): it must look tough without provoking Meta, so it sends the Trump suspension, COVID-19 policy and nudity questions back to Meta (pp. 431–433). Douek compares this to courts in authoritarian regimes (pp. 387, 439) and ends: "Watchdogs also need to be watched and made to show their work" (p. 444).

## Relation to the argument

D8 (courts, FX markets, platforms; "where the convention bends") and P10. This is the best-read source in facet J for a convention that the platform's owner created and can stop honouring: Meta's support is "contingent on these returns being greater than the costs of complying" (p. 439), and if compliance gets too costly "there is no way of forcing Meta to continue to pay attention" (p. 439). That supports P10. It is complicated by the Board's real independence inside a narrow remit and by Meta's acceptance of most recommendations.

The remit matters most. The Board "does not have the power to review decisions Meta makes about accounts, advertising, events, groups, amplification, or the platforms' features and affordances" (p. 387), and it has no engineers (p. 384). The layer where the DXD triad runs lies outside the court-shaped convention. ## Caution

One arithmetic slip: p. 393 says appeals are "significantly less than 0.0001%" of Meta's actions, but 3,537 / 35,608,178 is about 0.0099% (roughly 1 in 10,000); the 2.5% figure checks (3,537 / 140,598 = 2.52%).
