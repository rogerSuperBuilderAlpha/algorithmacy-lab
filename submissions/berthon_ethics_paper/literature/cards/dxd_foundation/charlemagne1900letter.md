## What it argues

The king tells Abbot Baugulf that bishoprics and monasteries should be zealous "in the culture of letters" (p. 12), so that "those who desire to please God by living rightly should not neglect to please him also by speaking correctly" (p. 13; Latin "ut, qui Deo placere appetunt recte vivendo, ei etiam placere non negligant recte loquendo", Boretius p. 79). The trigger is the monasteries' own correspondence: in letters sent to the court "we have recognized in most of these letters both correct thoughts and uncouth expressions" (p. 13; Latin "et sensus rectos et sermones incultos"), because "the tongue, uneducated on account of the neglect of study, was not able to express in the letter without error" (p. 13). The stake is Scripture: the king fears that "as the skill in writing was less, so also the wisdom for understanding the Holy Scriptures might be much less" (p. 13; Latin "sicut minor erat in scribendo prudentia"). He asks for teachers chosen for "the will and the ability to learn" (p. 13), and enforces the request through favour: "Do not neglect, therefore, if you wish to have our favor, to send copies of this letter to all your suffragans and fellow-bishops and to all the monasteries" (p. 14).

The letter is about correct Latin composition and study. It says nothing about copying books, scriptoria, script, layout, punctuation or word separation.

## Relation to the argument

**P10 and ruling D8 (who stood behind the page).** The letter shows a ruler setting a standard of correct written language and judging monastic writing against it, which supports the draft's point that an authority stood over the writers. It does not show an order to scriptoria to produce correct *books*; that comes from the *Admonitio generalis* c. 72 (`boretius1883capitularia`) and from Alcuin's poem (`dummler1881alcuini`).

## Caution

Read in a 1900 English translation (web transcription, then Munro's printed pages in OCR) and the Latin in OCR of Boretius. The date is a range (c. 780–800): Boretius sets it by Baugulf's abbacy (780–802) and the title "rex" (before 801), and finds Sirmond's dating to 787 weakly founded (p. 78). Boretius also judges that the letter went to the metropolitans and that the Fulda copy survives in a single twelfth-century manuscript, and he treats the bracketed last sentence on monks and courts as foreign to the letter (pp. 78–79). The letter concerns correct expression and study, so it cannot be cited for any claim about book copying or the layout of the page.
