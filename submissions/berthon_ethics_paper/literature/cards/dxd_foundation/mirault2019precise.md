## What it argues
Mirault, Snell and Grainger test the claim that skilled readers read unspaced text through efficient bottom-up word identification. If so, anything that degrades word identification should hurt more when no spaces are present. They degrade identification by transposing adjacent consonants inside words (e.g. "cuold" for "could" in Rayner et al.'s English paradigm), three words per sentence (p. 848).

They set out the baseline: "reading unspaced text is slower by about 40–70% relative to reading normally spaced text (Rayner & Pollatsek, 1996; Rayner et al., 1998)" (p. 846). Removal of spaces disrupts "saccade programming and word identification" (p. 846).

Results, Exp. 1 (silent reading): probe-word accuracy was 98.47% with spaces and 89.75% without (b = 2.91, t = 6.97; p. 849). On the three critical words the transposition cost in total viewing time was 83 ms unspaced against 67 ms spaced (interaction significant, p. 849). Sentence reading time and self-rated difficulty showed a larger transposition cost without spaces (reading time, interaction b = 0.02, t = 2.06, p. 850; difficulty, b = 21.29, t = 5.87, p. 851). Skipping went the other way: transposed words were skipped more in unspaced text and less in spaced text, an interaction the authors read tentatively (pp. 853, 856). The first-fixation and gaze-duration interactions were not significant. The interaction was strongest when the first or last letters of the target words were rare, which the authors read as the loss of a boundary cue (p. 853).

Exp. 2 (reading aloud) gave the same pattern more cleanly: speed interaction b = 54.29, t = 13.11; accuracy interaction b = 22.32, t = 14.29 (p. 854). Participants with higher vocabulary and spelling scores read unspaced text faster and more accurately, but not spaced text (p. 855).

The authors conclude that "efficient bottom-up orthographic processing and word identification in the absence of word boundary information" is "the primary factor at play" in how readers manage unspaced text (Conclusion, p. 856).

## Relation to the argument
It bears on premises P3 and P4 and on ruling D2 (the dispute over whether unspaced script burdened trained readers). First, it repeats the 40–70% cost figure in a modern French sample, and the facet's flag on that range ("[unverified]") can now be resolved to this page. Second, it shows what the cost is made of: the skilled reader does the segmenting by recognising words, so the load falls on lexical and orthographic skill (vocabulary and spelling predicted performance only for unspaced text). That supports the reading that spacing moves segmentation work from the reader to the page (P5), and it supports the idea that the benefit is largest for readers whose lexical skill is thinner, though the sample here is all university students and no learners were tested. It also supports P4's caution that skilled readers adapt, since the cost is large in speed but readers still read.

It does not test historical readers, other scripts, or the effect on learning to read. Under ruling D10 it is analogical background.

Facet check: the facet lists this paper as "not read", relying on a secondary summary that transpositions hurt unspaced reading more. The full text confirms that summary, and adds that the interaction was absent in first-fixation and gaze duration and was weaker when boundary letters were frequent.

## Caution
Exp. 2 is underpowered by the authors' own admission (footnote 5, p. 853). The sentences are neutral, seven-word French sentences with low predictability and one-line presentation, unlike running text. Participants are French university students used to spaced text. The vocabulary and spelling effects are correlational. The paper tests degradation by letter transposition; it does not show that spacing makes reading easier to learn.
