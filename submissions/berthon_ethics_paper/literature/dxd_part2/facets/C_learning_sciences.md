# C — Learning sciences: scaffolding and fading, cognitive load, productive failure, boosting, and mental models of moving systems

*Facet C of the Part 2 literature review for Decision Experience Design (DXD). Agent-drafted on 2026-10-01 (a research agent working in the `wt-dxd` worktree). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** What the learning sciences, human factors and HCI know about how people acquire operational skill with a complex interactive system, and under what conditions designed support (scaffolds, training wheels, worked examples, tutorials, onboarding, feedback) produces skill that outlasts the support. The facet follows the outline's RQ-C1 to RQ-C5: (1) scaffolding, fading, cognitive apprenticeship and training wheels, and the feedback and predictability conditions under which fading works; (2) cognitive load theory, worked examples and the expertise reversal effect, including the risk that permanent scaffolds hurt experts; (3) productive failure and legitimate peripheral participation as models for learning under mandatory participation; (4) whether boosting has been applied to operational competence with algorithmic systems rather than to information literacy; (5) mental models and learning a system whose rules move. I added two adjacent blocks the outline's Section 7 needs: the recent experimental evidence on learning with generative AI, and the automation-training literature. **The question the synthesis answers:** does the learning-sciences evidence license the Part 2 obligation (display the declared operating envelope, give a cheap channel for intent, make tests cheap, fade scaffolds, keep updates backward-compatible), and which pitch or outline claims does it contradict or complicate?

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the open-access (OA) full text this session, or the entry reuses a repo card built from full text. *Read in part* = OA full text obtained and the cited passages located and read, but not the whole paper. *Abstract only* = a database abstract or record only; no body text was read, and no page locators are claimed. *Not accessible* = no OA text and no abstract in any record I could reach. "pdf p." marks pages of an OA PDF or preprint that may not match the version of record. "Repo card" means an existing card in a lab library, whose read status I carry over unchanged and did not re-verify against the source.

**Tiers.** Each entry carries a tier. *A* = the Part 2 draft can rely on it for the stated claim. *B* = context, or it complicates; cite with the caveat given. *C* = canon cited for lineage only; I could not read it, so no specific claim should be drawn from it until a database record is matched.

**How many entries.** The brief asked for 20 to 35. There are 47, because five research questions plus the two adjacent blocks each needed at least one primary source and one counterweight. Each entry's tier is marked; the Tier A entries are the ones the draft can lean on.

### B1. Scaffolding, fading, training wheels and cognitive apprenticeship (RQ-C1)

**wood1976role** — Wood, D., Bruner, J. S., & Ross, G. (1976). The role of tutoring in problem solving. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 17*(2), 89–100. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x
- *Verification:* Crossref (volume 17, issue 2, pages 89–100; author list Wood, Bruner, Ross).
- *Read status:* **not accessible.** The Wiley PDF returned an HTML challenge page. Crossref, Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar hold no abstract, and the OpenAlex abstract belonged to a different work.
- *What it argues:* I did not read it. From the title and its standing in the literature, it is the origin paper for "scaffolding" in tutoring. I cannot supply its six tutor functions or any quotation from this session's reading, so nothing below depends on them.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* none. Do not attribute "fading" to this paper. The reviews I did read (van de Pol et al. 2010, below) treat contingency, fading and transfer of responsibility as the later-identified defining characteristics of scaffolding; whether Wood et al. use the word "fading" I could not check.
- *Bears on:* the outline's Section 7 claim (i) "scaffolding that fades (Wood, Bruner & Ross)". **Complicates:** the attribution of fading to this paper is unverified. Tier C.

**vandepol2010scaffolding** — van de Pol, J., Volman, M., & Beishuizen, J. (2010). Scaffolding in teacher–student interaction: A decade of research. *Educational Psychology Review, 22*(3), 271–296. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6
- *Verification:* Crossref; Semantic Scholar abstract. Springer's PDF is hybrid OA, but the script fetch returned a 3 KB wall page.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* A review of the decade's scaffolding literature that "discerned" "contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility" "as the three key characteristics of scaffolding"; it finds that "the small number of effectiveness studies available" suggest scaffolding "is effective" and that "the main challenge in scaffolding research appears to be its measurement" (abstract).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* the three-part definition (abstract). The definition matters because contingency means the support is tuned to the learner's current understanding. A uniform on-screen display is not contingent in that sense.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i) and (iii). **Supports** treating fading as a defining feature, and **complicates** calling a static envelope display a "scaffold": by this review's definition it would be a standing support unless it adapts. Tier A for the definition; the evidence base it summarizes is teacher–student, not interface.

**reiser2004scaffolding** — Reiser, B. J. (2004). Scaffolding complex learning: The mechanisms of structuring and problematizing student work. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 13*(3), 273–304. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1303_2
- *Verification:* Crossref; Semantic Scholar abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* Software scaffolds work through two complementary mechanisms. They structure the task, "guiding learners through key components and supporting their planning and performance," and they problematize it, forcing "learners to engage with this complexity," which makes the task "more difficult in the short term" but "more productive" for learning. The two mechanisms are "sometimes complementary and sometimes in tension" (abstract).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* the abstract's statement that some scaffolds deliberately add difficulty.
- *Bears on:* Section 4 and 7. **Complicates** the frictionless-adoption critique in one useful way: the learning sciences already distinguish friction that teaches (problematizing) from friction that merely taxes (verification burden). The claim must say which kind the conversational interface imposes. Tier A.

**belland2017synthesizing** — Belland, B. R., Walker, A. E., Kim, N. J., & Lefler, M. (2017). Synthesizing results from empirical research on computer-based scaffolding in STEM education: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 87*(2), 309–344. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316670999 (online 2016)
- *Verification:* Crossref (volume 87, issue 2, pages 309–344, first issued 2016); Consensus abstract. A pilot version is Belland et al. (2015), *Journal of Educational Technology & Society*, Consensus abstract only.
- *Read status:* abstract only (the SAGE PDF is hybrid OA but returned a wall page).
- *What it argues:* A random-effects meta-analysis of "144 experimental studies (333 outcomes)" finds computer-based scaffolding "showed a consistently positive (ḡ = 0.46) effect on cognitive outcomes". It reports that scaffolding's effect "did not vary on the basis of context-specificity, presence or absence of scaffolding change, and logic by which scaffolding change is implemented," and was "greatest when measured at the principles level and among adult learners" (abstract). The 2015 pilot had reported that "studies with no fading had higher effect sizes than studies with fixed fading" and that "fading or lack thereof explained 30% of the variability in outcomes"; the 2014 poster found "performance-adapted fading/adding led to significantly higher effect sizes than fixed fading" (Consensus abstracts).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* the full meta-analysis finding that presence or absence of scaffolding change did not moderate cognitive outcomes (abstract). The pilot's contrary result is superseded and should not be cited as a finding.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i) and (iii) "fade the scaffold". **Complicates:** the strongest meta-analytic evidence says scaffolds help, and that whether they are faded does not reliably change outcome. It does not say fading harms. The population is STEM students working on problem-centered curricula, not adults operating a system they cannot leave. Tier A.

**tawfik2018effect** — Tawfik, A. A., Law, V., Ge, X., Xing, W., & Kim, K. (2018). The effect of sustained vs. faded scaffolding on students' argumentation in ill-structured problem solving. *Computers in Human Behavior, 87*, 436–449. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2018.01.035
- *Verification:* Crossref (five authors: Tawfik, Law, Ge, Xing, Kim); Consensus abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* Two fading schedules for question prompts, sustained and faded, produced no difference on a rubric measure of argumentation, but "when using learning analytics to examine the conceptual linkages of students' knowledge structure, … students in the sustained scaffolding condition performed significantly better than the students in the faded condition." The authors conclude "scaffolds should not be faded before students have acquired the necessary problem-solving processes" and "novices might need a fuller set of scaffolds for a longer period" (abstract).
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (iii). **Complicates** a calendar-driven fade: premature fading hurt on one measure. Pairs with the expertise-reversal evidence below, which says the opposite error (leaving the scaffold in place) also costs. Tier B (one study, one task type).

**nuckles2010expertise** — Nückles, M., Hübner, S., Dümer, S., & Renkl, A. (2010). Expertise reversal effects in writing-to-learn. *Instructional Science, 38*(3), 237–258. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9106-9
- *Verification:* Crossref (issued online 2009; volume 38, issue 3 is 2010); Consensus abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* Two longitudinal studies of journal writing. Permanent prompts raised strategy use early, but late in term "the experimental group lost its superiority." In Experiment 2, "a gradual and adaptive fading-out of the prompts" meant that at term's end "the permanent prompts group showed substantially lower learning outcomes than the fading group." The authors explain it as redundant external guidance that "induced extraneous cognitive load" once students "internalized the desired strategies" (abstract).
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i)-(iii). **Supports** adaptive fading, in a design that tied the fade to learner competence. Tankelevitch et al. (2024, below) cite the same result in the GenAI setting. Tier A for the mechanism, B for transfer (university students, a writing task).

**carroll1984training** — Carroll, J. M., & Carrithers, C. (1984). Training wheels in a user interface. *Communications of the ACM, 27*(8), 800–806. https://doi.org/10.1145/358198.358218
- *Also:* Carroll, J. M., & Carrithers, C. (1984). Blocking learner error states in a training-wheels system. *Human Factors, 26*(4), 377–389. https://doi.org/10.1177/001872088402600402 (key `carroll1984blocking`). Catrambone, R., & Carroll, J. M. (1987). Learning a word processing system with training wheels and guided exploration. *Proceedings of CHI+GI '87*, 169–174. https://doi.org/10.1145/29933.275625 (key `catrambone1987learning`; Crossref issue-year 1986, conference year 1987).
- *Verification:* Crossref for all three; Consensus abstracts.
- *Read status:* abstract only for all three (the ACM PDF returned a wall page).
- *What it argues:* A training interface that made "typical and troublesome error states 'unreachable'" "afforded substantially faster learning coupled with better learning achievement and better performance on a comprehension post-test," while "a control group spent almost a quarter of their time recovering from the error states that the training interface blocked off" (CACM abstract). The 1987 follow-up reports that "training wheels subjects were better able to perform advanced full-system editing functions than subjects who were trained on the full system itself," so the reduced system transferred.
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* the two quoted abstract findings. Note the mechanism: the support worked by removing the learner's access to part of the system.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i), the training-wheels claim. **Supports** that restriction accelerates early skill and transfers. **Complicates** the DXD use: training wheels restrict what the novice may do, and the designer decides which functions to lock. Under mandatory participation, a lock imposed by the intermediary's owner is a power question that structural refusal and counter-delegation must govern; the learning-sciences result is silent on it. Tier A for the empirical claim.

**leutner2000double** — Leutner, D. (2000). Double-fading support — a training approach to complex software systems. *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 16*(4), 347–357. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2729.2000.00147.x
- *Verification:* Crossref (issued 2000; Consensus lists a 2008 deposit date, which I ignored).
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* Two 30-hour CAD training experiments. In Experiment 1 (88 participants), with a deeply structured menu system, "participants working with the initially reduced software outperformed participants of the full software functionality group" and "participants of the slowly faded guidance group outperformed participants receiving medium, fast or no fading of guidance." In Experiment 2 (120 participants), with an icon-based interface "in which most of relevant functions are permanently visible," the approach was "less effective"; the author suggests "the two factors (fading out the locking of software's functionality and fading out detailed guidance) overcompensate each other" (abstract).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* the slow-fading result and the Experiment 2 boundary.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (iii). **Supports** fading, **complicates** its generality: the schedule and the interface structure both mattered, and fading two supports at once failed in one design. Tier A.

**renkl2003structuring** — Renkl, A., & Atkinson, R. K. (2003). Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in cognitive skill acquisition: A cognitive load perspective. *Educational Psychologist, 38*(1), 15–22. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3
- *Also:* Atkinson, R. K., Renkl, A., & Merrill, M. M. (2003). *Journal of Educational Psychology, 95*(4), 774–783. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774 (key `atkinson2003transitioning`; Consensus abstract only).
- *Verification:* Crossref (the Crossref first-author field gave "Renkl"; the second author "Atkinson" comes from the Consensus record). Consensus abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it argues:* Worked examples are very effective early in skill acquisition; later, problem solving is superior. The authors propose "a fading procedure in which problem-solving elements are successively integrated into example study until the learners are expected to solve problems on their own," grounded in intrinsic load that "gradually decreases" (abstract). The companion 2002 paper (Renkl, Atkinson, Maier, & Staley, *Journal of Experimental Education*, doi 10.1080/00220970209599510) found fading helped near transfer, and that backward fading (omitting the last steps first) beat forward fading. The 2003 JEP paper reports the fading effect "is not reliable on far-transfer tasks" unless paired with self-explanation prompts.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i). **Supports** the cognitive-load rationale for fading in well-structured domains (mathematics, physics, programming). **Complicates** transfer to an adaptive intermediary: the effect is shown for near transfer in domains with stable rules. Tier A for the mechanism.

### B2. Cognitive load and the expertise reversal effect (RQ-C2)

**sweller2019cognitive** (reused key) — Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. (2019). Cognitive architecture and instructional design: 20 years later. *Educational Psychology Review, 31*(2), 261–292. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5
- *Verification:* Crossref; carried from F4 (`F4_ux_cognitive_foundations.md`, entry sweller2019cognitive), not re-verified here.
- *Read status:* abstract only (carried from F4).
- *What it argues:* All novel information is first processed by a "capacity and duration limited working memory" and then stored in an unlimited long-term memory (F4's paraphrase of the abstract). Its home domain is instruction, not interfaces.
- *Bears on:* Section 7 (i). **Supports** the load rationale as theory. Tier B (no passage read).

**kalyuga2003expertise** — Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. *Educational Psychologist, 38*(1), 23–31. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4
- *Also:* Kalyuga, S. (2007). Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction. *Educational Psychology Review, 19*(4), 509–539. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3 (key `kalyuga2007expertise`).
- *Verification:* Crossref confirms both. **Warning:** the Consensus record for the 2003 paper listed the wrong DOI (`10.4018/9781605660486.ch003`, a later book chapter that reprints the abstract). Use the Crossref DOI above.
- *Read status:* abstract only for both (the UNSW repository handle returned a JavaScript shell with no PDF).
- *What it argues:* "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners" (2003 abstract). The 2007 review links the effect to aptitude-treatment interactions and "learner-tailored instructional systems."

## (c) Thematic synthesis (answers to RQ-C1 to RQ-C5)

**RQ-C1: What do scaffolding, fading, cognitive apprenticeship and training wheels establish for operational skill, and when does fading work?** Three things hold up. Early restriction speeds learning and transfers: Carroll and Carrithers found training-wheels learners faster and better, and Catrambone and Carroll found they did better on advanced full-system functions than learners trained on the full system. Computer-based scaffolds help in general: Belland et al.'s meta-analysis of 144 studies finds g = 0.46. And fading, when it works, is tied to learner competence and rule stability: Nückles et al. found adaptive fading beat permanent prompts, Renkl and Atkinson's fading worked for near transfer in well-structured domains, and Leutner found slow fading of guidance best. The evidence for fading is weaker than the outline's Section 7 assumes. Belland et al. report that the presence or absence of scaffolding change "did not vary" the effect; Tawfik et al. found sustained scaffolds better on one measure; Leutner's second experiment failed where the interface already showed its functions; and van de Pol et al. note that fading belongs inside contingent support, which a uniform on-screen display is not. The conditions the outline names, predictability and feedback, are the right ones, and two independent statements of them exist (Kahneman and Klein 2009; Hogarth et al. 2015). But nobody has shown them as boundary conditions on fading specifically. I found no study that varies feedback timing in a fading experiment. "Fade the scaffold" should therefore be written as "support that tracks the learner's growing competence," and it should not be stated as an empirical law.

**RQ-C2: What does cognitive load theory imply for an interface that teaches while it is used, including the risk to experts?** The expertise reversal effect is solid: a 2025 meta-analysis of 60 studies (176 effects, N = 5,924) gives d = 0.505 for high assistance among low-prior-knowledge learners and d = −0.428 for low assistance among high-prior-knowledge learners (Tetzlaff et al.). It is also asymmetric, and weaker for younger students and for humanities and language content. Two design consequences follow. A permanent envelope display is cheap insurance against harm to novices, because the novice side is the larger effect. And any adaptive switch-off carries its own cost: adaptive interfaces were found to reduce predictability or awareness (Gajos et al.; Findlater and McGrenere), so adaptivity cannot be the default answer to expertise reversal without testing the trade. No study I found tests expertise reversal for an interface display of an intermediary's operating envelope; every result is from instructional materials. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) could not be read, so the "minimal guidance fails" argument is represented only by its title.

**RQ-C3: Do productive failure or legitimate peripheral participation offer a model for learning under mandatory participation?** Neither transfers cleanly. Productive failure has a moderate pooled effect (g = 0.36, Sinha and Kapur), but its benefit depends on a designed consolidation phase that builds on learners' own attempts (Loibl et al.), it is contested where element interactivity is high (Ashman et al.), and the meta-analysis found effect sizes favoring instruction-first for domain-general skills. Mandatory participation has no consolidation phase unless the design adds one. Legitimate peripheral participation presupposes an old-timer who holds the competence (the lab's card on Lave and Wenger makes the same point), and Beane's study of robotic surgery shows what happens when technology removes the approved participation route: only a minority reach competence, through unapproved practices. Rahman's field study shows the equivalent for platform workers whose criteria move. Together these support the outline's premise, that participation alone does not teach, and they weaken any suggestion that productive failure licenses leaving users to struggle.

**RQ-C4: Has boosting been applied to operational competence with algorithmic systems rather than to information literacy?** I found essentially no study of it. The boosting literature on algorithmic environments targets detection, resistance and information evaluation: Kozyreva et al.'s map, and Lorenz-Spreen et al.'s detection of microtargeted ads, where the information-only arm did not help and a self-reflection step did. The nearest direct tests of operational support are Chiang and Yin's tutorials (effective only for people already performing well), Mozannar et al.'s onboarding (+5.2% team accuracy in one of two tasks), Cabrera et al.'s behavior descriptions (improved accuracy across three domains), and Cen et al.'s finding that telling users what the algorithm weights changes how much they use likes. These are tests of reliance or behavior change within a session. None measures whether the competence outlasts the intervention, which is Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff's own test for boosts. So the outline's statement that boosting is "the behavioral version" of the scaffold is a lineage claim, and Part 2 should not report it as a result.

**RQ-C5: What does the mental-models literature say about a system whose rules move, and does any learning-sciences work address non-stationary task environments?** The mental-models work supports the envelope idea. Kieras and Bovair show a device model pays off when it "supports direct and simple inference of the exact steps required to operate the device." Gero et al. show that understanding the underlying technology was "insufficient" without analysis of behavior. Bansal et al. show that parsimonious, non-stochastic error boundaries are learned better, that equal accuracy gives unequal team performance, and that accuracy-improving updates can hurt a team because they break prior experience. For moving rules, the learning-sciences literature is thin. Speekenbrink and Shanks explicitly call it under-researched, and their lab participants tracked both abrupt and gradual change with insight; DeVito's folk-theorization study shows users re-theorizing continually when platforms change. No scaffolding, fading or worked-example study I found uses a non-stationary rule set. The backward-compatibility requirement therefore rests on the human-AI team studies (Bansal et al.), not on learning-sciences evidence about fading.

**What this facet adds on generative AI and automation.** The evidence on whether chat-style access harms skill is mixed, and the mix is itself informative. The one large field RCT shows harm to later unaided math performance from unguarded access (Bastani et al., 17% lower than control) and removal of that harm, but no gain, with a hint-first tutor. Two lab experiments find no overall effect and let usage mode decide (Lehmann et al., preprint) or find gains from explanation use (Contractor and Reyes, preprint), and a controlled novice-programming study found no harm (Kazemitabaar et al.). Self-report is unreliable in Bastani et al. and Lehmann et al. None of these studies measures learning to operate the AI. The automation literature adds the sharpest limit on the competence route: automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" (Parasuraman and Manzey), while exposure to failures during training reduced complacency partially (Bahner et al.). Bainbridge's remedy, a system that acts at a pace and by criteria the operator can follow, is the oldest statement of predictability over efficiency.

### Pitch and outline claims the evidence contradicts or complicates

1. **"Fade the scaffold" (Section 7 iii) is under-supported as stated.** The largest meta-analysis finds the effect of scaffolding does not vary with whether scaffolds change; one study favors sustained scaffolds; Leutner's second experiment fails. Supported only as adaptive fading tied to competence, in domains with stable rules.
2. **Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) as the source of "fading" (Section 7 i).** I could not read it, and the reviews I read attribute fading to later work. Do not cite the paper for fading.
3. **Predictability is not shown to beat accuracy.** Gajos et al. found accuracy had the stronger effect on performance in adaptive menus. The outline's priority claim needs the Bansal et al. compatibility evidence and the Bainbridge argument, not the menu results.
4. **"Not explanation" (ruling 2) needs a boundary.** Kulesza et al. (explaining a recommender's reasoning helped users steer it) and Tankelevitch et al. (explainability to cut metacognitive demand) both complicate a blunt version. Kieras and Bovair give the line: explain what lets a learner infer the operating steps. Gero et al. and Cabrera et al. support the behavior-description version.
5. **Productive failure (Section 7 i) does not license letting users struggle.** It needs a consolidation phase built on the learner's attempts, and the meta-analysis favors instruction-first for domain-general skills.
6. **"Unguarded chat harms learning" is one RCT, in mathematics, not an established finding.** Two lab studies are null or positive by usage mode, and none measures operational skill.
7. **Skill is not sufficient.** Parasuraman and Manzey (automation bias not preventable by training) and Chiang and Yin (tutorials help only high performers) imply that a skill-building obligation cannot stand alone. This supports the outline's pairing with counter-delegation and structural refusal, and it should be stated as a limit on the competence route.
8. **Training wheels are restriction by the designer.** The empirical result is positive, but in a mandatory setting the restriction's owner is the intermediary. The learning sciences cannot adjudicate that, so the standing argument must.

Practice with less AI makes perfect. *International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction, 41*, 2268–2288. doi:10.1080/10447318.2024.2319914 — Crossref-verified; Consensus abstract (N = 102): fully automated decision selection during training hurt skill acquisition and "partially automated AI enhanced motivation and engagement." Relevant to the training-wheels-versus-substitute question.
- Casner, S. M., Geven, R. W., Recker, M. P., & Schooler, J. W. (2014). The retention of manual flying skills in the automated cockpit. *Human Factors, 56*(8), 1506–1516. doi:10.1177/0018720814535628 — Consensus abstract: control skills "mostly intact," cognitive skills degraded and related to task-unrelated thought; 16 pilots.
- Soderstrom, N. C., & Bjork, R. A. (2015). Learning versus performance: An integrative review. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10*(2), 176–199. doi:10.1177/1745691615569000 — Crossref abstract: improvements in performance during acquisition "can fail to yield significant learning," and people "mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to long-term learning." The general principle behind the Bastani, Fan and Lehmann results.
- Gonzalez, C., Lerch, J. F., & Lebiere, C. (2003). Instance-based learning in dynamic decision making. *Cognitive Science, 27*(4), 591–635. doi:10.1207/s15516709cog2704_2 — Crossref abstract: a theory of learning in dynamic tasks with feedback updates; unread.
- McNeill, K. L., et al. (2006). Supporting students' construction of scientific explanations by fading scaffolds in instructional materials. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 15*(2), 153–191. doi:10.1207/s15327809jls1502_1 — Crossref-verified; no abstract retrieved; a canonical fading experiment.
- Eslami et al. (2016) and Cameron and Rahman (2022): see repo cards `lima_pdw/literature/cards/eslami2016.md` and `cameronrahman2022.md`; the Eslami card reports that users acted only on folk theories that gave them a lever, which bears on the channel-for-intent claim.
- Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. (2023): repo card `algorithmacy_scaffolding/literature/library/zamfirescupereira2023.md` (facet B territory): novice prompters were opportunistic, and none used the systematic testing tool offered.
- Dietvorst et al. (2018): repo card `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/dietvorst2018.md` (full text; the right to modify mattered more than its size). Not re-read here; it is the cited basis for the outline's "low-cost direct channel" claim.
