# B — Chat interfaces, verification burden, and the cognition of operating an intermediary

*Facet B of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 2 literature review. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-01 (a parallel research agent working in the `wt-dxd` worktree). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Four research questions from `part2_ethics/OUTLINE.md`, section (d), facet B. **RQ-B1:** controlled evidence comparing conversational or LLM interfaces with structured, direct-manipulation or visual tools on accuracy, verification time, overreliance and calibration. **RQ-B2:** CHI/CSCW work since 2023 on prompting difficulty, the gulf of envisioning and sensemaking with LLM output, and whether any of it measures skill gain over sessions. **RQ-B3:** pre-attentive and uncertainty-visualization findings that bear on displaying an intermediary's operating envelope (confidence, boundary, trade-off) without text, and where visual encoding misleads. **RQ-B4:** whether anyone has measured the verification burden, that is, the division of cognitive labor between reading and checking output and deciding. The facet serves outline section 4 (the conversational interface), part of section 6 (the deskilling evidence) and propositions P5–P7. **The question the synthesis answers:** what does the empirical record let Part 2 say about conversational interfaces, and which sentences of the pitch and outline does it not yet support?

Locators marked "pdf p." are pages of the OA PDF I read; where that PDF is an arXiv version, they are not the pages of the version of record. ### B1. Foundations: direct manipulation, the gulfs, and monitoring

**shneiderman1983direct** — Shneiderman, B. (1983). Direct manipulation: A step beyond programming languages. *Computer, 16*(8), 57–69. https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.1983.1654471
- *Verification:* Crossref (vol. 16, issue 8, pp. 57–69); Semantic Scholar record (OA "GREEN" link to the author's UMD copy, which I fetched).
- *Read status:* read in part. The UMD copy is a 13-page scan with OCR text; I read the introduction, the list of attributes and the discussion of graphic representations. PDF page *k* is journal page 56 + *k* (checked against the running heads on pp. 58, 62, 66). **Check every quotation against the page image before print;** the OCR garbles single letters.
- *What it establishes:* Shneiderman builds "an integrated portrait of direct manipulation" from four principles (p. 64): "Continuous representation of the object of interest"; physical actions or labeled button presses "instead of complex syntax"; "Rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose impact on the object of interest is immediately visible"; and a "Layered or spiral approach to learning that permits usage with minimal knowledge." He ties these to beneficial attributes, among them "Users can immediately see if their actions are furthering their goals," "Users experience less anxiety because the system is comprehensible and because actions are so easily reversible," and "Users gain confidence and mastery because they initiate an action, feel in control, and can predict system responses" (p. 65). He also gives the counterweight on p. 64: "using spatial or graphic representations of the problem does not necessarily improve performance" (subjects given a flowchart did no better than those given the code; a graphic representation can be "misleading"), and "the wrong information, or a cluttered presentation, can lead to greater confusion."
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (iii) and §7 claim (ii), in an unexpected way: the canonical statement of direct manipulation names *prediction* ("can predict system responses") and a learnable path from novice to expert as its design goals, which is Part 2's predictability-plus-learning framing and not a legibility framing (ruling 2). It also **complicates** the pre-attentive claim: the founding text already warns that a spatial display helps only if its content is right.

**hutchins1985direct** — Hutchins, E. L., Hollan, J. D., & Norman, D. A. (1985). Direct manipulation interfaces. *Human–Computer Interaction, 1*(4), 311–338. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0104_2
- *Verification:* Crossref; Part 1 facet F4 entry `hutchins1985direct` (full text from Hollan's author page).
- *Read status:* read in full (per F4; not re-read this session). Locators below are the F4 entry's.
- *What it establishes:* The authors offer "a cognitive account" of direct manipulation (abstract, p. 311). They define the gulf of execution (goals to system state) and the gulf of evaluation (system state back to goals), and say both bridges aim "to minimize cognitive effort" (p. 318). Two qualifications bear on Part 2: automated skill can make an interface *feel* direct without reducing semantic distance (pp. 326–327), and if the interface is "really invisible," difficulties in the task domain pass straight to the user (p. 336).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (i) as the source of the gulfs vocabulary. It **complicates** the claim that a conversational interface "maximizes the gulf of evaluation": the 1985 account defined the gulfs against a deterministic system whose state a display could model, so applying it to an LLM is an extension that Subramonyam et al. (below) make explicit and hedge.

**bainbridge1983ironies** — Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. *Automatica, 19*(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8
- *Verification:* Crossref; library card `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/bainbridge1983.md` (S2 adversarial check 2026-09-18, verdict "confirmed": twenty quotations and page numbers checked against the Pergamon PDF).
- *Read status:* read in full (per card; not re-read this session).
- *What it establishes:* The monitor's task is structurally impossible once the system decides faster and on more criteria than the human can follow: "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly… The human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776). Her remedy is a designed inefficiency: the computer should decide "using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (p. 777). Cognitive skill "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness" (p. 775).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (v) (verification as unpaid labor with no learning return) and §6 claim (iii) (Kahneman–Klein conditions). The 1983 argument is about industrial monitoring, so it licenses the structure of the claim, not a measurement in chat use. Lee et al. 2025 (below) cite it in their opening paragraph.

### B2. What conversational prompting asks of the user

**subramonyam2024bridging** — Subramonyam, H., Pea, R., Pondoc, C., Agrawala, M., & Seifert, C. (2024). Bridging the gulf of envisioning: Cognitive challenges in prompt based interactions with LLMs. In *Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–19). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642754
- *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex (closed); arXiv 2309.14459v2, whose title matches the CHI version. The arXiv search (log row 24) returned this one record.
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract, introduction, §on the three gaps, closing agenda; pdf pp. 1–3, 14). Peer-reviewed (CHI 2024). **A theory paper; it reports no user study.**
- *What it establishes:* The authors argue that LLMs "narrow the gulf of execution by eliminating conventional needs for action specification and execution, leaving only intention to the user. However, the gulf of evaluation may increase by challenges to perceive, interpret, and evaluate output given the LLMs' probabilistic process" (pdf p. 2; the hedge "may" is theirs). They name a new distance, the gulf of envisioning, between intention and a prompt that "foresees how LLM capabilities and training data can be leveraged," with three gaps: the capability gap (what the LLM can do), the instruction gap (how to instruct it) and the intentionality gap (what to expect of the output) (pdf p. 3). Their closing section says "An empirical agenda can determine" the value of their proposals (pdf p. 14).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claims (i)–(ii) as the most direct statement that conversational entry removes execution cost and leaves evaluation and capability-discovery cost with the user. It **complicates** them as evidence: the paper hedges the evaluation claim and tests none of it. The capability gap is the user-side counterpart of Part 2's "declared operating envelope" (ruling 2), so the paper supports the *need* for an envelope display and says nothing on whether visual forms work. Terminology: "gulf of envisioning" is the authors' coinage (Rule 4 applies; cite it, do not adopt it without a gloss).

**tankelevitch2024metacognitive** — Tankelevitch, L., Kewenig, V., Simkute, A., Scott, A. E., Sarkar, A., Sellen, A., & Rintel, S. (2024). The metacognitive demands and opportunities of generative AI. In *Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–24). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642902
- *Verification:* Crossref; arXiv 2312.10893v3 (12 Mar 2024).
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract; introduction; §3 on the demands, pdf pp. 1–3). Peer-reviewed (CHI 2024). **A perspective paper that synthesizes prior studies; it collects no data.**
- *What it establishes:* Generative AI systems "impose metacognitive demands on users, requiring a high degree of metacognitive monitoring and control" (abstract): prompting demands self-awareness of goals and task decomposition, evaluating output demands well-adjusted confidence in one's own evaluation, and the generality of the system adds a higher-level demand, knowing whether and when to use it at all (pdf p. 2). They propose two remedies: metacognitive support strategies built into the system, and designing systems to reduce the demand by "targeting explainability and customizability" (abstract).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claims (i) and (v) and gives the verification burden a name in cognitive psychology (metacognitive monitoring), which satisfies Rule 4's preference for the literature's term over "sounding out." Its remedy "explainability" runs against ruling 2 if read as explaining the model; read as *customizability* (a channel for intent) it matches the channel-for-intent obligation in §7.

**zamfirescupereira2023why** — Zamfirescu-Pereira, J. D., Wong, R. Y., Hartmann, B., & Yang, Q. (2023). Why Johnny can't prompt: How non-AI experts try (and fail) to design LLM prompts. In *Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (Article 437, pp. 1–21). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581388
- *Verification:* Crossref; library cards `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/zamfirescupereira2023.md` and `algorithmacy_scaffolding/literature/library/zamfirescupereira2023.md`.
- *Read status:* read in full (per the scaffolding pass's reading, as the cards state; **not re-read this session**). The design-ethics card's S2 check returned "could not verify": the ACM PDF is canvas-only and bot-walled, and I could not reach an author copy either (log row 25). The card's transcription matches the scaffolding card; nothing was checked against the paper.
- *What it establishes:* Ten non-expert participants designing a recipe chatbot explored prompts opportunistically and not systematically. None used the systematic-testing interface (the Error Browser), and most declared success after one improved utterance. The failures traced to over-generalization from single observations and a social lens carried over from human instruction (politeness, a bias toward direct instruction over examples, the assumption that "Do not say X" is understood as a person would understand it). The design section asks for tools that show cause and effect and for "seamful" interfaces (card).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (ii) ("prompting is opportunistic and socially miscalibrated") as stated in the outline. Do not cite it as showing experts differ; the card's caution holds (n = 10, one task, text-davinci-002, STEM early adopters, no expert group). It is also the only study here in which participants *had* a feedback tool and did not use it, which bears on §7: a scaffold that users skip does not teach.

### B3. Controlled comparisons of conversational and other interface designs

**spatharioti2025effects** — Spatharioti, S. E., Rothschild, D., Goldstein, D. G., & Hofman, J. M. (2025). Effects of LLM-based search on decision making: Speed, accuracy, and overreliance. In *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–15). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3714082
- *Verification:* Crossref; arXiv 2307.03744 (the OpenAlex record for the preprint carries the earlier title, "Comparing Traditional and LLM-based Search for Consumer Choice: A Randomized Experiment").
- *Read status:* read in part (Experiments 1 and 2, method and results, pdf pp. 6–12). **I read arXiv v2 (8 Nov 2023), which predates the CHI 2025 version; check every number against the version of record before print.** Peer-reviewed (CHI 2025).
- *What it establishes:* Experiment 1 randomized 90 MTurk workers to an LLM-based search tool (GPT-3.5, 51 participants) or a Bing-API search engine. LLM users finished faster with fewer queries; on four routine product comparisons accuracy was comparable (traditional 92.3%, LLM 95.3%; z = 0.99, p = 0.33). On a fifth comparison built so the LLM errs, accuracy was 93% for traditional search and 47% for the LLM tool (z = −3.46, p < .001); no participant who got an implausibly small cargo-space value re-queried it (§4.1, pdf pp. 8–9). Experiment 2 (120 participants, GPT-3) added confidence-based color highlighting of numerical values: on the hard task, accuracy was 26% with no highlighting, 53% with low-confidence-only highlighting and 58% with high-plus-low highlighting (§5.1, pdf p. 12), mostly because flagged users issued follow-up queries. **Caution for use as "chat" evidence:** the authors pre-prompted the tool "to signal to participants that it is not a conversational tool" (pdf p. 7), so this is natural-language answer search, not multi-turn chat.
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (ii) (overreliance when the model errs) and §4 claim (iii) (an in-place visual cue on the output moved accuracy, which is the closest thing in this facet to an envelope display tested against an LLM). It **complicates** the adoption claim: LLM users were faster and more satisfied with equal accuracy on routine items, so the benefit side of the trade is real. No skill or retention measure.

**anderl2024conversational** — Anderl, C., Klein, S. H., Sarigül, B., Schneider, F. M., Han, J., Fiedler, P. L., & Utz, S. (2024). Conversational presentation mode increases credibility judgements during information search with ChatGPT. *Scientific Reports, 14*, Article 17127. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-67829-6
- *Verification:* Crossref; Europe PMC full text PMC11272919 (open access).
- *Read status:* read in full (methods, results, discussion).
- *What it establishes:* Two preregistered experiments (1,222 participants in total) had people rate the credibility of accurate and partly inaccurate information shown as a typed-out LLM chat, a voice assistant, or static encyclopedia text. People were "better at detecting inaccuracies when identical information was provided as static text compared to both types of conversational agents" (abstract). The detail is narrower. Voice cut discernment relative to static text in both experiments (p < .001; p = .008). The dynamic text agent did so in Experiment 2 only (p = .029) and not in Experiment 1 (p = .212) (Results, Fig. 2). Participants only watched or listened; they did not interact with the applications (Methods). The authors read the pattern as a modality effect plus a conversational-nature effect working in parallel (Discussion).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claims (i) and (v) in the strict sense that conversational delivery lowered the ability to tell wrong from right with content held constant, which is a controlled isolation of the *form*. It **complicates** the strength of the claim for text chat (one of two experiments) and for interaction (none). Use "in one of two experiments" for text chat.

**he2025conversational** — He, G., Aishwarya, N., & Gadiraju, U. (2025). Is conversational XAI all you need? Human-AI decision making with a conversational XAI assistant. In *Proceedings of the 30th International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces* (pp. 907–924). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3708359.3712133
- *Verification:* Crossref; arXiv 2501.17546.
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract, introduction, participants, §6.1; pdf pp. 1–2, 9, and the participants paragraph). Peer-reviewed (IUI 2025).
- *What it establishes:* In a loan-approval task on Prolific, a conversational XAI interface was compared with an XAI dashboard (352 participants recruited for the Control, Dashboard, conversational and enhanced-conversational conditions, 107 excluded; a further 61 valid participants for an LLM-agent condition run as a follow-up). Participants gained higher *perceived* understanding and trust with the conversation, but "users of both… showed clear over-reliance" (abstract). §6.1 states that with the LLM-agent conversation "over-reliance was further reinforced (i.e., worse RSR) and users obtained significantly worse objective feature understanding compared to other conditions with XAI interfaces." The authors attribute this to an illusion of explanatory depth.
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (v) and the adoption claim: conversation raised trust and felt understanding without producing appropriate reliance, and the more fluent the conversation, the worse the objective understanding. The abstract's "better understanding" is perceived understanding; cite the §6.1 wording.

**kazemitabaar2024improving** — Kazemitabaar, M., Williams, J., Drosos, I., Grossman, T., Henley, A. Z., Negreanu, C., & Sarkar, A. (2024). Improving steering and verification in AI-assisted data analysis with interactive task decomposition. In *Proceedings of the 37th Annual ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology* (pp. 1–19). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3654777.3676345
- *Verification:* Crossref (title, venue, pages and the seven-author list checked); arXiv 2407.02651.
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract, §6.1–6.4, §8.1; arXiv pdf pp. 10–16). Peer-reviewed (UIST 2024).
- *What it establishes:* After a formative study (n = 15) that found verifying and steering the AI to be the main difficulties, the authors built two structured systems (Stepwise: editable assumptions-plus-code pairs per step; Phasewise: three editable phases) and compared them with a ChatGPT-like conversational baseline in a within-subjects experiment (n = 18, 6 tasks each, 108 task episodes). - *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §7: it is a randomized demonstration that operational skills with a generative system (prompting and verifying) are *teachable* with practice and feedback, so a learning-design obligation is not idle. It **complicates** the claim that the *interface* should carry the teaching: here the teaching is a separate course module, and the study does not compare it with an interface-embedded scaffold.

### B6. Pre-attentive features and uncertainty visualization

**treisman1980feature** — Treisman, A. M., & Gelade, G. (1980). A feature-integration theory of attention. *Cognitive Psychology, 12*(1), 97–136. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(80)90005-5
- *Verification:* Crossref; Consensus record carrying the abstract; Semantic Scholar (CLOSED).
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it establishes:* "The feature-integration theory of attention suggests that attention must be directed serially to each stimulus in a display whenever conjunctions of more than one separable feature are needed to characterize or distinguish the possible objects presented" (abstract). It offers "a new rationale for predicting which tasks will show attention limits and which will not."
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (iii) only at the level of the principle: a single separable feature can be found without serial scanning, a conjunction cannot. A display that encodes the envelope in one feature has a theoretical reason to be read faster than text. The paper does not test decision displays. The strict feature/conjunction split has since been contested (Wolfe et al. 1989 and Duncan & Humphreys 1992 appear in the Consensus results; abstracts only, not entered).

**healey2012attention** — Healey, C. G., & Enns, J. T. (2012). Attention and visual memory in visualization and computer graphics. *IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, 18*(7), 1170–1188. https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2011.127
- *Verification:* Crossref; Part 1 F4 entry `healey2012attention`.
- *Read status:* abstract only (per F4; the NCSU author copy 404'd this session).
- *What it establishes:* A survey of attention and visual-perception research "with direct relevance to visualization," from low-level preattentive processing to visual memory, with the observation that visualization problems now drive psychophysics (F4).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** the Rule 4 replacement "pre-attentive visual features" with a citable review; it carries no result Part 2 can quote on decision accuracy. Needs a read before it is cited for anything beyond the term.

**padilla2018decision** — Padilla, L. M., Creem-Regehr, S. H., Hegarty, M., & Stefanucci, J. K. (2018). Decision making with visualizations: A cognitive framework across disciplines. *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications, 3*, Article 29. https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-018-0120-9
- *Verification:* Crossref; Europe PMC full text PMC6091269. The record carries a correction notice (PMC6119555), which I did not read.
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract; the four cross-domain findings; the sections on bottom-up attention and visual-spatial biases). Article numbered, no page numbers in the version I read; locators are section titles. Peer-reviewed review article.
- *What it establishes:* The authors integrate visualization comprehension with a dual-process account of decision making and report four cross-domain findings: visualizations direct bottom-up attention, "which can both help and hinder decision making"; "visual-spatial biases comprise a unique category of bias that is a direct result of the visual encoding technique"; visualizations with better cognitive fit yield faster and more effective decisions; and knowledge-driven processes interact with the encoding. The common bias is a *containment* conceptualization of boundaries: viewers treat data inside a bounded region as more alike than data across it (McKenzie et al. found people used a containment heuristic with a bounded-circle location display and not with a Gaussian fade). The "deterministic construal error," reading an uncertainty graphic as a deterministic forecast, "was not observed with textual representations of the same data" (Grounds, Joslyn & Otsuka, as cited under visual-spatial biases).
- *Bears on the outline:* **complicates** §4 claims (iii)–(iv), and is the most important entry for RQ-B3's "where does visual encoding mislead." The encodings an envelope display would use (a boundary, a region, a band) are exactly the encodings that produce containment errors, and text did better in one cited case. The claim "moving state into perception rather than text" lowers the evaluation cost needs to read "for some encodings and tasks."

**padilla2020powerful** — Padilla, L. M. K., Creem-Regehr, S. H., & Thompson, W. (2020). The powerful influence of marks: Visual and knowledge-driven processing in hurricane track displays. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 26*(1), 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000245
- *Verification:* Crossref (issued March 2020; the PsyArXiv preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/5tg9y is the same work); Consensus abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it establishes:* In ensemble hurricane forecasts, "people estimate more damage to a location that is overlapped by a track in an ensemble hurricane forecast visualization compared to a location that does not coincide with a track." The effect fell when fewer paths were drawn, and "providing instructions about the information conveyed in the ensemble display also reduced the effect, but importantly, did not eliminate it" (abstract).
- *Bears on the outline:* **complicates** §4 claim (iv) and bears on §7: a mark on a display can drive judgment against what the display's own designers intend, and a one-time instruction (the scaffold) does not fully correct it. That is a case for *testing* any envelope encoding and for keeping the scaffold in place.

**hullman2015hypothetical** — Hullman, J., Resnick, P., & Adar, E. (2015). Hypothetical outcome plots outperform error bars and violin plots for inferences about reliability of variable ordering. *PLOS ONE, 10*(11), Article e0142444. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0142444
- *Verification:* Crossref; OA PDF (PLOS ONE).
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract; method).
- *What it establishes:* The authors animate a finite set of draws from a distribution (hypothetical outcome plots, HOPs) so viewers can use "mental processes like counting and integration." In an MTurk experiment (288 participants, 96 per condition) HOPs gave "much more accurate judgments about plots of two and three quantities" than error bars and violin plots; accuracy "was similar with all three representations for most questions about distributions of a single quantity" (abstract).
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §4 claim (iv): an encoding that uses a perceptual operation (counting outcomes) beat conventional abstract encodings for multi-quantity inference, and **complicates** it with the single-quantity parity: the benefit depends on the question the viewer must answer. It concerns statistical summaries, not an adaptive system's envelope.

**kusumastuti2023practice** — Kusumastuti, S. A., Pollard, K. A., Oiknine, A. H., Dalangin, B., Raber, T. R., & Files, B. T. (2023). Practice improves performance of a 2D uncertainty integration task within and across visualizations. *IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, 29*(8), 3949–3960. https://doi.org/10.1109/tvcg.2022.3173889
- *Verification:* Crossref (volume 29, 2023; the Consensus record dates the article to 2022, its online-first year); Consensus abstract.
- *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it establishes:* In two experiments (N = 118 and 56) on bivariate Gaussian uncertainty shown as ensemble, summary and distributional displays, "decision making was faster and more accurate after practice relative to before practice," and "the performance improvements transferred to use of display types that were not practiced" (abstract). The authors read this as practice with feedback improving underlying probabilistic-reasoning skill.
- *Bears on the outline:* **supports** §7 claim (ii) (feedback during practice builds a skill that transfers) for visual uncertainty displays specifically, which is the closest evidence that a *visual* envelope can be learned. It does not involve an adaptive system.

## (c) Thematic synthesis

**RQ-B1: What does the controlled evidence show about conversational versus structured or visual interfaces?** Eight studies make a controlled comparison of some kind (Spatharioti, Anderl, He, Kazemitabaar, Masson, Fan, Stadler, Melumad), and they do not agree. Speed and satisfaction favor the LLM or conversational form (Spatharioti et al.), as does lower reported load (Stadler et al.). Accuracy is about equal when the model is right and collapses when it errs (Spatharioti et al.: 93% against 47% on the hard item). Conversational *delivery* reduced the ability to tell wrong from right in Anderl et al., but for typed text in one of two experiments only. Conversational XAI beat a dashboard on perceived understanding and trust, and both produced overreliance; the LLM-agent version produced worse objective understanding (He et al., §6.1). Against that, structured and direct-manipulation alternatives raised *perceived control* and cut prompting effort (Kazemitabaar et al.; Masson et al.) but showed no objective gain in success, time or verification hints (Kazemitabaar et al., §6.4), and one 2026 abstract reports chat ahead on correctness for hard items without a time cost (Fan et al., unread). The honest summary is that the *direction* the outline assumes (structure beats chat on the verification and learning outcomes) is not yet demonstrated, and the *mechanism* it assumes (chat inflates the evaluation gulf) rests on Subramonyam et al.'s hedged argument and on Anderl et al.'s credibility measure.

**RQ-B2: Prompting difficulty, the gulf of envisioning and skill gain.** The prompting-difficulty claim has one in-depth study (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., n = 10) and two theory papers (Subramonyam et al.; Tankelevitch et al.). The qualitative record on novices is consistent (Prather et al.: the benefit and harm split by prior skill). On skill gain, six studies measure *something* over time or across sessions: Gajos & Mamykina (pre/post learning), Bastani et al. (an unaided exam after four assisted sessions), Cabrera et al. (learning slopes across rounds), Kusumastuti et al. (pre/post practice), Connell Pensky et al. (pre/post modules, randomized) and Prather et al. (qualitative). None of them compares conversational against structured *interfaces* on skill gain with the system's declared envelope held constant. I found no work that does this in the sources searched (Consensus, OpenAlex, arXiv, Crossref; Scholar Gateway and part of Semantic Scholar failed, see log rows 7, 9, 23). The nearest results say that the *design of what the tool does* decides learning (Bastani: answer-giving versus hint-giving, both in chat; Gajos & Mamykina: explanation without a recommendation taught, explanation with one did not) and that practice with feedback or explicit instruction raises operational skill (Kusumastuti; Connell Pensky).

**RQ-B3: Visual encoding of an envelope, and where it misleads.** The theory behind "pre-attentive" display is old (Treisman & Gelade, abstract only) and contested at the margin; the practical question is whether an encoding of an uncertainty or a boundary improves decisions. The record is mixed and encoding-specific. For: color highlighting of low-confidence values in LLM output more than doubled accuracy on the hard task (26% to 53–58%; Spatharioti et al.); animated outcome plots beat error bars for multi-quantity inference (Hullman et al.); behavior descriptions of subgroup failure raised accuracy in two of three tasks (Cabrera et al.); practice transfers across uncertainty displays (Kusumastuti et al.). Against: encodings produce their own errors, among them containment (a bounded region read as "inside is alike"), the mark effect in hurricane tracks, and the deterministic construal error that did not appear with text (Padilla et al. 2018, 2020). Text worked too: first-person hedges raised accuracy (Kim et al. 2024), and wording aligned to model confidence narrowed the calibration gap (Steyvers et al.). Nothing I read tests an *envelope display for an adaptive intermediary*, and the one study that tests an in-place visual cue on LLM output (Spatharioti et al.) overlays it on text, not in place of text.

**RQ-B4: Has the verification burden been measured?** Yes, in programming. Mozannar et al. measured 22.4% of session time in "verifying suggestion" (self-labeled, inline Copilot, n = 21), and Becker et al. found about 9% of time reviewing and cleaning output alongside a 19% net slowdown (preprint, 16 developers). Lee et al. report a self-reported shift toward verification and oversight (n = 319). Fok & Weld supply the theory that explains why the burden varies: verifiability is a property of the task. No study I found measures the burden *for conversational input specifically* against a structured alternative, except Fan et al. (abstract only) and the verification-hint count in Kazemitabaar et al., which did not differ. No study measures the burden as *unpaid* or as a cost shifted from provider to user; the normative frame is Part 2's own.

### Outline and pitch claims the evidence complicates or contradicts

1. **§4 claim (i): conversational interfaces "maximize the gulf of evaluation."** Not demonstrated. The only source (Subramonyam et al., pdf p. 2) says the gulf "may increase." The only direct structured-versus-chat test of verification (Kazemitabaar et al.) found no difference in verification hints. Rephrase as a hypothesis, or cite the Anderl et al. discernment result for the narrower statement that conversational delivery lowered credibility discrimination.
2. **§4 claim (v): the interface "gave nothing back as predictability or skill."** The skill half is not supported as a claim about conversational form. Bastani et al.'s harm and benefit arms were both chat; the guardrails decided the outcome. Gajos & Mamykina's taught condition was an explanation without a recommendation. The outline's own ruling (deskilling is a property of design, not technology) is supported, but it cuts against pinning the harm on chat.
3. **"Adoption bought by removing friction."** The record shows ease, speed and satisfaction (Spatharioti et al.), lower load (Stadler et al.) and higher trust with fluent conversation (He et al.). It does not test *adoption* as a choice outcome or show that friction removal caused it. P5 is the proposition that would test it; the outline's §4 sentence is an inference. The dissociation the sources do document is between assisted performance and unassisted skill (Bastani; Gajos & Mamykina), not between adoption and skill. P5 as drafted pairs adoption with competence gain, so it needs either a measure of adoption or a rewording.
4. **"Pre-attentive and uncertainty visualization … move system state into perception rather than text" (§4 claim iii).** Supported as a principle (Treisman & Gelade; Hullman et al.), complicated as a general claim: spatial encodings generate systematic errors (Padilla et al.), instructions do not remove them (Padilla et al. 2020), and text was the better medium in at least one comparison and the working uncertainty display in two LLM studies (Kim et al. 2024; Steyvers et al.). Shneiderman (1983, p. 64) had already warned that a graphic can mislead or confuse.
5. **Ruling 2 (predictability and learning, not legibility): the evidence supports it.** Explanations raise acceptance without raising appropriate reliance (Bansal et al.; Kim et al. 2025; He et al.), they help only where they enable verification (Fok & Weld), and a statement of where the system *fails* improved accuracy and carried a learning slope (Cabrera et al.). Cite these as evidence about when predictability holds, as ruling 2 instructs. Caveat: Cabrera et al.'s descriptions were authored for simulated subgroup failures.
6. **Kahneman–Klein conditions in chat use.** I found no study that tests rapid, unequivocal feedback or environment predictability in an LLM interface. Two studies show users cannot see their own gain or loss without it (Bastani et al.: students did not perceive the loss; Becker et al.: developers believed AI saved 20% when it cost 19%). That supports the outline's feedback argument by inference, not by direct test.
7. **"Unpaid" verification labor.** Measured as time (22.4% in one study, about 9% in another), never as unpaid. Keep "unpaid" as a normative claim in Part 2's voice.

**Safer phrasings the evidence supports now.** "Verification takes a measurable share of working time with AI coding assistants (Mozannar et al., 2024)." "In one randomized comparison, conversational presentation lowered people's ability to distinguish accurate from inaccurate content (Anderl et al., 2024)." "A structured, editable interface raised users' sense of control without changing success, time or verification hints (Kazemitabaar et al., 2024)." "Whether an AI tool taught or de-skilled students depended on how it was built, not on its being a chat (Bastani et al., 2025)." "Telling users where a system fails improved accuracy in two of three tasks (Cabrera et al., 2023)."
