# D — The deskilling debate: technology, design, and retained skill

*Facet D of the Part 2 literature review (Decision Experience Design, Part 2: What Design Owes a Person Who Cannot Leave). Agent-drafted on 2026-10-01 (a parallel research agent working in the `wt-dxd` worktree). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The labor-process deskilling debate (Braverman, Attewell, Spenner, Adler & Borys, Barley, Autor, Zuboff) and what it settled about whether deskilling belongs to technology or to the design of work; the current empirical status of the LLM upskilling/deskilling claims (Dell'Acqua, Noy & Zhang, Brynjolfsson, Lee, Gerlich, Kosmyna, plus the better-identified studies of Bastani, Shen & Tamkin, Budzyń, Lehmann, Melumad and others); cognitive offloading and when it degrades retained skill; the *Phaedrus* as archetype; and automation complacency as a measurable criterion for deskilling by design (Bainbridge, Parasuraman & Manzey, Onnasch, Bahner, Casner, Haslbeck). **The questions the synthesis answers:** RQ-D1 to RQ-D5 in `OUTLINE.md` §(d), and whether any pitch claim about deskilling is contradicted by the evidence.

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the full text this session, or the entry reuses an existing repo card built from full text. *Read in part* = full text obtained and the cited passages located and read, but not the whole paper. *Abstract only* = database abstract or record only. *Not read* = bibliographic record only. Locators: where the text I read has no journal pagination (Europe PMC XML, arXiv, working papers), I cite the section or the preprint's own page and say so. "Preprint" means not peer reviewed as far as I could establish; I record venue for every LLM study.

**Reading this facet against the rulings.** Ruling 1 (history is analogy, not mechanism) governs entries 26 to 28: I did not look for evidence that media "restructure cognition," and I found none in this literature. Ruling 2 (predictability and learning, not legibility) governs how I read the complacency and training studies: they bear on whether an interface lets an operator keep a working picture of what the system will do, which is the operational-predictability sense, and not on whether the operator understands the model.

### B1. What the labor-process debate settled

**1. braverman1974labor** — Braverman, H. (1974). *Labor and monopoly capital: The degradation of work in the twentieth century*. Monthly Review Press.
- *DOI:* none (book). ISBN 9780853453406 (Open Library record; first-publication year in that record is 1975, the usual citation year is 1974).
- *Read status:* **not read.** I cite the thesis only as stated in secondary abstracts that I did read: Consensus rows 4 and 5 (Smith 2015; Devinatz 2014 introduction) report that Braverman argued scientific management and mechanization deskilled and routinized work in order to cut cost and raise managerial control, and that "conception and execution" were separated (Frances et al. 1986 abstract, via Consensus).
- *What it argues:* progressive degradation of skill under monopoly capitalism, driven by management's pursuit of control, with technology as the instrument.
- *Locators:* none I can verify. Before the essay quotes Braverman, pull the book. Vallor (2015, p. 109, per the repo card) already imports the concept ("Braverman 1974") for moral skill.
- *Bearing on the outline:* **complicates** the pitch's use of "deskilling." Braverman's thesis is the original case that deskilling is an outcome of how work is organized, which supports Ruling 2's move from technology to design. It does not support the pitch's claim that deskilling is "nostalgic."

### B2. Vallor's moral-skill criterion

**9. vallor2015moral** — Vallor, S. (2015). Moral deskilling and upskilling in a new machine age: Reflections on the ambiguous future of character. *Philosophy & Technology, 28*(1), 107–124. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-014-0156-9
- *Verification and read status:* **repo card** `submissions/algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/vallor2015.md`, built from the full text (journal pagination 107–124) and twice adversarially checked (S2, 2026-09-18). I did not re-read it.
- *What it argues and the passages to rely on (all per the card):* moral skills are "typically acquired in specific practices which, under the right conditions and with sufficient opportunity for repetition, foster the cultivation of practical wisdom and moral habituation" (p. 109). The deskilling criterion is on p. 113: tools amplify capacities "yet we could be [diminished], if these tools somehow obviated the need for us to employ our own skillful moral judgment, rather than aiding us in its successful exercise." The question is what technologies do "with us" rather than "for us" (pp. 118–119). Design and participatory methods "will not take us all the way" (p. 122). The card also notes the paper contains no empirical study and no word "friction" (the friction reading belongs to Vallor 2016, pp. 161–163, per the card).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports** §6 and §8. It is the philosophical statement of the formation criterion: the test of deskilling is whether the arrangement keeps the person exercising the judgment in question, whatever it does to outcomes. The card's "Relation to the argument" notes that p. 115 cuts against competence-without-practice: the capacity to evaluate a delegate's calls decays when the underlying judgment is delegated. That is the same point as Shen & Tamkin and Bastani (entries 17 and 16), reached from the other direction.

- *Edition flag:* the outline names Nehamas & Woodruff or Hackett as the preferred translations. **I read the Fowler (1925) translation only**; before quoting, check the passage against the translation the essay will use. Stephanus numbers are stable across translations.
- *Read status:* **in part** (§§274d, 275a, 275d, as returned by the Perseus chunk service; §§274c, 274e, 275b, 275c and 275e returned empty or truncated, so my quotations stop where the returned text stopped).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on (Fowler):* §275a, Thamus to Theuth: "For this invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory. Their trust in writing, produced by external characters which are no part of themselves, will discourage the use of their own memory within them. You have invented an elixir not of memory, but of reminding; and you offer your pupils the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom, for they will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem" (text cut off at the chunk boundary). §275d, Socrates: "Writing, Phaedrus, has this strange quality, and is very like painting; for the creatures of painting stand like living beings, but if one asks them a question, they preserve a solemn silence... if you question them, wishing to know about their sayings, they always say only one and the same thing." And the preceding line in §275d: written words "are of any use except to remind him who knows the matter about which they are written."
- *What it argues:* the *Phaedrus* complaint is three claims: writing weakens practiced memory ("forgetfulness"), supplies "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom," and cannot answer questions or adapt to its reader.
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports the archetype and complicates its use.** The first two claims are the offloading and illusion-of-competence claims that Grinschgl et al., Bastani et al. and Fernandes et al. test empirically; the third (silence under questioning) is the inverse of a conversational interface, which answers every question fluently. That inversion is my observation, not a published reading. The *Phaedrus* does not support the pitch's claim that the shift was a "revolution" in interiority: Socrates' complaint is about practice and wisdom, and says nothing about a changed form of consciousness. This keeps with Ruling 1 (analogy only). The **loss was real in the narrow sense Socrates names** (offloaded items are remembered less; entry 26), and the gain from literacy is not contested by anything I read.
- *Scholarship:* **ferrari1987listening** — Ferrari, G. R. F. (1987). *Listening to the cicadas: A study of Plato's Phaedrus*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511659201 — Crossref book record only, **not read**; listed because it is the standard monograph on the dialogue's treatment of writing and speech. **staehler2013theuth** — Staehler, T. Theuth versus Thamus: The esoteric Plato revisited. *Journal of Ancient Philosophy, 7*(1), 65–94. https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.1981-9471.v7i1p65-94 — abstract only (Consensus): "the ambiguity of writing is revealed which does not allow for simple solutions" about the status of the dialogues as written texts. ### B7. Automation complacency as a deskilling criterion

**29. bainbridge1983ironies** — Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. *Automatica, 19*(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8
- *Verification and read status:* **repo card** `bainbridge1983.md`, **read in full** (pagination 775–779); in `references.bib` as `bainbridge1983ironies`. Not re-read.
- *Passages to rely on (per the card):* "the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator" (p. 775); manual skills decay when unused, and "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one" (p. 775); cognitive skill "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness" (p. 775); the operator has an "impossible task" monitoring a computer that applies more criteria than a human can check in real time (p. 776). Remedies are deliberate inefficiencies: the computer should decide "using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (p. 777); hands-on control for a short period each shift (p. 777); "automatic systems should fail obviously" (p. 777).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports** §6 and §7 and **contradicts the dismissal of deskilling.** This is the earliest statement that deskilling by automation removes the skill the operator is later asked to exercise as a monitor. The p. 777 remedy is, in the essay's terms, operational predictability by design.

**30. parasuraman2010complacency** — Parasuraman, R., & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Complacency and bias in human use of automation: An attentional integration. *Human Factors, 52*(3), 381–410. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055
- *Verification and read status:* **repo card** `parasuramanmanzey2010.md`, **read in full** (author deposit, journal pagination); in `references.bib` as `parasuraman2010complacency`. Not re-read.
- *Passages to rely on (per the card):* complacency is "poorer detection of automation failures under automation than under manual control" (operationalization, p. 384; Parasuraman et al. 1993: 82% detection under variable-reliability automation versus 33% under constant reliability, as reported in this review, not read at source); complacency "cannot be overcome with simple practice" (abstract); automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" (abstract). The card records the authors' limits: a proposed model, mostly student microworlds with failure rates the authors call "unrepresentative," and that complacency inferred from detection alone is exactly the inference Moray and Inagaki (2000) reject (p. 399).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports and complicates RQ-D5.** It gives the most-cited measurable criterion (detection of automation failures, and information sampling when measured directly). Its claim that training does not prevent bias is **in tension** with Bahner et al. (entry 32).

**31. onnasch2014human** — Onnasch, L., Wickens, C. D., Li, H., & Manzey, D. (2014). Human performance consequences of stages and levels of automation: An integrated meta-analysis. *Human Factors, 56*(3), 476–488. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720813501549
- *Verification:* Crossref (online 2013, volume 56 in 2014); abstract from Crossref JATS. Closed access.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *What it argues:* a meta-analysis of 18 experiments on degree of automation (DOA): "(a) a clear automation benefit for routine system performance with increasing DOA, (b) a similar but weaker pattern for workload when automation functioned properly, and (c) a negative impact of higher DOA on failure system performance and SA"; negative consequences are "most likely when DOA moved across a critical boundary, which was identified between automation supporting information analysis and automation supporting action selection."
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports** the argument that the design variable that matters is where in the decision the system acts. An aid that stops at information analysis stays on the safe side of the boundary in these 18 experiments; one that selects the action crosses it. This is the best quantitative statement I found that deskilling-like harm (poor failure performance and lost situation awareness) is a function of function allocation, not of automation as such. Relevant to the outline's counter-delegation principle.

**32. bahner2008misuse** — Bahner, J. E., Hüper, A.-D., & Manzey, D. (2008). Misuse of automated decision aids: Complacency, automation bias and the impact of training experience. *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 66*(9), 688–699. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2008.06.001
- *Verification:* Crossref; Consensus abstract. Closed access.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *What it establishes:* N = 24 in a process-control simulation. "Complacency was directly measured by the participants' information sampling behavior"; the results "provide clear evidence for complacency, reflected in an insufficient verification of the automation, while commission errors were associated with high levels of complacency." Exposing operators to automation failures during training "significantly decreased complacency and thus represents a suitable means to reduce this risk, even though it might not avoid it completely" (abstract). A companion conference paper (Bahner et al., 2008, *HFES Annual Meeting*, doi:10.1177/154193120805201906; Consensus abstract only) found that the experience of misses reduced complacency toward the alarm function but not toward the diagnostic function or commission errors.
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports §7** (a design-and-training intervention measurably reduced complacency and gave the operator an independent measure, information sampling) and **complicates** Parasuraman & Manzey's "cannot be prevented by training." Small N, a laboratory simulation, and an effect the authors call partial. It is the nearest evidence that exposure to the system's failure modes is a learning design that works, which is the essay's "declared operating envelope" taken from the training side.

**33. casner2014retention** — Casner, S. M., Geven, R. W., Recker, M. P., & Schooler, J. W. (2014). The retention of manual flying skills in the automated cockpit. *Human Factors, 56*(8), 1506–1516. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628
- *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract (complete). Closed access.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *What it establishes:* 16 airline pilots flew routine and non-routine scenarios in a Boeing 747-400 simulator at varied automation levels; "pilots' instrument scanning and manual control skills [were] mostly intact, even when pilots reported that they were infrequently practiced," but when they performed "the cognitive tasks needed for manual flight (e.g., tracking the aircraft's position without the use of a map display, deciding which navigational steps come next, recognizing instrument system failures), we observed more frequent and significant problems," and that performance was "associated with... task-unrelated thought" under automation (abstract).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports** §6: what erodes is the cognitive, judgment-bearing part of the task, not the manual routine. That matches Autor et al.'s task split and Macnamara et al.'s claim, and it places the deskilling risk in the skill the essay says algorithmacy requires. N = 16; simulator.

**34. haslbeck2016flying** — Haslbeck, A., & Hoermann, H.-J. (2016). Flying the needles: Flight deck automation erodes fine-motor flying skills among airline pilots. *Human Factors, 58*(4), 533–545. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816640394
- *Verification:* Crossref; the author's self-archived preprint at DLR (the pagination above is the journal's; I cite the preprint's abstract).
- *Read status:* **in part** (abstract and introduction).
- *What it establishes:* 126 randomly selected airline pilots in four groups (short- or long-haul fleet by captain or first officer) flew a manual raw-data approach; "the strongest predictor variable was fleet, indicating degraded performance among long-haul pilots"; "recent flight practice is a significantly stronger predictor for fine-motor flying performance than the time period since flight school or even the total or type-specific flight experience" (abstract).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **supports a design reading of erosion.** What predicted the loss was the amount of recent practice, an opportunity-for-practice variable that the operator's employer sets, not automation exposure in itself. The design is cross-sectional (group comparison), so it supports "recent practice predicts skill," not a causal erosion estimate. It also points the other way from Casner et al. on whether manual skill erodes (Casner found them "mostly intact"); the two differ in measure (fine-motor precision approach versus scanning and manual control) and sample, so the essay should not cite both for one claim.

## (c) Synthesis: answers to RQ-D1 to RQ-D5

**RQ-D1: What did the labor-process debate settle?** It settled very little as a matter of fact and one thing as a matter of method. *Not settled:* whether skill, on balance, rose or fell. Spenner's (1983) abstract says the finding depends on the research design (aggregate studies show "little net change"; case studies show "volatile upgrading and downgrading"), and Attewell's (1987) abstract lists methodological weaknesses in the deskilling position. *Settled enough to rely on:* the strong reading that technology as such deskills is not defensible, and the unit of analysis is the task and its organization. Adler & Borys (1996) say that whether formalization helps or harms depends on whether it "enables employees better to master their tasks"; Barley (1986) says "technology is a social rather than a physical object"; Autor et al. (2003) show displacement of explicit-rule tasks and complementarity with non-routine ones. Two cautions: Hunter (2010) disputes the numerical result of Barley's central comparison, and I could read only abstracts of the classics (Braverman not at all). Ruling 2's move, to treat deskilling as a property of design, therefore has canonical support (entries 2 to 6), and the essay can state it without relying on the contested empirical case.

**RQ-D2: Current status of the LLM up- and deskilling claims.** Sorted by what each study can show (table, B3). The productivity studies (Dell'Acqua, Noy & Zhang, Brynjolfsson) are peer reviewed and large, but measure performance during access; only Brynjolfsson's outage analysis speaks to retained skill, and there it suggests gains persist. The studies that measure retained or unaided skill point both ways. Harm: Bastani et al. (unaided exam -17% for a chat interface without guardrails), Shen & Tamkin (quiz -17%; preprint, n = 52), Melumad & Yun and Stadler et al. (shallower reasoning), Budzyń et al. (ADR 28.4% to 22.4%, observational). No harm: Kazemitabaar et al. (no loss on manual tasks; retention difference not significant), Lehmann et al. (no overall effect in two lab experiments), Bastani's GPT Tutor arm (null). **Self-report and correlational evidence** (Lee et al.; Gerlich) shows perceived reductions in effort and a correlation with offloading; it does not measure skill. **Kosmyna et al.** is an unrefereed preprint with a published critique and an n of 18 for the key session; I recommend not citing it as evidence. Net: the better-identified studies agree on one pattern that the essay can state: *the effect on unaided skill depends on what the interaction asks of the person* (delegating answers harms; hints, conceptual questions, and generate-then-comprehend patterns do not), and *people do not notice the harm* (Bastani et al.; Fernandes et al.; Macnamara et al.).

**RQ-D3: When does offloading degrade retained skill?** Grinschgl et al. give the experimental answer: when offloading is cheap and the person has no goal to learn; the harm "could almost completely" be counteracted when participants were forced to offload but aware of the memory test (Experiment 3). Macnamara et al. add that cognitive skills decay faster than proceduralized ones (Casner et al.). The task structure matters (Kazemitabaar et al.; Shen & Tamkin's six patterns), and the effect of offloading on the item offloaded is far better supported than a general claim about "thinking" (the priming half of Sparrow et al. did not replicate; Hesselmann). *Gap:* all the offloading experiments use reliable stores, not stochastic intermediaries, so transfer to adaptive systems is unestablished.

**RQ-D4: The *Phaedrus* as archetype.** The text supports a narrower claim than the pitch needs: Socrates fears forgetfulness from untrained memory, "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom," and a text that cannot answer questions (§§275a, 275d, Fowler). Each of these has a modern empirical counterpart (Grinschgl; Bastani and Fernandes; and, inverted, the fluent answers of a chat interface). The text does not say writing changed the structure of consciousness, so it cannot carry the pitch's "media revolution" mechanism (Ruling 1). I found no read secondary source (row 8 was thin and Ferrari and Derrida were not obtainable), so I cannot report what scholars have said about whether the loss was "real" or the gain "worth it"; that part of RQ-D4 remains open.

**RQ-D5: Does complacency research give a measurable criterion for deskilling by design?** Yes, with limits. Measurable: detection of automation failures (Parasuraman et al., 1993, as reported in the 2010 review), direct information sampling (Bahner et al.), return-to-manual performance, and performance on cognitive sub-tasks of the unaided task (Casner et al.), plus clinical outcome when unaided (Budzyń et al.). Design variables it identifies: degree of automation, especially whether the system selects the action (Onnasch et al.); practice opportunity (Haslbeck & Hoermann); failure-mode exposure in training (Bahner et al.); the pace and criteria of the system relative to the operator (Bainbridge). *Limit for the thesis:* every study has a single operator with a single non-adaptive automation (card, Parasuraman & Manzey); none involves an intermediary that reads two parties. The criterion transfers as a design for measurement; transfer of the effect sizes is untested.

### Pitch claims the evidence contradicts or complicates

1. **"Deskilling is a nostalgic distraction... People forgetting how to write standard essays is not the crisis" (PITCH.md, section 2 and the talk script) is contradicted as stated.** The measured losses (Bainbridge; Casner; Budzyń; Rinta-Kahila et al.; Shen & Tamkin) are in supervisory and verification skills in tasks where the person stays accountable. Those are the skills algorithmacy depends on (interpreting, keeping track). The essay can still keep the pitch's real point if it separates two cases: loss of a skill that has become obsolete (acceptable) and loss of the skill needed to check or steer the system (not acceptable); Rinta-Kahila et al. draw that same line. A claim about writing essays specifically would need separate evidence (the writing studies here are Kosmyna, not usable; Melumad and Stadler, which find shallower output).
2. **"Frictionless adoption keeps users functionally illiterate" is supported, but "conversational chat is the cause" is not.** Bastani's mitigation stayed in a chat interface; Brynjolfsson's learning evidence is for a non-chat recommender. What changes outcomes is the task structure (hints versus answers; conceptual questions versus delegation).
3. **"Media revolutions" mechanism: nothing in this facet supports it** (Ruling 1 stands). The *Phaedrus* offers a complaint about practice and the appearance of wisdom; the empirical literature offers measurable losses of practiced skill; neither shows restructured cognition.
4. **"Algorithmacy as the new literacy replaces the old" is weakened by task-level evidence** (Autor et al.; Macnamara et al.): judgment tasks gain value as routine tasks are displaced, and the judgment skill is what retained practice supports. The essay should not frame the old and new competences as alternatives.
5. **Equalizing narrative is mixed.** Noy & Zhang, Brynjolfsson and Bastani's practice sessions show leveling during access; Bastani's unaided exam and Lehmann et al. show the leveling does not persist or reverses.

### What the essay can say, and which falsifiable propositions this facet licenses

These are agent suggestions for the author's P5–P8, not author text. Each is stated so that a named study design could refute it.

- *Candidate P-a:* an interface that displays the declared operating envelope and requires an independent attempt before assistance will produce higher unaided performance than an answer-first interface of equal accuracy (Bastani et al.'s three-arm design is the template; GPT Tutor's null shows the control must include a learning-gain measure, not just harm avoidance).
- *Candidate P-b:* exposure to the system's failure modes during use reduces complacency, measured as information sampling, relative to equal-length exposure without failures (Bahner et al. at larger N and with an adaptive intermediary).
- *Candidate P-c:* the unaided-skill penalty of delegation is concentrated in the cognitive sub-tasks, not the routine ones (Casner et al.; testable in LLM tasks by separating verification from production).
- Evidence supports, but does not license as a proposition, the claim that harm goes unnoticed (Bastani; Fernandes).
