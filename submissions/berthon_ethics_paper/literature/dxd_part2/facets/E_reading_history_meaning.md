# E — Reading history, the meaning-in-life framework, and the script concept

*Facet E of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 2 literature review. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-01 (a parallel research agent working in the `wt-dxd` worktree). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** (1) The defensible chronology of interpuncts, scriptio continua and Insular word separation, with the "grammar of legibility" as the existing term for what the scribes did; (2) what the classicists (Knox, Gavrilov, Burnyeat, Johnson, Vatri) establish about silent reading in antiquity, and what modern eye-movement work adds; (3) the media-history register that Ruling 1 rules out as mechanism (Ong, Street, Havelock); (4) Martela and Steger's coherence, purpose and significance, their uptake in HCI and design (Mekler and Hornbæk; Peters, Calvo and Ryan; Desmet and Pohlmeyer; Lukoff et al.), and the replication record behind the coherence construct; (5) Vallor's communicative-friction and habituation passages; (6) Akrich's and Latour's script and Verbeek's uptake of it, plus Verbeek's own critique of opt-out and transparency. **The question the synthesis answers:** which parts of the pitch's scribe analogy and its meaning-and-virtue ethics survive contact with the sources, and which must be restated, softened or dropped before Part 2 is drafted.

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole open-access text this session, or the entry reuses a repo card built from full text. *Read in part* = I obtained the text (open access or, for three books, page-numbered Google Books search-inside snippets of about forty words) and located and read the passages cited, but not the whole work. *Abstract only* = database abstract, opening paragraph or record only. *Not accessible* = no text and no abstract. For snippet-read books, the page number is Google's index of the print edition and each quotation is a forty-word window; I could not see the surrounding paragraph, so the sense of every snippet quotation should be re-checked in context before it enters a draft. Quotations from snippets are normalised for OCR spacing only. Locators marked "reprint" or "preprint" are not the version-of-record pagination.

### B1. Chronology of word division and the grammar of legibility

**saenger1997space** — Saenger, P. (1997). *Space between words: The origins of silent reading*. Stanford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781503619081
- *Verification:* Crossref record (De Gruyter e-book of the Stanford volume, issued 1997); Consensus and publisher abstract. The extent (xxi + 482 pp.) comes from reviewers' headings (Hanna in *Speculum*; Aronoff gives xviii + 480 for the paperback), not from a catalogue I checked.
- *Read status:* read in part. Abstract plus about 40 page-numbered snippets (row 21). The repo card `saenger-1997-space-between-words.md` is a stub that records only the instruction to soften "made silent reading possible".
- *What it argues:* The abstract: "Ancient reading was usually oral, either aloud, in groups, or individually, in a muffled voice"; "the separation of words (and thus silent reading) originated in manuscripts copied by Irish scribes in the seventh and eighth centuries but spread to the European continent only in the late tenth century." The book's explanation of why separation came late is social, not only cognitive: "the ancient world had no desire to make reading easier and swifter."
- *Specific passages Part 2 can rely on (all snippets; re-check in context):*
  - Chronology of interpuncts and scriptio continua. Greek inscriptions "were written with separation by interpuncts" (p. 9); Roman books and inscriptions likewise, "by medial points or interpuncts placed at midlevel in the line," not accompanied by more space than ordinarily falls between letters (p. 26). The Romans then "gave up separation by interpuncts and adopted the Greek convention of scriptura continua. In Rome after the Golden Age, interword space, except as an occasional sign of punctuation, was virtually unknown" (p. 12). Saenger dates the end of interpunct-separated Latin to "prior to the end of the second century A.D." (p. 29; p. 53 gives the same date for Roman lapidary inscriptions, but its context is cut), and a further snippet puts the Roman adoption of scriptura continua in "the late second or third century A.D." (p. 69; the grammatical subject of that sentence is a punctuation sign, which I could not see).
  - Ancient aids were not spaces. Interpuncts and hederae "were ineffective aids to the physiological process of reading when compared with the interword spaces familiar in late medieval manuscripts and modern printed books" (p. 28; also pp. 26–27).
  - The cognitive argument. Without spaces the "ancient reader needed more than twice the normal quantity of fixations and saccades per line" and "a quantity of ocular regressions for which there is no parallel under modern reading" (p. 7; the snippet gives the comparison but not the experimental basis). At its most careful: "Ambrose read silently, given the constraints of ancient writing, he could not have read rapidly in the manner of the modern silent reader, even if he had wanted to do so" (p. 8). So the book itself concedes that Ambrose read silently; its claim is about speed and fluency. The ancient reader "in his initial preparation or praelectio of a text normally had to read orally, aloud or in a muffled voice" (p. 8).
  - Insular origin. "Aerated manuscripts were uniquely a transient phenomenon of the seventh century, after which all Latin writing was separated" in Ireland (p. 34); Anglo-Saxon scribes appear alongside Irish ones in his account of Insular practice (pp. 90, 97; the earliest vernacular Celtic and Anglo-Saxon texts in Latin characters "were aerated," p. 97).
  - Motive and audience. "The notion that the greater portion of the population should be autonomous and self-motivated readers was entirely foreign to the elitist literate mentality of the ancient world" (p. 11).
  - Codex. "Greek codices, written in scriptura continua, pagination or foliation can be documented as early as the third century A.D." (p. 77).
  - Saenger answers Knox. A note on p. 298 cites Knox (1968, p. 424) and says his commentary on Lucian "unfairly represents the balanced analysis of G. L. Hendrickson."
- *Bearing on the outline:* **Supports** the §5 chronology (Insular word separation, 7th–8th c.; interpunct-separated Latin before scriptio continua) and the design reading (the aid was an artefact of the page, and its audience mattered). **Complicates** the date "1st–2nd c. CE" for the standard of scriptio continua: Saenger's own dates are the later second or third century, so "from about the second century" is the safer span. **Complicates** the pitch's "silent reading unlocked new capacities": the book's claim is about rapid silent reading, and p. 8 concedes silent reading was possible. The cognitive model behind it is the contested part (Vatri; Mirault et al.).

**parkes1992pause** — Parkes, M. B. (1992). *Pause and effect: An introduction to the history of punctuation in the West*. Scolar Press. (Routledge reprint 2016: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315247243)
- *Verification:* Crossref (Routledge reprint record and chapter records, with page ranges); Google Books record `X11nAAAAMAAJ` ("Pause and Effect", 1992, University of Michigan copy). The Routledge chapter pages (ch. 1, pp. 9–19; ch. 2, pp. 20–29; ch. 3, pp. 30–34) agree with the page numbers in the 1992-edition snippets.
- *Read status:* read in part. Crossref and Consensus chapter abstracts for chs. 1–3 and 8 (each abstract read in full), plus 12 queries of the search-inside endpoint (row 21).
- *What it argues:* Ancient literacy was a "conservative educational tradition in which teaching was directed towards the preparation of pupils for effective public speaking" (ch. 1 abstract, pp. 9–19), and the "prehistory of punctuation" lies in the grammarians' marking of texts for the *praelectio* of "pupils or inexperienced readers." Christian communities gave the written word the status of "a medium in its own right," which produced a "decorum" governing "this complex of graphic conventions and the message of a text" that "may be described as 'the grammar of legibility'" (ch. 2 abstract, pp. 20–29). "Irish and Anglo-Saxon missionaries taught their scribal practices to pupils in new foundations"; Carolingian scribes then "developed a complex of new graphic conventions both to improve the intelligibility of texts copied in minuscule scripts, and to facilitate access to the information transmitted in the written medium" (ch. 3 abstract, pp. 30–34).
- *Specific passages Part 2 can rely on:*
  - The design purpose, in the author's frame: "word separation, features of layout and punctuation, were developed to make it easier for readers to extract the information conveyed in the written medium" (p. 1); where Latin was "a foreign language, there was a need for conventions which made it easier to read" (p. 1; the clause before "foreign language" was cut by the snippet).
  - Aids existed before spaces: "Marks were inserted linking or separating words (hyphen or diastole), long vowels were furnished with apices, and marks (positurae) were added to indicate the duration of pauses" (p. 12); manuscripts so pointed "became known as codices distincti. The earliest codices to survive were produced in the fifth and …" (p. 13; the sentence is cut, and I read "codices" there as the pointed codices).
  - **Parkes repeats the older view of silent reading.** "… silent reading were so rare that the young Augustine was astonished when he encountered Ambrose reading without making a sound" (p. 9; the sentence begins before the snippet).
- *Bearing on the outline:* **Supports** the Insular-origin claim and gives the field's own term for what the scribes did, *the grammar of legibility* (ruling 4: prefer the literature's term; the pitch's "visual syntax" is a coinage). **Complicates** any use of Parkes against Saenger: the book appeared in 1992, five years before *Space between Words*, and on silent reading it sits with the pre-correction consensus. I found no passage where Parkes contests Saenger (see synthesis, E1).

**rayner1998unspaced** — Rayner, K., Fischer, M. H., & Pollatsek, A. (1998). Unspaced text interferes with both word identification and eye movement control. *Vision Research, 38*(8), 1129–1144. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989(97)00274-5
- *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex W1976782915; Unpaywall lists a publisher PDF.
- *Read status:* not accessible (publisher PDF returned 403; no abstract in OpenAlex or Crossref). The Semantic Scholar machine TLDR says the removal of spaces "increased the effect of word frequency on the fixation times for selected target words," which I do not rely on.
- *What it establishes:* Taken from Mirault et al. (2019, p. 22): reading unspaced text "is slower by about 40% to 70% than reading normally spaced text" (citing Rayner et al., 1998, and Rayner & Pollatsek, 1996), with shorter saccades, longer fixations and more regressions, and an effect of word frequency that is greater without spaces.
- *Bearing on the outline:* **Supports** the weak claim (spaces lower the cost of reading); **does not support** an impossibility claim. Read the paper itself before quoting a figure.

**mirault2019reading** — Mirault, J., Snell, J., & Grainger, J. (2019). Reading without spaces revisited: The role of word identification and sentence-level constraints. *Acta Psychologica, 195*, 22–29. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2019.03.001
- *Verification:* Crossref; HAL deposit `hal-02138873` (carries the journal's pagination).
- *Read status:* read in part (abstract, introduction and the framing of the study, p. 22; I did not check the stimuli or statistics).
- *What it argues:* French sentences, spaced and unspaced, ordered and shuffled. Reading was hampered by removing spaces and by removing sentence structure; the authors "conclude that the relative ease with which skilled readers can read unspaced text is due to a combination of an increased use of bottom-up word identification in guiding the timing and targeting of eye movements, plus an increased interactivity between word identification and sentence-level processing" (abstract, p. 22).
- *Passages Part 2 can rely on:* p. 22: "Anecdotally, it is quite striking to note the ease with which skilled readers can read text presented without spacing between words," and the authors cite Saenger (1997) for the historical point that "Latin script was originally written without extra between-word spacing." The same paragraph reports the 40–70 per cent slowing.
- *Bearing on the outline:* **Supports** "made ordinary and lowered the cost, not made possible." The finding that experienced readers adapt is the empirical counterpart of Knox and Gavrilov. The participants were twenty native French speakers recruited at Aix-Marseille University and the texts were French sentences, so it says nothing directly about Latin readers.

### B2. Silent reading in antiquity

**knox1968silent** — Knox, B. M. W. (1968). Silent reading in antiquity. *Greek, Roman, and Byzantine Studies, 9*(4), 421–435. https://grbs.library.duke.edu/index.php/grbs/article/view/10731
- *Verification:* the journal's own page (citation meta tags: vol. 9, no. 4, pp. 421–435, dated 27 December 1968); no DOI; open-access PDF carries the original pagination.
- *Read status:* **read in full.**
- *What it argues:* Balogh's "Voces paginarum" (1927) made it "standard doctrine that silent reading (and writing) was, if not completely unknown in the ancient world, at least so rare that whenever it was observed, it aroused astonishment" (p. 421). Knox concedes the narrow point: "the normal way to read a literary text (non-literary texts are a different matter and will be dealt with later) was out loud, whether before an audience, in the company of friends or alone" (p. 421), and concludes that "Ancient books were normally read aloud" (p. 435). He refutes the wide one.
- *Specific passages Part 2 can rely on:*
  - Augustine's Ambrose (Conf. 6.3) is not a ready index of Mediterranean practice: Augustine "was not only a professor of rhetoric but also an African provincial from a poor family"; Ambrose had held imperial office, and "there is no reason to suppose that Ambrose's silent reading would have excited such comment in the Italian imperial circles" (p. 422). Augustine himself reads Romans "in silentio" at Conf. 8.12 (p. 422).
  - Cicero, *Tusc.* 5.116 (the deaf can take "much greater pleasure in reading [songs] than in hearing them"): "Cicero could not possibly have written the concluding phrase if silent reading of poetic texts had been impossible or even a nine-day wonder" (p. 427).
  - Documents: Suetonius, *Aug.* 39 (equites told to read tablets "silently and at once"; the silence marks leniency, p. 428); Horace *Sat.* 2.5 (furtive silent reading of a will, pp. 428–429).
  - Antiphanes' *Sappho*, a riddle whose answer is a letter whose "children" are inaudible "for anyone who happens to be standing near the man who is reading": silent reading of a letter in company "was taken completely for granted" in fourth-century Athens (pp. 432–433).
  - Euripides, *Hippolytus* 856ff. (Theseus reads Phaedra's letter on stage between a choral interval and his outcry) and Aristophanes, *Knights* 115ff. (Demosthenes reads an oracle while ordering wine): "silent reading of letters and oracles (and consequently of any short document) was taken completely for granted" for fifth- and fourth-century Athens (p. 434; the two stage scenes at p. 433).

  - Conclusion: "Ancient books were normally read aloud, but there is nothing to show that silent reading of books was anything extraordinary except the famous passage from Augustine's *Confessions*, and that is countered by the phrase of Cicero" (p. 435).

"Made ordinary" is accurate for literary reading; "existed" is accurate for both.

### E1. What is the defensible chronology of interpuncts, scriptio continua and Insular word separation, and which claims in Saenger do Parkes and the classicists contest?

**What the sources support.**
- Early Greek and Latin inscriptions and Latin books separated words with interpuncts, medial points placed at mid-height of the line (Saenger, pp. 9, 26). Interpuncts (and hederae) were visual marks, not spaces (pp. 26–28).
- Latin then adopted the Greek convention of continuous script. Saenger puts the end of interpunct separation "prior to the end of the second century A.D." (p. 29) and the adoption of scriptura continua in "the late second or third century A.D." (p. 69). The outline's "1st–2nd c. CE" is therefore a little early. A safer statement is "from about the second century CE" or "by the third century."
- Aids to reading existed in antiquity without word spacing: the grammarians' *praelectio* marks, the diastole and hyphen, apices, *positurae* for pauses (Parkes, pp. 12–13), and the *codices distincti* (p. 13). Systematic punctuation and the bound codex are therefore not eighth-century monastic inventions: the codex is documented in the third century (Saenger, p. 77), and Parkes (p. 13, sentence cut) dates the earliest surviving pointed codices to the fifth.
- Systematic word separation with space appears in Irish manuscripts of the seventh century and spreads with Irish and Anglo-Saxon missionaries (Saenger, abstract, p. 34; Parkes ch. 3 abstract). Parkes and Saenger agree on the Insular origin. Both give a design-for-new-readers motive: Parkes says that where Latin was "a foreign language, there was a need for conventions which made it easier to read" (p. 1). The Oxford thesis of Tunbridge (1992, abstract only, a lead) argues the same.
- Continental adoption is gradual and its date depends on the author. Saenger: separation "spread to the European continent only in the late tenth century" (abstract). Parkes: Carolingian scribes took the Insular "grammar of legibility" and added new graphic conventions from the eighth to ninth century (ch. 3 abstract). The essay should say "spread to the Continent over the following centuries" and cite both.

**What is contested.** Not the Insular chronology. The disputes are:
1. *Silent reading in antiquity.* The classicists (Knox 1968; Gavrilov and Burnyeat 1997; Johnson 2000; Vatri 2012) deny that scriptio continua made silent reading impossible. Saenger's own p. 8 concedes that Ambrose read silently but slowly, which is a narrower claim than "impossible"; Vatri reports the "impossible" version as Saenger's, so the book may have moderated the claim.
2. *The physiological mechanism.* Saenger's model (more fixations and regressions without spaces, vocal rehearsal to hold ambiguous phonemes) is partially true as a cost: unspaced text is read about 40–70 per cent more slowly (Rayner et al., 1998, via Mirault et al., p. 22), but "skilled readers" read it "with ease" (Mirault et al., p. 22) and Greek, Latin and Insular readers evidently did.
3. *Parkes versus Saenger.* I found no passage where Parkes contests Saenger. *Pause and Effect* (1992) predates *Space between Words* (1997) and, on silent reading, repeats the earlier view (p. 9, "so rare that the young Augustine was astonished"). Parkes is a co-source on the Insular origin and the grammar of legibility, not a critic. If the author wants a Parkes-versus-Saenger contrast, I have no evidence for it, and Parkes's Saenger-era articles (collected in *Pages from the Past*, 2012) are unread.

### E2. What does the Knox/Gavrilov/Burnyeat/Johnson literature establish about silent reading in antiquity, so that the analogy says "made ordinary," never "made possible"?

- Knox (1968, read in full) establishes that silent reading of letters, oracles and short documents was taken for granted in fifth- and fourth-century Athens (pp. 432–434), that Cicero treats reading verse silently as a pleasure the deaf may have (p. 427), that Augustine himself reads silently at a crisis (Conf. 8.12, p. 422), and that the astonishment at Ambrose is not a mark of general practice (p. 422). He agrees that books (literary texts) were "normally read aloud" (p. 435).
- Gavrilov and Burnyeat (1997), as Johnson (2000, p. 593) reports them, add that there was "no cognitive difficulty" for fully literate ancient readers in reading silently and that the act was "neither extraordinary nor noticeably unusual." Johnson then questions the terms of the debate and turns to the social setting of reading.
- The safe wording is therefore: silent reading existed and was ordinary for documents and for expert readers; literary reading was usually aloud; visual aids lowered the cost and widened the group for whom fluent private reading was practical. That wording is accurate to Knox (reading aloud for literature, silent for documents), to Mirault et al. (cost, not possibility) and to Saenger p. 8 ("silent … not rapidly"). It is also exactly the form of the analogy Ruling 1 allows.
- **Caution.** "Made ordinary" is itself an inference. None of the sources I could read measures when silent literary reading became typical; the late-medieval dates are Saenger's (not read in full here) and rest on the contested model.

### E3. How do Martela and Steger's coherence, purpose and significance map onto design obligations, and has any HCI or design work used the tripartite this way?

**Prior uses in HCI and design.** None of the sources I found derives design *obligations* from the Martela and Steger tripartite.
- Mekler and Hornbæk (2019) take three of its elements (coherence, purpose, significance) into a five-component HCI framework and pose open questions, among them designs that "help foster predictability" (p. 10); they claim no duties.
- Peters, Calvo and Ryan (2018) use self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness), not the tripartite, and treat compulsory use only as a motivation type (p. 9).
- Desmet and Pohlmeyer (2013) use a different triad (pleasure, personal significance, virtue; pp. 5, 8) and add a paternalism caveat (p. 15).
- Lukoff et al. (2018, 2021) supply field evidence that interface features bear on experienced meaning and agency, but in recreational use.
- Nyholm and Rüther (2023) connect meaning in life to AI ethics through the *philosophical* meaning literature and say little has been published (§5.2, p. 11).
- Rapp and Boldi (2023) and Nguyen et al. (2022) are leads, not read.

**A mapping the author may propose (author-side inference, flagged).** Everything in this table is a candidate reading, not a finding of any source.

| Dimension (Martela & Steger, 2016) | What the source says | Candidate design obligation in the outline | Source support | Evidence caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | Comprehensibility; "predictable and conform to recognizable patterns" (p. 534); "accurate mental models … to facilitate predictability and consistency" (p. 536) | Display the declared operating envelope; keep updates backward-compatible | Martela & Steger pp. 533–536; Mekler & Hornbæk pp. 5, 10 | Empirical anchor (Heintzelman et al.) failed a preregistered replication (Ratner et al.); Costin and Vignoles find coherence did not predict meaning judgments |

Two observations the author may want. (i) The tripartite does not contain a competence leg. The natural home for "algorithmacy as acquired operational skill" is SDT's *competence* need (Peters et al., p. 2) or Antonovsky's manageability (via Martela and Steger's note 2), not coherence. (ii) Martela and Steger themselves say that coherence "might be a necessary condition for significance, but … not a sufficient condition" (p. 539), and offer a case (Heikinheimo) where coherence rose and significance fell. That limits any argument that predictability alone ends the duty.

### E4. Where in Vallor (2016) are the occasions-for-practice and communicative-friction passages, and which technomoral virtues bear on steering an intermediary?

- *Communicative friction:* §7.1.1, pp. 161–164 (not ch. 6, as the card states). The ideal of "frictionless interactions" (p. 161), the Facebook Home example (p. 162), the three-part criterion (pp. 162–163, card), and the consequence "a frictionless world … would diminish us all. In particular, it would render meaningless technomoral virtues such as care" (p. 164).
- *Occasions for practice:* not found as a phrase. The substance is in the habituation passages (pp. 63, 66, 74–75, 97) and, verbatim, in the 2015 paper: "with sufficient opportunity for repetition" (p. 109).
- *Virtues:* the habits of moral attention (pp. 99–105) and prudential judgment (p. 99), and among the twelve technomoral virtues *flexibility* (§6.10, pp. 145–149) and *perspective* (§6.11, p. 149); flexibility, defined as a "reliable and skillful disposition to modulate action, belief, and feeling as called for by novel, un…" (p. 147, snippet cut), is the one that most directly fits operating an adaptive system. I could not locate "technomoral wisdom" in the snippets I obtained, so I make no claim about it.
- Vallor's own remedy is the cultivation of persons, with institutions as supports (pp. 10–11, per the card), and the friction criterion is on practices, not on a system that sets the practice's tempo. So the claim "design owes the occasion to practise" is an extension of her criterion, not a restatement.

### E5. What exactly do Akrich and Latour mean by *script*, and how does Verbeek take it up, so that the pitch's attribution is corrected?

- Akrich (1987 French original, ¶ 10, read; 1992 English chapter) coins the term: the designer writes into the object "un script, un scénario" that predetermines the roles users are asked to play; the script is a hypothesis about the world the object will enter, and it is realised or defeated only when actual users take or refuse the roles (¶ 10). The method is the back-and-forth between "l'utilisateur-projet du concepteur" and "l'utilisateur réel" (¶ 11).
- Latour (1992) elaborates the vocabulary: inscription, prescription ("the moral and ethical dimension of mechanisms," p. 157 reprint), subscription, circumscription (pp. 161–162), and the removal test (pp. 154–155).
- Verbeek (2006, preprint §1; 2011, p. 10; 2008, p. 94 per the card) takes the script from Akrich and Latour into design ethics. Tromp, Hekkert and Verbeek (2011, p. 4) say it directly: "The concept of script had been introduced by Akrich to describe the 'implicit manuals' that products embody, and Latour elaborated on this concept."
- **The attribution fix is confirmed.** The pitch's "Verbeek's invisible scripts" should read "Akrich's (1992) and Latour's (1992) scripts, which Verbeek (2006, 2011) takes up." I found no Verbeek text using "invisible scripts"; the nearest documented idea is the hidden-versus-apparent axis in Tromp et al. (2011, p. 12), and the Thaler–Sunstein publicity point Verbeek (2015) reports (card, not re-read).
- **Two cautions.** First, the script is a *design-time* object (Akrich ¶ 10; Latour card). For adaptive intermediaries the prescriptions change at run time, which is exactly where "declared operating envelope" has work to do. Second, Dorrestijn and Verbeek (2013, pp. 52–53) already say that opt-out and total transparency "cannot function as fundamental ethical conditions" under technical mediation, so Part 2's thesis is an extension of a position Verbeek holds, to adaptive systems and a no-exit setting.

### Pitch claims the evidence contradicts or qualifies

1. **"Silent reading internalized human consciousness and unlocked entirely new cognitive capacities"** (PITCH.md). Contradicted as stated: silent reading existed (Knox, Gavrilov, Burnyeat, Johnson), skilled readers read unspaced text with ease (Mirault et al., p. 22), and Saenger himself says Ambrose read silently but slowly (p. 8). This is Ruling 1's point and should not be asserted.
2. **"Eighth-century monks invented word separation, punctuation, and the bound codex."** Three claims, one correct. Insular scribes did introduce systematic word separation by space (7th–8th c.; Saenger p. 34, Parkes ch. 3). Punctuation had a long prehistory (Parkes ch. 1, pp. 12–13), and the codex was documented by the third century (Saenger p. 77; see also Parkes p. 13, sentence cut).
3. **"Visual syntax … taught the brain a new way of processing."** The only support is Saenger's physiological model, which the classicists dispute (Vatri 2012). The literature's own term is *grammar of legibility* (Parkes), and its stated aim was easier extraction of information, with Latin as a foreign language as the motive (Parkes p. 1).
4. **"Scriptio continua became the standard around the 1st–2nd c. CE"** (outline). Slightly early: Saenger puts the end of interpunct-separated Latin around the end of the second century and Roman adoption in "the late second or third century" (pp. 29, 69).
5. **"Verbeek's invisible scripts."** Misattributed: Akrich 1987/1992, Latour 1992, taken up by Verbeek; no "invisible scripts" phrase found (rows 7–8).
6. **Coherence as the empirical ground of the ethics.** Martela and Steger's coherence is predictability-shaped (supports the pitch's use), but the one experiment behind it did not replicate (Ratner et al., 2016), and the strongest longitudinal predictor of meaning judgments is mattering (Costin and Vignoles, 2020). The coherence leg is a conceptual argument, not a finding.
7. **"Occasions for practice" as a Vallor term.** Not found; closest verbatim is "sufficient opportunity for repetition" (2015, p. 109).
8. **Parkes against Saenger.** If the essay sets the two against each other, I found no support; Parkes (1992) predates Saenger (1997) and repeats the "rare" view of silent reading (p. 9).
