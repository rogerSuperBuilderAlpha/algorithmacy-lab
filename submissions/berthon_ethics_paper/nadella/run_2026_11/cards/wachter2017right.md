## Summary

Read: the abstract, the legal-basis sections and the conclusion; other pages searched by keyword. The authors argue that the GDPR does not give a right to an explanation of a specific automated decision. They find only a "right to be informed" about system functionality (arts. 13-15), given before or on request, and limited by the controller's interests. The one place a post-decision explanation appears, recital 71, is non-binding, and the authors read the legislative history as a deliberate choice to keep it out of art. 22(3). Art. 22 also covers only decisions "based solely on automated processing" with legal or similarly significant effects. They propose legislative steps, including putting a right to explanation into a binding article. The paper is a doctrinal reading of the 2016 text, not an empirical study of whom explanations reach.

## Key facts it relies on

- The abstract says the GDPR "lacks precise language as well as explicit and well-defined rights and safeguards against automated decision-making, and therefore runs the risk of being toothless". [quote] (manuscript p. 1, Abstract)
- "Despite claims to the contrary, a meaningful right to explanation is not legally mandated by the General Data Protection Regulation." [quote] (p. 41, section 5, Conclusion)
- What the GDPR does give is a "right to be informed" about system functionality, "restricted by the interests of data controllers and future interpretations of Article 15". [quote] (p. 41, section 5)
- The distinction drawn: "After a decision has been made (ex post), an explanation of system functionality can still be provided to the data subject", but this is not an explanation of the rationale of the specific decision. [quote] (p. 7)
- The explanation of a specific decision appears only in recital 71, whose wording the authors read as non-binding; they infer that "legislators intentionally chose to make the right to explanation non-binding by placing it in Recital 71". [quote] (p. 12, section 3.1)
- Art. 22(3) safeguards (human intervention, expression of views, contest) apply only where automated decision-making falls under art. 22(2)(a) or (c), that is, contract or consent. [paraphrase] (p. 36, section 4)
- Recommendation: if a right to explanation is intended, it "should be explicitly added to a legally binding Article of the GDPR". [quote] (p. 43, recommendations)

## Critical notes

- Contested: the paper rebuts the claim, made by other scholars it cites (p. 5), that the GDPR gives a right to explanation of specific decisions. Events after 2017 (later case law, AI Act art. 86) are outside this text and were not checked here.
- The paper concerns the legal content of the right, not whether explanations reach or serve people; it does not test that. It sits on STRUCTURE 4.2 as the source for "the law's narrow, after-the-fact rights".
