## Summary

Read in full: the 47-page author manuscript, body and footnotes. The authors argue that the GDPR does not give a right to an explanation of a specific automated decision. They test three possible bases: the art. 22(3) safeguards with recital 71, the notification duties of arts. 13-14, and the right of access in art. 15 (section 3). They find only a "right to be informed" about the existence of automated decision-making and system functionality, limited by the controller's interests such as trade secrets (pp. 29-30, 41). The one place a post-decision explanation appears, recital 71, is not legally binding, and the authors read the drafting history as a deliberate choice to keep it out of art. 22(3) (pp. 10-13). Art. 22 also covers only decisions "based solely on automated processing" with legal or similarly significant effects, and it can be read either as a prohibition or as a right to object, readings the authors say favour data subjects and controllers respectively (pp. 33-35, 37-40, 42). They do not rule the right out for the future: they name four routes to it, call the third and fourth the most plausible, and make eight recommendations, among them a binding right to explanation in art. 22(3) and auditing by a trusted third party (pp. 31-32, 43-47). The paper is a doctrinal reading of the 2016 text written before the GDPR applied, not an empirical study of whom explanations reach.

## Key facts it relies on

- The abstract says the GDPR "lacks precise language as well as explicit and well-defined rights and safeguards against automated decision-making, and therefore runs the risk of being toothless". [quote] (manuscript p. 1, Abstract)
- The distinction drawn: an explanation may concern system functionality or a specific decision, and may come before or after the decision. "After a decision has been made (ex post), an explanation of system functionality can still be provided to the data subject"; the provider can then also explain the specific decision, and it is that kind the authors find missing from the binding text. [quote] (pp. 6-7, section 2)
- The explanation of a specific decision appears only in recital 71, which as a recital is not legally binding (pp. 9-10); from the drafting history the authors infer that "legislators intentionally chose to make the right to explanation non-binding by placing it in Recital 71". [quote] (p. 12, section 3.1)
- The door left open: "This is, however, only one possible future. A right to explanation is thus not currently legally mandated by the requirements set in Article 22(3)." The possible future is jurisprudence that reads a right to explanation into the art. 22(3) safeguards. [quote] (p. 10, section 3.1)
- "only an ex ante explanation of system functionality is explicitly required by Articles 13(2)f and 14(2)g". [quote] (p. 15, section 3.2)
- On art. 15 the authors concede that "one cannot be certain, on the basis of semantics alone, that the right of access is limited to explanations of system functionality", and then argue that "the GDPR's right of access only grants an explanation of automated decision-making addressing system functionality, not the rationale and circumstances of specific decisions". [quote] (p. 19, section 3.3)
- "a right of access that grants data subjects some explanation of automated decision-making is not new, and has not proven an effective transparency mechanism". [quote] (pp. 19-20, section 3.3.1; the sentence runs across the page break and cites one case at n. 38)
- Under the 1995 Directive, "In general, data subjects are entitled to receive some information about the general functionality of an automated decision-making system, but little to no information about the rationale or circumstances of a specific decision." Trade secrets and intellectual property are the main limits. [quote] (p. 21, section 3.3.1)
- Because art. 22(1) covers only decisions based "solely" on automated processing, even nominal human involvement may take a decision outside art. 22 and the art. 15(1)(h) information right; the authors call this a loophole and ask that it be closed. [paraphrase] (pp. 25-26, 33, 44-45; sections 3.3.1 and 4.1, recommendation 3)
- "The GDPR appears to offer less protection to data subjects concerning explanations of automated decision-making than some current data protection laws in Europe based on the Directive." [quote] (p. 27, section 3.3.2)
- Four routes to a right to explanation of specific decisions: Member State law, voluntary offer by controllers, jurisprudence on art. 22(3), jurisprudence on art. 15. "Of these scenarios, the third and fourth seem to be the most plausible at the moment." The fourth is later called "plausible (but unlikely)". [quote] (pp. 31-32, section 4)
- "The right to contest has already been interpreted by Member States, in enacting the 1995 Directive, as merely a right to force a controller to make a new decision." [quote] (p. 31, section 4)
- "A right to contest realised through expert human intervention may be the most pragmatic safeguard against automated decisions. Elsewhere it has been argued that transparency disclosures prove more impactful if tailored towards trained third parties or regulators as opposed to data subjects themselves." [quote] (p. 32, n. 91)
- Art. 22(3) safeguards (human intervention, expression of views, contest) apply only where automated decision-making falls under art. 22(2)(a) or (c), that is, contract or consent. [paraphrase] (p. 36, section 4.2)
- On the contract exception: "Data controllers are therefore allowed to decide that automated decision-making is necessary for contractual obligations, while the data subject is unable to object to it." [quote] (p. 37, section 4.2)
- On art. 22 read as a right to object: "Once an objection is entered, decision-making must be shown to meet one of these requirements or must stop altogether. As a right, the data subject's interests in not being subjected to automated decision-making are undermined, insofar as significant effort (i.e. entering an objection) is required from the subject to protect her interests." [quote] (p. 42, section 5)
- "Article 22 therefore roughly favours the interests of data subjects when interpreted as a prohibition, and the interests of data controllers when interpreted as a right." The authors recommend the prohibition reading. [quote] (p. 42, section 5; recommendation 6, pp. 45-46)
- "Despite claims to the contrary, a meaningful right to explanation is not legally mandated by the General Data Protection Regulation." [quote] (p. 41, section 5, Conclusion)
- What the GDPR does give is a "right to be informed" about system functionality, "restricted by the interests of data controllers and future interpretations of Article 15". [quote] (p. 41, section 5)
- Recommendation: if a right to explanation is intended, it "should be explicitly added to a legally binding Article of the GDPR". [quote] (p. 43, recommendation 1)
- "The rights to contest a decision, to obtain human intervention or to express views granted in Article 22(3) may be meaningless if the data subject cannot understand how the contested decision was made." [quote] (p. 43, recommendation 1)
- "An ideal solution would allow for examination of automated decision-making systems, including the rationale and circumstances of specific decisions, by a trusted third party." Such auditing would give "an oversight mechanism for data subjects that can operate when explanations are infeasible or too complex for lay comprehension". [quote] (p. 46, recommendation 7)
- "the GDPR can be a toothless or powerful mechanism to protect data subjects depending on its eventual legal interpretation". [quote] (p. 47, section 5)

## Critical notes

- Contested: the paper rebuts the claim that the GDPR gives a right to explanation of specific decisions, which it attributes chiefly to Goodman and Flaxman (nn. 2, 11, 14; pp. 3, 5, 8). It records one opposing view on the force of recitals (Pagallo, n. 17, p. 10). Within this library the thesis is contested by Bayamlıoğlu 2021 (card bayamlioglu2021contest, sections 2.3.1-2.3.2, pp. 5-6) and by Kaminski 2019 (card kaminski2019right, which has her arguing that the GDPR does contain a right to explanation; her text cites this paper repeatedly and was not re-read here).
- Timing: written before the GDPR applied; the Abstract and Conclusion speak of 2018 as ahead. The authors say the outcome will turn on later interpretation by supervisory authorities, the Article 29 Working Party, the European Data Protection Board and the courts (pp. 30, 47). Events after 2017 (later case law, AI Act art. 86) are outside this text and were not checked here; the library's cards cjeu2023schufa and cjeu2025dun carry later judgments of the Court of Justice and belong beside this one.
- The paper concerns the legal content of the right, not whether explanations reach or serve people; it does not test that. It makes two passing claims on the question, neither tested: that the Directive's right of access "has not proven an effective transparency mechanism" (pp. 19-20) and that disclosures may do more when aimed at trained third parties or regulators (n. 91, p. 32).
- The paper is not an argument against explanation. Its recommendations ask for a binding right to explanation, a prohibition reading of art. 22 and third-party auditing (pp. 43-46). It also warns that a right requiring human-interpretable methods carries "risks for innovation and beneficial processing" (p. 44).
- On timing within the law: the information the GDPR requires comes before the decision and concerns the system (arts. 13-14, p. 15); the safeguards that come after the decision (art. 22(3): human intervention, expression of views, contest) carry no right to an explanation (pp. 8, 36). On this reading the GDPR's information rights are not after-the-fact rights; only the art. 22(3) safeguards are.
