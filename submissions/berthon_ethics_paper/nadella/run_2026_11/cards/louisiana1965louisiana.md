## Summary

Read in full: the official U.S. Reports print, syllabus through the closing note of Harlan J. The Attorney General
sued Louisiana, the three members of its State Board of Registration and the Board's Director-Secretary under 42
U.S.C. § 1971(c), charging violations of § 1971(a) and the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. The Court (Black J.)
affirmed a three-judge District Court that had held
Louisiana's "interpretation test" invalid on its face and as applied, because it gave registrars uncontrolled
discretion without objective standards and had been used in 21 parishes to keep Black applicants off the rolls. The
decree enjoined the test statewide, barred the State's new multiple-choice "citizenship" test in those 21 parishes
until a complete reregistration of voters, and required monthly registration reports. The Court expressly reserved
the constitutionality of the new citizenship test as a voting qualification. The opinion rests on the absence of
standards and on racial purpose and effect, not on any holding that a reading or comprehension qualification is
itself unlawful. Harlan J. would rest the result on the Fifteenth Amendment alone.

## Key facts it relies on

- The 1921 constitution required an applicant to be able to "give a reasonable interpretation" of any clause of the State or Federal Constitution. p. 148 [quote]
- The 1960 amendment required every applicant to "be able to understand" as well as interpret a section "when read to him by the registrar"; under the 1921 text the read-aloud comprehension branch "applied only to illiterates." p. 149 and n. 8 [quote]
- Who administered it: the registrars decided the manner of the test, "whether it is to be oral or written, the length and complexity of the sections ... and what interpretation is to be considered correct"; the State Board supervised and could remove a registrar "at will." p. 150; p. 151 n. 10 [quote]
- A contemporary named the wrong in 1898: a convention delegate criticized an interpretation test because the "arbitrary power, lodged with the registration officer, practically places his decision beyond the pale of judicial review." p. 152 (quoting Kernan 1898-1899, pp. 59-60) [quote]
- The Governor in 1898 preferred the grandfather clause to methods that left qualification "largely to the arbitrary discretion of the officers administering the law." p. 152 (quoting Louisiana Senate Journal 1898, p. 33) [quote]
- The State admitted the provisions imposed "no definite and objective standards upon registrars of voters for the administration of the interpretation test." p. 152 [quote]
- The District Court found Louisiana "provides no effective method whereby arbitrary and capricious action by registrars of voters may be prevented or redressed." p. 152 (quoting 225 F. Supp. at 384) [quote]
- "This is not a test but a trap, sufficient to stop even the most brilliant man on his way to the voting booth." p. 153 [quote]
- The Court called *Schnell v. Davis*, 336 U.S. 933, affirming 81 F. Supp. 872, "Squarely in point" for the Alabama "understand and explain" requirement. p. 153 [quote]
- In the 21 parishes Black registration fell from 25,361 to 10,351 while white registration rose. p. 155 [paraphrase]
- The new citizenship test, as the State described it, had the applicant draw one of ten cards of six multiple-choice questions, four to be answered correctly; the Court expressed "no opinion as to the constitutionality" of it. p. 154 and n. 17 [quote]
- Remedy: postponement of the new test in the 21 parishes, "as to persons who met age and residence requirements during the years in which the interpretation test was used," until a complete reregistration "so that the new test will apply alike to all or to none," and monthly reports. pp. 155-156 [quote]

## Critical notes

- Limits: the holding strikes a standardless discretionary test used for racial exclusion; it reserves a uniform
  objective test (n. 17) and does not reach a literacy qualification as such. *Lassiter v. Northampton County Board
  of Elections*, 360 U.S. 45 (1959), which upheld a literacy test on its face, is not cited in this opinion; the 1961
  Commission report on disk reports that holding at its p. 34 (file of card usccr1961voting, outside that card's
  pages_read). A summary that says the Court struck down literacy tests overstates it.
- Form of correction (question 3): prohibition (injunction) plus a remedy that equalizes exposure (reregistration of
  all, not just the excluded) and a reporting duty to the court. No helper, check of understanding or shift of risk.
- The remedy is court-administered and after the fact; the opinion itself records the absence of any route to
  prevent or redress a registrar's decision (p. 152).
- Harlan J. concurs on the Fifteenth Amendment only (p. 156), so the Fourteenth Amendment ground is not unanimous.
- Bearing: supports 3.2 (the gate is the registrar's discretion, named as a wrong by an 1898 contemporary); narrows
  3.2 (discretion and racial purpose, not literacy, are condemned); supports 7-D02 as a prohibition precedent;
  4.4-contest on the missing route of redress.
- Corrected by V-A-2: the card-draw description of the new citizenship test is attributed to the State ("according to the State", p. 154), not stated as the Court's finding.
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-2-1, 2026-11-18): the Summary had the suit brought "under § 1971(a)"; the opinion says the Attorney General sued under § 1971(c), charging violations of § 1971(a), and names the three Board members as defendants (p. 147). The remedy fact now carries the opinion's limit to persons who met age and residence requirements while the interpretation test was in use (p. 155). The *Lassiter* reference under Limits came from outside the file; it is now marked as not cited in the opinion and tied to the 1961 Commission report on disk (printed p. 34).
