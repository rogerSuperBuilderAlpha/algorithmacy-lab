## Summary
Read in full. Vieira, a composition scholar, argues that after the rebuttal of Goody and Watt's "autonomous" theory the question "What are the consequences of literacy?" is still worth asking. She takes Brandt and Clinton's view that literacy is an object that helps constitute the social, and offers three ethnographic vignettes of undocumented and immigrant people in the United States and Brazil. Literacy there acts as a "navigational technology" that opens some paths and closes others: a citizenship reading test failed three times, passports and identity documents that regulate movement, and computers that shift a family's sense of who is literate. She concludes that literacy "often oppresses, disenfranchises, regulates" as well as empowers. The evidence is a handful of interviews from her own fieldwork, offered as "suggestive".

## Key facts it relies on
- "In short, Goody and Watt’s “autonomous” theory of literacy has been rebutted." [quote] p. 26.
- "There are consequences to literacy—large ones and, my own fieldwork suggests, often troubling ones." [quote] p. 26.
- "They argued that literacy is not only a social product, but that it is also an object that actively constitutes the social." [quote, describing Brandt and Clinton 2002] p. 27.
- A Portuguese immigrant of 34 years' residence reports that she failed the citizenship literacy test three times: "That’s why I lost [failed] three times on the citizen papers." [quote] p. 27.
- "Literacy can sometimes empower, but often it oppresses, disenfranchises, regulates." [quote] p. 28.
- "Texts are as strong as the strongest make them." [quote] p. 30.
- "That literacy is shaped by its social context has been irrefutably established." [quote] p. 30.

## Critical notes
- Contested by Street (note 4, p. 31) on Brandt and Clinton's global-local move, which he says risks making global forces seem "autonomous"; Vieira reports this and does not resolve it.
- Limits: a few interviews, no sampling, present-day United States and Brazil; it contributes no evidence on earlier centuries. The author says "No field site, of course, is representative" (p. 30).
- It also reports gains: reading a street sign in English helped one interviewee orient himself (p. 27).
- Bearing: supports 3.2 in the present tense (a citizenship literacy test as a gate) and 2.1's guardrail (literacy also helps); narrows 1.1 (it declines the autonomous-skill reading that an "-acy" invites).
