## Summary

Read: ch. 1, selected pages of later chapters, and the conclusion to the chapter on children; not the whole book. Johnes argues that the Welsh Not, the token passed to children caught speaking Welsh, was real and was punished, but was a practice of individual teachers and not a state policy. He says its direct goal was to help children learn English and not to end Welsh in their adult lives. He holds that popular memory has turned a varied classroom practice into a story of state coercion. He reports that school inspectors and the 1847 commissioners largely accepted the use of Welsh as a scaffold for teaching English. He still concludes that children beaten and punished for speaking their mother tongue suffered an injustice. The book therefore both confirms the punishment and contests the claim that the state or the school system enforced a language rule.

## Key facts it relies on

- The Welsh Not was real and many children were physically and psychologically punished for speaking Welsh at school. [quote] p. 3: "The Welsh Not was real and many children were physically and psychologically punished at school for speaking Welsh."
- The practice was implemented by teachers on their own, without parental opposition. [quote] p. 3: "It will show that the Welsh Not was implemented by individual teachers, largely of their own accord and without opposition from parents."
- Its aim was learning English, not ending Welsh in adult life. [quote] p. 3: "its direct goal was not to stop children speaking Welsh in their future lives but to help them learn English."
- Popular memory treats it as official policy. [quote] p. 2: "It started to be assumed that it was some kind of official policy."
- The 1847 Blue Books commissioner Lingen found the English-only schoolbooks a barrier to Welsh-speaking children and still held the purpose to be superseding the language. [quote] p. 80: "impossible to exaggerate the difficulties"; same page: "if not lost, at least stowed away".
- Johnes criticises Phillipson and Welsh historians for treating the suppression of Welsh as official policy without documenting a policy. [paraphrase] p. 23.
- Lingen later headed the education department and was not sympathetic to Wales. [paraphrase] p. 201.
- Conclusion on the injustice. [quote] p. 270: "But none of this changes the bare fact that there were children beaten and punished for speaking their mother tongue."

## Critical notes

Contested by the Welsh-nationalist and linguistic-imperialism literature (Phillipson; W. G. Evans; H. G. Williams), whom Johnes names on p. 23. His view that the Welsh Not was teacher-led narrows any claim that a state rule enforced one language in Wales. Limits: I read about eight pages of 458 and did not test his logbook evidence. The author is a Welsh-history specialist writing against a popular narrative; the University of Wales Press edition is open access (front matter, per scout).
Bearing: narrows 3.7 (the British case supports a punishment and a language standard, but not a statutory enforcement rule); counter to any claim that the state compelled the language standard in Wales.
