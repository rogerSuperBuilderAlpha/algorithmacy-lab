## Summary

Read in full: the body (pp. 1-14) and the footnotes (pp. 20-22); the reference list was not read. The authors define online manipulation as the use of information technology to covertly influence another person's decision-making by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities, and say its deeper harm is to autonomy, from which the harm to economic interests derives (pp. 1, 10). They treat manipulation as hidden influence and set it against persuasion and coercion, which are forthright; deception is for them a special case of manipulation, and a nudge is manipulative or not according to whether it is hidden and whether it exploits a vulnerability or corrects one (pp. 4-6). They place "dark patterns" in the same family, as interface designs that nudge people to act against their own interests, while saying not all of them reach manipulation (pp. 7-8). Section 3 makes four suggestions, which the authors themselves call moderate: curtail digital surveillance, problematise personalisation, promote awareness and understanding, and attend to context (pp. 12-13). Under the third they say that merely notifying a person of manipulation is not enough, citing the "transparency paradox", and that people need tools to understand and manage a digital environment designed to influence them (p. 13). The Conclusion asks for both things at once: depriving manipulation of personal data and empowering its targets with awareness, understanding and savvy (pp. 13-14). The cases are behavioural advertising, political micro-targeting, interface dark patterns and gig-work apps; the text names no conversational or agentic AI, and mentions machine learning and artificial intelligence once, as an analogy (p. 13).

## Key facts it relies on

- Definition: "online manipulation is the use of information technology to covertly influence another person's decision-making, by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities". [quote] (p. 1, Abstract)
- "manipulation is hidden influence", unlike persuasion and coercion, which are forthright. [paraphrase] (p. 4)
- The fuller statement adds intent: "manipulating someone means intentionally and covertly influencing their decision-making, by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities". The authors say their account "operationalises the notion of trickery more concretely, thus offering more specific tools for diagnosing cases of manipulation". [quote] (p. 4)
- "deception is a special case of manipulation"; of nudges, "some are manipulative and some are not—the distinction hinging on whether or not the nudge is hidden, and whether it exploits vulnerabilities or attempts to rectify them". [quote] (pp. 4, 6)
- Scope of "online": the term "designates manipulation facilitated by information technology", and "we should expect to encounter online manipulation beyond our computer screens". [quote] (p. 6)
- Dark patterns are described as "design strategies that exploit users' decision-making vulnerabilities to nudge them into acting against their interests (or, at least, acting in the interests of the website or app)"; the authors add that not all rise to manipulation, since some overtly inconvenience users. [quote] (p. 7, with continuation on p. 8)
- Of dark patterns and the gig-work prompts: "In their current form, not all such practices necessarily manipulate—people are savvy, and many likely understand what they are facing." [quote] (p. 8)
- "law and policy around information and internet practices (at least in the US) assume that individuals are for the most part capable of safeguarding their interests" (citing Solove, 2013). [quote] (p. 10)
- "the fundamental harm of manipulation is to the process of decision-making, not its outcome"; yet "we have not demonstrated that manipulation is necessarily wrong in every case—only that it always carries a harm". [quote] (p. 11)
- On their own proposals: readers "may be struck by how moderate our responses are". [quote] (p. 12)
- First suggestion: "To curb manipulation and strengthen autonomy online, efforts to curtail digital surveillance ought to be redoubled." The passage points to new privacy legislation in US states and to the GDPR's limits on data collection. [quote] (p. 12, "Curtail digital surveillance")
- "merely notifying someone that they are the target of manipulation is not enough to neutralise its effects". [quote] (p. 13, "Promote awareness and understanding")
- "Doing so would require understanding not only that one is the target of manipulation, but also who the manipulator is, what strategies they are deploying, and why." [quote] (p. 13)
- Because of the "transparency paradox" (concise notices deprive, thorough ones overwhelm), "there is little reason to believe standard forms of notice alone can equip users to face the challenges of online manipulation". [quote] (p. 13)
- "Though privacy notices are, in themselves, insufficient for shielding individuals from the effects of online manipulation, that does not mean that they are entirely without value. They might support individual autonomy, even if they can't guarantee it". [quote] (n. 27, p. 22)
- The authors compare calls to make delegated machine decision-making "more explainable, transparent, and accountable" with the need to "give people tools to understand and manage a digital environment designed to shape and influence them". [quote] (p. 13)
- Conclusion: "Combating online manipulation requires both depriving it of personal data—the oxygen enabling it—and empowering its targets with awareness, understanding, and savvy about the forces attempting to influence them." [quote] (pp. 13-14)

## Critical notes

- The paper makes four suggestions, not one (pp. 12-13). Two fall on data collectors and lawmakers: curtailing digital surveillance through privacy law, and refusing personalisation as a ground for more data collection. The third falls on the user's side: awareness, understanding and "tools". The fourth asks that responses be sensitive to the sphere of life. None is a record, a brake, sight of an operating limit, standing to contest or cover for loss. A reader holding the responsibilization view could apply the objection to the third suggestion and to the Conclusion's "savvy"; the text does not discuss it (file searched for "responsib": no hit).
- The text names no chatbot, conversational system or software agent (file searched for "chatbot", "conversational", "agentic", "agent", "assistant": no hit). Machine learning and artificial intelligence appear once, as an analogy for the demand to explain delegated decisions (p. 13, n. 29). The definition covers any "information technology" and reads "online" broadly (p. 6); applying it to conversational or agentic AI is the reader's extension, not a statement in the text. Susser (2019b), "Invisible Influence: Artificial Intelligence and the Ethics of Adaptive Choice Architectures", is cited for adaptive choice architectures (p. 7, n. 20) and was not opened in this run.
- The authors say their account "operationalises" trickery (p. 4), but the test stays conceptual: influence a person is not aware of and could not easily become aware of (p. 4). They allow that manipulation may not be "a binary phenomenon" (p. 10). The dark-patterns discussion is a short aside (pp. 7-8) and cites Brignull and others rather than adding evidence. A longer version of the account, Susser, Roessler and Nissenbaum (2018, SSRN), is cited at n. 7 and was not opened in this run.
- Against a flat reading that disclosure fails: the authors hold that notices are insufficient, not worthless (n. 27, p. 22), and that any response "must involve a drive toward increased awareness" because the problem is hiddenness (p. 13). They also concede that many people already understand the practices they face (p. 8).
- Bearing: supports (notice alone insufficient, p. 13) and contests in part: one of its two answers is to equip the user (pp. 13-14); the other is to cut data collection by law (p. 12).
