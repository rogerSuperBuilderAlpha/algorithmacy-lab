## Summary

Read: pp. 1, 4, 6-8 and 13; other pages searched by keyword. The authors define online manipulation as the use of information technology to covertly influence another person's decision-making by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities, and say its deeper harm is to autonomy. They separate manipulation from persuasion, coercion, deception and nudging, treating it as hidden influence. They place "dark patterns" in the same family, as interface designs that nudge people to act against their own interests, while saying not all of them reach manipulation. On remedies they say that merely notifying a person of manipulation is not enough, citing the "transparency paradox", and that people need tools to understand and manage a digital environment designed to influence them. The paper is about online manipulation in advertising and interfaces; it does not discuss conversational or agentic AI.

## Key facts it relies on

- Definition: "online manipulation is the use of information technology to covertly influence another person's decision-making, by targeting and exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities". [quote] (p. 1, Abstract)
- "manipulation is hidden influence", unlike persuasion and coercion, which are forthright. [paraphrase] (p. 4)
- Dark patterns are described as "design strategies that exploit users' decision-making vulnerabilities to nudge them into acting against their interests (or, at least, acting in the interests of the website or app)"; the authors add that not all rise to manipulation, since some overtly inconvenience users. [quote] (p. 7, with continuation on p. 8)
- "merely notifying someone that they are the target of manipulation is not enough to neutralise its effects". [quote] (p. 13, "Promote awareness and understanding")
- Because of the "transparency paradox" (concise notices deprive, thorough ones overwhelm), "there is little reason to believe standard forms of notice alone can equip users to face the challenges of online manipulation". [quote] (p. 13)
- The authors compare calls to make delegated machine decision-making "more explainable, transparent, and accountable" with the need to "give people tools to understand and manage a digital environment designed to shape and influence them". [quote] (p. 13)

## Critical notes

- The paper's own remedy is awareness and understanding on the user's side, with context-sensitive regulation; it names no institutional duty such as a record or brake. A reader holding the responsibilization view could apply the objection to that passage; the paper does not discuss it.
- The paper predates conversational and agentic AI; its application to them is by extension, not by any statement in the text.
- Definition is conceptual, not an operational test; the dark-patterns discussion is a short aside (pp. 7-8) and cites Brignull and others rather than adding evidence.
- Bearing: supports (disclosure alone insufficient, p. 13) and contests (its own answer is to equip the user). Person acted on gets: record no; brake no; envelope no; standing no; loss cover no. STRUCTURE 4.2.
- Corrected by V-B-2: the p. 7 dark-patterns quotation was closed at "against their interests" and dropped the authors' own hedge "(or, at least, acting in the interests of the website or app)"; restored.
