## Summary

Abstract only; paraphrase. Lyons asks who used scribes in modern Europe, for what purposes, who the scribes were, and how they stood to the people who employed them. He draws on existing work on nineteenth- and early twentieth-century delegated writing and adds original research on the writing of ordinary people in the First World War and the age of mass migration. He sorts scribes into three types: the local notable, the family member or close colleague, and the professional writer. He stresses the scribe's power: delegated writing was a collaboration in which the scribe used stock phrases, censored and advised, and calling on a scribe meant giving up part of one's authorial autonomy. He closes by noting the scribe's return since the late twentieth century.
