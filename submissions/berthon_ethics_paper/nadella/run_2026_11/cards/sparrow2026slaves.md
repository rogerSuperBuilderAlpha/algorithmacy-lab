## Summary

Read: pp. 1-7 and 12 of 14, with part of p. 8 and section 9 (pp. 10-12) at the Fable read; sections 7 and 8 (pp. 8-10) are unread. Sparrow applies Pettit's republican account of liberty to "algocracy", government that relies on AI to make or inform decisions. He gives four reasons for concern: AI decisions are hard for citizens to contest because their reasons are inscrutable; that inability makes the decisions arbitrary in the republican sense; overreliance erodes civic virtue; and AI may free governments from fear of revolution. The domain is the citizen and the state; private dealings appear only as passing examples, among them algorithms "deciding" who is offered a housing loan or what a plane ticket costs, and shoppers who use a VPN so that their search history does not raise the price, which he reads as a sign of domination (p. 6). It concedes that an AI that tracks citizens' shared interests, with its use open to contestation, would be compatible with and even promote liberty, so it does not reject automation outright. It places the burden of contestation "in most cases" on the decision to adopt AI, not on each individual output (p. 7); where much is at stake it adds human review of the output, which those affected can demand (pp. 8, 11). It notes that governments contracting with private firms meet "commercial in confidence" barriers to contesting the system.

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract: decisions made using AI "will often be impossible for citizens to contest because the reasons for the decisions will be inscrutable". [quote] p. 1
- The remedy named in the abstract is that "decisions made by AI can be publicly contested". [quote] p. 1
- Pettit's test as Sparrow reads it: it is the capacity of the dominator to act without regard to the interests of the person affected, not any particular act contrary to her interests, that makes her unfree. [paraphrase] p. 4
- Where citizens endorse an AI application as serving their interests, "algocracy will be compatible with liberty — indeed, it will serve and promote it." [quote] p. 6
- "the only way that citizens can be confident that this is not true in any particular case is for the reasons for the decision to be publicly available and open to contestation." [quote] p. 6
- "Anyone who has every used a VPN while shopping for a plane ticket to prevent the prices of desirable tickets being put up because of one’s search history, or to express political opinion online with reduced fear of surveillance, demonstrates their awareness of this truth." The truth is that we already live at the sufferance of algorithms; changing one's conduct to avoid interference is, after Pettit, an indicator of domination. [quote] p. 6 ("every" is the source's)
- "Because machines do not intend anything, they do not interfere in anyone’s life, exercise power, or dominate anyone. Rather, the relevant intentions are those of the people who have delegated tasks to AI systems and (perhaps) those who have designed these systems." [quote] p. 7
- "I suspect that politicians and bureaucrats will often be tempted to hide behind their machines." [quote] p. 7
- "in most cases" contestation falls on the argument that AI decides better than humans, rather than on the individual decisions of the AI. [paraphrase] p. 7
- Where governments contract with private corporations, the corporations are likely to treat source code, training data and capability information as commercial in confidence, which blocks contestation. [paraphrase] p. 7
- Where a lot is at stake for those affected, "a strong argument can made that the decisions of AI should be subject to review by human beings", with GDPR Article 22 among the citations; his example is an AI refusing a housing loan or parole. [quote] p. 8 ("can made" is the source's)
- Once the stakes pass a threshold set by a democratic process, "AI should not be used unless it is possible for those who might be affected by decisions made on the basis of the outputs of AI to demand and secure a human review of the outputs of the AI." [quote] p. 11
- Government might require that the design specifications and performance data of each AI be made public, and an "AI ombudsman" might review the case for using AI in particular roles. [paraphrase] p. 11

## Critical notes

- Scope: citizens and government. Sparrow offers no test for a one-off dealing with a firm. His plane-ticket and housing-loan examples (pp. 6, 8) show that he counts private algorithmic pricing and lending under the same worry, but he does not argue the private case; extension to a retail customer is the reader's inference from those sentences.
- Who dominates: not the machine but those who delegate tasks to AI and "(perhaps)" those who design it (p. 7); the Conclusion speaks of "those who design, and/or authorise the use of, the algorithms" (p. 12).
- Contestation is placed first at the procedural level, the decision to use AI (pp. 7, 10). For decisions with a lot at stake he adds an individual route: human review that those affected can "demand and secure" (pp. 8, 11).
- Concedes that AI can promote liberty if it tracks shared interests and its use is contestable (Q2: automation lessens domination, conditionally).
- Footnote 24 (p. 12) records that surveillance can reduce domination by checking private actors; not pursued.
- Phase A/B bearing. Person acted on gets: record partial (design specifications and performance data made public, p. 11; to the public, not to the individual); brake partial (human review on demand above a democratically set threshold, p. 11); envelope no; standing partial (public contestability as the condition of non-arbitrariness, p. 6; an "AI ombudsman", p. 11); loss cover no. Not read: the rest of p. 8, pp. 9-10 (sections 7 and 8), and the references (pp. 13-14).
