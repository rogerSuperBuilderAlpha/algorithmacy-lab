## Summary

Abstract only; paraphrase. Heckelman argues that the secret ballot lowered turnout because it ended the market for votes; the abstract does not name the country, and its measure, gubernatorial turnout, points to the US states. Once a party could no longer check how a person voted, it stopped paying for votes, and many voters who had been paid to vote stayed home. He tests this with a series of empirical tests that end in a multivariate regression the abstract calls legislative, without saying what data it uses. With other electoral laws controlled, the abstract puts the secret ballot's effect at 7 percentage points lower turnout in gubernatorial elections. The abstract does not mention literacy or race.

## Key facts it relies on

- The mechanism offered is the end of vote-buying, since parties could no longer verify votes (abstract) [paraphrase].
- The estimate: with other electoral laws controlled, the secret ballot accounts for a drop of 7 percentage points in gubernatorial turnout (abstract) [paraphrase].
- The method is a series of empirical tests ending in a multivariate legislative regression (abstract) [paraphrase].

## Critical notes

- Abstract only; the data, the states covered and any treatment of illiteracy are in the unread article.
- Contests the reading in Kousser (card kousser1974shaping, ch. 2) only by offering a different cause for lower turnout after the secret ballot: that ballot's drop in turnout was in part the end of paid voting. Kousser measures declines of 8 to 28 percentage points for white men and 15 to 45 for Black men in three Southern states, which is larger than the seven points here and concerns a different set of elections. Whether the two accounts conflict is not settled by an abstract.
- Bearing: narrows (STRUCTURE 3.2, candidate 1 of PLAN 4.5): the fall in turnout after the secret ballot has an explanation that does not run through literacy. This rests on the abstract alone.
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-1-1): the Summary placed the study "in the United States", which the abstract does not say (the verifier's note); it called the final test a regression "on legislative data", where the abstract speaks only of a legislative regression; and it wrote "about seven" for the abstract's 7. All three now follow the abstract. The Kousser figures in the note above were checked against Kousser p. 56 and Table 2.2 (p. 55) and stand.
