## Summary

Read: pp. 201-202 and 215-219 (journal pagination), with pp. 220-224 at the Fable read; the rest searched by keyword. Stamboliev argues that explainable AI (XAI) turns transparency into an apolitical remedy for opacity, and that this keeps attention on the algorithm and away from who holds power over AI's explanations, uses and critique. Using the Dutch childcare-benefit scandal, she says the people harmed had little to use against the system. She proposes a "post-critical" AI literacy that learns from the media-literacy record and asks for citizen participation and organised opposition, while not dismissing XAI. She also warns that literacy can be read as a depoliticised "good thing" and that skills training can be offered as the practical answer to a structural problem. Her proposal still asks citizens to act: to gain access and "a basic understanding" of an AI's design, purpose and effects, a seat at the table, and alliances for antagonistic debate (pp. 219-221). She says more than once that she has no worked-out solution (pp. 221, 222) and that "no literacy can fully empower" (p. 223).

## Key facts it relies on

- Those affected by AI, as in the Dutch case, "have little tools to deal with the opacity of AI as a system", while those who focus on data opacity are "shaping the literacy discussion". [quote] (p. 201, Abstract)
- Transparency in XAI is presented as non-ideological, which the author calls "an ideological move with consequences". [paraphrase] (p. 201, Abstract)
- On the Dutch scandal: "those who are harmed are powerless to stop, alter, or intervene in this code". [quote] (p. 216)
- Of the individuals affected by the Dutch algorithm: "XAI would not have altered much", a point the author marks as speculation. [quote] (p. 216)
- Citing Eubanks, she writes that the problem with automating discrimination is not only that it is opaque, but also that "there is no mechanism to intervene and stop it". [quote] (p. 216)
- Transparency in XAI is used to erase power relations and feed "an illusion that we democratise AI by explaining it". [quote] (p. 217)
- "Teaching skills seems to be the pragmatic solution to a complex problem", and it is advertised as such. [quote] (p. 218)
- "media literacy may overpromise in producing critical media citizens". [quote] (p. 218)
- Following Buckingham, she warns that literacy is constructed as a common-sense "good thing" and that the authority of those teaching it, and to whom, goes unquestioned. [paraphrase] (p. 218)
- First steps for a post-critical literacy: "demand that citizens get a seat at the table" and organise inclusive hubs for antagonistic debate. [quote] (p. 219)
- "First, people should be able to access and evaluate AI decision-making and critique AI as a power structure by having a basic understanding of its design, purpose and effects. This applies to public service systems in particular as it might be harder to demand for corporate AI." [quote] (p. 219)
- On timing: "It would be much more difficult for people to complain to or get involved in a dynamic techno-bureaucratic machine once they have been impacted by an algorithmic system"; debate in the test phase would give "more power to halt or intervene". [quote] (p. 222)
- A post-critical AI literacy may "dispel the myth that literacy is neutral, even if it empowers. But no literacy can fully empower nor reveal any algorithmic truths". [quote] (p. 223)

## Critical notes

- The proposal remains one for citizens to learn and organise; it gives no list of what the deploying institution must supply (a record, a brake). It also says it does not dismiss XAI or transparency as an intention (p. 219).
- The statement that XAI "would not have altered much" is the author's own speculation, flagged as such; she gives no test. The Dutch case is a public-sector system; she notes that demands on corporate AI would be harder (p. 219).
- The disk copy's download origin is not recorded; the page-1 header and the Crossref record agree on title, author and pagination.
- Already on the table for the paper: a critique of AI literacy as skills training that overpromises and leaves the authority of the teacher unquestioned (pp. 218-219). The paper's claim about the history of literacy as a lesson for this remedy is not made here; the media-literacy comparison is to the media-education literature (Livingstone 2004; Buckingham 2018, 2019; De Abreu 2022; p. 218), not to the literacy myth; Graff is not cited (file searched).
