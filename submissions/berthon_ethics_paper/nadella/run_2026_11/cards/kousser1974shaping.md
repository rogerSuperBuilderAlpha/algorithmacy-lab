## Summary

Read: ch. 2, pp. 45-62, in the author-deposited Caltech copy (second printing, 1975; the text layer is OCR, and the printed page numbers below are the book's own). Kousser argues that Southern election law of 1880-1910 reduced the electorate on purpose, and that the devices worked together. In ch. 2 he sorts them into fraud, registration, ballot design and literacy qualifications. He treats the eight-box law and the secret ballot as "de facto literacy tests", because a voter who could not read the boxes or the printed ticket lost the vote with no examiner involved. He estimates the secret ballot's effect by comparing turnout before and after its passage in Alabama, Arkansas and Louisiana. For the formal literacy qualification he describes the "understanding clause" and the grandfather clause, which let registrars pass or fail a man at will, and he reads registration returns after 1890. He ties the secret ballot to Northern reformers who also wanted to remove "ignorant" voters. The ch. 2 evidence runs from speeches in the constitutional conventions to election returns, and he flags where it is only circumstantial.

## Key facts it relies on

- South Carolina (1882) and Florida (1889) adopted eight-box laws; North Carolina had a multi-box law for the 1900 referendum (p. 50) [paraphrase].
- Under the eight-box laws, ballots deposited in the wrong box "were not counted", and the boxes "were constantly shifted to prevent a literate voter from arranging the tickets of an uneducated friend" (p. 50) [quote for the first phrase; paraphrase for the rest of the rule].
- Illiterates "could ask the election judges to read the names on the boxes", but all election officials were appointed by the Democratic governor, so "it is doubtful that Republicans got much assistance" (p. 50) [paraphrase].
- 55 percent of adult Black men in South Carolina, 53 percent in North Carolina and 39 percent in Florida were classed illiterate in 1900, and Kousser calls the effect of the boxes "these de facto literacy tests" (p. 50) [quote: "de facto literacy tests"].
- Until 1888 parties printed the ballots, and "the party ballot insured illiterates the right to vote"; the reformers who introduced the secret ballot in eight Southern and 30 non-Southern states between 1888 and 1900 "were more concerned with eliminating fraud than safeguarding the rights of illiterates" (p. 51) [paraphrase].
- The printed ticket "demanded not merely literacy, but fluency in the English language" (p. 52) [quote]. Florida dropped party designations, and a Virginia district in 1894 printed its ballot in German Fraktur script (p. 52) [paraphrase].
- Northern evidence: Kousser calls the evidence of Northern intent "only circumstantial and suggestive", but quotes the editor George Gunton that the Australian system was adopted "to eliminate the ignorant, illiterate voters" (pp. 52-53) [quote].
- Eight states outside the Confederacy barred election officials from assisting illiterates (pp. 52-53) [paraphrase].
- Of the Southern case, Kousser writes that "there is little question that the secret ballot was adopted in the South primarily to purge the electorate of illiterates"; the president of the Alabama Senate said that under the Alabama law "the ignorant are practically disfranchised" (p. 53) [quote].
- Tennessee Democrats "beat down GOP attempts" to let election officials aid the unlettered or print party initials after the names (p. 53) [paraphrase].
- Though officials could help illiterates in other states, Kousser judges that many unschooled people "probably hesitated to expose their ignorance or did not trust the partisan election officials" (p. 54) [quote; the word "probably" is his].
- Virginia's governor reported that defective or improperly marked ballots had been thrown out, "in many instances as many as one-third or one-half of the ballots deposited" (p. 54) [quote].
- By 1895 eight of the eleven ex-Confederate states had secret-ballot laws covering at least their large centres (p. 54) [paraphrase].
- Table 2.1: Black adult-male illiteracy in 1900 ran from 39.4 percent (Florida) to 61.3 percent (Louisiana); white from 8.2 percent (Mississippi) to 18.8 percent (North Carolina) (p. 55) [paraphrase of the table].
- Table 2.2 estimates turnout before and after the secret ballot: Alabama 1892 to 1896 governor, white 80 to 68 and Black 64 to 49; Arkansas 1890 to 1892, white 75 to 67 and Black 71 to 38; Louisiana April to November 1896, white 79 to 51 and Black 69 to 24 (p. 55) [paraphrase of the table].
- Footnote 23 warns that such before-and-after comparisons "also reflect the power of registration laws passed at the same time in Alabama and Arkansas" (p. 55, n. 23) [quote].
- "No other explanation accounts for such large declines in turnout" (p. 56) [quote]. The Populists and Republicans "repeatedly denounced the secret ballot laws as attempts to disfranchise and defraud the voters" (p. 56) [quote].
- Nine states outside the South made English literacy a voting qualification between 1889 and 1913 (p. 57) [paraphrase].
- Seven ex-Confederate states adopted literacy qualifications between 1890 and 1908; in Virginia the voter "also had to convince a registrar that he understood what he had read" (p. 58) [quote].
- The understanding clause let an illiterate register "if he could understand a section of the state constitution which was read to him and explain it to the registrar's satisfaction" (p. 58) [quote].
- Asked whether Christ could register under the good-character clause, an Alabama convention leader answered "That would depend entirely on which way he was going to vote" (p. 59) [quote].
- Carter Glass at the Virginia convention: "Discrimination! Why, that is precisely what we propose" (p. 59) [quote].
- Poor illiterate white men were "often too humiliated to register" under the escape clauses; a Mississippian found about a dozen white men in the state's largest county in 1902 "willing to expose their illiteracy publicly" (p. 59) [quote].
- Where registration figures exist, "In no case did black registration exceed 16 percent" after the constitutional changes; white registration reached only 53 percent in Louisiana in 1904 and 63 percent in Mississippi in 1908 (p. 62) [quote for the first clause; paraphrase for the rest].

## Critical notes

- Read ch. 2 only, plus the front matter; the other chapters (the Populist and one-party chapters, ch. 5 on the secret ballot in Tennessee and Arkansas) were not opened. The Holmes review (card holmes1975kousser) reports the book's 37 percent estimate of the laws' effect on turnout; that figure is not in ch. 2 and this card does not rely on it.
- Kousser flags the weak spots himself: Northern intent is "only circumstantial and suggestive" (p. 53); the secret-ballot comparison is confounded by registration laws in Alabama and Arkansas (p. 55, n. 23); and registration figures are "fragmentary" and may reflect apathy after party competition fell (p. 60).
- Who contests: Holmes (1975) doubts how far the laws, rather than earlier violence and election law, explain the fall in turnout. Heckelman (1995; card heckelman1995effect, abstract only) offers a different account for the secret ballot's turnout effect, the end of vote-buying. The Gingerich and Fujiwara cards in this facet test the ballot-design mechanism outside the US.
- The estimates in Table 2.2 are Kousser's own ecological estimates by race; the table gives no confidence intervals.
- Bearing: supports (STRUCTURE 3.2). The secret ballot and the eight-box laws worked as literacy tests with no examiner, and delegates at the conventions said that discrimination was the purpose. Contemporary objectors: Populists, Republicans and Black voters (p. 56); the Mississippi Farmers' Alliance memorial against education and property tests (p. 58, n. 29).
