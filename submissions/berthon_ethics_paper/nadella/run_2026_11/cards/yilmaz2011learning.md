## Summary

Abstract only; paraphrase. Yilmaz reconsiders Turkey's 1928 alphabet reform by looking at the social experience of changing script and not at the state alone. She sets aside the assumption of an obedient, indifferent public following the decrees of an authoritarian regime. Drawing on unpublished archival evidence and oral interviews, she describes the variety of experiences of learning to read and write the new letters. She notes that the Ottoman script persisted and that state authorities dealt with that persistence in several ways. She says the analysis has implications for state-society relations and the nature of the Kemalist state.
