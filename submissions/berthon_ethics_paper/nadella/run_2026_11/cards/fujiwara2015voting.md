## Summary

Read: the open online supplement only, pp. 1-4 (Appendices A and B); the article is behind a subscription login. The supplement describes the electronic voting machine that Brazil introduced and compares it with the paper ballot. Fujiwara points to three features that could cut the errors of poorly educated voters: candidate photographs, instant feedback when a wrong number is typed, and guidance through the five races on the ballot. He says that a completely illiterate voter would notice that no picture had appeared, and that the number of digits asked for signals which office is being voted. Appendix B splits the machine's effect on invalid votes into blank and null votes and compares races where paper ballots required writing a name or number with races where they required a tick. Its reading is that the machine raises valid voting by making ballot operation easier and cutting errors. The card does not carry the article's headline results (the effect on valid votes and on infant health), which sit in the unread article.

## Key facts it relies on

- Three features of the electronic technology can reduce invalid votes, "especially from less educated voters": photographs, instant feedback and guidance through the many votes (Appendix A, p. 1) [quote for the phrase; paraphrase for the list].
- "even a completely illiterate voter that could not read the “wrong number” message on the screen would notice that a picture has not appeared on the screen" (Appendix A, p. 1) [quote].
- "a piece of paper cannot provide this type of feedback" (Appendix A, p. 1) [quote].
- Under paper ballots "a voter may misvote by, for example, writing the number of a state legislator in the federal legislator box" (Appendix A, p. 1) [quote].
- The machine asks for a 5-digit number for a state legislator, 4 digits for federal legislators and 2 for other offices, "so that even a completely illiterate voter can take this as a cue" (Appendix A, p. 1) [quote].
- On the 1998 paper ballot, "13.8% of voters left their paper ballots blank and 9.3% cast a null vote" (Appendix B, p. 3) [quote]. As printed, this conflicts with the clause it sits in, which gives null votes the "higher prevalence", and with Table A-I (p. 4), whose pre-treatment means are 0.134 for null votes and 0.093 for blank votes as shares of turnout [paraphrase of the table]. The two labels look transposed in the sentence.
- Electronic voting "reduces null voting by an amount equivalent to 8 p.p. of total turnout" and blank votes "by about 4 p.p." (Appendix B, p. 3) [quote for the first phrase; paraphrase for the second].
- The effect is larger in the races where a paper ballot requires writing a name or number than in those where it requires a tick; Fujiwara reads this as support for the view that the technology "facilitates the task of voting" (Appendix B, p. 3) [quote for the phrase].
- To explain all the additional valid votes by fraud, he reasons, paper-ballot fraud would have to touch "more than 10% of all ballots cast (and up to 15%–20% in high-illiteracy municipalities)" (Appendix A, p. 2) [quote; the figure is a calculation about what fraud would need, not the headline estimate].

## Critical notes

- The supplement is an appendix to an article this card does not cover. The identification (a regression discontinuity at a municipal electorate cutoff) and the headline estimates are in the article; the appendix says Section 2 of the article describes the procedure (p. 3).
- The supplement takes the view that fraud does not explain the change, and gives reasons (Appendix A, p. 2). Whether the machine acted through fewer ballot errors or through other channels is for the article.
- The voting technology here (a machine with photographs and a numeric keypad) differs in kind from a printed secret ballot. The card documents design that helps people who cannot read well; it does not document a prior gate that the machine removed beyond the paper ballot's blank and null rates.
- Contested by: no source in this facet disputes the supplement.
- Bearing: counter (a design change with a measured fall in invalid votes, which Fujiwara reads as easing the task of voting for the less educated, STRUCTURE 3.2) with the measurement incomplete: only the blank and null effects and the by-office comparison are read here, and the supplement does not break the effect down by voters' literacy.

(2) The Bearing line said the change "enfranchised voters who could not read well"; the supplement measures invalid votes for all voters and offers the less-educated reading as interpretation (pp. 1, 3), so the line is narrowed.
