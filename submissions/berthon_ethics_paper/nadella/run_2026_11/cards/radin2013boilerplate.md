## Summary

Read: ch. 1, pp. 4-18 only, from the publisher's open excerpt (Internet Archive capture of the Princeton University Press page's chapter file, which the live page withheld behind a bot challenge). Radin argues that firms use standard-form paperwork to move recipients into a private legal order in which rights to a remedy, a jury and a public record vanish. She asks why people do not read such terms and lists seven answers. She holds that "agreement" has become a label for the firm's wish that the recipient be bound, and she names the problem of consent a "normative degradation" for the legal system. She names a second problem, "democratic degradation": mass-market forms delete rights that legislatures grant. The excerpt stops before the book's remedies and before its treatment of the case law on duty to read.

## Key facts it relies on

- Radin gives seven reasons recipients do not read boilerplate; the first is "We wouldn’t understand the terms if we did read them, so it isn’t worth our time." [quote] (p. 12)
- Reasons two, three and six of the seven are that the reader needs the product and has no alternative supplier, does not know the terms apply, and believes the company has power over her. [paraphrase] (p. 12)
- Radin states a test for agreement: "whatever you are agreeing to has to be presented to you as a matter for your decision before, not after, the agreement takes place" [quote] (p. 14)
- She says that declaring a person bound before she could know it "has an Alice-in-Wonderland quality to it". [quote] (p. 14)
- She says courts are likely to find a contract formed from a click on "I agree", and that firms hope courts will extend this to terms reached only through a link. [paraphrase] (p. 13)
- She calls the problem of consent a "normative degradation" for our legal system. [quote] (p. 15)
- She names a second problem, "democratic degradation", which she holds equally serious: "these systems of contracts can delete rights that are granted through democratic processes", and where courts uphold them they replace the law of the state with the "law" of the firm. [quote] (p. 16)
- Ch. 1's recap of the prologue cases (Tonya; Garrit's father) shows paperwork sending claimants to arbitration, which removes jury trial, a public record and class actions. [paraphrase] (pp. 4-5)

## Critical notes

- Only the opening chapter was read; her treatment of courts' reasoning on the duty to read, and of proposed cures, lies outside the excerpt.
- The excerpt's argument is American and about consumer and employment forms, not about people who cannot read; the link to literacy is the paper's, not hers.
- The defenders' case appears in the excerpt only as Radin's own summary of it (pp. 15-16: narrowing recipients' rights contains costs, lowers prices, and is what recipients would choose); she says the argument should not be ignored and defers her answer to chs. 2 and 3. No defender of standard forms is carded here; Rakoff 1983 is a target not opened in this run.
- Bearing: narrows, 3.4. It documents the modern presumption of assent without reading and its limits; it does not show a duty to read was ever applied to a signer who could not read. For 5 it supports the point that the form, not a person, now holds the weaker party's assent.
