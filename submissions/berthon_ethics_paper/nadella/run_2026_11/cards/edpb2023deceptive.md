## Summary

Read: pp. 1-5 and 8-14, and parts of pp. 50-53; other pages searched by keyword. The Board's guidelines tell social media providers how to recognise and avoid "deceptive design patterns" that breach the GDPR. It defines them as interfaces and user journeys that try to influence users into unintended, unwilling and potentially harmful decisions, against their interests and in favour of the platform's, about their personal data. It sorts them into six categories: overloading, skipping, stirring, obstructing, fickle and left in the dark (listed on pp. 3-4), and illustrates each with use cases across the life of an account. The fairness principle (art. 5(1)(a)) is the starting point for the assessment, with transparency, accountability and data protection by design (art. 25) also relevant. It puts the responsibility on the provider and the sanctioning power on data protection authorities, and it also says it aims to raise users' awareness and to educate users to recognise the patterns (p. 8). Its scope is social media platforms, though the Board says such patterns are not unique to them (p. 11) and that some observations may apply to voice-controlled interfaces (p. 9); a keyword search of the extracted text for "chatbot", "artificial intelligence", "conversational" and "assistant" found no hit, so it says nothing about conversational or agentic AI.

## Key facts it relies on

- Definition: "deceptive design patterns" are "interfaces and user journeys implemented on social media platforms that attempt to influence users into making unintended, unwilling and potentially harmful decisions". [quote] (p. 3, Executive summary)
- Such decisions are "often toward a decision that is against the users' best interests and in favour of the social media platforms interests". [quote] (p. 3)
- "Social media providers remain responsible and accountable for ensuring the GDPR compliance of their platforms." [quote] (p. 3)
- "Data protection authorities are responsible for sanctioning the use of deceptive design patterns if these breach GDPR requirements." [quote] (p. 3)
- The principle of fair processing "is a starting point for an assessment of existence of deceptive design patterns"; the Board calls fairness an "umbrella" principle under which all deceptive design patterns would fail. [paraphrase] (p. 11)
- Data protection by design and default (art. 25) applied before launch "would help social media providers avoid deceptive design patterns in the first place". [quote] (p. 4)
- The list of patterns, best practices and use cases "are not exhaustive". [quote] (p. 3)
- "These Guidelines also aim to educate users to recognise “deceptive design patterns” (as defined in the following), and how to face them to protect their privacy in a conscious way." [quote] (p. 8, section 1)
- "The document focuses on graphical user interfaces (e.g. used for computer and smartphone interfaces), but some of the observations made may also apply to voice-controlled interfaces (e.g. used for smart speakers) or gesture-based interfaces (e.g. used in virtual reality)." [quote] (p. 9, section 1)
- Vulnerable groups, the elderly among them, are said to be often less able to identify manipulative design practices and less aware that their digital behaviour is subject to influence. [paraphrase] (p. 10)
- "deceptive design patterns are not unique to social media platforms"; variations elsewhere "may still infringe upon the rights of data subjects or consumers", yet "these Guidelines focus solely on deceptive design patterns in social media platforms". [quote] (p. 11, section 1)
- On accountability: "The user interface and user journey can be used as a documentation tool to demonstrate that users, during their actions on the social media platform, have read and taken into account data protection information, have freely given their consent, have easily exercised their rights, etc." [quote] (p. 12, section 2.1)
- From the Board's Article 25 guidelines, as excerpted here: "Power balance should be a key objective of the controller-data subject relationship. Power imbalances should be avoided. When this is not possible, they should be recognised and accounted for with suitable countermeasures." [quote] (p. 13, section 2.3)
- The platform should explain why a request cannot be fulfilled and inform the user of the possibility to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority (Art. 12(4) GDPR); Example 46 treats a vague statement of that right as the pattern "Ambiguous wording or information". [paraphrase] (pp. 51-52, use case 4)

## Critical notes

- A regulator's guidance, not a statute; the Board's legal assessment binds no court. It rests on GDPR principles, so it covers personal-data decisions on social media only (p. 3), and it does not define a design that works against the user in general.
- It has no sections on conversational or agentic AI (keyword search, see Summary). The only openings beyond graphical social media interfaces are the two sentences on voice-controlled and gesture-based interfaces (p. 9) and on patterns outside social media (p. 11); extension to AI agents would be an inference from those. Another source in this facet (Tang et al. 2025, tang2025darkgui) addresses GUI agents.
- The duty lies on the provider (controller) and the enforcement on authorities (p. 3). The Guidelines do not leave the user out: they call themselves suitable for raising users' awareness and aim to "educate users" (p. 8). Section 2.1 treats the interface as the provider's "documentation tool" for proving compliance (p. 12); the record it describes is the provider's.
- Version 2.0 is the post-consultation text; version 1.0 was not compared.
- Bearing: supports. STRUCTURE 4.2 (law and regulators on design against the user).
