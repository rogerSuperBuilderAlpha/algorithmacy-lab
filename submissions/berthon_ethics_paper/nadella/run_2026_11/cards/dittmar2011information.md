## Summary

Read: pp. 1133, 1142 and 1155 only, located by text search; the rest was not read. The copy is the journal's own text carrying an Oxford University Press download watermark, re-hosted on a university course page, so its origin is doubtful under PLAN 8.2: it served to locate passages and the card carries no quotation. Dittmar argues that European cities where presses were set up before 1500 grew about 60 percent faster between 1500 and 1600 than comparable cities, using distance from Mainz as an instrument. For the monopoly question, the paper matters for its account of the first fifty years of print, when entry was unregulated and the barrier to entry was capital, not charter.

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract: cities with presses in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster over 1500-1600 than otherwise similar cities. [paraphrase] (p. 1133)
- Between 1450 and 1500 the real price of books fell by about two-thirds; between 1500 and 1800 further falls were modest, with technology largely unchanged. [paraphrase] (p. 1142)
- In the infant-industry period (1450-1500) the printing trade fell outside existing guild regulation, and Dittmar cites Fussel (2005) and others for the claim that governments, the Church and guilds imposed no restriction. [paraphrase] (p. 1155)
- The barrier to entry he names is financial: a full set of movable type was a large share of a printer's output. [paraphrase] (p. 1155)

## Critical notes

- The regulation-free claim is limited to 1450-1500; it says nothing about England's 1557-1695 regime, which began after the window.
- The paper is about city growth, not readers or licensing; the price figures are secondary citations to Clark and Zanden, not Dittmar's own.
- Not read beyond three pages; the identification strategy and robustness checks were not checked.
- Bearing: narrows 3.5. It shows price fell without charter control in print's first decades, so the charter was not the only device shaping cost; it does not address stamp duties, which came later.
