## Summary

Read: pp. 1133, 1142, 1153 and 1155 only, located by text search; the rest was not read. The copy is the journal's own text carrying an Oxford University Press download watermark, re-hosted on a university course page, so its origin is doubtful under PLAN 8.2: it served to locate passages and the card carries no quotation. Dittmar argues that European cities where presses were set up before 1500 grew about 60 percent faster between 1500 and 1600 than comparable cities, using distance from Mainz as an instrument. For the monopoly question, the paper matters for its account of the first fifty years of print, when entry was unregulated and the barriers to entry were semi-secret type-casting knowledge and capital, not charter.

## Key facts it relies on

- Abstract: cities with presses in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster over 1500-1600 than otherwise similar cities. [paraphrase] (p. 1133)
- Between 1450 and 1500 the real price of books fell by about two-thirds; between 1500 and 1800 further falls were modest, with technology largely unchanged. [paraphrase] (p. 1142)
- In the infant-industry period (1450-1500) the printing trade fell outside existing guild regulation, and Dittmar cites Füssel (2005) and others for the claim that governments, the Church and guilds imposed no restriction. [paraphrase] (p. 1155)
- Entry in 1450-1500 was limited by the technology being quasi-proprietary: the process for casting movable metal type was complex and semi-secret, the alloy a trade secret, and the knowledge stayed quasi-proprietary for almost a century; the first known printed manual on producing type dates from 1540. [paraphrase] (p. 1153)
- The barrier to entry he names on p. 1155 is financial: for a printer unable to make his own type, a complete set of equipment cost the equivalent of 4 to 10 years of a craftsman's wages (n. 40: 250-600 livres in the early 1500s, from bequest data). [paraphrase] (p. 1155)

## Critical notes

- The regulation-free claim is limited to 1450-1500; it says nothing about England's 1557-1695 regime, which began after the window.
- The paper is about city growth, not readers or licensing; the price figures are secondary citations to Clark and Zanden, not Dittmar's own.
- Not read beyond four pages; the identification strategy and robustness checks were not checked.
- Bearing: narrows 3.5. It shows price fell without charter control in print's first decades, so the charter was not the only device shaping cost; it does not address stamp duties, which came later.
- Corrected by V-A-2: entry-cost fact rewritten to the source (p. 1155 and n. 40): the cost was of a complete set of equipment, measured as 4 to 10 years of a craftsman's wages, not "a full set of movable type" as "a large share of a printer's output"; Füssel spelled as in the source.
- Corrected at the Fable read (sitting V-A-2-2): p. 1153, which the drafter had not opened, names a second limit on entry in 1450-1500 (quasi-proprietary, semi-secret type-casting knowledge), so the card no longer presents capital as the only barrier Dittmar names: one fact added from p. 1153, the p. 1155 fact scoped to its page, the Summary's last sentence and `pages_read` amended to match. The verifier's p. 1155 fix was confirmed against the source.
