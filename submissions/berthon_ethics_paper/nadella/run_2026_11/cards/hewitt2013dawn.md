## Summary

Known through Melissa Score's review in Reviews in History (Internet Archive capture; the live page returned a bot challenge); paraphrase. Hewitt's book covers the 1849-1869 campaign against the press taxes: the advertising duty, the newspaper stamp, the paper duty, and the securities and registration rules. Score reports that the taxes began under Queen Anne to raise money for war, and by the late eighteenth and early nineteenth century served political ends as well, as part of the suppression of revolutionary ideas in the cheap press. Hewitt, she says, corrects Collet's partisan account, which stressed a continuous radical struggle against "taxes on knowledge"; he shows the 1850s campaign was driven by publishers and fiscal politics, and that repeal came through Gladstone's budget manoeuvres. He also finds repeal changed the regional press far more than the London dailies.

## Key facts it relies on

- Press taxes were introduced under Queen Anne for war revenue and later used also to suppress dissemination of revolutionary ideas. [paraphrase] (Score, review para. 2)
- The 1836 cut of the newspaper stamp from 4d to 1d came with tighter penalties and registration rules, and wiped out the unstamped radical penny press. [paraphrase] (Score, para. 2)
- Hewitt shows the taxes also hindered the timely circulation of news and were a brake on publishers' profitability. [paraphrase] (Score, para. 5)
- Many publishers opposed the paper and advertising duties on commercial grounds and rejected the radical phrase "taxes on knowledge"; only the Association for the Promotion of the Repeal of the Taxes on Knowledge used it. [paraphrase] (Score, paras. 5-8)
- The stamp was applied unevenly, as in the 1851 Dickens case, and the securities for libel and blasphemy were required before printing began. [paraphrase] (Score, paras. 8, 14)
- Hewitt finds the 1855 stamp repeal intensified competition and cheap provincial titles, with little change to London dailies. [paraphrase] (Score, paras. 11-13)

## Critical notes

- Secondary only: no quotation from Hewitt, and the review's page-less paragraph numbering is the card's own count.
- Score faults the book for being primarily a political history; she wishes for the author's view on the symbolic weight of "taxes on knowledge" for readers excluded from the franchise.
- Hewitt contests Collet's moral framing; the price-as-gate reading (PLAN 4.5 candidate 3) is therefore disputed even within the historiography, with fiscal motive prominent.
- Bearing: narrows 3.5. Candidate 3 (price of the medium as a separate gate) is documented by this source as real but mixed in purpose (revenue, political control, commercial interest); it is not a clean case of a charter-like wrong.
