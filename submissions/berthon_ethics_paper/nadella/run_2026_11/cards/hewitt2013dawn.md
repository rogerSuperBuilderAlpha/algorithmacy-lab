## Summary

Known through Melissa Score's review in Reviews in History (Internet Archive capture; the live page returned a bot challenge); paraphrase. Hewitt's book covers the 1849-1869 campaign against the press taxes: the advertising duty, the newspaper stamp, the paper duty, and the securities and registration rules. Score reports that the taxes began under Queen Anne to raise money for war, and by the late eighteenth and early nineteenth century served political ends as well, as part of the suppression of revolutionary ideas in the cheap press. Hewitt, she says, corrects Collet's partisan account, which stressed continuities in the struggle against the press taxes; he shows that the 1850s campaign set trade interests (publishers, printers, paper makers), who opposed the taxes on commercial grounds, beside the ideologically opposed Association for the Promotion of the Repeal of the Taxes on Knowledge (APRTOK), that removal was bound up with Whig and Liberal fiscal politics, and that repeal came through Gladstone's parliamentary and budget manoeuvres. He also finds repeal changed the regional press far more than the London dailies.

## Key facts it relies on

- Press taxes were introduced under Queen Anne for war revenue and later used also to suppress dissemination of revolutionary ideas. [paraphrase] (Score, review para. 2)
- The 1836 cut of the newspaper stamp from 4d to 1d came with tighter penalties and registration rules, and wiped out the unstamped radical penny press. [paraphrase] (Score, para. 2)
- Hewitt shows the taxes also hindered the timely circulation of news and were a brake on publishers' profitability. [paraphrase] (Score, para. 5)
- Special-interest groups representing publishers, printers and paper makers, among others, opposed the commercial limits the taxes set and rejected the radical terminology of "taxes on knowledge" (para. 3). Antipathy to the advertising duty was widespread and publishers of titles for lower-income readers opposed the paper duties, while support within the press for removing the newspaper stamp was small (para. 6). APRTOK differed from these groups in opposing the duties on ideological grounds, and its name invoked the phrase (para. 7). [paraphrase] (Score, paras. 3, 6-7)
- The stamp was applied unevenly, as in the 1851 Dickens case, and the securities for libel and blasphemy were required before printing began. [paraphrase] (Score, paras. 7, 11)
- Hewitt finds the 1855 stamp repeal intensified competition and cheap provincial titles, with little change to London dailies. [paraphrase] (Score, para. 10)

## Critical notes

- Secondary only: no quotation from Hewitt, and the review's page-less paragraph numbering is the card's own count.
- Score describes the book as primarily a political history of the campaign and does not call that a fault (para. 12). Her one stated criticism is that Hewitt perhaps underplays the Daily Telegraph's relaunch at 1d in explaining the Morning Star's difficulties (para. 10). She would like the author's view on the symbolic capital of the newspaper stamp campaign, given radicals' claims about timely access to news for readers largely excluded from the franchise, and asks how far the campaign was gendered (paras. 13-14).
- Hewitt contests Collet's moral framing; the price-as-gate reading (PLAN 4.5 candidate 3) is therefore disputed even within the historiography, with fiscal motive prominent.
