## Summary

Read in full, in the Caselaw Access Project's OCR text of the printed reporter, with the reporter's star pages
(*873-*881) as page markers; the OCR has scattered character errors, and quotations below were taken only from
clean stretches. Ten Black citizens of Mobile County sued the county Board of Registrars over the 1946 "Boswell
Amendment" to section 181 of the Alabama Constitution, which dropped the property alternative and added to the
read-and-write test a requirement to "understand and explain" any article of the U.S. Constitution. The three-judge
court held the amendment unconstitutional. Under the Fourteenth Amendment, the words gave registrars arbitrary power
with no reasonable standard; under the Fifteenth, its history and administration showed a purpose to exclude by
race. The court reaffirmed that a state may prescribe a literacy test. It also said a uniform, standardized test
would let a rejected applicant make a record for judicial review. It held the plaintiffs entitled to injunctive
relief.

## Key facts it relies on

- Rule and administrator: registration required the applicant to "understand and explain" any article of the Federal Constitution to the "reasonable satisfaction" of a majority of one of 67 county boards of registrars, whose members need not be lawyers. pp. 874, 877; n. 2, n. 3 [paraphrase]
- The court accepted literacy qualifications as such: "The States have a right to prescribe a literacy test for electors." p. 876 (the file has an OCR artefact "■" before "have") [quote]
- The original section 181 required ability to "read and write any article of the constitution of the United States in the English language," or a $300 property alternative. p. 877 [quote]
- On the missing standard and the record: "Under such a test with proper questions or guides a record could be made which would give a rejected applicant a definite basis upon which he could seek and obtain a proper judicial review of the board’s action" (the sentence runs on: ", and the reviewing court would have something definite to act upon"). p. 877 [quote]
- "To state it plainly, the sole test is: Has the applicant by oral examination or otherwise understood and explained the Constitution to the satisfaction of the particular board ?" p. 878 [quote]
- Administration by race: the board required Black applicants to explain constitutional provisions and "did not generally require white applicants to do so"; "more than 2800 white persons" and "approximately 104 Negroes" were registered during the board's tenure. p. 876 [quote]
- Before the suit the board "did not keep records of rejected applicants, whether white or Negro." p. 876 [quote]
- Contemporaries named the wrong in 1946: an opponent in the State Bar's journal wrote the amendment was expected to "give the Registrars arbitrary power to exclude Negroes from voting"; a Talladega editorial called it a measure "to enable registrars legally to hold down the number of Negro registrants." pp. 879-880 [quote]
- Holding: the amendment, "both in its object and the manner of its administration, is unconstitutional, because it violates the Fifteenth Amendment"; plaintiffs are entitled to injunctive relief. pp. 880-881 [quote]

## Critical notes

- The text is OCR of a scanned reporter; any quotation should be rechecked against a page image (Westlaw or the
  printed F. Supp. volume in a law library) before print. The rendering "Ainendment", "tó" and stray symbols show
  the noise level.
- Limits: a district-court opinion, affirmed by the Supreme Court in a one-line memorandum (336 U.S. 933), so the
  reasoning is the district court's, not the Supreme Court's. It upholds the state's power to set a literacy test
  (p. 876) and condemns the indefinite standard and racial purpose. A summary that says *Davis* struck down literacy
  tests overstates it.
- Form of correction (question 3): prohibition (injunction). The opinion also names the missing correction it would
  have accepted: a uniform test that produces a record for review (p. 877).
- Bearing: supports 3.2 (the gate as a discretion named as arbitrary by contemporaries in 1946); narrows 3.2
  (literacy as a qualification left standing); 4.4-record and 4.4-contest (no record of rejections, no basis for
  review); 7-D02 as a prohibition precedent.
