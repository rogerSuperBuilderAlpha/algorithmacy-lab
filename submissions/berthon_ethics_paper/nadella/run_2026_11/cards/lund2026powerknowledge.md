## Summary

Read in full, pp. 1-7 (references skimmed). The paper argues that existing AI-literacy frameworks, built on technical competency and "responsible use", train a "consumer" and leave the learner without an account of power. Using Foucault's power-knowledge and Freire's critique of "banking" education, it proposes a three-part framework: Contextual Use, Critical Interrogation, Participatory Governance. It illustrates the pattern with four public frameworks (U.S. Department of Labor 2026, Penn State 2026, UNESCO 2024, the EC/OECD AILit framework) and finds the same implicit learner in all four. It adds that unequal access to AI tools reproduces epistemic hierarchy. It calls its framework a theoretical frame, not a curriculum or policy prescription. It grants that the four frameworks differ in ambition and offers the comparison as an illustration, in one paragraph and with no stated method (p. 4).

## Key facts it relies on

- "What is notably absent from these existing AI literacy frameworks is a robust account of power (Stamboliev 2023)." [quote] (p. 1, Introduction)
- The abstract says existing AI-literacy frameworks "enforce a "consumer" orientation toward AI rather than fostering genuine epistemic agency". [quote] (p. 1, Abstract)
- The competency model "is a sophisticated version of the banking model of education: institutions deposit AI skills into users so that users can more efficiently participate in the knowledge economy". [quote] (p. 4, end of "AI Literacy as Empowerment, Not Competency")
- The omission of power "reflects the interests of the institutions that produce AI literacy curricula, which are typically the same institutions that create AI systems". [quote] (p. 4)
- Across the four frameworks examined, competencies are organised around operational proficiency, output evaluation and responsible use, with no substantive treatment of training-data politics, governance participation or the structural conditions of knowledge production. The authors grant differences: UNESCO frames students as "co-creators", the EC/OECD framework wants learners to be "not just consumers of AI, but its ethical stewards", and Penn State attends to equity, labor and democracy. [paraphrase] (p. 4)
- The agent/consumer distinction "is not primarily a distinction of technical sophistication". [quote] (p. 4)
- Unequal access to AI is called "a structural reproduction of an epistemic hierarchy". [quote] (p. 3, "Differential Access")
- Participatory Governance is described as "the most ambitious and the most neglected in existing AI literacy frameworks"; examples are public consultation on the AI Act, feedback to AI systems and community data-governance initiatives. [paraphrase] (p. 6)
- The authors say the framework "is not a complete curriculum or a policy prescription". [quote] (p. 7, Conclusion)
- "AI literacy discourse that evades that politics is not neutral but complicit." [quote] (p. 7, Conclusion)

## Critical notes

- Preprint, not peer reviewed on the evidence in hand; journal venue not confirmed. The affiliation given is the University of North Texas (p. 1) and the text speaks from "the discipline of information science" (p. 5); the work is theoretical, with no empirical test. The PDF is set in a conference proceedings template, which is no evidence of acceptance anywhere.
- Its remedy stays on the user's side: it asks learners to interrogate, resist and join governance. It does not say what an institution must hand the person it acts on (a record, a brake, a standing). A reader who holds the responsibilization view could apply that charge to it; the paper does not address that.
- The "banking" criticism of competency training is close to the paper's claim that the institution keeps its position while the person is told to read better, but it is framed as epistemic agency, not as institutional position, and it makes no historical argument about literacy.
- Claim about the four frameworks rests on the authors' reading of them, given in one paragraph without a method or page references (p. 4); the four frameworks were not opened for this card (see the UNESCO and OECD entries in the facet report).
- Its history of literacy is one sentence citing Houston 1983 on who should be taught to read (pp. 1-2). It does not cite Graff, and the words "literacy myth" and "responsibilization" do not occur (file searched). It cites Stamboliev 2023 (card stamboliev2023postcritical) for the absence of power from AI-literacy frameworks (p. 1).
