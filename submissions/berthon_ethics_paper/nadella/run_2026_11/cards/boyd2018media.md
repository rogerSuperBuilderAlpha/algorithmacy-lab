## Summary

Read: all but the closing paragraphs. The essay is the text of a 2018 SXSW EDU keynote. boyd argues that media literacy is being offered as the solution to "fake news" and that this puts the work of judging truth on individuals. She says critical-thinking training can be turned against its aims, since people who distrust the media already look for its flaws and groups such as RT use the language of questioning. She reports Nabiha Syed's view that the "marketplace of ideas" frame is naive, and she calls investing in individuals as a solution to a systemic abuse of power "very American". She does not abandon education: she says producing critical thinkers is not "inherently a bad thing" and that she does not want "a world full of sheeple", but she will not naively assume what media literacy can do against a culture war already under way. The essay carries no evaluation data of its own and works from anecdote and cited scholars.

## Key facts it relies on

- Individualising the response. [quote] section "Weaponizing Critical Thinking": "Since we live in a neoliberal society that prioritizes individual agency, we double down on media literacy as the “solution” to misinformation."
- The onus on the public. [quote] same section: "The onus is on the public to interpret what they see."
- Systemic abuse and individual investment. [quote] section "Who Do You Trust?": "Doubling down on investing in individuals as a solution to a systemic abuse of power is very American."
- Skill does not secure good use. [quote] same section: "Developing media making skills doesn’t guarantee that someone will use them for good."
- Literacy as authority over knowing. [quote] section "Epistemological Warfare": "If we’re not careful, “media literacy” and “critical thinking” will simply be deployed as an assertion of authority over epistemology."
- Media literacy proposed as the "solution" by funders, journalists, platforms and officials. [paraphrase] introduction.

## Critical notes

A keynote essay with no locators beyond section headings and no empirical test of its claim, which boyd states as a concern, that media-literacy interventions will backfire as "the crass versions of critical thinking already have" (introduction). Bulger and Davison, whom she cites, hold that programmes show some positive outcomes. The essay moves the burden claim from the learner to the funders and platforms who propose the remedy, but names no institution that carried the burden instead. It does not show a campaign blaming the learner.
Bearing: supports 3.6 (a later literacy offered as a remedy for a structural condition, with the burden placed on the person).
