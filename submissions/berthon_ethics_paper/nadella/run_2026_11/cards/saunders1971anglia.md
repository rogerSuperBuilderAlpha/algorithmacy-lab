## Summary

Read in part, in a public Internet Archive capture of BAILII's transcription of the House of Lords print. Page numbers
of that print survive as bare numerals in the text and serve as locators below; this is not the [1971] AC pagination.
Mrs Gallie, aged 78, signed an assignment of her leasehold house to Lee, believing it a gift to her nephew. Her
spectacles were broken and she did not read it. Lee borrowed £2,000 from the building society on the security of the
house. The House unanimously dismissed her executrix's appeal and restated the plea of *non est factum*. The plea
began as relief for those who could not read and had to trust another to tell them what they signed. It now reaches a
person who, without fault, cannot understand a document, but only where the difference between the document and what
was believed is radical and the signer took reasonable care. The burden is on the signer. Lord Reid and Lord
Wilberforce both justify the restriction by the innocent third party who relied on the signature.

## Key facts it relies on

- Lord Reid on the plea's origin: "Originally this extension appears to have been made in favour of those who were unable to read owing to blindness or illiteracy and who therefore had to trust someone to tell them what they were signing." p. 1 [quote]
- Lord Reid on whom it now covers: those "permanently or temporarily unable through no fault of their own to have without explanation any real understanding of the purport of a particular document, whether that be from defective education, illness or innate incapacity. But that does not excuse them from taking such precautions as they reasonably can." p. 1 [quote]
- Lord Reid on the burden: "So there must be a heavy burden of proof on the person who seeks to invoke this remedy." p. 1 [quote]
- Lord Reid: the extension "must be kept within narrow limits if it is not to shake the confidence of those who habitually and rightly rely on signatures when there is no obvious reason to doubt their validity." p. 1 [quote]
- Lord Reid: there must be "a radical difference between what he signed and what he thought he was signing." p. 2 [quote]
- Facts (Lord Hodson): she was 78; "The Appellant had broken her spectacles so that she could not use them. She could not read without them so did not read the document." p. 3 [quote]
- Lord Wilberforce on other systems: execution "before a notary who, if he is competent and honest, as he usually is, can do much to ensure that the signer understands and intends what he is doing"; in systems such as England's "dependence has to be placed on the level of education and prudence of the signer and on the honesty and competence of his professional adviser." p. 7 [quote]
- Lord Wilberforce on the medieval plea: "illiterate people who either could not read, or could not under- stand, the language in which the deed was written, were allowed this plea" (hyphen as in the print). p. 8 [quote]
- Lord Wilberforce: "As to persons who are illiterate, or blind, or lacking in understanding, the law is in a dilemma." Relief should go where consent was truly lacking, but signers must "act responsibly and carefully according to their circumstances." p. 10 [quote]
- Disposition: appeal dismissed; Mrs Gallie "fell short, very far short" of the required case (Lord Wilberforce). p. 10 [quote]

## Critical notes

- What the usual summary overstates: *Saunders* is often read as abolishing protection for illiterate signers. The
  House kept the plea open to those who cannot read or understand through no fault of their own (Reid p. 1;
  Wilberforce p. 10), but tied it to reasonable care, a radical difference and a heavy burden of proof, and lost
  the case for an elderly signer who could not read the deed.
- Who carries the loss: as between the signer and an innocent lender, the rule puts it on the signer unless she
  proves her case; the justification named is the confidence of those who rely on signatures (Reid p. 1;
  Wilberforce p. 7).
- Lord Wilberforce names the institutional alternative English law lacks: a notary charged with ensuring the
  signer understands (p. 7). That is a contemporary statement of the missing check of understanding.
- Lord Pearson's leading speech, with which Lord Reid agreed, was not read beyond its opening; Lord Hodson's and
  Viscount Dilhorne's reasons were not read beyond the facts.
- Form of correction (question 3): a narrow shift of risk (the plea), conditioned on care; the notary is named as
  the check of understanding that English law does not provide.
- Bearing: supports 3.4 (the presumption that the signer read, with the plea as its narrow exception; consistent
  with ruling 8 on *L'Estrange*); 4.4-loss (loss allocated to the signer); 7-D06 (the notary as a check of
  understanding, absent here).
- Corrected by V-A-2: Lord Wilberforce's "dependence" quotation (p. 7) was cut before "and on the honesty and competence of his professional adviser"; restored, since the omitted half names the adviser as the second safeguard.
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-2-1, 2026-11-18): the Summary said Lee "mortgaged the house ... and defaulted". The speeches say the Society advanced £2,000 to Lee on the security of the house (Hodson p. 3; Pearson p. 12) and that Lee used the money on his own debts and gave Parkin nothing (Pearson p. 12); no default on the loan is stated anywhere in the file, so the word is removed. Lord Reid's "rely on signatures" quotation (p. 1) now runs to its own end, "when there is no obvious reason to doubt their validity". Lord Wilberforce's speech occupies pp. 7-10 of the print, not 7-11; Lord Pearson's begins on p. 11.
