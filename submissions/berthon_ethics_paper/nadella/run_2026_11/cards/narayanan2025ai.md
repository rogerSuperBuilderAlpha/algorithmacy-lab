## Summary

Read in full: the whole main text of a web essay the page times at 93 minutes; footnotes 1-119 not read. The authors
argue that AI is a "normal technology" like electricity or the internet: transformative, but diffusing over decades,
controlled by people and organisations, and shaped by institutions. In high-consequence applications diffusion is
slow, chiefly for reasons of safety, under limits that regulation often enforces (FDA, EU AI Act), and new
high-consequence uses "can and must" be regulated as they arise. Control comes in many forms between full model
alignment and a human approving every action: auditing, monitoring, fail-safes, circuit breakers, least privilege,
reversible actions. They predict that market forces will be the main driver of the shift of human work toward AI
control, because poorly controlled AI will not make business sense, and they say regulation should bolster that
control and fill the gaps the market leaves. They hold that, if AI is normal technology, the risks that matter most
are systemic (bias, inequality, concentration of power, labour conditions) and arise from people and organisations
using AI for their own interests, for which history offers precedent. Part III concedes a case that went the other
way: with social media, market forces were "arguably" insufficient and regulators slow, and some future AI
applications "will be more like social media". Part IV asks policymakers for resilience in place of nonproliferation,
for evidence-gathering and transparency rules, and for regulation that recognises degrees of human oversight; it
says liability "fares better" than ex ante rules and names its limits.

## Key facts it relies on

- Opening: "The normal technology frame is about the relationship between technology and society. It rejects technological determinism, especially the notion of AI itself as an agent in determining its future." [quote]
- Opening: the frame "is guided by lessons from past technological revolutions, such as the slow and uncertain nature of technology adoption and diffusion." [quote]
- Part I, "AI diffusion in safety-critical areas is slow": the sub-section starts from about 50 applications of machine learning "to make decisions about individuals by predicting their future behavior or outcomes" (criminal risk, insurance risk, child maltreatment). "While these applications have proliferated, there is a crucial nuance: In most cases, decades-old statistical techniques are used". The lag the authors report is in the methods used ("AI diffusion lags decades behind innovation"), in domains where such decisions about people are already made. [quote; paraphrase]
- Same sub-section: "there are already extremely strong safety-related speed limits in highly consequential tasks. These limits are often enforced through regulation, such as the FDA’s supervision of medical devices, as well as newer legislation such as the EU AI Act". [quote]
- Same sub-section: "as and when new areas arise in which AI can be used in highly consequential ways, we can and must regulate them." The 2010 Flash Crash led to circuit breakers. [quote; paraphrase]
- Part I, "Diffusion is limited by the speed of human, organizational, and institutional change": "the speed of diffusion is inherently limited by the speed at which not only individuals, but also organizations and institutions, can adapt to technology." [quote]
- Part I, "The External world puts a speed limit on AI innovation": "This “capability-reliability gap” shows up over and over. It has been a major barrier to building useful AI “agents” that can automate real-world tasks." The agent tasks the authors name are "booking travel or providing customer service". [quote]
- Part II, "Control comes in many flavors": the authors define human-in-the-loop control as a system in which "every AI decision or action requires review and approval by a human". "In most scenarios, this approach greatly diminishes the benefits of automation, and therefore either devolves into the human acting as a rubber stamp or is outcompeted by a less safe solution." They add that "human-in-the-loop control is not synonymous with human oversight of AI; it is one particular oversight model, and an extreme one." [quote]
- Same sub-section: control techniques include auditing, monitoring, fail-safes, "circuit breakers that automatically stop operations when predefined safety thresholds are exceeded", least privilege, and making "state-changing actions" reversible. [quote; paraphrase]
- Same sub-section: "Poorly controlled AI will be too error prone to make business sense. But regulation can and should bolster the ability and necessity of organizations to keep humans in control." [quote]
- Part III, opening: "deployers and developers should have the primary responsibility for mitigating accidents in AI systems"; "In many cases, market forces will provide an adequate incentive, but safety regulation should fill any gaps." [quote]
- Part III, "Arms races are an old problem": "It is hard for consumers to assess product safety (and for workers to assess workplace safety), so market failures are common in the absence of regulation." The regulatory strategies named are processes, "outcomes (liability), and correcting information asymmetry (labeling and certification)". [quote]
- Same sub-section, on social-media recommendation algorithms: "Arguably, market forces were insufficient to align revenues with societal benefit; worse, regulators have been slow to act." The authors give two reasons (harm from social media is hard to attribute; transport has had over a century of safety standards) and conclude: "AI is broad enough that some of its future applications will be more like transportation, while others will be more like social media." [quote; paraphrase]
- Part III, "History suggests normal AI may introduce many kinds of systemic risks": such risks "arise from people and organizations using AI to advance their own interests, with AI merely serving as an amplifier of existing instabilities in our society." [quote]
- Part IV, "The case for resilience": the authors agree with Marchant and Stevens that "ex ante approaches are poorly suited to AI"; "Liability fares better, but also has important limitations, including uncertainty about causation and the chilling effects it might exert on technology development." [quote]
- Part IV, "Nonproliferation is infeasible to enforce and leads to single points of failure": the "hierarchy of loci of control over AI systems" runs "governments, model developers, application developers, deployers, and end users"; in the resilience mindset control "is usually exercised at the lowest possible level". The list ends at the end user. [quote; paraphrase]
- Part IV, "Realizing the benefits of AI": "When regulation makes a binary distinction between decisions that are and are not fully automated, and does not recognize degrees of oversight, it disincentivizes the adoption of new models for AI control." Calling domains such as insurance, benefits adjudication or hiring "high-risk" "may be a category error". [quote]
- Part IV names AI literacy twice, both times as public capacity: "improving AI literacy" among the prerequisites for technical defences and policymaking ("The case for resilience"), and "promoting AI literacy as well as workforce training" among the "complements of automation" ("Realizing the benefits of AI"). The latter sub-section gives governments a role in "compensating those who stand to lose as a result of automation". [quote]

## Critical notes

- Contest: the essay's own note says it states "a worldview rather than defending a proposition" and does not answer counter-arguments point by point; its main opponents are superintelligence and fast-takeoff views, not accounts of power over the people acted on. No published critique of it was opened in this run.
- Record: the page header and its metadata give 15 April 2025 (datePublished 2025-04-15; dateModified 2026-11-17); the page's own "Cite as" line gives "25-09 Knight First Amend. Inst. (Apr. 14, 2025)". The page calls "Artificial Intelligence and Democratic Freedoms" a project, not an essay series.
- Strongest use against the paper (the card's inference; the essay does not speak of a person acted on): if agents are normal technology, the person acted on is protected the way consumers and workers were protected from earlier technologies, by sector regulation that makes firms "internalize the costs" (Part III, "Arms races are an old problem") and by firms' own interest in control; the paper's "transition" framing and its call for new duties may overstate the novelty. The essay calls the control problem "much more tractable" (Part II) and says market forces give an adequate incentive "in many cases", with regulation to fill gaps (Part III, opening); it does not say its control menu is sufficient. That menu overlaps Nadella's P8 and P11-P19 on limiting privileges, auditing, observation and a stop; Nadella's text names no reversibility (his text searched for "revers": none).
- What it leaves standing: the essay addresses its control techniques to "developers, deployers, and operators" (Part II) and ranks the loci of control from governments down to end users (Part IV); the main text gives no control, record or claim to a person an AI system acts on who is not its user (main text searched for redress, recourse, appeal, contest, compensat, affected: the hits concern contested research, an "appealing" policy, compensation for job loss and humanity's power). Its social-media case concedes that market forces and regulators can both fail where harm is hard to attribute. Its systemic-risk section names concentration of power and people and organisations pursuing their own interests, and uses the Industrial Revolution as precedent, which supports reasoning from history.
- Person acted on gets: record — no; brake — no (circuit breakers and fail-safes are tools for "developers, deployers, and operators", Part II); envelope — no; standing — no (protection runs through regulators and sector regulation, Part I and Part III "Arms races are an old problem"; the essay names no route by which a person contests a decision); loss cover — partial (liability is named as a regulatory strategy, Part III "Arms races are an old problem", and as faring better than ex ante rules, with limits, Part IV "The case for resilience"; compensation from government is for those who lose to automation, Part IV "Realizing the benefits of AI"; nothing is said of loss to a person an agent acts on).
