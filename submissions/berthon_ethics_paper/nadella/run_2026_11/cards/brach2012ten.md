## Summary

Read: pp. 1-3, 10 and 13; the other pages were searched for key terms only. This discussion paper sets out ten attributes of health care organizations that make it easier for people to find, understand and use health information and services. It starts by treating health literacy as the product of a person's skills and the demands of the health care system, and it calls for system changes to fit those demands to the public's abilities. Attribute 5 recommends "universal precautions": simplify all communication and verify comprehension with everyone, so that no patient is singled out. The paper says the list is not exhaustive and that implementation strategies are not prescriptive. It states that the paper was not reviewed under Institute of Medicine procedures and reports the views of its authors.

## Key facts it relies on

- Health literacy is a relation between the person and the system. [quote] p. 1: "Health literacy is the product of individuals’ capacities and the health literacy–related demands and complexities of the health care system"
- The remedy sits in the system. [quote] p. 1: "System changes are needed to align health care demands better with the public’s skills and abilities"
- Universal precautions. [quote] p. 10: "They simplify all communication to the greatest extent possible and verify comprehension with everyone, because they do not make assumptions about who understands or needs extra assistance."
- Shame is a recognised effect. [quote] p. 10: "Individuals with limited literacy, in particular, may feel ashamed"
- Equal treatment to reduce stigma. [quote] p. 10: "Treating everyone equally, such as making offers of assistance to all, can reduce the stigma associated with limited health literacy."
- Limits of the list. [quote] p. 2: "The list of attributes is by no means exhaustive"
- Page numbers are the paper's own printed numbers (PDF page minus one). [paraphrase] file header.

## Critical notes

A discussion paper by roundtable participants, with the disclaimer on its cover that it did not go through IOM review. It describes an aspiration and gives no outcome data in the passages I read. It is evidence that authors in one field, health literacy, argued for moving part of the burden from the person to the organization, which counts against any claim that every later literacy places the burden on the individual; it does not show that organizations made the move. The AHRQ toolkit it cites for universal precautions was not opened.
Bearing: counter to 3.6 (a field with an institutional-duty frame); supports D06 and D07 as a worked correction (check understanding with everyone; change the medium at the point of use).
- Corrected by V-A-1: "AHRC toolkit" corrected to "AHRQ toolkit" (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, as the paper cites it, PDF line ~517).
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-1-2): the Critical note said the field "moved part of the burden"; the paper is a statement of aims ("an aspirational vision", p. 2), so the note now says its authors argued for the move. Outside `pages_read`, the paper does cite outcome studies for single strategies, for example a randomized trial of the Re-Engineered Discharge that "reduced rehospitalizations by 30 percent" (p. 17); it reports no outcome for the ten attributes as a set (Conclusion, p. 19).
