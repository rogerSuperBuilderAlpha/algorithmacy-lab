## Summary

Read in full. Ragas asks how far people who could not read took part in Peruvian politics between 1810 and 1900. He argues that for most of the century they did, and that the 1896 electoral law ended this by making reading and writing a condition of citizenship. His sources are newspapers, electoral regulations, civic registers and travellers' accounts. He reports that the civic registers he consulted (Lima 1834-1839 and four other districts) do not list literacy as a condition of voting. He describes ways of voting that did not need a voter to write: oral declaration of the choice to the polling-table officials, ballots written by others or distributed ready-made by electoral clubs, and delegates who could read and sign for a voter. He also reports the contemporary prejudice against Andean voters as unable to read, write or think, and the opposed claims of reformers who wanted illiterate people brought into politics. The article is a counter-case to a simple history of literacy as a requirement: here a requirement was postponed for decades and then imposed, with an aim that Ragas, in his conclusion, attributes to the legislators: keeping rural highland groups from the polls (p. 133). He cites no legislator stating it.

## Key facts it relies on

- The 1896 norm "solo concedía la ciudadanía a aquellos que pudiesen leer y escribir"; the 1979 constitution restored the vote to illiterate citizens (p. 108) [quote].
- Ragas's thesis: between 1810 and 1896 illiterate people "participaron de manera efectiva" in decisions and political processes (p. 109) [quote].
- He grants that the electoral regulations "establecieron efectivamente que la alfabetización era un requisito necesario para obtener el derecho al sufragio"; his claim is that not knowing how to read and write "no siempre fue una barrera" in practice (p. 109) [quote; bullet added at the Fable read].
- Legislators "postergaron el requisito de la literacidad por varias décadas", hoping the state would have taught the population by mid-century (p. 111) [quote].
- The authorities' ideal citizen was literate, but literacy was not the requirement that weighed most for voters (p. 127) [paraphrase].
- In the civic registers he studied, literacy "no aparece en ninguno de ellos como un requisito que condicionara el voto", and none records whether a person could sign (p. 128) [quote]. The registers (Lima, 1834-1839, and those of Lircay, Moya, Acoria and Santiago de Chocorvos) are footnoted at p. 128, n. 49 [paraphrase].
- Critics held that Indigenous people could not decide for themselves: "no saben leer, escribir ni pensar", a writer of mid-century said (p. 129) [quote].
- Indigenous people sometimes used an intermediary to sign for them; Wiener, the traveller, claimed only one in a thousand highland Indigenous people could "firmar su nombre" (p. 130) [quote for the phrase; paraphrase for the claim, which is a traveller's estimate].
- The Sociedad Amiga de los Indios urged Indigenous people to learn Spanish so that their children could read the laws and hold office (pp. 130-131) [paraphrase].
- Ways of voting without writing: on some occasions ("En algunas ocasiones") voting days were extended to nearly a week, and "quienes no pudiesen escribir su nombre en las boletas podían expresar su preferencia oralmente a los encargados de la mesa" (p. 131) [quote].
- Ballots were small unprinted slips legitimised by the signature of the register official or table chief, and parties and clubs could prepare them in advance; a voter could memorise how to write a candidate's name (p. 131) [paraphrase].
- The 1833 law let parish voters who could not read and write name trusted persons to read the papers and sign the records for them (p. 131, n. 58) [paraphrase].
- An alternative proposal, voting by lot with balls, "hubiese hecho aún más innecesario el requisito de saber leer y escribir" (pp. 131-132; the sentence runs across the page break) [quote]. The proposals for it "fracasaron antes de ser implantados" (p. 132) [quote].
- Conclusion: late in the century legislators changed the electoral regulation to restrict the broad participation Cádiz had allowed, and "El criterio sobre el cual se basaron para regular el número de votantes fue la literacidad" (p. 133) [quote].
- The measure "iba de la mano con el intento de mantener alejados de las urnas a los grupos rurales populares de la sierra" (p. 133) [quote].
- Ragas ends on the later road to inclusion through reading, "marcado por el recelo y el prejuicio hacia los pobladores andinos" (p. 134) [quote].

## Critical notes

- The article reaches the 1896 law only in the introduction and conclusion (pp. 108-109, 133-134). It does not analyse the debate on the law or its operation. For that Ragas cites Chiaramonti (1995, "Andes o nación: la reforma electoral de 1896 en Perú", p. 108, n. 1), which this run has not read. The article cannot say how the 1896 test worked in practice or who objected in 1896.
- His claim that illiterate people voted rests on the content of civic registers and on what electoral regulations required; it does not rest on a count of illiterate voters. Footnote 49 names the registers.
- The article is one author's interpretation of one country. It documents the gate (a literacy requirement for citizenship, imposed in 1896) and its target as Ragas reads it (highland rural voters, p. 133), but gives no measured effect.
- Who objected: the editors of El Zurriago (1848) set out to bring illiterate people into politics against Bartolomé Herrera's claim that only the educated should decide (p. 132); the Sociedad Amiga de los Indios (1867) argued for schooling instead (pp. 130-131).
- Bearing: supports (STRUCTURE 3.2) as a non-US instance of a literacy gate imposed on a population that had been voting; also counter, because it shows that illiterate voting worked for decades by oral declaration and prepared ballots. Candidate 6 of PLAN 4.5 (blame and stigma): documents the stigma against Indigenous voters (p. 129).
- Corrected at the Fable read (V-A-1-1): (1) "a stated aim" (Summary) and "its explicit target" (notes) presented Ragas's own reading on p. 133 as the legislators' declared purpose; both now say whose reading it is (the verifier's OVERSTATED, settled). (2) A Key fact added from p. 109: Ragas grants that the regulations made literacy a formal requirement throughout, and argues it was not always a barrier in practice. Without it the card read as if no requirement stood before 1896. (3) "highland" removed from the description of the four district registers; n. 49 names the districts and does not say where they are. (4) "On some occasions" restored to the bullet on week-long voting and oral declaration (p. 131). The original quotations were exact and are unchanged.
