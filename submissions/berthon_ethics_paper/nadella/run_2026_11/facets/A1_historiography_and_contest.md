## (5) Claims in the paper's documents that these sources contradict or press

1. **The strongest case against the frame, in the sources' own terms.** Graff, whom the paper relies on, holds that every new "-acy" re-runs the literacy myth: "Most of them resemble illogical, unfounded metaphors rather than reputable literacies" (graff2022new, p. 49), and "Many of these proliferating 'new literacies' derive from formal organizations founded to promote and sell them" (p. 49). On that reading "algorithmacy", defined without reading or writing as its base, is the next entry on his list, and the paper's plan to measure the history of literacy against it is the metaphor he rejects. Street's side of the same objection, in Vieira's report, is that to infer a society's capabilities from a medium's technical potential is "technicist" (vieira2013social, p. 26, paraphrase). Vieira adds that Goody and Watt's autonomous view "has been rebutted" (p. 26), so any argument that a medium brings a competence needs a context-bound account, not a technical one. STRUCTURE s1.2 answers this by comparing institutional positions, not cognition; the sources here do not say whether that answer meets Graff's test, which asks for a tie to reading, writing and a documented history.
2. **STRUCTURE s2.1 guardrail.** It says people sought literacy "because it gave real agency". The benefit cards show real average gains in survival and earnings, but for schooling, in the late twentieth century, and as associations, with one causal study in two African countries. None speaks to nineteenth-century or earlier cases; the guardrail rests on the Johansson and Washington cards, not on these.
3. **Graff's own caveat on the myth.** graff2022new p. 48: he "did not argue that these foundational assumptions were completely false". A paper that cites him for the claim that literacy's spread was managed through wrongs should not present him as holding that literacy did harm; he holds that its effects depend on context and its promise is oversold.
