## Set A (Branch 2015; Naidu 2012; Barakat 2016; Quigley 2021)

### branch2015mockery — full (journal pp. 44–65; journal page = PDF page + 43)
Central claim: Congress in 1864–1869 already understood the literacy test as a tool to disfranchise by race while appearing to measure literate ability; the "self-evident good" of literacy shielded the test for 75 years.
- "they had fully articulated an understanding of the test as easily manipulable, capable of disfranchising or enfranchising citizens based on race while seeming to do so on the basis of literate ability." (p. 46)
- "Literate ability became nearly synonymous with race, useful as a way of describing a population in other than racial terms" (p. 46)
- "the abuse of the literacy test was made easier by the absence of any well-founded measure of function and competence" (p. 48, quoting Stevens)
- "discussions of literacy supported ethnic and racial oppression under the guise of promoting an ideal of better citizenship." (p. 50)
- "The idea of literacy, however, created a powerful cover for the test's racist agenda" (p. 55)
- "Disfranchisement on the basis of ignorance, then, instead of creating a more educated electorate, offered an incentive for continued educational inequity." (p. 59)
- "it used an arbitrary measure to target a particular population, and it encouraged continued neglect of educational opportunities for African Americans" (p. 62); the test "served as an explicit model for similar literacy requirements targeting Indians in South Africa and Asians in Australia" (p. 62)
Contemporaries: Cowan, "this is a mockery in the name of a barrier"; "I tremble for my sable brother when I reflect that he may be at the mercy of some political board" (p. 45; dated 13 Dec 1866 on p. 44, "1865" on p. 55 — internal inconsistency). Scofield (Jan 1866): "They debased him by law to fit him for slavery, and justified slavery because he was debased." (p. 59). Boutwell (Jan 1866): "do you suppose the men who will preside and decide this question will come to the conclusion that a negro can read when the result is that he must also vote?" (p. 59). Shellabarger (29 Jan 1869): "This colored race cannot now read because we have for these centuries shut them from the light" (p. 60). Warner (1869): "it is the disfranchisement of the poor and the ignorant which it is our duty to guard against" (p. 61).
Present: "the impetus behind the literacy test in American suffrage law still plays a central role in American politics." (p. 49); "standardized tests of literacy continue to have high-stakes consequences." (p. 50)

### naidu2012suffrage — full (NBER WP 18129; printed page = PDF page − 3)
Central claim: border-county comparisons show poll taxes and literacy tests cut turnout and cut black school inputs, not white; black labour lost at least 15% of annual income, landowners gained 12%.
- "disenfranchisement reduced the teacher-child ratio in black schools by 10-23%, with no significant effects on white teacher-child ratios." (abstract)
- "black labor bore a collective loss from disenfranchisement equivalent to at least 15% of annual income, with landowners experiencing a 12% gain." (abstract)
- "the literacy test was likely to bind for a large segment of the population, particularly for poor blacks and whites." (p. 7)
- "the effects of black political exclusion and educational under-provision persist through the intergenerational transmission of human capital and wealth" (p. 27)
Contemporaries: none objecting to the test itself.

### barakat2016improving — full author manuscript (review-stage; manuscript pages = PDF − 1, not WD pp. 242–257)
Central claim: most measured gains in the adult literacy rate come from literate youth ageing into the adult bracket, not from adults learning; the indicator misleads policy.
- "observed gains in overall adult literacy greatly overstate the degree to which adults have gained literacy at adult ages." (p. 1)
- rates can improve "even in situations where not a single illiterate adult was made literate." (p. 3)
- "Perhaps more importantly, focusing exclusively on young illiterates would be inequitable." (p. 27)
- "the same quantitative target can place vastly divergent demands on different countries." (p. 28)

### quigley2021naming — full (journal pp. 41–46; two-column extraction, quotes taken only from clean single-column runs)
Central claim: adult literacy's low funding and status come from "literacy classism", an inherited prejudice against adults with low literacy; calls for a human-rights framing.
- "We have also inherited an age-old prejudice towards those with lower levels of formal education." (p. 43)
- literacy "was one of the distinguishing qualities of aristocracy" (p. 43, quoting Fischer)
- Pole 1816 on the Bristol adult schools: "they will have also learned better to practise meekness, Christian fortitude, and resignation" (p. 43)
- Coolidge 1926: "ignorance is the most fruitful source of poverty, vice, and crime" (p. 44)
- Present: "Why should we invest more money into programs for 'losers?'" (p. 41); "it is acceptable to denigrate…illiterates." (p. 42, quoting Beder 1991)

## Set B (Jaywant 2022; Moje and Luke 2009; Cushing 2020; Boughton 2023)

### jaywant2022reshaping — full (journal pp. 380–408, CUP OA PDF)
Central claim: Padwal's *Jatibhed Viveksar* (1861/1865) seeded modern anti-caste critique by recasting Shudras as conquered indigenes. Literacy is a secondary thread.
- "Lower-caste efforts to criticize caste called into question Brahman monopoly over knowledge, and attempts to disseminate education among the Shudras and the Ati-shudras ('Untouchables') were often ridiculed…" (p. 392)
- "native presses run by upper-caste editors refrained from publishing the writings of lower-caste authors." (p. 392)
- Padwal: "the conquerors kept the vanquished people at a distance, regarded them as lowly, and prohibited their access to the Vedas and knowledge (vidya)." (p. 402)
- Padwal: "they also conjure fake papers to plunder people's homes." (p. 388); fn 108: Patils "were swindled by the Brahman accountant, or the Kulkarni, of the village" (p. 406)
- Ambedkar's view reported: "the Shudras were Aryan-Kshatriyas who were degraded by Brahman refusal to perform the Upanayana" (p. 407)
Contemporaries: Padwal 1861, "Is there no end to this injustice?" (p. 388); *Dnyanodaya* letter 15 Mar 1855 (fn 95, p. 403, paraphrased in the article).

- the standard belongs to "powerful social groups (western; literate; white; male; middle-upper class), who manage access to opportunities such as employment and education, using standardised language benchmarks as a gatekeeping mechanism." (pp. 322–323)

### boughton2023mass — full (journal pp. 20–32; checked against layout-preserving extraction)
Central claim: UNESCO and western donors, under Cold War and later "innovation" ideology, abandoned universal mass campaigns for selective vocational "functional literacy"; the pattern recurred in Timor-Leste and First Nations Australia.
- "decision to abandon the universal provision of literacy education to adults, in favor of a selective approach, prioritizing only those sections of the population who, it was believed, would be most likely to contribute to more rapid economic development." (p. 22)
- national campaigns were "ruled ineligible, even where, as in Guinea, this was the government's priority" (p. 23)
- human capital theory is "ideological, because it implies that people can escape from poverty by raising their education level, without any need to transform the social system which creates the inequality in the first place." (p. 30)
Contemporaries: Czechoslovakia, Ukrainian SSR, Venezuela at the 1961 UN General Assembly: "the colonial powers had held up the development of education in the countries under their rule in order to ensure a supply of cheap labour" (p. 21, citing United Nations 1963); the colonial powers replied that "no direct relationship existed between illiteracy and colonialism" (p. 21). UNESCO/UNDP 1976 evaluation: "EWLP's framers did not assess (or assess with more care) earlier successful mass literacy campaigns" (p. 22). Kozol 1980 on US programmes: "begun in generosity, carried out in condescension, and concluded in benign defeat" (p. 29).

## Set C (Blasutto and de la Croix 2023; Nipps 2014; Bai, Jia and Yang 2024)

### blasutto2023catholic — full (author-hosted copy of the published EJ PDF, journal pp. 2899–2924)
Central claim: Index censorship cut knowledge production both by removing books and by steering talent into compliant work; a structural model for Italian scholars 1400–1750 gives output 43% higher without censorship, half via talent reallocation.
- "Censorship makes new ideas less available to others, but also reduces the number of people choosing to develop non-compliant ideas." (p. 2899)
- "The entirety of ideas accessible to citizens had to be controlled to maintain the predominance of the Church." (p. 2905)
- "the punishments for reading and keeping censored books included excommunication, eternal damnation and confiscation of assets, which helped finance the inquisitory apparatus" (p. 2905)
- Numbers: "43% higher" (pp. 2899, 2901, 2917); censorship rate "18%" (p. 2901; Table 4 p. 2915); non-conformist authors "from 52% in 1470–550 to 29% in 1680–750" (p. 2901); robustness "27%–59%" (p. 2919). Macro comparison worded three ways (pp. 2899, 2901, 2920).

### nipps2014cum — full (Harvard DASH bitstream, published version, journal pp. 494–500)
Central claim: the Licensing of the Press Act 1662 controlled print through Stationers' registration, a cap on presses, search and licensing; copyright served as one more control; enforcement uneven; lapse in 1692.
- "giving the Stationers the ultimate say in what got printed and what did not." (p. 495)
- "The right to copy was yet another means of quashing the press." (p. 496)
- "authority was exercised in extremely uneven fashion" (p. 496)
- "full rein to search and confiscate any printed material deemed 'treasonable and schismatical'" (p. 498)
- Numbers: London printing offices limited to twenty, actual presses "perhaps as much as triple that number" (p. 496).
Contemporaries: by 1692 "the hue and cry over state censorship and the rights of the stationers and of an ever-increasing number of professional authors (including Defoe and Swift) had reached a critical mass" (p. 500); no one quoted.

### bai2024knowledge — full (NBER WP 33258, Dec 2024, not peer-reviewed; printed page = PDF − 1)
Central claim: the Siku Quanshu bans (1772–1783) cut publications in heavily censored categories for about seven decades, mainly through chilling and publisher exit.
- "Both government officials and the public were encouraged to report controversial content, creating an atmosphere of fear and self-censorship among academics." (p. 7)
- "the death penalty for the author, the editor, the publisher, and their family members." (p. 8)
- "Censorship might result in the silence of authors who remain unobserved." (p. 22)
- Numbers: 3,102 books banned (p. 7); one SD more censorship, "18% drop" in titles 1773–1839 (p. 3; Poisson −0.182, Table 2 p. 34; OLS about 15%, p. 14); "22% reduction in the number of publishers" (pp. 20–21).

## Set D (Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024; España-Eljaiek et al. 2023; Becker, Pino and Vidal-Robert 2021; Nichols, Thrall and Quiros 2024)

### castillogomez2024writing — full (JEMS 13: 21–37; journal page = PDF + 20)
Central claim: elites policed and denied writing to common people and archives kept elite traces; subalterns still used writing for their own memory.
- "the ways in which literacy has been carefully policed in different societies, giving way to structures of exclusion" (p. 21)
- "for many subaltern subjects of the past writing meant more often than not taxes, punishment, and forced labor" (p. 22)
- "it was the king who repeatedly ordered to burn all documentary records produced by the rebels" (p. 23, Comunero revolt 1522)
- "The active denial of access to literacy is a key factor in explaining the social evolution of writing and its conceptualization as a knowledge restricted, to a greater or lesser extent, to the ruling class" (p. 25)
- "women were expected to read what was permitted and to write primarily within the private sphere" (p. 26)
- archives "tend to perpetuate a form of documentary elitism" (p. 27)
Contemporaries: Nicolás Antonio (c. 1648, printed 1672) on women's "unfair fate of ignorance and exclusion from all disciplines, if not from the usurpation by men, who, to maintain their command, have always kept women away from the instruments of command" (pp. 25–26); Alonso de Cartagena (after 1442) defended withholding study from labourers (p. 25); 1381 rebels attacked "the writing by which power was exercised" (Justice, quoted p. 23).

### espanaeljaiek2023education — full (IHE 20 (2024): 18–32; journal page = PDF + 17)
Central claim: racially exclusionary policy in Colombia's National Territories and colonial Mozambique gave indigenous children low-quality "civilising" mission schooling and far lower public investment, with persistent literacy gaps.
- "emphasis on acculturation into Western values, the elimination of native languages, and their replacement through the teaching of Spanish" (p. 23)
- schooling "barely allowed even for the acquisition of literacy" (Newitt, quoted p. 25)
- Numbers: Colombian primary spending per capita 1934, 0.988 pesos non-indigenous vs 0.047 indigenous (Table 4, p. 26); literacy, 1920–24 cohort, 59% mestizo/white vs 19% indigenous born in the National Territories (p. 27).
Contemporaries: paraphrased only (1950s Catholic complaints; 1960s rejection in Colombia).

### becker2021freedom — full (Warwick WP 1356, April 2021, pre-print with an editorial note left in fn 14; check for a published version)
Central claim: Catholic indexes cut printing of forbidden authors; compliant cities attracted fewer famous people and grew more slowly.
- "Book censorship was a mechanism to shape and control the ideas to which citizens had access" (p. 5)
- Numbers: Paris editions by indexed authors cut "by about two thirds" (p. 23); Spanish and Portuguese indexes no significant effect (pp. 21–23).
Contemporaries: the 1559 Index "caused a strong opposition from printers and booksellers and a lack of consensus among intellectuals" (p. 8, paraphrasing De Bujanda).
