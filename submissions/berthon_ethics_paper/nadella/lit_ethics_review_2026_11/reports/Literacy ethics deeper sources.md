# Seventeen errors trace literacy's unequal distribution

*Drafted by Claude (Opus 5.5), 19 Nov 2026. These are working notes for the author, not paper prose. Every reference
comes from the five research notes in `research_notes/Literacy ethics deeper sources/` or from `REVIEW.md` and
`READING_NOTES.md` in this folder, which rest on database records or texts read this session. No independent verifier
has yet checked any quotation below against its PDF, page image or edition. Status tags: **read** (read in full),
**partial** (named pages or sections read), **abs.** (abstract only), **n/a** (not accessible, with the HTTP result),
**lib.** (an existing library card read in place of the source). Flags carried from the notes: **[OCR]** (archive.org
OCR text, check against the page image), **[OCR mended]** (OCR letters silently repaired in the notes), **[my
translation]** (the notes' own English gloss of a Russian quotation, not a published translation), **[second-hand]**
(the quoted words belong to someone the read source quotes), **[unverified]** (the notes flag the wording, the
attribution or the claim as unchecked). Local copies sit under `~/iit-playground/berthon_ethics_local/lit_ethics_review/`
(`deep/<key>/` for this round).*

Scholars have documented literacy's distribution wrongs in every region the notes searched — colonial New England,
Tudor England, Qing China, Bombay Presidency, the Cape, the Ottoman Empire, Soviet Kazakhstan, Tokugawa Japan,
Australia — and the evidence fits the fourteen errors and the boundary case of the earlier review, plus three new
errors the notes support as distinct: **protective withholding** (denial presented as a favour to the denied),
**taxed for one's own exclusion** (the excluded fund provision that others capture), and **the accommodated veto**
(a rule open to all that yields to the dominant group's boycott). The strongest new evidence is institutional: the
**1883 Hunter Commission Report** records caste violence, names the motive as keeping low castes "in a state of
subjection", and admits the state did not enforce its own 1858 open-admission rule; **Matthew Arnold's 1867
inspector's report** says the grant examination certified reading children had not learnt, which turns error 14 from
a statistical artefact into a wrong recognised in its own time. Women's literacy adds the receive-only split (read,
not write) across New England, England, Qing China and German East Africa; caste and colonial records add fiscal
extraction and delegated vetoes; non-Western histories add restriction by script and the meritocratic gate. Contemporaries objected to
nearly every error at the time; only error 3 (record over the voice) and error 6 (oversold promise) still lack a
named, quoted contemporary. On the second question, the AI-ethics literature holds both halves of the argument but not
the join: Kalantzis and Cope (2024) give literacy's history of domination and carry it to generative AI, and Oleart
and Flores Moleon (2026), Srivastava (2026) and others show AI literacy and disclosure shifting responsibility onto
individuals, yet I found no work that sets the historical errors against the remedy family error by error, or that
uses Microsoft or Nadella as the case.

## Each of the fifteen original entries now carries regional evidence

The numbering below keeps REVIEW.md's 1–14 and B. Each entry gives the carried evidence in one clause, the new
evidence, the strongest contemporary objection on file, and the sub-cases the notes support. Present-day evidence
appears where the notes hold it.

### 1. The gate

The test's arbitrariness did the work: "the abuse of the literacy test was made easier by the absence of any
well-founded measure of function and competence" (Branch 2015, p. 48, quoting Stevens; read)
([Branch 2015](https://doi.org/10.21623/1.3.2.4)), and Louisiana's Understanding Clause cut Black registration by
"nearly 30 percentage points" (Keele, Cubbison and White 2021; abs.)
([Keele et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003055421000034)). The new notes add the plain gate of exclusion from
school. **"In Western Australia, Aboriginal students were excluded from state schools well into the 1930s and
later"**, and Northern Territory government responsibility "commenced only in the 1950s" (Renganathan and Kral 2018,
p. 146; partial) ([Renganathan and Kral 2018](https://doi.org/10.18251/ijme.v20i1.1530)). Burridge and Chodkiewicz
(2012; abs.) report that until the late 1960s Aboriginal children were "separated, segregated, excluded, 'protected' or
removed" ([Burridge and Chodkiewicz 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-6091-888-9_2)). The contemporary objection
remains Cowan in Congress: "this is a mockery in the name of a barrier" (Branch 2015, p. 45). Aboriginal residents made
their own objection in the 1939 Cummeragunja walk-off, demanding a curriculum "at the same standard as that delivered
in other government schools in NSW" (Marsden 2022; abs.) ([Marsden 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/0046760x.2022.2098389)).
The Chinese examination and the Natal test are gates too, but their design work lies in the cover they give, so they
sit under error 10.

### 2. Withheld competence, including the partial (read, not write)

The carried evidence is Castillo Gómez and Martínez's "women were expected to read what was permitted and to write
primarily within the private sphere" (2024, p. 26; read)
([Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024](https://doi.org/10.36253/jems-2279-7149-15269)) and Padwal's charge that the
conquerors "prohibited their access to the Vedas and knowledge (vidya)" (in Jaywant 2022, p. 402; read)
([Jaywant 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0026749x21000767)). Caste now has its primary texts. **Phule's *Slavery*
(1873; 1991 Patil translation, partial)** says the Bhats "forbade the Shudras any access to learning and condemned
them to the eternal perdition of ignorance" (p. 50) and wrote the ban into "their unholy books like the 'Manusmriti'"
(p. 57) ([Phule 1873/1991](https://archive.org/details/jotirao-phule-slavery-e)). Ambedkar restates his thesis in the
Reply to the Mahatma: "the effect of the Varna Vyavastha is to degrade the masses by denying them opportunity to
acquire knowledge and to emasculate them by denying them the right to be armed" [OCR; no page resolved] (*Annihilation
of Caste*, 3rd ed. 1945; partial) ([Ambedkar 1945](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.71655)). The Hunter
Commission reports the Mahar and Dher castes of Bombay "said to be practically excluded" from indigenous schools
(para. 134; partial) ([Hunter 1883](https://archive.org/details/ReportOfTheIndianEducationCommission)). Paik's
monograph on Dalit women's "double discrimination" (2014; abs.) and Viswanath's review of it, on "the glacial pace at
which Dalit girls entered school" (2017; abs.), point to the next library fetch
([Paik 2014](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315770741); [Viswanath 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/ahr/122.4.1186)).

Women's history supplies the **receive-only split**, a sub-case of this error. Monaghan's 1988 article, read only
on its first page (partial), opens on Hanna Newberry, Windsor's leading mortgage-holder, who "was unable to sign the
many documents to which she affixed her name", and reports Lockridge's counts: women's will signatures rose from 31
percent before 1670 "only to 46 percent by 1790s", against men's near 90 percent
([Monaghan 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/2713140)). The instructional explanation rests on a database abstract and on
David D. Hall, not on Monaghan's own text: "Others were taught to read but not to write … the distinction between
reading literacy and writing literacy reinforced women's subordinate status" (Chamberlain 2014; abs.)
([Chamberlain 2014](https://doi.org/10.5250/legacy.31.1.0033)). Hubbard finds in London that "reading literacy was far
more broadly socially diffused than the ability to write" (2015; abs.) ([Hubbard 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/jbr.2015.61)).
In German East Africa, Montgomery (2017; read) finds that "missionaries preferred the education of men to women, as
women made up only 33 percent of the student body", and that women "were supposed to be able to read and write at the
time of baptism, but the required level of skill and knowledge was generally very low" (p. 234)
([Montgomery 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0032329217704432)). A second sub-case, the **revived prohibition**, comes
from Wright (2021; abs.): a ḥadīth "prohibiting women's literacy", "rejected by most ḥadīth scholars and ignored by
jurists", "gained prominence in the second half of the nineteenth century through the works of scholars who weaponized
it as a response to colonial education projects" ([Wright 2021](https://doi.org/10.1163/15700607-61020019)). The title
"Teaching Women to Write" suggests the ban targeted writing; the abstract does not say so [unverified]. The women's
notes would file it under 10 or 11 as a counter-move; it sits here because what the revival did was withhold.

The contemporaries are strong. Nicolás Antonio (c. 1648) wrote of men "who, to maintain their command, have always kept
women away from the instruments of command" (Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024, pp. 25–26). Wollstonecraft asked "why
should they be kept in ignorance under the specious name of innocence?" (*Vindication*, 1792, ch. II; partial,
Wikisource, chapter locators only) ([Wollstonecraft 1792](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman)).
Abigail Adams, writing on 30 June 1778, quotes an unnamed writer: "tis an inhumane Tyranny to debar them of priviliges
of ingenious Education" (partial, read through a fetch summary of the MHS transcription; [unverified] verbatim; the
phrase is a quotation inside her letter, not her coinage)
([Adams 1778](https://www.masshist.org/publications/adams-papers/index.php/view/ADMS-04-03-02-0045)). Rokeya Sakhawat
Hossain "wrote back to the empire against both colonialism and patriarchy" (Bagchi 2009; abs.; [second-hand])
([Bagchi 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/00309230903335652)).

### 3. Record over the voice

Archives "tend to perpetuate a form of documentary elitism", and the king "repeatedly ordered to burn all documentary
records produced by the rebels" (Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024, pp. 27, 23; read). The new notes add three
recording asymmetries. The receive-only split leaves a person able to take in the institution's texts but unable to
answer, record or correspond. People who could not write "had to rely on the assistance of professional or social
scribes", whose role "often remain[s] unclear" (van der Wal 2021; abs.)
([van der Wal 2021](https://doi.org/10.1515/jhsl-2020-0018)). The 1849 Cape mission census simply left women out: "At
a few stations, indeed, the literacy of women was not ascertained" (Fourie, Ross and Viljoen 2014, WP p. 7; partial)
([Fourie et al. 2014 WP](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)). Kalantzis and Cope trace
first writing to "inventories of ownership" (2024, preprint p. 3; partial) ([Kalantzis and Cope 2024](https://osf.io/es5kb/download)).
**This error still lacks a quoted contemporary.** The 1381 rebels attacked "the writing by which power was exercised",
but those are a modern historian's words (Justice, quoted in Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024, p. 23; [second-hand]).

### 4. Custody of the medium

Control of print has measured costs: Italian scholarly output "43% higher" without the Index (Blasutto and de la Croix
2023, p. 2899; read) ([Blasutto and de la Croix 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/uead053)); an "18% drop" in Qing
titles and a "22% reduction in the number of publishers" per standard deviation of censorship (Bai, Jia and Yang 2024,
pp. 3, 20–21; read, working paper) ([Bai et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33258)); the Stationers' "ultimate say in
what got printed" (Nipps 2014, p. 495; read) ([Nipps 2014](https://doi.org/10.1086/677787)). The new notes extend custody
in three directions. In the Ottoman Empire, Müteferrika's 1727 press was licensed with conditions — "granting
permission only to selected individuals, prohibiting publication in religious subjects, and appointing a committee of
scholars to review" — and **"only 33 books were published"** in the eighteenth century (Coşgel, Miceli and Rubin 2012,
WP p. 30; read, working paper 2010-02R) ([Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)).
The contested question of a sultanic ban appears in the section on contested claims. Under apartheid the *Rand Daily
Mail* warned in 1951 that the Minister could "order mission and other private schools to conform to his ideas and to
use official syllabuses" (Moore 2017, p. 124; partial) ([Moore 2017](https://newcontree.org.za/index.php/nc/article/download/90/90)).
In Soviet Kazakhstan "both the general plan of agitation work and the plans of individual campaigns should be approved
by the party committees" (archival quote, Toimbek 2023, p. 27; read) ([Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)).

Two sub-cases belong here. **Restriction by script or register** controls the medium through its code rather than
a rule about persons: Ottoman authorities "banned printing in Arabic characters but allowed religious minorities to set
up their own presses" (Coşgel et al., WP p. 21); in Japan kanbun was "a de facto foreign language" (Hmeljak Sangawa
2017, p. 153; read) ([Hmeljak Sangawa 2017](https://doi.org/10.4312/as.2017.5.2.153-166)); in Ethiopia the language of
learning was Ge'ez, "a now extinct Semitic language", and "limited to the Bible and other" religious texts (Lanza and
Woldemariam 2014, PDF p. 15; partial; journal page [unverified]) ([Lanza and Woldemariam 2014](https://doi.org/10.14426/mm.v1i1.1457)).
The non-Western notes propose this as a new error. I treat it as a sub-case of 4: the move is still custody of the
medium, and when the register keeps a reader class out (kanbun, Ge'ez) it overlaps 9 and B. **Custody by price** is
the second sub-case. Hewitt's history (lib. card `hewitt2013dawn`, via a review) finds the British press taxes mixed in
purpose — revenue, suppression, trade interest — and the card judges the price-as-gate reading "real but mixed in
purpose". The only contemporary on file is a producer's cost complaint, Charles Knight's "fifty thousand pounds paper
duty" (Collet 1899, p. 39; partial; volume unclear) ([Collet 1899](https://archive.org/details/historytaxesonk00collgoog)),
so this sub-case still needs a reader-side source. Contemporaries: printers and booksellers opposed the 1559 Index
(Becker, Pino and Vidal-Robert 2021, p. 8, paraphrasing De Bujanda; read, working paper); Milton's *Areopagitica* sits
in the library, not re-read; Müteferrika's own 1727 treatise argued the press's benefits to the Sultan, an objection
from within (Coşgel et al., p. 16; Swanick 2016, p. 281) ([Swanick 2016](https://doi.org/10.33137/pbsc.v52i1.22262)).

### 5. Notice she cannot answer; the presumption of reading

REVIEW.md reported "nothing new beyond the run's cards", which undersells the library. **This error has the
best-documented contemporary objection in the whole set.** Las Casas called the Requerimiento "cosa es de reir, ó de
llorar por mejor decir" and asked what force a command has "que se hace á gente que la lengua en que se dice no
entiende" (*Historia de las Indias*, ed. 1876, Bk III ch. 57, pp. 162–163; lib. card `lascasas1876historia`); "creer
que entendieron ellos cosa del requerimiento es falsísimo" (ch. 59, p. 167). Oviedo, an officer on the 1514
expedition, told Pedrarias: "estos indios no quieren escuchar la teología deste requirimiento, ni vos tenes quien se la
dé á entender" (*Historia general*, tomo III, Lib. XXIX cap. VII, p. 31; lib. card `oviedo1853historia`). The device of
**literacy demanded in a language the person does not speak** belongs here and under 10, not as a new error: in the
Requerimiento it made notice unanswerable, while in Natal it disguised a racial target (Lake, lib. card
`lake2006mississippi`, pp. 213, 221).

### 6. The oversold promise

UNESCO/UNDP's own 1976 evaluation found EWLP's framers did not assess earlier successful campaigns, and human capital
theory "implies that people can escape from poverty by raising their education level, without any need to transform
the social system" (Boughton 2023, pp. 22, 30; read) ([Boughton 2023](https://doi.org/10.35847/bboughton.5.2.20)). The new
notes add three voices. Kalantzis and Cope write that mass literacy "had to regulate inequality in new ways. It did this
through the ideology of opportunity" (2024, preprint p. 4; partial). Paik's monograph "challenges the triumphant
narrative of modern secular education" for Dalits (2014; abs.). Gluschenko's English abstract claims that countries
copying the Soviet scheme without social change saw people lose "their newly-acquired skills quickly" (2015; read)
([Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)). Tschurenev and Mhaskar record a turn in
anti-caste opinion: writers who "welcomed the new educational opportunities" in the 1850s were "highly critical of
colonial education policy in the 1880s" (2021; abs.) ([Tschurenev and Mhaskar 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/00309230.2021.1920986)).
That is disappointment recorded in its own time, but **no named individual's quoted objection to the promise itself is
on file**; the strongest contemporary remains an institutional evaluation.

### 7. Capture by intermediaries

Padwal's Brahmans "conjure fake papers to plunder people's homes" (in Jaywant 2022, p. 388), and for subalterns
"writing meant more often than not taxes, punishment, and forced labor" (Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024, p. 22). Phule
now supplies the institutional version: "Perhaps the most glaring tendency of the Government system of high class
education has been the virtual monopoly of all higher offices under them by the Brahmins" (*Slavery*, Preface, p. 23),
and "not a single such Brahmin officer has been able to educate and equip even a single Shudra student" (p. 77). Phule
also quotes an unnamed English writer who asks whether graduates "have they kept their knowledge to themselves, as a
personal gift, not to be soiled by contract with the ignorant vulgar?" (pp. 22–23; "contract" as printed, probably for
"contact"; [second-hand]). Rao (2018; abs.) argues that "The colonial government used these terms to actually
strengthen the structures of the traditional hierarchy", Minto's 1811 Minute "strengthening and empowering the
priestly class" ([Rao 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0973184918807812)). Coşgel et al. make the Ottoman ulema the
intermediary that print threatened: "The religious authorities would have lost their monopoly in the transmission of
knowledge" (WP pp. 19–20), and by the eighteenth century "a virtually closed aristocracy of the 'ulamā' had come into
being" (Repp, quoted p. 31; [second-hand]). Kalantzis and Cope name the priests who served "as interlocutors between
mystically inaccessible ruling text and popular belief" (preprint p. 4). Tokugawa village headmen show the intermediary
made by taxation: they "had to acquire reading and writing skills on the level of their samurai supervisors" to run tax
paperwork (Hmeljak Sangawa 2017, p. 154, citing Rubinger). The present-day form is on record: under the EU's 2022 Code
of Practice, "the companies that are creating the problem in the first place are supposed to also be part of the
solution by contributing to design 'tools to improve media literacy'" (Oleart and Flores Moleon 2026, §4; read; section
locators only) ([Oleart and Flores Moleon 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/socsci15070430)). Contemporaries: Padwal 1861 and
Phule 1873.

### 8. Blame and stigma

The carried evidence is Quigley's "age-old prejudice towards those with lower levels of formal education" (2021, p. 43;
read) ([Quigley 2021](https://doi.org/10.35847/bquigley.3.3.41)) and Kozol's "begun in generosity, carried out in
condescension, and concluded in benign defeat" (in Boughton 2023, p. 29). Soviet planners blamed Kazakh deficits on
"the 'backwardness' of Kazakhs, by overstressing the 'cultural backwardness' for being bespis'mennye narody (people
without scripts)" (Toimbek 2023, p. 25). Ferguson and Suzuki ask for attention to "the negatively charged side of the
literacy/illiteracy dyad" (2015; abs.) ([Ferguson and Suzuki 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/lic3.12281)), and Vee
traces how in medieval England "those who could not read text began to be recast as 'illiterate'" (2013, p. 51; lib.
card `vee2013understanding`). Two sub-cases fit here. **Stigma on the competent** inverts the error: in late imperial
China talented women "were often deemed as 'a moral stigma'" (You and Nussey 2022, MS p. 6, quoting Lee 1995; read,
author manuscript; [second-hand]) ([You and Nussey 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09540253.2022.2123897)), and Ferguson
finds "the image of the skeptical or resistant female" in male-authored texts (*Dido's Daughters*, p. 96, via
Goldstein 2004, p. 122; review read, book n/a; [second-hand]) ([Goldstein 2004](https://doi.org/10.33137/rr.v40i4.9049)).
The evidence is thin — one secondary paper and one review — so it stays a sub-case. **Denial of demand** claims the
excluded do not want the competence: headmaster Kunte told the Hunter Commission that admission was "a groundless
agitation caused by sentimental English officials and unpractical native reformers", and the Commission rejected the
claim with enrolment and night-school data (para. 588). It could equally sit under 11. A weak addition comes from
Kay-Shuttleworth (1861), who says the Revised Code would "turn out dull scholars, sluggards, and truants, though the
fault might be in the want of local civilisation and home training" [OCR mended; attribution rests on the archive.org
record; the sentence's subject lies outside the extract] ([Kay-Shuttleworth 1861](https://archive.org/details/lettertoearlgra00shugoog)).
Contemporaries: Kozol 1980; Feng Menglong and Yuan Mei "strongly criticised the distorted connotation of 'ignorance is
women's virtue' and the stigmatisation of talented women" (You and Nussey, MS p. 6; [second-hand], no primary text read).

### 9. Restriction by reader class

Literacy "was one of the distinguishing qualities of aristocracy" (Fischer, in Quigley 2021, p. 43), and Alonso de
Cartagena defended withholding study from labourers (Castillo Gómez and Martínez 2024, p. 25). The colonial Indian
form was **downward filtration**. Sir Erskine Perry, President of the Bombay Board from 1843 to 1852, favoured "leaving
the education of the masses to the indirect influence of the downward filtration policy, holding that it was better to
concentrate on the higher education of the few"; in Madras in 1841 the University Board's President held that "the
light must touch the mountain tops before it could pierce to the levels and depths" (Hunter 1883, ch. II; chapter of
the Madras passage unresolved). In Tokugawa Japan the official schools "catered to the upper military class of the
samurai", and "classes below the samurai (peasants, merchants and artisans) had little access to formal education"
(Hmeljak Sangawa 2017, pp. 153, 155). Most Tudor women "were excluded from male centres of learning—the grammar schools,
universities and Inns of Court" (Daybell 2005; abs.) ([Daybell 2005](https://doi.org/10.1080/00467600500313989)).

The **licensed canon** is the sub-case in which the reader is admitted but the corpus is chosen by class and sex.
The 1543 Act for the Advancement of True Religion reversed the 1540 Great Bible's address to "all manner of persons,
men, women": "Reading the Bible in English was prohibited outright for women, artificers, journeymen, serving[men]…"
(Sanders and Ferguson 2002; abs.) ([Sanders and Ferguson 2002](https://doi.org/10.3167/001115702782352169)). Sima Guang
(1019–1086) held that "it is not appropriate to teach women making poems and playing vulgar music" but favoured
teaching them the Confucian classics (You and Nussey 2022, MS p. 5; [second-hand]). Contemporaries: Wharton in the
Commons, 13 June 1807, asked whether clergy should "hold a critical examination in order to ascertain who were fit to
be exalted to the counting-house, and who were only fit for the plough?" (HC Deb vol 9 c. 801; lib. card
`hansard1807parochial`); Du Bois reports, to reject it, the view that "an education that encourages aspiration ... is
the privilege of white men and the danger and delusion of black" (*Souls of Black Folk*, 1903, ch. VI; partial; no
page numbers; elision in the notes) ([Du Bois 1903](https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/cache/epub/408/pg408.txt)); and
Wollstonecraft's remedy was schooling "absolutely free and open to all classes" (ch. XII).

### 10. Competence as a cover

Congress understood by 1869 that the test could disfranchise "based on race while seeming to do so on the basis of
literate ability" (Branch 2015, p. 46), and the US model spread to tests "targeting Indians in South Africa and Asians
in Australia" (p. 62). Natal went "one step further" because its targets were "perfectly well able to read and write in
their own language", and Chamberlain recommended the Natal Act "as a model to the Australians in 1901" (Lake, lib. card
`lake2006mississippi`, p. 221). China supplies a sub-case, **the open meritocratic gate**, in which formal openness
legitimates near-total failure. Magone summarises a historiography in which "almost anyone from among the empire's male
population could sit", while "only a couple of hundred out of a million or often more examinees attained final
examination success" (2014; abs.) ([Magone 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780199920082-0078)). Elman asks whether
classical training served "as a gatekeeper to keep non-elites out" and answers that "true social mobility (i.e., peasants
becoming officials) was never the goal of the imperial state"; peasants, traders and artisans, "over 90 percent of the
population, were not among those 100 annual or 50,000 total palace graduates between 1371 and 1904" (2014; abs.)
([Elman 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004279278_009)). Jiang and Kung find that ancestors' official rank predicts
palace ranking and that "the civil examination system transmitted hidden advantages among those endowed with more than
just human capital" (2020; abs.) ([Jiang and Kung 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0097700420914529)); Zhang and Zheng,
on 12,427 Ming graduates, find the palace exam assessed "organizational fit in line with an emperor's political
calculations" (2023; abs.) ([Zhang and Zheng 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/cars.12452)). The ladder view contests this
reading; see below. Contemporary: Wilson's 1917 veto rejects the test because "Tests of quality and of purpose can not
be objected to on principle, but tests of opportunity surely may be" (lib. card `wilson1917message`).

### 11. The self-justifying gate

Scofield in 1866 — "They debased him by law to fit him for slavery, and justified slavery because he was debased" — and
Shellabarger in 1869 named the loop (Branch 2015, pp. 59, 60), and Naidu measured it: disenfranchisement cut Black
teacher-child ratios "by 10-23%, with no significant effects on white" (2012, abstract; read, working paper)
([Naidu 2012](https://doi.org/10.3386/w18129)). The new notes add **deferral** as the temporal sub-case. Mill set the
standard in 1861: "Justice demands, even when the suffrage does not depend on it, that the means of attaining these
elementary acquirements should be within the reach of every person", and "universal teaching must precede universal
enfranchisement" (*Considerations on Representative Government*; partial; chapter heading [unverified])
([Mill 1861](https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/cache/epub/5669/pg5669.txt)). Mill is a designer stating the condition the
self-justifying gate breaks, not an objector. Du Bois objected to the deferral Washington proposed: "Mr. Washington
distinctly asks that black people give up, at least for the present, three things,— First, political power, Second,
insistence on civil rights, Third, higher education of Negro youth" (*Souls*, ch. III). Mill's condition separates a
just deferral from a self-justifying one.

### 12. Selective provision by usefulness

Donors abandoned universal adult provision for "a selective approach, prioritizing only those ... most likely to
contribute to more rapid economic development" (Boughton 2023, p. 22), and Colombia spent 0.988 pesos per non-indigenous
person on primary schooling against 0.047 per indigenous person (España-Eljaiek et al. 2023, Table 4, p. 26; read)
([España-Eljaiek et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.33231/j.ihe.2023.03.003)). The Cape made the design explicit:
"coloured and African children were to be fitted to be employees and labourers, white children were to be educated to
employ and to lead" (Duff 2010, quoted in Fourie et al. 2014, WP pp. 5–6; [second-hand]). Verwoerd called it an
"absurdity" to teach average Black pupils academic subjects; "they should only be taught skills deemed to be useful in
their future life" (Moore's paraphrase, 2017, p. 119), and Moore reports a "Marxist school" holding that "securing a
cheap, unskilled labour force was already on the agenda" before 1953 (abstract, p. 106). Women's mission schooling in
German East Africa offered "vocational training for women consisted of domestic skills, such as sewing and knitting",
aimed at "creating Christian housewives" (Montgomery 2017, pp. 234, 229). **Error 12 now has a named contemporary.** Du
Bois accepted industrial training — "Teach workers to work,—a wise saying; ... Teach thinkers to think" (ch. VI) — and
objected to its being the whole of provision for one race. Against Bantu Education the *Christian Recorder* (August
1954) called it "not education at all", and the Methodist J. B. Webb called it "ethically indefensible and wrong from a
Christian point of view" (Moore 2017, p. 120). Du Bois was arguing against a Black leader's strategy as well as white
policy, so his objection is partly an argument inside the community.

### 13. Literacy taught for docility

The Bristol adult schools promised learners would "practise meekness, Christian fortitude, and resignation" (Pole
1816, in Quigley 2021, p. 43), and mission schooling emphasised "acculturation into Western values, the elimination of
native languages" (España-Eljaiek et al. 2023, p. 23). **Soviet likbez is the strongest non-Western case.** The first
1918 decree was titled "О мобилизации грамотных и организации пропаганды советского строя" ("On the mobilisation of
the literate and the organisation of propaganda for the Soviet order" [my translation]), and Gluschenko writes that the
campaign "не предполагала жесткого разделения между собственно обучением и политическим воспитанием (пропагандой)"
("did not presuppose a strict separation between instruction proper and political upbringing (propaganda)" [my
translation]), done "совершенно открыто и осознанно" ("entirely openly and deliberately" [my translation]) (2015,
pp. 250, 247, 249). Lenin's 1921 line — "Безграмотный человек стоит вне политики, его сначала надо научить азбуке"
("An illiterate person stands outside politics; first he must be taught the ABC" [my translation]) — appears on p. 247
[second-hand]. In Kazakhstan, likpunkts "came with ideological 'stuffing'", the sixth month covering "the dictatorship
of the proletariat" (Toimbek 2023, p. 30), and Toimbek concludes that the programme aimed at "training citizens loyal to
the regime" (p. 33). The Cape tradition did the same with readers that taught "self-help, punctuality, industriousness,
benevolence, truthfulness, thrift and obedience" (Kallaway 2022, p. 63; partial)
([Kallaway 2022](https://doi.org/10.17159/2223-0386/2022/n28a2)). Rutz finds that for German Catholic girls "the
acquisition of literacy by the female population was not an end in itself", serving "denominational, gender- and
class-specific socialisation" (2011; abs.) ([Rutz 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00309230.2011.603344)); elite Song and
Ming girls learned to read and write "in order to better understand and embody morality" (You and Nussey 2022, MS p. 5).
Kalantzis and Cope quote Graff's schooled "controllable, docile, respectful workforce, willing and able to follow
orders" (preprint p. 4; [second-hand]).

**Error 13 now has affected-person contemporaries.** Zitkala-Ša, a Yankton Dakota pupil and later teacher, wrote: "For
the white man's papers I had given up my faith in the Great Spirit. For these same papers I had forgotten the healing
in trees and brooks" ("An Indian Teacher Among Indians", *American Indian Stories*; partial; imprint and first-printing
dates [unverified]) ([Zitkala-Ša](https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/cache/epub/10376/pg10376.txt)). Her objection targets the
terms on which literacy arrived, not reading itself. Du Bois reports, to reject it, the claim "that the Negro can
survive only through submission" (ch. III). Kazakh Alash intellectuals called the changes a "method of Russification
of the Kazakh language and writing" (Toimbek 2023, p. 26, via Sembayev 1962; [second-hand]). The Lovett and Collins
*Chartism* (1840) objection, the obvious British working-class case, was n/a (HTTP 401).

### 14. Mismeasured progress

Adult literacy rates can rise "even in situations where not a single illiterate adult was made literate" (Barakat 2016,
MS p. 3; read, author manuscript) ([Barakat 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2016.06.015)). REVIEW.md judged this
a failure of evaluation, ethical only when an institution uses the measure to claim success. **Matthew Arnold supplies
that institution and that objection.** Robert Lowe introduced the Revised Code in 1862: "If it is not cheap it shall be
efficient; if it is not efficient it shall be cheap" (HC Deb 13 Feb 1862 vol 165 c. 229; partial)
([Hansard 1862](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1862/feb/13/education-the-revised-code-o-regulations)).
Arnold, as HM Inspector, reported in 1867 that "it is now found possible, by ingenious preparation, to get children
through the Revised Code examination in reading, writing, and ciphering, without their really knowing how to read,
write, or cipher" (General Report for 1867, p. 124; partial; page from OCR running heads)
([Arnold 1889](https://archive.org/details/reportsonelement00arnouoft)), and in 1869 called the examinations "a game of
mechanical contrivance in which the teachers will and must more and more learn how to beat us" (p. 136; [OCR mended]).
Kay-Shuttleworth's 1861 letter reports managers' objection that the Code "discourages it because it cannot test it, and
therefore excludes it from all aid" [OCR mended; printed page not located; attribution from the archive.org record].
Lowe and Arnold make a matched designer–objector pair inside one institution. Fitzpatrick judges it "сомнительно"
("doubtful" [my translation]) that Soviet campaigns "прибавили нечто существенное к естественному приросту" ("added
anything substantial to the natural growth" [my translation]) (Fitzpatrick 1979, p. 169, quoted in Gluschenko 2015,
p. 251; [second-hand]). A 2001 letter in the *Journal of Asian Studies*, evidently by Unger though the record names no
author [unverified], says "the government routinely inflated literacy statistics" in prewar Japan
([JAS 2001](https://doi.org/10.2307/2700022)).

Two sub-cases belong here. The **gated instrument** builds the gate into the measure. "the criterion of measuring
literacy by the signature is biased against women of various ranks as well as against poor men" (Ferguson and Suzuki
2015; abs.). The 1849 Cape census was ordered because missions "were seen as withholding labour from the farmers", and
it defined literacy as reading "a few verses" from "a copy of the Bible in Dutch", which "would have ruled out those who
were literate in isiXhosa" (Fourie et al. 2014, WP pp. 6–7, fn 30). The caste-and-colonial notes propose this "census as
labour surveillance" as a new error; it fits better here, because what it adds is the measure's purpose and its
built-in standard. **Recitation without comprehension** is the second sub-case: Tokugawa pupils "learned to 'read' the
texts by memorising them rather than by understanding the actual words" (Hmeljak Sangawa 2017, p. 162, citing Ingulsrud
and Allen). It becomes an ethical error where an institution certifies it, as Arnold's case shows; for Ethiopian church
schools Yigezu disputes the charge, calling the memorisation reading "simplistic and shallow" (2023, p. 90; read)
([Yigezu 2023](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003424956-8)).

### B. Boundary: compulsion and an enforced standard

Standard-language policy works as "a gatekeeping mechanism" and legitimises "language policing, punishment and
surveillance" in present-day England (Cushing 2020, pp. 322–323; read) ([Cushing 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/07908318.2020.1840578)).
The 1919 Soviet decree made literacy "не правом, а именно обязанностью" ("not a right but precisely a duty" [my
translation]) for everyone aged 6 to 50, with sanctions "вплоть до уголовной ответственности" ("up to criminal
liability" [my translation]) (Gluschenko 2015, p. 250). Australian inquiries found schooling "a site of surveillance
facilitating child removal" (Keynes and Marsden 2026; abs.) ([Keynes and Marsden 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/14767724.2026.2666230)).
Zitkala-Ša gives the boundary case its first affected-person voice: "now I was only one of many little animals driven by
a herder", and at the cutting of her braids, "Then I lost my spirit" ("School Days of an Indian Girl", ch. II). The
**displacement of oral or vernacular competence** overlaps this entry; an objection to writing as such would break the
paper's guardrail that the critique concerns design and allocation, not the competence.

## Three candidates earn their own numbers; the rest fold into existing errors

The five notes disagree on how far to extend the list. The objections note recommends no new number and three
sub-entries; the women's, caste and non-Western notes each propose new errors. I keep three as distinct because each
names a design move none of the fifteen covers and each has a contemporary who objected at the time. The rest are
devices or forms of existing errors.

### 15. NEW: Protective withholding

The institution denies the competence and presents the denial as good for the person denied. Chen Jiru (1558–1639),
the first recorded author of "ignorance is women's virtue", held that women who "enjoy reading poems and novels, which
could raise immoral thoughts… Thus, they are rather remaining illiterate" (You and Nussey 2022, MS p. 5; [second-hand]).
Settler-colonial schooling in Australasia was "directly imbricated in liberal, progressive, 'protective' and
humanitarian responses" (McLeod and Paisley 2023; abs.) ([McLeod and Paisley 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/0046760x.2023.2251928)).
The error differs from 2 because the wrong lies in the pretext, and from 13 because 13 shapes the competence it gives
while protective withholding justifies giving none. Wollstonecraft attacked the pretext directly — "kept in ignorance
under the specious name of innocence" (ch. II); "Without knowledge there can be no morality! Ignorance is a frail base
for virtue!" (ch. IV) — and Feng Menglong and Yuan Mei did so within the Confucian order [second-hand].

**Rationing competence to manage expectations** is a sub-case. Verwoerd told Parliament that "Racial relations could
not improve so long as the wrong kind of education was given to Natives by persons who, by instilling into them false
expectations, turned them into frustrated people who expected more than their country could provide" (Moore 2017,
p. 119, via *Keesing's* 1953; [second-hand]). The caste notes treat it as a separate error. It shares 15's structure —
the learner's own good (here, freedom from frustration) justifies withholding — and adds a second reason, the
order's unwillingness to meet the hope. The 1953 objectors (Webb, the *Christian Recorder*, the *Rand Daily Mail*,
United Party MP P. A. Moore) answered it at the time. Contemporary Black objections (the ANC questionnaire, Shepherd
1955, Blamires 1955) were n/a.

### 16. NEW: Taxed for one's own exclusion

The excluded pay for provision that others capture. Phule: "That Government should expend profusely a large portion of
revenue thus raised, on the education of the higher classes, for it is these only who take advantage of it, is anything
but just or equitable" (*Slavery*, Preface, p. 22); closing the department would be "a great boon to us … for, in that
case, the Shudras would be free from (the burden of) taxation" (p. 76). The Hunter Commission conceded the point: low
castes "contribute to the local cesses which support the State primary schools, they have a claim to some return for
their contributions" (para. 587). It differs from 12 because the wrong is the extraction, not the selection. A sub-case
is **paid but voiceless**: Kazakh communities built, repaired and heated schools, yet "such assistance from the
population did not mean they could influence the course or content of literacy programs" (Toimbek 2023, p. 29). The
evidence is thin — two sources from one presidency plus one Kazakh case — and the caste notes found no comparative
fiscal-incidence study of colonial education cesses by caste.

### 17. NEW: The accommodated veto

The rule opens the school to all, and the state yields to the dominant group's boycott in practice. The Court of
Directors' Despatch No. 58 of 1858 declared government institutions "open to all classes", in a case arising from the
local government's "refusal … to interfere in the case of a low caste boy who was denied admission" (Hunter 1883,
para. 589). The Commission recorded what the veto looked like: at Calicut, Nayars and Tiyars "used to waylay the boys as
they went to school and snatch their [books] out of their hands"; at Dharwar, when low-caste boys entered the high
school, "a large number of Brahman boys seceded at once" (para. 588 [OCR]); and "even where their right is admitted in
theory by local sentiment, low-caste boys actually receive few or none of the advantages of education" (para. 202
[OCR]). The Commission named the motive: "the desire of the upper classes to keep the low castes in a state of
subjection and [servility]" (para. 588 [OCR]). Nambissan attributes the narrowness of public schooling to "the strong
opposition of dominant castes … as well as ambivalences and compromises of the colonial state" (2020; abs.)
([Nambissan 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0973184920946966)). In Australia, settler school strikes led departments to
respond "by adapting school systems to allow the exclusion of Aboriginal students" (Marsden 2026; abs.)
([Marsden 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/14490854.2026.2624731)). Ethiopian church schools show a softer form: "In
principle, boys and girls ... are eligible to enrol in church schools. But girls seldom join the school due to cultural
perception that they are destined for household activities" (Yigezu 2023, p. 93). The veto differs from 1 because the
institution's own rule is open; the gate belongs to a third party the institution accommodates. The Commission itself
is the contemporary witness, and it also foresaw a loophole: schools reclassified as "special" to escape open admission
[OCR].

## Five claims are contested, and the paper should state both sides

**The Ottoman print "ban".** The case for a ban rests on the reported decrees of 1485 and 1515. Swanick (2016; read)
grants "a complete absence of primary archival corroboration" yet judges that, given millet printing in Hebrew, Greek
and Armenian, "it becomes even more plausible that the fermans were issued" (p. 278). Coşgel, Miceli and Rubin argue
that the effect shows without the decree: "some type of severe restriction on printing in Ottoman Turkish was in
place"; the 1727 permission said print would be "unveiled like a bride and will not again be hidden"; and "The only
non-manuscript books in Arabic script published before the eighteenth century were imported from abroad" (WP p. 16).
They reject technical difficulty — the script's challenges "were not insurmountable" (p. 17) — and reject low demand as
sufficient: "Despite relatively low demand for books and rates of literacy, the expected effect of mass printing on the
society's surplus ... was still positive" (pp. 18–19). The case against the ban narrative is equally strong on the
documents. Coşgel et al. concede that "The authenticity of the decree that officially banned the printing press ... has
not been well-established"; Efdaleddin Bey challenged it in 1916 because "Ottoman archives do not house edicts issued
prior to 1553", and "no such edicts have since been uncovered", though "the secondary literature has for the most part
accepted the presence of the edict as a matter of established fact" (pp. 15–16). The low-demand view has its own
evidence: "wages and literacy rates were significantly lower in the Ottoman Empire than in Western Europe, indicating a
lower demand for books" (p. 18), with literacy "around 2-3 percent" in the early nineteenth century — a figure the
authors say no "systematic studies" can confirm (p. 19 fn 18). The religious and aesthetic view holds that "Mechanical
presses threatened the sanctity of the written word" (Swanick, p. 281). Even the monopoly mechanism has a rival inside
Coşgel et al.: the ulema may have worried about "maintaining cohesion in their ranks after the advent of the press, not
the loss of their monopoly" (fn 19, p. 21). Schwartz 2017 argues against the ban narrative; the notes take this from a
library card at abstract level, not re-read ([Schwartz 2017](https://doi.org/10.1353/bh.2017.0000)). The scribes'-guild
explanation appears in older literature but in no text read. The paper can state as fact only the output record: no
Muslim Arabic-type printing before 1727, 33 books in the eighteenth century, religious titles barred until 1802 (WP
p. 30). "The sultans banned print" cannot stand as fact.

**The Chinese examination as ladder or gate.** Ho (1962) and Kracke (1947) held that the examination promoted mobility,
and Yuan and Zhang (2025; abs. and excerpt) build on "the consensus among scholars that the imperial examination
facilitated social mobility" ([Yuan and Zhang 2025](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05366-x)). Elman calls "social
mobility" in Ho's sense an "anachronistic term", better described as "a 'healthy circulation' of lower and upper elites
via classical literacies" (2014; abs.). Jiang and Kung split the difference: the system "unwittingly facilitated
mobility" while transmitting hidden advantages (2020; abs.). Error 10's sub-case needs the Elman reading; the paper
should say the ladder view is a live position.

**Script as barrier.** Earlier scholarship treated character complexity as "an obstacle to mass literacy"; Kuzuoğlu
"profoundly complicates the phonocentric narrative", arguing the alphabetic order "de-skilled mental and clerical
laborers in China" (Zhao 2025, review; abs.) ([Zhao 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ahr/rhae543)). For Japan, the 2001
*JAS* letter [author unverified] argues that if complexity caused illiteracy, "correlations with social, economic, and
demographic variables could not arise". The Occupation, by contrast, held that the script had "predisposed them to
submit to the will of the militarists" (Nomoto 1993; abs.) ([Nomoto 1993](https://doi.org/10.1080/09386491.1993.11827042)).
Restriction by script is therefore a claim about who controlled a script, not about scripts as such.

**Likbez as control or as agency.** Gluschenko documents the fusion of literacy with propaganda, yet her English abstract
argues that the "lingua Sovietica" "allowed people to communicate with authorities and to achieve their specific goals
through playing with official notions and terms". Murtazina holds that "literacy" meant only ABC literacy, while
Fitzpatrick (1992) argues the campaign aimed at "новой идейной гегемонии" ("a new ideological hegemony" [my
translation]) (Gluschenko, pp. 247–248; [second-hand]). Learners gained a tool while the state shaped it; both claims
hold.

**Mission schooling as harm or as provision.** Frankema cautions that the impact of British policy "should not be
overstated. Until 1940, mission schools, mainly run by African converts, provided the bulk of education at extremely low
costs" (2012; abs.) ([Frankema 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/ereh/hes009)). Fourie et al. show the same Cape missions
raising literacy sharply: being born on a station meant a "27% higher likelihood" of literacy, and at Genadendal 66.60%
of teenagers could read against 23.75% of adults (WP pp. 14–15). Yigezu defends Ethiopian church pedagogy against the
memorisation charge. Every historian read treats literacy as neither liberating nor oppressive in itself, which keeps
the paper's critique on design and allocation.

## AI scholarship holds both halves of the argument but not the join

**I found no work that sets literacy's historical distribution errors against the AI-ethics movement's remedies —
disclosure, explanation, human oversight, AI literacy — one error at a time, or that uses Microsoft's or Nadella's
governance architecture as the case**, in Consensus (four queries), OpenAlex (six queries, top 12 each), Semantic
Scholar and Crossref DOI lookups, CORE (which returned a Cloudflare page) and one web search, all on 19 Nov 2026, added
to the earlier review's Consensus query and four OpenAlex queries of the same date. I found no scholarly critique of
Nadella's own "trust" framing or of Microsoft's agent-governance architecture in Consensus (one query) and two OpenAlex
queries on 19 Nov 2026. The gap queries did not target caste, women's or non-Western literacy by name, so the
statement covers those facets only as far as the general queries reached them. The ACM Digital Library (CHI, FAccT,
AIES contents), business-ethics journals by name, Google Scholar and Unpaywall went unsearched, and Scholar Gateway
returned ACCESS_DENIED.

The literature divides into a history side and a remedy side. On the history side, **Kalantzis and Cope (2024;
partial, EdArXiv preprint, pp. 1–9 read and the rest searched; pagination from the preprint, check against the RRQ
version)** go furthest: literacy "and its means of production have been as menacing in social practice as often as they
have been liberatory" (p. 2); the press served "as an instrument of power and to draw illiteracy as a line of
exclusion" (p. 4); literacy became "a vector of gender inequality … and colonialism" (p. 4). They carry this to AI —
LLMs "have privatized collective human intelligence in new ways" (p. 6); AI "is a new colonialism … the colonization of
social intelligence by these insurgent moguls of human meaning" (p. 9) — and answer with a commons: "We need to
reappropriate the general intellect for the general public" (p. 9). Their history covers errors 1, 4, 6 and 7 directly,
8 in part (the normal curve's labelling), and 2 and 3 only by implication. They do not treat disclosure, explanation,
oversight or AI literacy as remedies that repeat these errors, and they name no firm's governance framework. Kamperman
and Graff (2026; lib.) argue that technological literacy campaigns "repeat many of the conceptual errors of 19th- and
early 20th-century print literacy campaigns" (p. 36), yet their own remedy includes "Establish norms for disclosure"
(p. 37). Vee (2013; lib.) carries the gate and the stigma to programming, where "the power balance is once again
shifting toward those who are skilled" (pp. 54–55). Cheema (2026; abs.) finds software requirements "replacing colonial
English-language requirements" in a way that functions "identically", with Adobe as the firm
([Cheema 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/03063968261422606)); Migge and Schneider (2025; abs.) find "striking continuities"
from colonial language-making to AI language technology ([Migge and Schneider 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02389-5)).
Both draw on language policy, not literacy's distribution.

On the remedy side, Oleart and Flores Moleon (2026; read) argue that literacy works "as a technology of governance that
redistributes political responsibility from infrastructures and corporate actors towards individuals", and that AI
literacy serves "not only an educational function but also one of Big Tech legitimisation"; their historical parallels
are Hearst and the Luddites, not literacy. Srivastava (2026; partial) finds that "many Responsible AI initiatives remain
thin by demanding disclosures without systemic repair" (abstract) and analyses Microsoft's 2023 Azure human-rights
assessment: "Microsoft ducked behind the inert platform defense, long used by Uber, Airbnb, Facebook, and the like"
(p. 49), quoting the assessors' finding that "in no instances did the Assessors find evidence that Microsoft causes
human rights harms" (p. 48; [second-hand] from Foley Hoag 2023, which was not read)
([Srivastava 2026](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009680882)). That is the nearest scholarly critique of Microsoft's
framing, and it concerns the inert-platform defence, not the trust and oversight architecture; it treats literacy as a
remedy, not a precedent of harm. Nama (2026; read) locates agentic "literacy debt" with deployers — it "is incurred by
the organizations that deploy agents but paid by the users, patients, and citizens on whose behalf the agents act" (p. 1)
— notes that "The default for irreversible actions is to execute first and report after" (p. 7), and cites the EchoLeak
vulnerability in Microsoft 365 Copilot (p. 2) ([Nama 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.27396)); its precedent is
Ladson-Billings's education debt, not literacy history, and its remedy is transparency by design. Lund and Teel (2026;
lib.) find that unequal AI access "recapitulates longstanding epistemic injustices" with one sentence of literacy
history ([Lund and Teel 2026](https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v9i3.43263)); Paljor et al. (2026; abs.) warn of "displacing
responsibility for AI harms away from institutions and educational structures onto individual learners"
([Paljor et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796496.3811753)); Burrell (2016; lib.) finds end-user explanations give
"at best incomplete and at worst false reassurance". Rodríguez López et al. (2025; partial) run the other way, telling a
celebratory history of literacy and endorsing explainability as the remedy
([Rodríguez López et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.61530/redtis.vol9.n1.2025.254)). Trainor (2026; partial) reports Byrd
"extending Brandt's framework of literacy sponsorship to the age of AI", "less focused on systemic critique than on
systemic navigation" (pp. 63, 62) ([Trainor 2026](https://doi.org/10.21623/1.12.2.5)); Byrd's article was not read.

Two items remain open. **Nichols, Thrall, Quiros and Dixon-Román (2024)** was n/a by every route (Wiley HTTP 403,
Consensus blocked, no Wayback snapshot). Its abstract defines "speculative capture" as literacy education made
"uniquely susceptible" to AI platforms' "extrapolative prediction and algorithmic reasoning", which points away from
historical distribution errors, but whether it draws historical parallels is [unverified]
([Nichols et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.535)). Semantic Scholar lists it as hybrid open access under CC
BY-NC, so a browser download should work. **Pangrazio (2026)**, "The (im)possibility of AI literacy", was n/a (HTTP 403;
truncated abstract) ([Pangrazio 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2026.2615553)). Of the error categories, **one
found no counterpart in any AI work read: error 3, record over the voice.** Nama's invisible default action log is the
nearest, without history. None of the three new errors (15–17) appears in the AI literature found.

## Conclusion

The regional evidence changes the list's centre of gravity. The earlier fourteen errors were mostly things an
institution did to people directly — tested, withheld, recorded, censored. The three new errors are about how an
institution's formal fairness coexisted with exclusion: a rule open to all that yielded to a boycott, a public budget
funded by the people it left out, and a denial offered as protection. In each case the institution's own documents (the
1858 Despatch, the Hunter Commission's para. 587, Verwoerd's speech) stated the fair principle or the benign motive,
and contemporaries (Phule, Wollstonecraft, Webb) answered that the principle was not kept or the motive was a pretext.
That pattern bears on the paper's case more closely than the older errors do, because a responsible-AI framework is
also a formal statement of fairness that can coexist with the practice it disclaims.

For the gap claim, the defensible novelty is narrow and real. Kalantzis and Cope already argue that AI repeats
literacy's history of domination, and four or five papers already argue that AI literacy and disclosure shift
responsibility onto individuals. The open ground is the join: the claim that the remedy family itself reproduces named
historical distribution errors, tested error by error, with Microsoft's architecture as the case. Errors 3, 15, 16 and 17
have no counterpart in the AI works found, though the gap queries were written before 15–17 existed; a targeted search
for each is the step before the paper calls them its least-trodden contributions.
