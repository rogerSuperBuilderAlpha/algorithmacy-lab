## Q1. For each setting: what design choices restricted or shaped access, who benefited, and what evidence (including quantitative) do scholars give?

### Takeaway
In every setting, a small group held the medium, which was a script, a classical register, a press licence or a church language. The rest of the population met literacy either as an examination they almost always failed (China), as a recitation without comprehension (Tokugawa kanbun, Ge'ez church schooling) or, under the Soviets, as a duty imposed with sanctions and packaged with ideology. The strongest quantitative evidence comes from China (examination pass rates and the class origins of graduates) and from the Ottoman case (book counts after 1727).

### Cited Findings

**China: examinations and the classical script**
- Elman (2014) frames the gate in so many words. He asks, "Did the standard training program in the classical language serve as a gatekeeper to keep non-elites out?" He answers that "true social mobility (i.e., peasants becoming officials) was never the goal of the imperial state." Status: abstract only. — [Elman 2014, "Unintended Consequences of Classical Literacies for the Early Modern Chinese Civil Examinations," Brill, DOI 10.1163/9789004279278_009](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004279278_009)
- Quantities from the same abstract: over 90 percent of candidates failed. Perhaps 10 percent of the population (200–250 million in 1600) was classically literate. "peasants, traders, and artisans, who made up over 90 percent of the population, were not among those 100 annual or 50,000 total palace graduates between 1371 and 1904." Status: abstract only. — [Elman 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004279278_009)
- Elman's book (2013) reports that "Only one percent of candidates would complete the academic regimen" and that the examinations "served to tie the dynasty to the privileged gentry and literati classes--both ideologically and institutionally." Status: abstract only (Consensus record). — [Elman 2013, *Civil Examinations and Meritocracy in Late Imperial China*, Harvard UP, DOI 10.4159/harvard.9780674726048](https://doi.org/10.4159/harvard.9780674726048)
- Jiang and Kung (2020) test the ladder for 1796–1905. Ability and "the upper gentry family type" predict jinshi success, while direct proxies for wealth do not. At the palace stage, ancestors' official rank, used as a proxy for "cultural capital", predicts the final ranking. "the civil examination system transmitted hidden advantages among those endowed with more than just human capital." Status: abstract only. — [Jiang & Kung 2020, *Modern China*, DOI 10.1177/0097700420914529](https://doi.org/10.1177/0097700420914529)
- Zhang and Zheng (2023) studied 12,427 Ming graduates (1400–1580). They find that the palace exam assessed "organizational fit in line with an emperor's political calculations", and that sons of bureaucrats were favoured while "those from affluent families were discriminated against." Status: abstract only. — [Zhang & Zheng 2023, *Canadian Review of Sociology*, DOI 10.1111/cars.12452](https://doi.org/10.1111/cars.12452)
- Magone (2014) summarises a historiography that treats access as open "in principle, if not necessarily in practice": "almost anyone from among the empire's male population could sit." He also notes that "only a couple of hundred out of a million or often more examinees attained final examination success." The exclusion of women is built into the phrase "male population." Status: abstract only (Oxford Bibliographies). — [Magone 2014, DOI 10.1093/obo/9780199920082-0078](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780199920082-0078)

**China: script reform as a reply to the script-as-barrier view**
- Kuzuoğlu (2022) shows that the 1920s–30s mass-literacy reformers tried "to reduce the number of Chinese characters to a 'basic' set of around one thousand." That raised the question of "which characters, after all, were 'essential'", and the search for efficiency "quickly turned into an ideological inquiry into the politics of language." Status: abstract only. — [Kuzuoğlu 2022, *Modern Chinese Literature and Culture*, DOI 10.3366/mclc.2022.0003](https://doi.org/10.3366/mclc.2022.0003)
- Premaratne (2012): "The first Chinese script reform took place in 1956 and the first Japanese script reform in 1946, both for the purpose of simplifying the scripts to facilitate mass education." In the 1980s, "both countries backtracked." Status: abstract only. — [Premaratne 2012, *Current Issues in Language Planning*, DOI 10.1080/14664208.2012.754328](https://doi.org/10.1080/14664208.2012.754328)
- Premaratne (2014) charges both states with "indifference shown towards language rights and social justice issues caused by their respective policies" in the 2009 script measures. Status: abstract only. — [Premaratne 2014, DOI 10.1080/14664208.2014.979677](https://doi.org/10.1080/14664208.2014.979677)

**Ottoman Empire: script-specific restriction of print**
- Coşgel, Miceli and Rubin's restriction targeted a script, not a technology: "they banned printing in Arabic characters but allowed religious minorities to set up their own presses ... whereas Muslims were not allowed to publish the Qur'an or any other religious or secular text in Arabic characters" (WP p. 21 [pdf 23]). Jewish printers had a press in Istanbul by 1493 and Armenians by the 1560s (p. 17 [pdf 19]). Status: read in full (working paper 2010-02R, rev. Jan 2012). — [Coşgel, Miceli & Rubin 2012, *Journal of Comparative Economics*, DOI 10.1016/j.jce.2012.01.002; WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- The case for who benefited: printing would have weakened "the ability of religious authorities to confer legitimacy". "The religious authorities would have lost their monopoly in the transmission of knowledge and their power in convincing the public on the legitimacy of the ruler" (WP pp. 19–20 [pdf 21–22]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- Quantities after 1727. Müteferrika's press was licensed with conditions: "granting permission only to selected individuals, prohibiting publication in religious subjects, and appointing a committee of scholars to review" its output. "only 33 books were published" in the 18th century. The ban on Islamic subjects was "lifted in 1802". In 1831–41, six presses issued 278 books, 22% of them religious (WP p. 30 [pdf 32]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- Literacy: "around 2-3 percent, even as late as the beginning of the nineteenth century, compared to much higher rates ranging between 10 and 30 percent in Western Europe in the sixteenth century" (WP p. 19 fn 18 [pdf 21], citing Quataert 2000 and Baten and van Zanden 2008). The authors add that there are "no systematic studies that can confirm this reliably." — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- The ulema's closure: half of the 58 chief jurisconsults appointed between 1708 and 1839 came from 11 families (WP p. 31 [pdf 33], citing Zilfi 1988). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)

**Soviet likbez: mass access as a duty**
- The 26 Dec 1919 decree required everyone aged 6 to 50 who could not read or write to learn ("обязано обучиться грамоте на родном или русском языке, по желанию" — "is obliged to learn to read and write in their native language or Russian, as they choose"). In Gluschenko's words, mastery was "не правом, а именно обязанностью" ("not a right but precisely a duty"), with sanctions "вплоть до уголовной ответственности" ("up to criminal liability") for evasion (p. 250 [pdf 5]). Status: read in full (Russian). — [Gluschenko 2015, *Voprosy Obrazovaniya* no. 3, 246–282, DOI 10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Baseline figures: the 1897 census found "лишь 39% взрослых" literate outside Finland (p. 249 [pdf 4]). Fn 5 gives Elkina's competing 1897 figures: 24% overall, women 12.4%, men 35.8%, urban 52.3%, rural 19.6%, rural women 8.6%. The 1926 census found 51% of those over 9 illiterate (p. 251 [pdf 6]). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Kazakhstan: literacy in the KASSR at the start of the 1920s "did not exceed 2%". An archival report gives indigenous illiteracy as 95.9% (Toimbek p. 26 [pdf 8]). In 1921/22, schools fell on average by 60%, and by 86% in the Kostanay region (p. 31 [pdf 13]). Status: read in full. — [Toimbek 2023, in *The Steppe and Beyond*, Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)

**Ethiopia: Ge'ez and church custody**
- Lanza and Woldemariam (2014): "In previous centuries, literacy practices in Ethiopia were left to members of the elite and those belonging to religious communities of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church ... The language of learning was Ge'ez, a now extinct Semitic language, and it was limited to the Bible and other" religious texts (PDF p. 15; the journal pagination is unclear in the PDF, issue range 74–100, so verify). Status: partial (read the conclusion). — [Lanza & Woldemariam 2014, *Multilingual Margins* 1(1), DOI 10.14426/mm.v1i1.1457](https://doi.org/10.14426/mm.v1i1.1457)
- Yigezu (2023) on EOTC schools: "In principle, boys and girls as well as members of all ethnic groups and members of different classes are eligible to enrol in church schools. But girls seldom join the school due to cultural perception that they are destined for household activities" (p. 93 [pdf 5]). The course runs from age four to about 35 for the highest qualification (p. 93). Status: read in full. — [Yigezu 2023, Routledge chapter, DOI 10.4324/9781003424956-8](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003424956-8)

**Japan: Tokugawa status order and kanbun**
- Hmeljak Sangawa (2017): the official schools "catered to the upper military class of the samurai" and taught the Neo-Confucian canon in kanbun. "Up to the 19th century, classes below the samurai (peasants, merchants and artisans) had little access to formal education" (pp. 153, 155 [pdf 1, 3]). Village headmen nonetheless "had to acquire reading and writing skills on the level of their samurai supervisors" because they ran the paperwork of taxation (p. 154, citing Rubinger 2007). Status: read in full. — [Hmeljak Sangawa 2017, *Asian Studies* 5(2), 153–166, DOI 10.4312/as.2017.5.2.153-166](https://doi.org/10.4312/as.2017.5.2.153-166)
- The 1946 tōyō kanji list cut the official set to "about 1850" characters. The Occupation held that the script "had contributed to a supposed low rate of literacy among the Japanese and predisposed them to submit to the will of the militarists," and a national Literacy Test followed in 1948. Status: abstract only. — [Nomoto 1993, *Japanstudien*, DOI 10.1080/09386491.1993.11827042](https://doi.org/10.1080/09386491.1993.11827042)

### Inferences
- The Ottoman and Chinese cases add a design move the list lacks, **restriction by script**. The press was permitted for Hebrew and Armenian type and denied for Arabic type, so the restriction fell on the majority faith's sacred script while minorities printed freely. The classical register did the same work in China and in Tokugawa kanbun.
- China gives the cleanest case of a **self-justifying, open-in-principle gate**. Formal openness to "almost anyone" male legitimated a pass rate near 1%, and family cultural capital decided the final stage (Jiang and Kung). This is error 10 (competence as cover) in a meritocratic register.

### Gaps
- I could not reach Rawski's *Education and Popular Literacy in Ch'ing China* (1979) or any per-capita Qing literacy estimate through these searches. The figure of over 90% illiteracy appears only in a low-quality conference paper (Chen, Li and Zhan 2023, SHS Web of Conferences, authored from secondary schools), and I exclude it.
- Dore's and Rubinger's Tokugawa literacy estimates did not surface as readable texts. Rubinger 2017 (DOI 10.1515/9780824863975-004) has a record only, with no abstract.
- Ethiopia: I found no quantitative estimate of historical Ge'ez literacy, and no study of the Ethiopian state's deliberate restriction of literacy as opposed to church custody.

## Q2. Where is the claim contested? State the Ottoman print debate fairly (ban vs low demand vs scribal/clerical interests)

### Takeaway
The Ottoman debate has at least four positions. (a) A sultanic ban, the 1485/1515 decrees. (b) A ban whose decree text is lost but whose effect is visible in the record. (c) Low demand, through low literacy and low wages. (d) Interest-group resistance by the ulema, the scribes or the calligraphers. Coşgel, Miceli and Rubin hold (b) plus a legitimacy mechanism, and they explicitly reject (c) as sufficient. Schwartz 2017 (already carded, abstract only) argues against the ban narrative. China and Japan have their own contested claims: whether the examination produced mobility, and whether kanji themselves caused illiteracy.

### Cited Findings

**Ottoman: the decree is unattested**
- Coşgel et al. concede the decree's weakness at full strength. "The authenticity of the decree that officially banned the printing press ... has not been well-established" (WP p. 15 [pdf 17]). Efdaleddin Bey challenged it in 1916 because "Ottoman archives do not house edicts issued prior to 1553." "no such edicts have since been uncovered", yet "the secondary literature has for the most part accepted the presence of the edict as a matter of established fact" (p. 16 [pdf 18]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- Swanick (2016) agrees there is "a complete absence of primary archival corroboration" (p. 278 [pdf 10]). He still finds the decrees plausible: given millet printing in Hebrew, Greek and Armenian, "it becomes even more plausible that the fermans were issued" (p. 278). Status: read in full. — [Swanick 2016, *Papers of the Bibliographical Society of Canada* 52(1), 269–292, DOI 10.33137/pbsc.v52i1.22262](https://doi.org/10.33137/pbsc.v52i1.22262)

**Ottoman: the restriction is visible without the decree**
- "Regardless of questions about the authenticity ... other evidence suggests that some type of severe restriction on printing in Ottoman Turkish was in place." The 1727 permission says print would be "unveiled like a bride and will not again be hidden," and "The only non-manuscript books in Arabic script published before the eighteenth century were imported from abroad" (WP p. 16 [pdf 18]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)

**Ottoman: low demand, stated and rejected as sufficient**
- Coşgel et al. state the demand case themselves. "wages and literacy rates were significantly lower in the Ottoman Empire than in Western Europe, indicating a lower demand for books." They then reject it: "Despite relatively low demand for books and rates of literacy, the expected effect of mass printing on the society's surplus ... was still positive" (WP pp. 18–19 [pdf 20–21]). Demand for the Qur'an "was presumably high, regardless of the literacy rate" (pp. 17–18). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)

**Ottoman: technical difficulty, rejected**
- "While the peculiarities of the Arabic script presented unique challenges, they were not insurmountable"; the Qur'an was printed in Venice (WP p. 17 [pdf 19]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)

**Ottoman: religious and aesthetic objections, the calligraphy and sanctity view**
- Swanick: "Islamic religious documents are believed to contain the literal word of God ... Mechanical presses threatened the sanctity of the written word" (p. 281 [pdf 13]). Müteferrika used naskh and thuluth type "to lessen the foreignness of the printing press" (p. 274 [pdf 6]). — [Swanick 2016](https://doi.org/10.33137/pbsc.v52i1.22262)

**Ottoman: the ulema as a closed interest, and a concession**
- Coşgel et al. allow a rival mechanism in fn 19 (WP p. 21 [pdf 23]): "It is also possible that the religious authorities were worried with maintaining cohesion in their ranks after the advent of the press, not the loss of their monopoly." — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- They quote Repp: by the 18th century "a virtually closed aristocracy of the 'ulamā' had come into being which had little to do with the traditional roles of the 'ulamā' as transmitters of Islamic learning" (WP p. 31 [pdf 33]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf)
- Schwartz 2017, "Did Ottoman Sultans Ban Print?" (Book History, DOI 10.1353/bh.2017.0000), is carded at abstract level in the run library and was not re-read here. Its position must be taken from that card, not from this note.

**China: was the examination a ladder?**
- The ladder view: Ho (1962) and Kracke (1947) held that the examination promoted mobility, as reported in Yuan and Zhang (2025), which builds on "the consensus among scholars that the imperial examination facilitated social mobility." Status: abstract and excerpt only. — [Yuan & Zhang 2025, *HSSC*, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05366-x](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05366-x)
- The counter-view: Elman calls "social mobility" in Ho's sense an "anachronistic term" better described as "a 'healthy circulation' of lower and upper elites via classical literacies" (abstract). — [Elman 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004279278_009). Jiang and Kung find the system "unwittingly facilitated mobility" while it transmitted hidden advantages (abstract). — [Jiang & Kung 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0097700420914529)
- Magone records the historiographic swing. Modern historians blamed the literary curriculum for China's failure to develop science, and this "paradigm has become gradually more nuanced" (abstract). — [Magone 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780199920082-0078)

**China: is the character script the barrier?**
- Zhao's review of Kuzuoğlu's *Codes of Modernity* reports that earlier scholarship saw character complexity as "an obstacle to mass literacy". Kuzuoğlu "profoundly complicates the phonocentric narrative": the alphabetic information order "de-skilled mental and clerical laborers in China." Status: abstract only (review). — [Zhao 2025, *AHR*, DOI 10.1093/ahr/rhae543](https://doi.org/10.1093/ahr/rhae543)

**Japan: kanji and literacy**
- In a published letter to *JAS* (2001), the author of a book on Occupation script reform — evidently Unger, though the record names no author, so confirm before citing — argues against kanji-complexity as the cause. If complexity were the cause, "correlations with social, economic, and demographic variables could not arise." He adds that "the government routinely inflated literacy statistics" (crediting Hane) and names "hentaigana, anachronistic kana spellings, multiple kanji readings, unregulated okurigana usage, and the coexistence of diverse writing styles as additional impediments to full literacy." Status: abstract and full-text snippet only, author unconfirmed. — [Communications to the Editor, *JAS* 2001, DOI 10.2307/2700022](https://doi.org/10.2307/2700022)

### Inferences
- A fair statement of the Ottoman case runs as follows. No decree text survives, and the "ban" story was challenged as early as 1916. Yet the output record (no Muslim Arabic-type printing before 1727, 33 books in the whole 18th century, religious titles barred until 1802) shows a regulated restriction, whatever its legal form. The live dispute is about the mechanism: legitimacy (Coşgel et al.), sanctity and calligraphic aesthetics (Swanick's sources), low demand, or ulema self-interest. The paper should not write "the sultans banned print" as fact.

### Gaps
- Sabev's 2025 chapter "The Adoption of Printing in the Ottoman World" (DOI 10.1515/9783111386621-001) is listed as open access, but the De Gruyter PDF returned HTTP 202 with an empty body, so it was not accessible. Nemeth 2022, on craft and typography (DOI 10.1515/9783110776485-002), failed the same way. Both bear on the "craft and scribes" explanation.
- The scribes'-guild explanation for the Ottomans (as opposed to Paris) is asserted in older literature but not documented in any text I read.

## Q3. Did Soviet likbez combine mass access with political control? Evidence and locators

### Takeaway
Yes, and openly. The founding decrees fused literacy and propaganda in their titles. The 1919 decree made literacy a legal duty with sanctions up to criminal liability. Likpunkt syllabi were political curricula. Gluschenko's point is that the fusion was "совершенно открыто и осознанно" ("entirely open and deliberate"). Fitzpatrick, as reported by Gluschenko, doubts the campaigns added much beyond the trend already under way. In Kazakhstan, local communities paid for the schools but had no say over content.

### Cited Findings
- The first 1918 decree was titled "О мобилизации грамотных и организации пропаганды советского строя" ("On the mobilisation of the literate and the organisation of propaganda for the Soviet order"). Gluschenko reads the title as containing "обе составляющие советского просветительского проекта" ("both components of the Soviet enlightenment project") (p. 250 [pdf 5]). Status: read in full. — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Lenin (1921, quoted on p. 247 [pdf 2]): "Безграмотный человек стоит вне политики, его сначала надо научить азбуке" ("An illiterate person stands outside politics; first he must be taught the ABC"). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- "Кампания по борьбе с неграмотностью не предполагала жесткого разделения между собственно обучением и политическим воспитанием (пропагандой)" ("The campaign against illiteracy did not presuppose a strict separation between instruction proper and political upbringing (propaganda)") (p. 247 [pdf 2]). The Soviet specificity lay not in the combination itself but in that it was done "совершенно открыто и осознанно" ("entirely openly and deliberately") (p. 249 [pdf 4]). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Partial literacy by design: Murtazina (2002), quoted on p. 247 [pdf 2], says that in likbez "под словом „грамотность" подразумевалась лишь азбучная грамотность" ("'literacy' meant only ABC literacy"). Western historians (Fitzpatrick 1992) dispute this narrow reading and argue that the campaign aimed at "новой идейной гегемонии" ("a new ideological hegemony") (pp. 247–248). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Compulsion: under the 1919 decree, literacy was a duty, with sanctions up to criminal liability (p. 250 [pdf 5]; see Q1). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- Mismeasured or overclaimed progress: Fitzpatrick (1979, p. 169), quoted on p. 251 [pdf 6], judges it "сомнительно, что советские кампании по борьбе с неграмотностью (за исключением, может быть, осуществленных в Красной армии) прибавили нечто существенное к естественному приросту" ("doubtful that Soviet campaigns ... added anything substantial to the natural growth") produced by late-tsarist mass schooling. — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- The English abstract argues that learners also gained agency. The new "lingua Sovietica" "allowed people to communicate with authorities and to achieve their specific goals through playing with official notions and terms." It also claims that countries copying the scheme without social change saw people lose "their newly-acquired skills quickly." — [Gluschenko 2015, English abstract](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- The likpunkt syllabus in Kazakhstan: "likpunkts ... came with ideological 'stuffing'". Month 1 covered socialist and Soviet states, month 3 "the separation of religion from the state and school and eradication of 'traditional prejudices'", and month 6 "the dictatorship of the proletariat" (Toimbek p. 30 [pdf 12], citing Fund 81, Cases 562 and 607). Status: read in full. — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- Party control of all agitation plans: "both the general plan of agitation work and the plans of individual campaigns should be approved by the party committees" (archival quote, Toimbek p. 27 [pdf 9]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- The locals paid and had no voice. "such assistance from the population did not mean they could influence the course or content of literacy programs". An attempt at a religious private school "stopped at the very beginning" (pp. 29–30 [pdf 11–12]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- Stigma: deficits were blamed on "the 'backwardness' of Kazakhs, by overstressing the 'cultural backwardness' for being bespis'mennye narody (people without scripts)" (p. 25 [pdf 7]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- Contemporaries objected. Kazakh Alash-educated intelligentsia called the changes a "method of Russification of the Kazakh language and writing" (p. 26 [pdf 8], via Sembayev 1962). The Party's 1929 resolution then ordered purging "ideological alien elements" from education bodies, and opponents were later "eliminated" in the purges (p. 27 [pdf 9]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- Toimbek's verdict: equal opportunity "was based on a highly politicized and fixed 'scientific' knowledge of the Marxist-Leninist dogmas", aimed at "training citizens loyal to the regime" (p. 33 [pdf 15]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)

### Inferences
- Likbez is the strongest non-Western case for error 13 (literacy taught for docility) joined to the boundary case (compulsion). It is the converse of the gate: access was forced on people rather than withheld, and the harm lies in the content and in the sanction. Toimbek's "paid but voiceless" evidence also fits a candidate new error (below).

### Gaps
- Clark (1995, 2000) and Kenez's *Birth of the Propaganda State* surfaced only as records (Clark, *Europe-Asia Studies*, DOI 10.1080/09668139508412323; Georgeoff and Clark, *HEQ*, DOI 10.2307/369728). I did not read them. The Russian primer slogan "Мы не рабы, рабы не мы" is often cited, but I did not locate it in Gluschenko's text in the pages I read, so it is not cited here.

## Q4. Any distinct design error not in the list?

### Takeaway
Three candidates are distinct from the fifteen listed: restriction by script or register, recitation without comprehension, and access paid for by the excluded but governed by others. Two others refine existing errors: the meritocratic gate as cover, and inflated literacy statistics.

### Cited Findings
- **Candidate A: restriction by script or register.** The control falls on a script, so one group may print or read while another may not, without any rule naming the people. Ottoman Arabic type was barred while Hebrew and Armenian type were allowed (Coşgel et al. WP p. 21 [pdf 23]). — [Coşgel et al. WP](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2010-02R.pdf). In Japan, kanbun was "a de facto foreign language" (Hmeljak Sangawa p. 153 [pdf 1]). — [Hmeljak Sangawa 2017](https://doi.org/10.4312/as.2017.5.2.153-166). In Ethiopia, the language of learning was Ge'ez, "a now extinct Semitic language" (Lanza and Woldemariam, PDF p. 15). — [Lanza & Woldemariam 2014](https://doi.org/10.14426/mm.v1i1.1457)
- **Candidate B: recitation without comprehension, a counterfeit competence.** Tokugawa pupils "learned to 'read' the texts by memorising them rather than by understanding the actual words" (Hmeljak Sangawa p. 162 [pdf 10], citing Ingulsrud and Allen 2009). The abstract says the complexity "acted as an obstacle to the development of widespread functional literacy" (p. 153). — [Hmeljak Sangawa 2017](https://doi.org/10.4312/as.2017.5.2.153-166). For Ethiopian church schools, Girma Amare (1967) said memorisation "is the only method of teaching", while Yigezu contests this as "simplistic and shallow" (p. 90 [pdf 2]). — [Yigezu 2023](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003424956-8). This differs from "withheld competence (partial)": the competence is displayed but hollow.
- **Candidate C: access paid for by the excluded and governed by others.** Kazakh communities built, repaired and heated schools, yet "such assistance from the population did not mean they could influence the course or content of literacy programs" (Toimbek p. 29 [pdf 11]). — [Toimbek 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-99-8517-3_2)
- **Refinement of error 10 (competence as cover): the open meritocratic gate.** The examination was formally open to almost any male, yet "true social mobility ... was never the goal" (Elman 2014, abstract), and family cultural capital decided the palace ranking (Jiang and Kung 2020, abstract). — [Elman 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004279278_009); [Jiang & Kung 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0097700420914529)
- **Refinement of error 14 (mismeasured progress).** "the government routinely inflated literacy statistics" in prewar Japan (JAS 2001 letter, author unconfirmed). — [JAS 2001](https://doi.org/10.2307/2700022). Fitzpatrick doubts the Soviet campaigns added to the natural trend (via Gluschenko p. 251). — [Gluschenko 2015](https://doi.org/10.17323/1814-9545-2015-3-246-282)
- **Contemporaries' objections, in each setting:**
  - Müteferrika's own 1727 treatise argued the benefits of print to the Sultan, an objection from within (Coşgel et al. p. 16; Swanick p. 281).
  - Meiji reformers such as Maejima Hisoka called for "abolishing kanji and taking kana (i.e., hiragana) as the national script" to educate the people. Status: full-text excerpt via Consensus. — [Ueda 2021, *Language, Nation, Race*, UC Press, DOI 10.1525/luminous.103](https://doi.org/10.1525/luminous.103)
  - Kazakh intellectuals called likbez Russification (Toimbek p. 26).
  - Qu Qiubai attacked Gwoyeu Romatzyh and supported Latinisation. Status: review abstract only. — [Fang 2023, review of Zhong, *Chinese Grammatology*, DOI 10.1515/jciea-2023-0009](https://doi.org/10.1515/jciea-2023-0009)

### Inferences
- Candidate A maps onto proprietary formats and model-only interfaces: control by the medium's code rather than by a rule about persons. Candidate B maps onto "AI literacy" taught as prompt recipes without understanding. Candidate C maps onto users who supply the data and labour but have no say in the system's design. These pairings are hypotheses only; none is supported by the sources read here.
- Candidate B may overlap with error 13 (docility) when the memorised text is moral or political, as with Teikin Ōrai's "models of personal conduct" (Hmeljak Sangawa p. 162). The author should decide whether it is separate.
