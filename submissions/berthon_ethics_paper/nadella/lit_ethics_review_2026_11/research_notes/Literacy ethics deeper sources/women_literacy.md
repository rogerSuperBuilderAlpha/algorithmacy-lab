## 1. Monaghan (1988) and Ferguson (2003) on separating reading from writing

### Takeaway
The database record for Monaghan 1988 states the measurement problem she addressed: the signature counts that literacy historians used ran far lower for women than for men. The accessible secondary record says the cause was instructional. New England taught every child to read for the Bible, but girls' schooling often stopped before writing, and writing was "considered superfluous" for women's duties. Ferguson argues that literacy is "a shifting and plural phenomenon" bound up with gender and nation-building. With Suzuki (2015), she argues that the signature test is itself biased against women, because early modern literacy "does not necessarily include both reading and writing."

### Cited findings
- **Monaghan 1988 (opening of the article, from the OpenAlex/Consensus record; partial, first page only).** She opens on Hanna Newberry, "the most prominent mortgage-holder in Windsor, Connecticut" for most of the second quarter of the eighteenth century, who "was unable to sign the many documents to which she affixed her name. Instead, she subscribed her mark, the initials HN." She then reports Lockridge's will counts: male signing rose "from 60 percent in the 1660s to 85 percent by 1760, and almost 90 percent by 1790," while "Some 31 percent of women signed their wills before 1670; this average increased, but only to 46 percent by 1790s." (*American Quarterly* 40(1), 18–41; p. 18.) — [OpenAlex/DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2713140). The full text sits on JSTOR; I found no open copy.
- **Monaghan's broader study, as a reviewer describes it (abstract only).** Hemphill (AHR 2006) reports that Monaghan traces the Puritan "ordinary road" to literacy, "from hornbook to primer to Psalter to Bible", and that the New England school laws expressed a "commitment to literacy as the path to Protestant piety." — [Hemphill 2006, AHR 111(5):1500, DOI 10.1086/ahr.111.5.1500](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a60413ef-3e57-440b-a9bd-b2972afa0c59/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **The reading/writing split stated in a database abstract (abstract only).** Chamberlain (2014): women "Others were taught to read but not to write. In colonial New England … all persons, irrespective of gender, were encouraged to read the Bible for themselves. Nevertheless, the distinction between reading literacy and writing literacy reinforced women's subordinate status." The reason she gives: "Because they could fulfill their duties as both Christians and goodwives without writing, such education was considered superfluous, especially for women in lower socioeconomic statuses (Hall 32)." — [Chamberlain 2014, *Legacy* 31(1), DOI 10.5250/legacy.31.1.0033](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:a3f92805-c978-4215-a350-e2c1e8694bb9/?utm_source=claude_desktop). The source of this claim is David D. Hall, not Monaghan.
- **Ferguson, *Dido's Daughters* (2003), via Goldstein's review (review read in full; book not accessible).** The book's central claim is that literacy is "a shifting and plural phenomenon" (Ferguson p. 3, quoted in Goldstein 2004, p. 122), "enmeshed in processes of gender differentiation and nation-building" (Goldstein's paraphrase, p. 122). Goldstein reports that Ferguson finds in male-authored texts "the image of the skeptical or resistant female" (Ferguson p. 96). He reads that image as evidence "not [of] silence, but rather an active struggle against the clerkly hierarchy that privileged literacy (in the narrow sense of being able to read Latin) above the oral or written command of various vernaculars" (Goldstein p. 122). On language and gender: "the masculine Latin versus the vernacular 'mother tongue'… had complex effects on how literacies were defined and measured" (p. 122). Goldstein faults "the frequent disjunction between Ferguson's evidence and her conclusions" (p. 123). — [Goldstein 2004, *Renaissance and Reformation* 40(4), DOI 10.33137/rr.v40i4.9049](https://doi.org/10.33137/rr.v40i4.9049); book: [Ferguson 2003, DOI 10.7208/chicago/9780226243184.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226243184.001.0001).
- **Ferguson and Suzuki 2015 (abstract only).** "Early modern literacy, for example, does not necessarily include both reading and writing: the criterion of measuring literacy by the signature is biased against women of various ranks as well as against poor men." They call for attention to "the so-called 'unlettered women,'" and to "the negatively charged side of the literacy/illiteracy dyad." — [*Literature Compass* 12(11), 575–590, DOI 10.1111/lic3.12281](https://doi.org/10.1111/lic3.12281).
- **Sanders and Ferguson 2002 (abstract only; the publisher returned a JavaScript shell, not the PDF).** Literacy was "subject to redefinition by edict from above." In 1538 every parish had to buy an English Bible. The 1540 Great Bible preface addressed "all manner of persons, men, women, … virgins, wives, widows". The 1543 Act for the Advancement of True Religion then lowered the gates "to allow for only a trickle of elite readers to have access to Scripture. Reading the Bible in English was prohibited outright for women, artificers, journeymen, serving[men]…" — [*Critical Survey* 14(1), 1–8, DOI 10.3167/001115702782352169](https://doi.org/10.3167/001115702782352169).

### Inferences
- The colonial New England regime and the 1543 Act are two different designs. New England imposed a **ceiling** on women's competence: read, yes; write, optional. The Act imposed a **licence** by class and sex on one text. Both rest on the same reasoning: women need only as much literacy as their assigned role requires.
- Ferguson and Suzuki add a second-order wrong. The **instrument** used to measure literacy (the signature) builds the reading/writing split into the record, so the people kept from writing show up as illiterate.

### Gaps
- Monaghan 1988's own argument about instruction (pp. 19–41) was not read. Before the paper attributes "taught to read but not to write" to Monaghan, someone must get the JSTOR text through a library.
- I read no page of *Dido's Daughters* itself. Its claims about women's literacy as "partial" or reading-only were not checked.

## 2. Other scholarship on reading-without-writing and "permitted reading" regimes, and why institutions designed them that way

### Takeaway
The pattern recurs across the regions searched. Institutions gave women the receptive half of literacy, or a licensed corpus, sized to a domestic or devotional role. Writing and "literary talent" were withheld as morally dangerous. The reasons the institutions themselves gave were piety (reading for scripture or the classics), role-fit (wife, mother, housewife), and protection of virtue (keeping women away from poems, novels and letters).

### Cited findings

**Early modern Europe**
- **London, reading far wider than writing (abstract only).** Hubbard uses initialing as a proxy for reading and finds that "reading literacy was far more broadly socially diffused than the ability to write"; women "found reading to be useful and relevant to their lives, and … literacy carried social prestige." — [Hubbard 2015, *J. British Studies* 54(3), 553–577, DOI 10.1017/jbr.2015.61](https://doi.org/10.1017/jbr.2015.61).
- **Exclusion from institutions, Tudor England (abstract only).** "most women were excluded from male centres of learning—the grammar schools, universities and Inns of Court"; "the majority of women were taught within the household or the Church." — [Daybell 2005, *History of Education*, DOI 10.1080/00467600500313989](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:476d07bf-4312-4fb0-88c8-c8bb9a083f56/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Catholic girls' schools in Germany taught literacy for socialisation (abstract only).** "the acquisition of literacy by the female population was not an end in itself. It rather served the denominational, gender- and class-specific socialisation of the girls. Nevertheless, learning to read and write enabled the girls to participate in the literate culture." — [Rutz 2011, *Paedagogica Historica*, DOI 10.1080/00309230.2011.603344](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:2679dee7-0fc8-4122-b539-42c6623fe332/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Delegated writing in the Netherlands (abstract only).** People who could not write, or could write only a little, "had to rely on the assistance of professional or social scribes", and the scribe's role "often remain[s] unclear." — [van der Wal 2021, *J. Historical Sociolinguistics*, DOI 10.1515/jhsl-2020-0018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:52a019b5-496e-4c66-9040-23742986a246/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Patriarchy and literacy campaigns (abstract only).** "Literacy campaigns can be linked both to attempts to consolidate patriarchalism in early modern Europe and to complex struggles attending its 18th-century demise"; schools were asked to "teach females how to read or change their reading habits, instruct children in writing or give them to understand that their mother tongue did not count as school knowledge." — [Miller 2003, *Interchange*, DOI 10.1023/b:inch.0000015906.19694.4a](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:011af951-69a2-4741-af2f-841753e7d23a/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Scotland (abstract only).** Stevenson finds that Scottish culture "seems to have presented particular difficulties to women" and asks about "the preconditions which permit, or prevent, creative expression"; Scotland produced "one major published woman poet," Elizabeth Melville. — [Stevenson 2012, *The Seventeenth Century* 27(3), 335–374, DOI 10.7227/tsc.27.3.5](https://doi.org/10.7227/tsc.27.3.5).

**East Asia: China**
- **You and Nussey 2022 (read in full; UCL author manuscript, so locators are manuscript pages; published pp. 1058–1073).** This is a permitted-reading regime by genre. Sima Guang (1019–1086) "once stated that 'it is not appropriate to teach women making poems and playing vulgar music' … However, he was in favour of women being educated to read Confucian classics" (MS p. 5). Hence "'ignorance' specifically indicated the paucity of literary and artistic talents that were believed to lead to the loss of virtue" (MS p. 5). The dictum "ignorance is women's virtue" (女子无才便是德) first appears in Chen Jiru (1558–1639), who wrote that women who "enjoy reading poems and novels, which could raise immoral thoughts… Thus, they are rather remaining illiterate" (MS p. 5). Talented women "were often deemed as 'a moral stigma'" (MS p. 6, quoting Lee 1995). Its elite schooling: "women in elite families were taught to read and write in order to better understand and embody morality" (MS p. 5). — [DOI 10.1080/09540253.2022.2123897](https://doi.org/10.1080/09540253.2022.2123897).
- **Wong 1995 (abstract only; T&F PDF returned HTTP 403).** Before the late Qing, "the aim of women's education was limited to the teaching of social ethics and family tradition with an emphasis on how to behave as a virtuous wife and good mother." — [*Women's History Review* 4(3), 345–367, DOI 10.1080/09612029500200092](https://doi.org/10.1080/09612029500200092).

**Islamic world**
- **Wright 2021 (abstract only; no OA copy).** This is the key find for this region. A ḥadīth "prohibiting women's literacy", "rejected by most ḥadīth scholars and ignored by jurists", "gained prominence in the second half of the nineteenth century through the works of scholars who weaponized it as a response to colonial education projects", in British India and Egypt. — [*Die Welt des Islams* 62(1), 78–108, DOI 10.1163/15700607-61020019](https://doi.org/10.1163/15700607-61020019). The title, "Teaching Women to Write", indicates that the prohibition targeted **writing**. The abstract does not say so outright, so check this in the full text.
- **Counter-tradition (abstract only).** Geissinger examines a tradition in which the Prophet asks a woman to teach Ḥafṣa an incantation "just as you taught her writing", and notes that it is "often employed as a proof-text testifying to Ḥafṣa's literacy, and the Prophet's approval of that" in debates about women's education. — [Geissinger 2020, Brill, DOI 10.1163/9789004427952_012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5e440390-efba-458d-a5af-7dacdf015dab/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (abstract only).** He "disapproved the modern education for women and limited his mission of education only to men. He upheld the traditional model of education suitable for women." — [Hussain 2022, *Rashhat-e-Qalam*, DOI 10.56765/rq.v2i2.74](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:14552677-e8bc-45de-8f07-aaf80b757f9b/?utm_source=claude_desktop). This is a low-tier venue; use it with care.

**South Asia**
- **Kumari 2024 (abstract only).** "until the mid-nineteenth century, zenana education or instruction imparted at home was the accepted norm … women's education was viewed by most communities as meaningless. Large sections of the population of both Hindu and Muslim communities … were vehemently opposed t[o]…" (the abstract is truncated in the record). — [*History and Sociology of South Asia*, DOI 10.1177/22308075241273724](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:d346af60-cb7f-4233-b5c9-810a063cf2d5/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Masum 2015 (read in full; weak source).** Zenana teachers in Gopalganj, East Bengal "taught writing, reading, Mathematics and Stitching in nine centres", c. 1911–12 (PDF p. 11). The article is descriptive and offers little analysis. — [*J. Social Sciences and Humanities* 54(2), DOI 10.46568/jssh.v54i2.66](https://doi.org/10.46568/jssh.v54i2.66).
- **Moitra 2017 (abstract only).** Bengali women's reading was "reading aloud, reading together", "at a time when public spaces remained largely inac[cessible]". — [*Hypatia*, DOI 10.1111/hypa.12345](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:73ac2715-7ece-4755-86f9-c8ba5b03033b/?utm_source=claude_desktop).

**Africa**
- **Montgomery 2017, German East Africa (read in full; journal pages derived from the PDF header "Montgomery 231" on PDF p. 8, so PDF p. 11 = p. 234).** "missionaries preferred the education of men to women, as women made up only 33 percent of the student body"; "Women were supposed to be able to read and write at the time of baptism, but the required level of skill and knowledge was generally very low"; "vocational training for women consisted of domestic skills, such as sewing and knitting, and behavioral as well moral education on how to be a housewives." A 1902 Bagamoyo report: "girls were trained by sisters to perform the house- keeping tasks relevant to their social status" (all p. 234). The schooling aimed at "creating Christian housewives" (p. 229). The literature he reviews credits gender-biased mission education "with isolating women in the domestic realm" (p. 227). — [*Politics & Society* 45(2), 225–268, DOI 10.1177/0032329217704432](https://doi.org/10.1177/0032329217704432).
- Not read: Baten, de Haas and Kempter 2021 ([DOI 10.1111/padr.12430](https://doi.org/10.1111/padr.12430), HTTP 403) and Gaitskell 1988 (thesis, Wits).

### Inferences
- Institutions gave three reasons, and they recur across regions: **piety** (read scripture or the classics; New England, Sima Guang, the German nuns), **role-fit** (enough literacy for goodwife or housewife; New England, German East Africa, Qing "virtuous wife and good mother"), and **protection of virtue** (the productive or imaginative arts endanger women; Chen Jiru, the ḥadīth revival). Writing was withheld because it gave women the means to correspond, compose and act in public.
- Wright's case adds a timing: a dormant prohibition was **revived in response to** a rival's education project. The gate was raised as a counter-move.

### Gaps
- I found no open full text of Wright 2021, and none of Metcalf's work on the *Bihishti Zewar*. Any claim about letter-writing fears in South Asian Muslim debates is unverified.
- For medieval Europe I found only titles (Zieman 2003, DOI 10.1484/m.usml-eb.3.2365; Bawcutt 2000) and read none.
- I found nothing usable on Japan or Korea.

## 3. Contemporaries who objected (exact quotations, locators)

### Takeaway
The objection was made in its own time, and in the terms the paper needs. Wollstonecraft names the protective pretext ("kept in ignorance under the specious name of innocence") and proposes free co-educational day schools. Abigail Adams calls the debarring of women from education, in words she quotes from another writer, "an inhumane Tyranny". Feng Menglong and Yuan Mei opposed the "ignorance is virtue" dictum inside the Confucian order. Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain "wrote back to the empire against both colonialism and patriarchy."

### Cited findings
- **Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) (read: Introduction and chapters II, IV, XII, XIII from Wikisource; chapter locators, because the page anchors did not survive extraction).**
  - Ch. II: "If then women are not a swarm of ephemeron triflers, why should they be kept in ignorance under the specious name of innocence?"
  - Ch. IV: "Without knowledge there can be no morality! Ignorance is a frail base for virtue! Yet, that it is the condition for which woman was organized, has been insisted upon by the writers who have most vehemently argued in favour of the superiority o[f man]."
  - Ch. XII: "whilst they are kept in ignorance they become in the same proportion the slaves of pleasure as they are the slaves of man."
  - Ch. XII (the remedy): "day schools, for particular ages, should be established by government, in which boys and girls might be educated together. The school for the younger children, from five to nine years of age, ought to be absolutely free and open to all classes."
  - Ch. XIII, on "permitted" reading: women who find history dry "are necessarily dependent on the novelist for amusement"; "any kind of reading I think better than leaving a blank still a blank."
  - Source: [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman). Long-s characters were normalised in quoting. Gutenberg returned HTTP 503.
- **Abigail Adams to John Adams, 30 June 1778 (partial; read through a fetch summary of the MHS transcription, so recheck verbatim before use).** "But in this country you need not be told how much female Education is neglected"; "I regret the trifling narrow contracted Education of the Females of my own country." She quotes an unnamed writer: "tis an inhumane Tyranny to debar them of priviliges of ingenious Education." — *Adams Family Correspondence* vol. 3, [MHS ADMS-04-03-02-0045](https://www.masshist.org/publications/adams-papers/index.php/view/ADMS-04-03-02-0045); also [Founders Online](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Adams/04-03-02-0045). "Inhumane Tyranny" is a quotation inside her letter, not her own coinage.
- **Feng Menglong (1574–1646) and Yuan Mei (1716–1798), as secondary report (You and Nussey 2022, MS p. 6).** They "strongly criticised the distorted connotation of 'ignorance is women's virtue' and the stigmatisation of talented women (Liu 2009)". No primary text was read.
- **Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932), as secondary report (abstract only).** She and her colleagues "wrote back to the empire against both colonialism and patriarchy, and created innovative educational discourses and practices", including "ideologies and views widely at variance with conserva[tive views]". — [Bagchi 2009, *Paedagogica Historica* 45(6), 743–755, DOI 10.1080/00309230903335652](https://doi.org/10.1080/00309230903335652).
- **Rahmah El Yunusiyah, Minangkabau, 1923 (full-text excerpt via Consensus; low-tier venue).** She founded a girls' madrasa after finding that the co-educational Diniyah School "inadequately addressed women-specific religious issues". — [Lestari and Triwahana 2026, DOI 10.61677/satmata.v3i3.670](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:019a4f1a-340a-489e-ad91-d20c2f1e5c23/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Bathsua Makin, *An Essay to Revive the Antient Education of Gentlewomen* (1673): database records only.** The records are [Helm 1993, DOI 10.1177/018476789304400109](https://doi.org/10.1177/018476789304400109), [Myers 1985, DOI 10.1353/sec.1985.0009](https://doi.org/10.1353/sec.1985.0009) and an excerpt in Hodgson-Wright 2002 (DOI 10.1515/9781474497138-021). EEBO-TCP returned HTTP 403, so I have **no quotation**.
- **Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, *Respuesta a Sor Filotea* (1691): not accessible.** Cervantes Virtual returned HTTP 403 and es.wikisource HTTP 404. A related article exists: Brooke 2017, *Bulletin of Hispanic Studies* 94(7), DOI 10.3828/bhs.2017.43 (OA at ORA; not read).
- **Mary Astell, *A Serious Proposal to the Ladies* (1694): not accessible** (Gutenberg HTTP 503). Database records: Mang 2025 (DOI 10.5206/sc.v14i1.22997), Taylor 2005 (DOI 10.1353/sib.0.0003).

### Inferences
- The objectors attack the **justification**, not only the exclusion. Wollstonecraft's "specious name of innocence" and the Feng and Yuan critique both reject the claim that ignorance protects or ennobles women. That rejection supports treating "protective withholding" (below) as an error recognised in its own time.
- Wollstonecraft's remedy is structural: free, co-educational, open to all classes. She does not ask for a better curriculum for women alone.

### Gaps
- No primary quotation from Christine de Pizan, Makin, Astell, Sor Juana, Judith Sargent Murray, Rassundari Devi, Tarabai Shinde or Qasim Amin was verified. These are the obvious next fetches, through a library or EEBO.
- Re-fetch the Adams letter's full text and verify it word for word.

## 4. Does this name a design error beyond the existing fourteen?

### Takeaway
Two candidates look distinct. (a) **Protective withholding**: denying the competence as a favour to the person denied, framed as innocence or virtue. (b) **Stigma on the competent**: the woman who does acquire the forbidden half is marked as morally suspect. Three more refine existing entries: the **receive-only split** (under 2, withheld competence), the **licensed canon** (between 4 and 9), and the **gated instrument** (under 14, mismeasured progress). The **revived prohibition** (Wright) reads as a sub-type of 11, the self-justifying gate, or of 10, competence as cover, used as a counter-move.

### Cited findings (evidence for each candidate)
- **(a) Protective withholding (candidate new error).** The wrong lies in the pretext, not the gate: deprivation is presented as good for the deprived. Evidence: Chen Jiru, poems and novels "could raise immoral thoughts… Thus, they are rather remaining illiterate" (You and Nussey MS p. 5). The objection in its own time: Wollstonecraft, "kept in ignorance under the specious name of innocence" (Ch. II), and "Ignorance is a frail base for virtue!" (Ch. IV). This differs from error 13, literacy for docility, which shapes the competence that is given. Protective withholding justifies withholding it.
- **(b) Stigma on the competent (candidate new error).** This inverts error 8, blame and stigma. The stigma falls on those who **have** the competence, not on those who lack it. Talented women were "deemed as 'a moral stigma'", treated "as though they were loose-living" (You and Nussey MS p. 6, quoting Lee 1995, p. 354); Ferguson's "skeptical or resistant female" figure (Goldstein p. 122).
- **Receive-only split (refines 2).** Read but do not write: Chamberlain's abstract (goodwives need no writing) and Hubbard (reading "far more broadly socially diffused than the ability to write"). Montgomery's baptismal standard, "able to read and write … but the required level … generally very low" (p. 234), is the same move. The person can take in the institution's texts but cannot answer, record or correspond. This ties directly to error 3 (record over the voice).
- **Licensed canon (refines 4 and 9).** The reader is admitted but the corpus is chosen for her: Confucian classics yes, poems no (Sima Guang); Bible reading licensed by sex and rank (the 1543 Act, Sanders and Ferguson abstract). Error 9 restricts the reader class and error 4 controls the press. The licensed canon controls **what a given reader class may read**.
- **Gated instrument (refines 14).** The signature measure "is biased against women" because it counts the half they were denied (Ferguson and Suzuki 2015 abstract). The metric does more than miss progress: it encodes the gate and makes the gated group look less capable than it is.
- **Revived prohibition as a counter-move (sub-type of 10 or 11).** A ḥadīth "rejected by most ḥadīth scholars" was "weaponized … as a response to colonial education projects" (Wright 2021 abstract).

### Inferences
- If the author adds only one new error, (a) protective withholding has the strongest evidence, an objection in its own time, and a direct present-day analogue: capabilities or explanations withheld from users "for their safety" or to avoid "confusion". The analogue is a hypothesis for the author, not evidence.
- (b) is real but thinner. Its evidence is Chinese (via one secondary paper) plus Ferguson's figure, known only through a review.

### Gaps
- None of the five candidates is named as an ethical category by any scholar read here. I constructed the labels from the evidence, and they are my inferences.
- To confirm (a) outside China and Wollstonecraft's England, the next fetches are Makin (1673) and the full text of Wright (2021).
