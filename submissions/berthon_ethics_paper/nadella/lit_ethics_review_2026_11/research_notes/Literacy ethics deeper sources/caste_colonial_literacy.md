## Caste: Brahmanical control of letters, the Phules and Ambedkar, Paik, and colonial policy

### Takeaway
The primary texts and the 1883 Hunter Commission Report together document three wrongs. Upper castes physically and socially vetoed low-caste schooling, and the colonial state formally affirmed open admission but yielded to that veto in practice. The state also funded higher-caste education out of revenue drawn from the excluded. Phule (1873) named the fiscal injustice and the Brahman capture of the education department in his own time. Ambedkar (1936/1945) named the denial of knowledge as a structural function of varna. Paik's monograph was reachable only as an abstract and through reviews.

### Cited Findings

**Primary: Jotirao Phule, *Slavery (Gulamgiri)*, 1873; English translation by P. G. Patil, Govt of Maharashtra, 1991. - On capture of offices (Preface, p. 23): "Perhaps the most glaring tendency of the Government system of high class education has been the virtual monopoly of all higher offices under them by the Brahmins." - Phule quotes an English writer against downward filtration (Preface, pp. 22–23): the graduates may "have they kept their knowledge to themselves, as a personal gift, not to be soiled by contract with the ignorant vulgar?" - In Kaira the Collector reported that Christians and low castes "had been banished from [six] schools in the District" (para. 588) [OCR]. — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/ReportOfTheIndianEducationCommission)
- The formal rule (para. 589) quotes Court of Directors Despatch No. 58, 1858: "The educational institutions of Government are intended by us to be open to all classes, and we cannot depart from a principle which is essentially sound". ### Inferences
- The Hunter Report is unusually useful because it is the state's own record of the wrong. It names the motive ("subjection and servility"). It records the violence, quotes the state's 1858 rule, and admits that the rule was not enforced. This is contemporary recognition in the paper's sense, made by the institution itself, with Phule's 1882 memorial (carded) and *Gulamgiri* as the objection from below.
- Phule's two charges are distinct wrongs. The first is fiscal: the excluded pay for schooling that others capture. The second is capture: Brahman officials staff the department meant to reach Shudras. Both have analogues in publicly funded AI programmes whose benefits accrue to incumbents.

## Colonial Africa, the Pacific, Australia and Southeast Asia: mission literacy, language, schooling designed to limit, and the objectors

### Takeaway
South Africa supplies the strongest sourced material. The 1849 Cape mission census measured literacy because missions were accused of "withholding labour", and it tested reading only in the Dutch Bible. Verwoerd justified Bantu Education by the danger of "false expectations", and missionaries, liberal MPs and newspapers objected at the time. For Australia, scholars document exclusion of Aboriginal children at white settlers' demand, and Aboriginal protest in the 1930s. Pacific and Southeast Asian material reached me only as abstracts.

### Cited Findings

**South Africa: 1849 mission census.** Fourie, Ross and Viljoen 2014, "Literacy at South African Mission Stations", *JSAS* 40(4), 781–800, doi:10.1080/03057070.2014.931057. **Partial**: I read the Stellenbosch WP 06/13 version (introduction, data and results; pp. are WP pages). Local key: `fourie2014literacy`.
- Why literacy was counted (WP p. 6): the survey was ordered because of "the mission stations which were seen as withholding labour from the farmers", and "Special attention was paid to the work performed outside the stations by the adolescents" (p. 7). — [WP PDF](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)
- The test (p. 7, quoting the 1849 instructions, *Master and Servant: Addenda*, p. 27): magistrates were to "take with you a copy of the Bible in Dutch, from which you will require those who are represented as able to do so to read a few verses". — [WP PDF](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)
- Mismeasurement by language (fn 30, p. 15): a Dutch-only test "would have ruled out those who were literate in isiXhosa but could not speak, let alone read, Dutch." Women went uncounted: "At a few stations, indeed, the literacy of women was not ascertained" (p. 7). — [WP PDF](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)
- Colonial design (pp. 5–6, quoting Duff 2010, p. 295): "coloured and African children were to be fitted to be employees and labourers, white children were to be educated to employ and to lead." — [WP PDF](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)
- **Quantitative** (p. 14): arriving at a station after emancipation (1838) meant "a 15.2% lower probability of being literate in 1849", and being born there meant a "27% higher likelihood". At Genadendal 66.60% of teenagers could read against 23.75% of adults; at Elim, 66.99% against 15.55% (pp. 14–15). N = 4,678 residents. — [WP PDF](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 1849 Cape census shows measurement working as labour discipline. The state counted literacy because it suspected missions of keeping labour off the farms, and it defined literacy as reading the coloniser's Bible in Dutch. That definition made isiXhosa literates invisible.
- Verwoerd's "false expectations" argument inverts the oversold promise. Here the state withholds competence on the stated ground that it would raise hopes the order will not meet. That bridges "selective provision" and "literacy for docility".
- In Australia and in India the exclusion was delegated to the dominant group's veto (school strikes, Brahman secession), and the state accommodated it.

- **Pacific mission literacy and orthography** (Hawaiʻi, Tonga, Samoa, Fiji) and **colonial Southeast Asia** (Dutch East Indies, French Indochina, the Philippines): no full text and no quantitative evidence this session.

## Design errors not in the existing list

### Takeaway
Four candidates look distinct from the 14 errors and the boundary case: (a) the excluded are taxed to fund the gate; (b) the state yields to the dominant group's veto; (c) literacy is counted as labour surveillance, against the coloniser's standard; (d) competence is rationed to manage expectations. One further item, (e) denial of demand, is better treated as a sub-case of blame.

### Cited Findings
- **(a) Taxed for one's own exclusion.** The excluded pay for provision that others capture. Phule: revenue from "the Ryot's labor" is spent "on the education of the higher classes" (*Slavery*, p. 22), and closing the department would free Shudras "from (the burden of) taxation" (p. 76). The Hunter Commission concedes the low castes "contribute to the local cesses" (para. 587). This differs from error 12, "selective provision": the wrong is the extraction, not only the selection. — [Phule](https://archive.org/details/jotirao-phule-slavery-e); [Hunter](https://archive.org/details/ReportOfTheIndianEducationCommission)
- **(b) The accommodated veto.** The rule is formally universal, but the dominant group's boycott sets practice. Hunter para. 589 quotes the 1858 Despatch ("open to all classes") and the local refusal "to interfere". Para. 588 records Brahman secession at Dharwar. Marsden 2026 (abs.) has departments "adapting school systems to allow the exclusion" after settler school strikes. Hunter also flags a loophole: reclassifying schools as "special" to escape the open-admission rule. — [Hunter](https://archive.org/details/ReportOfTheIndianEducationCommission); [Marsden 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/14490854.2026.2624731)
- **(c) The census as labour surveillance, against the coloniser's standard.** The 1849 Cape count was motivated by "withholding labour" and tested only Dutch Bible reading (Fourie et al., WP pp. 6–7, fn 30). This differs from error 14, "mismeasured progress": the metric was built for control and ruled out vernacular literacy by design. — [Fourie et al.](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2013/wp062013/wp-06-2013.pdf)
- **(d) Rationing competence to manage expectations.** Verwoerd's "false expectations" (Moore 2017, p. 119) treats the learner's raised aspiration as the harm and withholds competence to prevent it. — [Moore 2017](https://newcontree.org.za/index.php/nc/article/download/90/90)
- **(e) Denial of demand.** Kunte's claim that admission is "not raised by the Mahars and Dhers themselves … a groundless agitation" (Hunter para. 588) asserts that the excluded do not want the competence. The Commission rebutted it with enrolment and night-school data. This is better treated as a sub-case of error 8, "blame and stigma", or error 11, "self-justifying gate". — [Hunter](https://archive.org/details/ReportOfTheIndianEducationCommission)

### Inferences
- Present-day analogues to test, not yet evidence: (a) public funds for AI skilling that reaches mainly incumbents; (b) "opt-in" access rules that yield to incumbent users or employers; (c) "AI literacy" metrics defined in one vendor's interface, which miss people competent in other tools; (d) firms limiting what users are told about agents "so as not to confuse them".
- Several existing errors gain caste and colonial evidence. Error 7, capture by intermediaries: Phule on the Brahman education officers. Error 9, restriction by reader class: downward filtration. Error 11, self-justifying gate: Kunte. Error 13, literacy for docility: Lovedale readers and Duff's "fitted to be employees". Boundary B, enforced standard: the Dutch Bible test.

### Gaps
