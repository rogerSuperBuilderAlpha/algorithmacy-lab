## What the scholarship adds

The 15 papers read in full, 16 read as abstracts and the existing library confirm the nine errors in the
restructured outline and add five. The five new ones are not variations on the old: each names a distinct design
move, and each has a present-day analogue in the AI-ethics movement's remedies.

## Where the scholarship agrees and disagrees

**Agreement.** Every historian read treats literacy as neither liberating nor oppressive in itself. What harmed
people was what institutions did with it. Matusov and St. Julien say so in terms (abs.: "neither essentially
liberating nor oppressive"), Castillo Gómez and Martínez pair policing with subaltern use, and Jaywant's
lower-caste writers used print against the Brahman monopoly. This matches the paper's existing guardrail (STRUCTURE
§2.1): the critique is of design and allocation, not of the competence.

**Disagreement and limits.**
- The censorship papers measure costs to *knowledge production* (authors, titles, publishers). They are not about
  readers' access, so they support "custody of the medium is costly", not "custody harmed the weaker party" directly.
- Branch's contemporaries were Republicans arguing in Congress; their objections are partisan as well as moral,
  which counts for the paper's "recognised in its own time" test but should be said.
- Barakat writes about a statistical artefact, not a wrong done on purpose. Error 14 is a failure of evaluation.
  It becomes an ethical error only when, as with EWLP (Jones 2018; Boughton 2023), an institution uses the measure
  to claim success.
- Jaywant's literacy material sits in footnotes and quotations within an intellectual history of caste. A study
  centred on caste and literacy is still needed; Paik's thesis on Dalit women's education was found as an abstract
  and not read.

## Gap check

I found no work that sets literacy's historical distribution errors against the AI-ethics movement's remedies error
by error, with a firm's architecture as the case. Sources: Consensus (one gap query, 25 results) and OpenAlex (four
gap queries, top 10 each), 19 Nov 2026. The nearest works are:
- Kamperman and Graff (2026, lib.): the AI-literacy movement repeats the literacy myth.
- Lund and Teel (2026, lib.): unequal AI access "recapitulates longstanding epistemic injustices".
- Paljor et al. (2026, abs.): AI literacy shifts responsibility onto learners.
- Kalantzis and Cope (2024, abs.): generative AI is a writing technology as momentous as print "and in its peculiar
  ways just as problematic".
- Nichols, Thrall and Quiros (2024): "speculative capture" of literacy by AI platforms. Its full text was blocked,
  so whether it draws historical parallels is **unverified**. It is the one paper to get through a library before
  claiming the gap.
