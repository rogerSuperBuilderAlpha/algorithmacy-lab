# F — Parallel moves: other single conventions credited with opening a competence

*Corrected on 2026-10-12 against `../audit/AUDIT_02.md`: 22 factual fixes.*

*Facet F of the foundation paper's literature run. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-12. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Cases outside the history of reading where one representational or interface convention is credited with widening who can do something: Hindu-Arabic place value (with the 1299 Florence "ban" and the Roman-numeral dispute), the musical staff and Guido of Arezzo, finding devices in the book (alphabetical order, index, chapter division), double-entry bookkeeping, algebraic symbolism and calculus notation, direct manipulation and the spreadsheet, the Cherokee syllabary and Hangul, and counter-cases that were clear improvements and did not spread (Dvorak, spelling reform, decimal time). I add one set the brief did not name: format interventions inside the numeracy literature itself (Peters, Hibbard, Gigerenzer), because the author's standing model for algorithmacy is numeracy.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Is "one move on the page widened a competence" a recurring, well-evidenced pattern, or a retrospective selection? Which parallels help the paper, which embarrass it, and what do they imply for H-cost, H-cause and problem 4 ("one similar move may be the wrong shape")?

**Legend for read status.**
- *Read in full*: I read the whole text this session.
- *Read in part*: I read the text but not all of it, or I read it by keyword search and say where. For Chrisomalis (2010, 2020) the only access was Google Books search-inside snippets of about 40 words, so every Chrisomalis quotation is a fragment and its sense needs checking in context.
- *Abstract only*: database abstract or record, no body text.
- *Not accessible*: no text and no abstract.

Quotations carry a locator. "OCR §" means a section number in an archive.org OCR text. Locators for Guido are the part of the text (Prologue or *Epistola* excerpt), because the workbook's page breaks split sentences and I could not map them to Strunk and Treitler's pagination.

### Case 1. Hindu-Arabic numerals, place value and zero

**zhang1995representational** — Zhang, J. and Norman, D. A. (1995). A representational analysis of numeration systems. *Cognition*, 57(3), 271–295. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(95)00674-3
- *Verification:* Crossref record resolved (title, authors, volume, pages match).
- *Read status:* **not accessible.** No open copy found; the article is not in Unpaywall. I know it only through three readers: Schlimm and Neth (2008), Buijsman (2020) and Chrisomalis (2020), all below.
- *What it is cited for:* a framework in which numeration systems are representations whose structure affects how easily a task is performed, so the same arithmetic costs different amounts in different notations. Schlimm and Neth (p. 2097) place it with Larkin and Simon (1987) and Norman (1993). Chrisomalis (2020, p. 22 snippet) criticises it: "numeracy (e.g., Zhang and Norman 1995) under the guise of the assumption that it is reasonable to compare numerical notations on the basis of their efficiency for written computations."
- *Bearing:* supplies the strongest theory for H-cost (work moves to the representation). I could not check its own claims, so the paper should not cite specific findings from it until a human with library access reads it.

**schlimm2008modeling** — Schlimm, D. and Neth, H. (2008). Modeling ancient and modern arithmetic practices: Addition and multiplication with Arabic and Roman numerals. In V. Sloutsky, B. Love and K. McRae (Eds.), *Proceedings of the 30th Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society* (pp. 2097–2102). Cognitive Science Society. https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/cognition/publikationen-dateien-neth-3/2008_schlimmneth_modeling-ancient-and-modern-arithmetic-practices.pdf/@@download/file/2008_SchlimmNeth_Modeling%20ancient%20and%20modern%20arithmetic%20practices.pdf
- *Verification:* author copy downloaded; header carries the citation; not in Crossref.
- *Read status:* **read in full** (6 pages; card: library/schlimm2008modeling.md, no contradiction).
- *What it argues:* computational models (four agents, not human participants) show that addition and multiplication are possible with Roman numerals. Abstract: Roman numerals "are not only informationally equivalent to Arabic ones but also computationally similar—a claim that is widely disputed" (p. 2097). Conclusion (p. 2102): "the Achilles heel of Roman numerals is not that they are (qualitatively) unsuitable for algorithmic usage, but their greater cost in terms of the number of necessary elementary information processes, especially those using external resources." Roman addition needs no memorised addition facts (p. 2101) but many more writing and perceptual-motor steps; the Arabic agent's advantage depends on the problem being laid out in right-justified columns ("the precise format of external inputs", p. 2101).
- *Limit:* simulation of 1,000,000 additions and 10,000 multiplications of numbers up to 100, no human data (p. 2098 explains why). The authors say the models would change with an abacus.
- *Bearing:* complicates H-cost. "Cheaper" depends on which resource you count (memory, motor steps, columnar layout). It also supports the point that the place-value advantage is partly an advantage of the page layout, which is a design move of the right kind for the paper.

**buijsman2020numerals** — Buijsman, S. (2020). How numerals support new cognitive capacities. *Synthese*, 197, 3779–3796. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-018-01989-7
- *Verification:* Crossref; open access (PDF downloaded).
- *Read status:* read in full (card: library/buijsman2020numerals.md).
- *What it says:* reports Krajcsi and Szabó (2012): when taught a completely new sign-value system and a new place-value system, "performance on simple addition and comparison tasks is better for the sign-value system", so "sign-value systems are easier to learn and work with for those kinds of tasks" (pp. 3787–3788). I did not read Krajcsi and Szabó.
- *Bearing:* complicates H-cost. For novices the simpler, older notation can be easier. Buijsman's text states neither a practice effect nor a column-layout effect for place value; the column point comes only from Menary's multiplication layouts (pp. 3786–3787), without a cost figure (card). The Krajcsi and Szabó sentence runs from the foot of p. 3787 to the top of p. 3788.

**chrisomalis2010numerical** — Chrisomalis, S. (2010). *Numerical Notation: A Comparative History*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511676062
- *Verification:* Crossref record.
- *Read status:* **read in part, by Google Books snippets only** (pp. 21, 28–32, 116–117, 123, 125, 131, 173, 194–195, 272, 277, 350). Not the body text.
- *What the fragments show:* a section titled "Technology, Function, and Efficiency" (p. 29) begins from the historian's habit of ranking notations. P. 32 (fragments): efficiency "for computation is a Western-centered and historically inaccurate benchmark for comparing"; a notation needs "not only … a better mousetrap, but also that an entire social group learn it before its usefulness will be evident. A system with many users is functional for that reason alone". P. 116: "efficiency of the Roman numerals is a common subject in the history of mathematics. The consensus of these …". P. 123: "Yet the debate was not only about efficiency. In 1299, the Arte del Cambio or moneychangers' guild of Florence prohibited the use of Western numerals in its …". P. 125: the view that Roman numerals were worse for computation "is correct but incomplete. The Roman numerals were used alongside the Greek alphabetic numerals for well over a thousand years".
- *Bearing:* refutes the cleanest version of "better notation wins" and supplies the social-adoption alternative (network effects). Chrisomalis (2010, p. 123 snippet) still treats the 1299 prohibition as a fact; Nothaft (2020) below shows what it covered.

**chrisomalis2020reckonings** — Chrisomalis, S. (2020). *Reckonings: Numerals, Cognition, and History*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/13381.001.0001
- *Verification:* Crossref record.
- *Read status:* **read in part, snippets only** (pp. xii, xiii, 21–24, 75, 79, 108). MIT preview PDF was bot-walled. Overmann's review (see `overmann2022review`) was not read.
- *What the fragments show:* p. xii: "numeracy is not preordained". P. 22: the criticism of Zhang and Norman quoted above. P. 24: "cognitive effects from written number notations is not to say that there are no such effects" (fragment; sense incomplete). P. 75: "the East Asian evidence rebuts the idea that an integrated computational-representational system should presumptively be considered superior to a division of cognitive labor." **nothaft2020satanic** — Nothaft, C. P. E. (2020). Medieval Europe's satanic ciphers: On the genesis of a modern myth. *British Journal for the History of Mathematics*, 35(2), 107–136. https://doi.org/10.1080/26375451.2020.1726050
- *Verification:* Crossref; accepted manuscript from Oxford ORA (CC BY-NC-ND). Page numbers below are those of the 56-page accepted manuscript, not the journal.
- *Read status:* read in full (card: library/nothaft2020satanic.md; the reference list from p. 44 skimmed).
- *What it argues:* the story that zero or Hindu-Arabic numerals were banned as diabolical is "false or unsubstantiated at nearly every level of analysis" (abstract). On Florence: the provision is Article 102 of the statutes of the Arte del Cambio, edited by Camerani Marri (1955, 72–73), headed *Quod nullus de arte scribat in suo libro per abbacum*. It bars members from writing, or letting others write, credits and debits in their account books "by means of or in the letter of the abacus, but let him write it openly and in full by way of letters" (*aliquod quod per modum vel licteram abbachi intelligatur, sed aperte et extense scribat per licteram*; pp. 14–15, translation from Struik 1968). "The reasons behind this prohibition are a matter of speculation." Nothaft lists fraud-prevention as a "popular" explanation but does not endorse it, and "no such comment appears in the source text" (p. 15, n. 22, on Gazalé). The same article reappears in the revisions of 1300, 1313 and 1316 (n. 21). Other provisions: Padua statutes of 1331 (not 1348; the substance of the "1348" rule is found in 1331) required booksellers to display prices in clear letters rather than ciphers, a rule about display to buyers, not about the booksellers' own accounts (pp. 16–17); Trieste 1350 barred money changers and merchants from keeping books *per abachum*; Frankfurt 1494 told clerks to stop using the numerals in public books (p. 17). "Neither of these examples documents a blanket 'ban' of the sort imagined by some modern authors" (p. 17). The popular "non per cifras sed per literas claras" wording appears in Padua's 1465 statutes, and "the year 1348 … may have been a mistake on Ball's part" (p. 16).
- *Bearing:* **contradicts** the often-repeated 1299 story as evidence of church or civic resistance to a better notation. What survives is a narrow guild rule about account books, with motives unknown. Note also what the rule shows: by 1299 the numerals were common enough among Florentine money changers for the guild to regulate them. The paper should drop the ban or cite it with Nothaft's wording.

**danna2022numerical** — Danna, R., Iori, M. and Mina, A. (2022). *A numerical revolution: The diffusion of practical mathematics and the growth of pre-modern European economies* (LEM Working Paper 2022/18). Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna. http://hdl.handle.net/10419/266088
- *Verification:* EconStor record; PDF downloaded. Working paper, not peer reviewed in this form.
- *Read status:* read in part (card: library/danna2022numerical.md; main text read in full, appendix tables A1–A9 and reference list skimmed).
- *What it argues:* abstract: "This numerical revolution laid the foundations for the commercial revolution of the 13th century, and the diffusion of knowledge through organised learning had positive and significant effects on the growth of pre-modern European economies." Evidence: a dataset of "over 1050 manuals" (abstract; the body gives over 1,290 in the full dataset, p. 9, and 1,054 in the 815-city growth sample, p. 13) of practical arithmetic and city-level growth regressions. Fibonacci's *Liber abaci* (first completed 1202) is "not … a text exclusively addressed to merchants" (p. 5). "While there is no direct evidence of the first adoption of Hindu-Arabic numerals for economic purposes, they surely had been adopted by the end of the 13th century" (p. 5). The carrier was the abacus school: "lay and vernacular schools where pupils between the age of ten and thirteen (the curriculum lasted on average two years) were trained to use Hindu-Arabic numerals" (p. 6). Fees were "low enough to make it possible also for children of small shopkeepers to afford" it (p. 6). The manuals "only present calculations with Hindu-Arabic numerals" (n. 3).
- *Bearing:* the best available evidence that the numerals mattered, and in the same paper the evidence that they travelled with a schooling system, a vernacular text genre and a commercial demand. The measured unit is the manual (and so the school), not the numerals alone, which is why I read it as supporting "bundle" (Danna et al. do not use the word; they say "organised learning"). Their identification (distance-based instrument) is theirs; I have not checked it. Main limitation (card): city growth proxies income and manuals proxy schools and skills, the instrument's exclusion restriction is argued but untestable, and nothing here concerns a notation's effect on how fast an individual learns.

**james2024otis** — James, K. (2024). Review of J. M. Otis, *By the Numbers: Numeracy, Religion, and the Quantitative Transformation of Early Modern England* (OUP, 2024). *Journal of Religious History*, 49(1), 94–95. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9809.13111

### Case 2. Musical staff notation and Guido of Arezzo

**haines2008origins** — Haines, J. (2008). The origins of the musical staff. *The Musical Quarterly*, 91(3–4), 327–378. https://doi.org/10.1093/musqtl/gdp002
- *Verification:* Crossref; PDF from cartusiana.org.
- *Read status:* read in full (card: library/haines2008origins.md; endnotes skimmed).
- *What it argues:* historians credit Guido with inventing the staff (Apel, Palisca, Lang, the *Oxford History of Western Music*; pp. 327–328). "In sum, the claim implied by Guido's status as 'the one who invented the staff' is untenable. The Arezzo master's role in the staff's development lies less in any one of his presumed contributions than in a clever pedagogy of synthesis backed up by a fair bit of self-promotion" (p. 331 by the PDF's page headers). The ruled staff with letters at the start of each line is in the *Musica enchiriadis* tradition a century and a half before Guido; "This popularization, then, rather than an invention of the staff, was Guido's historical contribution" (p. 344). Guido's own humility topos, quoted: when "those who come after learn with the greatest ease the chants that I and all before me learned with the greatest difficulty" (about p. 331, from Pesce 1999, 445).
- *Bearing:* a documented case where a single-inventor credit for a page convention was taken apart by specialists. Guido's own contribution is dated only through manuscripts of the late 11th or early 12th century, since no copy of the prologue survives from his time (p. 345), so any claim about what Guido proposed rests on later copies. It also places the claimed widening in "pedagogy" and in moving the layout into ordinary liturgical books, not in the notation as such.

**strunk1998guido** — Guido d'Arezzo, Prologue to his Antiphoner and *Epistola de ignoto cantu* (excerpts), trans. in O. Strunk and L. Treitler (Eds.), *Source Readings in Music History* (Norton, 1998), reproduced in the A-R Music Anthology *Workbook: The Medieval Period, Part 1*, Supplemental Reading 1 (Prologue and *Epistola* excerpts). https://www.armusicanthology.com/media/PDFs/Workbook-Medieval-Part1.pdf
- *Verification:* the workbook states its source; I could not check the translation against Strunk and Treitler or against Pesce (1999). Marked "Do Not Copy" by its publisher; I quote only short passages.
- *Read status:* read in full as the workbook reproduces the excerpts (card: library/strunk1998guido.md; page breaks split one sentence). The Prologue excerpt does describe the ruled lines, letters and colours (workbook p. 3); the "Epistola ... not about the ruled staff" point holds for the *Epistola*.
- *What Guido says:* "the wretched singers and their pupils, though they sing every day for a hundred years, will never sing by themselves without a master a single antiphon, not even a short one" (Prologue, first paragraph). He writes the antiphoner so that "any intelligent and studious person may learn the chant by means of it; after he has thoroughly learned a part of it through a master, he will unhesitatingly understand the rest of it by himself without one" (Prologue, close of the paragraph on his plan for the antiphoner). Boys "who until now have been beaten for their gross ignorance of the Psalms and vulgar letters" sing the antiphon "correctly by themselves without a master" (Prologue). The notation alone is not enough: "however perfect the formation of the neumes might be, it is altogether meaningless and worthless without the addition of letters or colors" (Prologue). The *Epistola* excerpt (about his memorising method, with a teacher present, not about the ruled staff): "After I began teaching this procedure to boys, some of them were able [page break] before the third day to sing an unknown melody with ease, which by other methods would not have been possible in many weeks." The *Epistola* method presupposes "someone present to teach the pupil, not merely from a written textbook, but rather by our practice of informal discussion", and, per the Prologue, learning "by heart a fair number of melodies" so that the pupil recognises the sounds.
- **The "ten years to one or two" claim is not in these excerpts.** The figures I found in Guido's own words are rhetorical ("a hundred years") and "before the third day … in many weeks" (the latter is about his memorising method, not the staff). I found "ten years" attributed to Guido only in popular web pages that gave no citation. Mark the claim **unverified**; the full Prologue and *Epistola* in Pesce (1999), pp. 437–531 for the *Epistola* (page range from a catalogue record), would settle what Guido actually claims.

### Case 4. Double-entry bookkeeping

**sangster2024emergence** — Sangster, A. (2024). The emergence of double entry bookkeeping. *The Economic History Review*, 78(2), 499–528. https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13358. Read in full (card: library/sangster2024emergence.md; Aberdeen repository version of record). The method appears in a Florentine moneychanger-bankers' ledger of 1211: "the origin of this bookkeeping method is attributed to northern Italian moneychanger-bankers in the twelfth century", and it "addressed the evidential demands of multiple legal systems relating to use of credit". The abstract reads "The reason for the emergence of such a detailed bookkeeping method is unknown." **Under Sangster's input definition (accounts with duality of entry, p. 501) the 1211 ledger has double entries (pp. 504–507), 283 years before Pacioli's 1494; under the output definition earlier historians used, the earliest case is 1340 (p. 501). Contra entries almost never survive, and double entry is confirmed mainly from cross-references and offset (p. 506). He dates the origin to the twelfth century, earlier than 1211 (p. 520); the ledger is evidence of a method already running. The text never discusses Pacioli's *Summa* (the name appears only in a cited title, n. 48): Sangster targets the Yamey–Besta–Sombart definitional tradition, and the Pacioli comparison is this facet's. He does not rebut "double entry produced capitalism": he reports Yamey's view and cites Yamey 1949, p. 103, for credit dealings leading to systematic accounting (n. 154, p. 523).** Print date 2025 (online 2024-05-23).

**sangster2025reboot** — Sangster, A. (2025). The 1211 ledger and the entity-wide double entry details cited from this paper in earlier drafts belong to Sangster 2024; here 1211 is the earliest paragraph-format ledger (p. 378) and the confirmed double-entry dates are 1296 and 1299. ### Case 8. Writing systems designed for learnability

### (ii) Conditions under which a notational or interface change widened a competence

What the sources support, no more:

1. **The cost saving is task- and user-specific.** Place value cuts perceptual-motor steps when the problem is laid out in columns, but loads memory (100 addition facts); Roman numerals cut memory and raise perceptual-motor steps (Schlimm and Neth 2008, p. 2101); sign-value systems were easier for novices to learn (Krajcsi and Szabó via Buijsman); Dvorak's gain was about 5% in simulation (Norman and Fisher). A claim that a notation "lowered the cost" needs "of what, for whom, after how much practice".
2. **In most cases a teaching or carrying institution is present; Cherokee is the exception its own source stresses.** Abacus schools and vernacular manuals (numerals), a teacher plus memorised melodies (Guido), community networks and a national council (Cherokee; Walker and Sarbaugh stress dissemination "without the aid of Euroamerican institutions"), schooling and state campaigns (Hangul in the 20th century), the Cambridge group (Leibniz in England).
3. **A demand or incentive, usually commercial or political, is present.** Merchants (numerals), moneychanger-bankers and lawyers (double entry, indexes), political self-determination (Cherokee).
4. **A widening for one group can narrow it for another.** Arabic numerals presumed writing; counters did not (James on Otis).
5. **Users must adopt together.** Chrisomalis: a notation's usefulness is "evident" only when "an entire social group" learns it, and "a system with many users is functional for that reason alone". Bunčić finds that, in mainly Slavic and post-Soviet reforms, linguistic factors play "a very minor role" and "the most decisive factor seems to be timing".
6. **Specialist users adopt first and the benefit to a wider population is inferred, not measured.** This holds for indexes and double entry; for the spreadsheet it is my inference [unverified: see AUDIT_02].

### (iii) Which cases serve the paper, and which embarrass it

Best parallels, in order:
1. **Format interventions in the numeracy programme (Peters, Hibbard, Gigerenzer).** Peters et al. 2007 (Medical Care Research and Review; three studies) report that a format change helped the less numerate decide better; Hibbard and Peters 2003 and Peters et al. 2007 (Health Affairs) are reviews. It is a design-precedent argument for H-cost with human data, and it sits where the author wants it (numeracy). *Caveat:* abstracts only; effect sizes unknown.
2. **Hindu-Arabic numerals with the abacus schools.** The best-documented historical case with a numeracy label, a quantitative study of the carrier, and an active scholarly argument against both the efficiency story and the ban story. It has to be told as a bundle.
3. **Direct manipulation and the spreadsheet.** The closest interface precedent to the paper's own subject, with a design manifesto by the person who named it. Use it for the shape of the argument (visibility, reversibility, no syntax), not for a population-level effect, which I could not find.

A fourth, with care: **Cherokee**, as the single documented case of a writing system followed by fast literacy, with the caveats in section (b).

Cases that would embarrass the paper if used as written:
- **The 1299 Florence "ban".** It is not a ban on a better notation, and its motive is unknown. Using it as resistance to a superior system repeats a modern myth documented by Nothaft.
- **Guido "invented the staff"** and **"cut training from ten years to one or two"**. Both fail (Haines; no source found for the figure).
- **Pacioli and double entry as the cause of capitalism.** The causal claim is contested by Yamey and defended at the macro level by Ryan (2014); Sangster does not rebut it. Under Sangster's input definition double entries are 283 years older than Pacioli (the 1211 ledger; 1340 under the output definition), with entity-wide double entry roughly two centuries older (Sangster 2024 [online 2024], pp. 501, 507).
- **Hangul "built so common people learn it in days".** True of the design intention, false as a story of use. It shows spacing's problem (low cost does not spread a script) and so tells against H-cause.
- **Dvorak** as a "clear improvement that did not spread". The dispute is live.

### (iv) What Chrisomalis and the other sceptics imply

I read Chrisomalis only in fragments, so what follows is bounded by those fragments (pp. 22, 32, 79, 108, 123, 125) and by Nothaft and Schlimm and Neth.

- **Efficiency is not the explanation.** Chrisomalis calls efficiency for computation "a Western-centered and historically inaccurate benchmark" and says adoption needs "an entire social group" to learn the system. For the paper this removes the sentence "spaces were better, so they won". It leaves the more modest statement that spaces lowered a cost.
- **The numeracy–literacy link is historically contingent.** Chrisomalis (2020, p. 108 fragment) calls the pairing of literacy and numeracy as going "hand in hand" "a modern idea". The author's pairing of literacy and numeracy as the analogy structure needs a caveat; the model is borrowed from decision research, not from history.
- **No single origin.** Haines on Guido, Nothaft on the zero myth, Sangster on the definitional tradition behind the Pacioli story (the Pacioli comparison is this facet's), and Cajori on Leibniz's notation in England each take apart a one-agent, one-device story. The reading-history claim about spaces and "ignition" is of the same genre, and facets A to C need to test it with the same rigour. This is a pattern in how credits are made, not evidence about spaces.
- **Cognitive effects of notation are real but narrower than the tradition claims.** Chrisomalis (2020, p. 24 fragment) allows that there are effects; Schlimm and Neth show them as trade-offs; Zhang and Norman (unread) argue for representational effects. The author's "similar move" is defensible as design only if the claim stays at this scale.
- **I did not find a sceptic who denies that a convention can lower a cost.** The sceptics deny the *explanatory* weight of the convention in adoption and the *ranking* of notations.

### (v) Bearing on H-cost and H-cause

**H-cost.** Supported in a qualified form by the parallels. Each case where I could read evidence shows that a representation can move work from the person to the page (columnar place value, a staff that holds pitch, a display that holds state) and that some users then do the task more easily. The evidence that this is true of *spacing* is facet D's task, not mine. What the parallels add are two conditions: the saving depends on task and user (Schlimm and Neth; Krajcsi and Szabó; Norman and Fisher), and a convention that helps one group can burden another (James on Otis). The brief's "widened the group who could read without long training" survives only if the paper names which group and which training.

**H-cause.** Not supported by any parallel as a claim about a convention alone. The cases that came nearest are Cherokee (a new script, a motivated community, institutions improvised in a decade) and Guido (boys, a teacher, memorised melodies). The cases with the most literal "design for the common people" intention, Hangul, show the opposite. In numerals, the one quantitative study measures schools and manuals, not numerals. If H-cause is kept, it should read: "a convention was one necessary component of a bundle that widened literacy", which is H-cost plus a weak necessity claim. The parallels do not let me say more.

### (vi) Problem 4: is "one similar move" the right shape?

**No, in the shape the author uses.** Every case with usable evidence is a bundle, and the scholars who set the standard (Danna et al. on numerals; Haines on the staff; Carruthers and Espeland on double entry; Shneiderman on his own definition) say something close to it in their own words (Danna et al.: "organised learning"; Haines: "popularization", "pedagogy of synthesis"); only Guido and Shneiderman state a bundle themselves. The pattern the author reaches for is real as a genre of historical credit and as an observation that some conventions measurably lower a task's cost; the "single move ignites" form is the part that comes from retrospective hero-credit. Two readings keep the paper's idea intact without the weak form:
- Treat the space between words as *one component with a measured effect on cost* (H-cost) and say the paper wants the equivalent component for algorithmacy, accompanied by the equivalents of schools, manuals and incentives. DXD's own claim that the expertise is structural intervention (Part 1, lines 34, 142, 150) fits a bundle better than a move.
- The adoption cases (decimal time, spelling reform, Dvorak) imply the "move" must be adoptable by the party that controls the mediator, since users cannot switch together. This fits Part 1 line 118 (a coordination, not a medium).

### Claims in the author's framing, or in the wider story, that the sources contradict

1. "Florence banned Arabic numerals in 1299 as a rejection of the new system." Nothaft (2020, pp. 14–17): a guild rule on account books, motive "a matter of speculation"; the fraud rationale is not in the text.
2. "Roman numerals could not do arithmetic." Schlimm and Neth (2008): they could; the cost is in steps, not feasibility.
3. "Place-value notation is simply the more efficient system, which is why it spread." Chrisomalis (2010, p. 32; 2020, p. 22 fragments); Danna et al. (spread through schools); James on Otis (counters served people who could not write).
4. "Guido invented the staff." Haines (2008, p. 331): "untenable".
5. "The staff cut singers' training from ten years to one or two." Not found in Guido's text; the figures I found are "a hundred years" and "before the third day … in many weeks" (his memorising method, not the staff). Unverified; see the Pesce gap.
6. "Pacioli introduced double entry" and "double entry produced capitalism". Sangster (2024) on the origin date; Yamey and Sangster (2025) on the evidence for the causal claim; Ryan (2014) defends the causal claim; Carruthers and Espeland.
7. "The Cherokee nation was literate within months." No scholarly source supports "months"; Parins (as reviewed) gives seven years for almost all young and middle-aged men and about two-thirds by the mid-1830s.
8. "Hangul was built so that common people learned it in days, and it was therefore adopted by them." The design claim is in the 1446 text; use was marginal in elite and official life for centuries (Ivanova; Baek; Hangeul Museum for 1945).
9. "Dvorak is clearly better and lost only to habit." Contested (Liebowitz and Margolis; Norman and Fisher; Kay).
10. "Literacy and numeracy go together." Chrisomalis (2020, p. 108 fragment): a modern idea; in England counters served the unlettered (James on Otis).
