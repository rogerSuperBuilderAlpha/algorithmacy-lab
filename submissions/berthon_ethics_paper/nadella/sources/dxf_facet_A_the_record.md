**What this facet covers.** The documentary record of the move from *scriptio continua* to word separation: interpuncts in early Greek and Latin; the Roman abandonment of them; the aids that existed meanwhile; Insular (Irish, then Anglo-Saxon) separation in the seventh and eighth centuries; "aerated" script; the Continental spread; the vernaculars; Greek and Byzantine practice; what else changed on the page alongside spacing; and who read before and after. It also records exactly what Saenger claims about consequences, and how strongly. It supersedes the snippet-level work in `decision_experience_design/part2_ethics/literature/facets/E_reading_history_meaning.md`, section B1 and E1.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What does the record show about who changed the page, for whom and why; was spacing one move or one of a bundle; and what does that leave standing of H-cost (design precedent) and H-cause (ignition)?

### A1. Saenger, *Space Between Words* (1997)

**saenger1997space** — Saenger, P. (1997). *Space between words: The origins of silent reading*. Stanford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781503619081

- *What it argues.* A single thesis about the physiology of reading, stated at p. 2: "It is a premise of this book that given such differences in transcription, there is a correlation between a propensity to read orally in both past and contemporary cultures and the threshold in the duration of cognitive activity needed to achieve lexical access in that culture's script." Word separation lowers that threshold; the book then narrates, manuscript by manuscript, when and where it arrived. The author says what the book is not: "this book, which, I would stress, is not a general history of literacy" (Preface, p. x).

**Chronology, with locators.**
- *Interpuncts.* "While the very earliest Greek inscriptions were written with separation by interpuncts, points placed at midlevel between words, Greece soon thereafter became the first ancient civilization to employ scriptura continua" (pp. 9–10). Before vowels were added to the Phoenician alphabet, "all the ancient languages of the Mediterranean world … were written with word separation by either space, points, or both in conjunction" (p. 9). Saenger links scriptio continua to vocalic alphabets: "the adoption of vowels and of scriptura continua went hand in hand" (p. 9).
- *Roman abandonment.* The Romans "maintained the earlier Mediterranean tradition of separating words by points far longer than the Greeks, but they, too, after a scantily documented period of six centuries, discarded word separation as superfluous and substituted scriptura continua for interpunct-separated script in the second century A.D." (p. 10). Elsewhere: "A century after Seneca's death in A.D. 65, the Roman page had become increasingly similar to that of Greek, as the Romans gave up separation by interpuncts and adopted the Greek convention of scriptura continua" (p. 12); "Separation by interpuncts alone was primarily a phenomenon of Latin antiquity prior to the end of the second century A.D." (p. 29). The endnotes to pp. 9–11 (p. 300) cite Wingo (1972) for Latin separation "before the end of the second century"; the OCR interleaves the note text, so I cannot read Wingo's page. **Saenger's own dates differ by passage** (second century; a century after 65; and "the late second or third century A.D." at p. 69, where the subject is the Greek hyphen entering Latin "when the Romans adopted scriptura continua in emulation of the Greeks"). Facet E took p. 69 as a date for the adoption of scriptio continua in general. The audit finds that the dependent clause at p. 69 does date the Roman adoption of scriptura continua "in emulation of the Greeks" to "the late second or third century A.D.", so E's use is not a misreading; the sentence's subject is the hyphen, and pp. 10 and 29 give "the second century A.D.".
- *Interpunct page, not spaced page.* Roman interpuncts "were not usually accompanied by quantities of space any greater than that ordinarily placed between adjacent letters within a word" (p. 26); the largest space he recorded with an interpunct is "0.67 unities of space", against a minimum of 1.5 for rapid perception (p. 28); interpuncts and hederae "were ineffective aids to the physiological process of reading when compared with the interword spaces familiar in late medieval manuscripts and modern printed books" (p. 28) [page to check in a library copy]. He also notes a hedera in second-century inscriptions (p. 26).
- *Aids during the scriptio continua centuries.* In the imperial period "punctuation notes (which grammarians termed positurae) were tools intended primarily for the inexperienced reader, who employed them when mastering a text by copying it onto wax tablets, and they were not generally considered appropriate for a formal book" (p. 72). Such marks "were added by the reader rather than the scribe" (p. 72). "At the end of antiquity, the only 'punctuation' sometimes present in formal Roman books was spaces setting off paragraphs" (p. 72). Cassiodorus is the first he sees imagining the scribe inserting positurae (p. 72). *Per cola et commata* is Jerome's format for the Vulgate, "creating contextual space in which each line represented either a phrase, a clause, or a sentence" (p. 16); Saenger reads it as "a level of legibility in Latin appropriate to the middlebrow Christian audience of the late Roman world" (p. 124, with the remark that he has no textual evidence for the Semitic-influence theory). Foliation and pagination: attested in Greek codices from the third century, "never employed by the Romans" (p. 12; p. 77: "No evidence of foliation exists on any surviving Latin codex"). He mentions the *distinctiones* only as the sentence-divisions an Irish grammarian wanted readers to recognise (p. 83); I found no passage in the parts I read that discusses *paragraphos*.
- *Irish origin.* "The first separated Latin manuscript books in western Europe were Irish, and their text format was probably inspired by Syriac Gospel books" (p. 83). The first datable word-separated manuscripts: Dublin, Trinity College 60 (the Book of Mulling, "before 692") and Schaffhausen, Stadtsbibliothek Generalia 1 (Adomnán's *Vita Columbae*, "before 713") (p. 83). Earlier Irish manuscripts were aerated: "In Ireland, aerated manuscripts were uniquely a transient phenomenon of the seventh century, after which all Latin writing was separated" (p. 34). Within "three-quarters of a century of their initial experimentation" Irish scribes had moved from letter blocks to syllable blocks and word-and-morpheme spacing (p. 34). The Anglo-Saxon side: "Indeed, space for word separation was regularly inserted in the earliest surviving English books, and the first English documents, which date from the end of the seventh century, were all separated" (p. 87 per the audit's Google Books check; the earlier p. 86 came from OCR with merged page heads). Anglo-Saxon grammarians trained by Irish masters (Aldhelm, Bede, Tatwine, Boniface) wrote in separated script and wrote about it (pp. 83–89).
- *Aerated script on the Continent.* Aerated Latin codices "were common from the seventh century through the eleventh" in France, the Low Countries, Germany and Iberia, and "prevailed in central and southern Italy even into the thirteenth century"; formal Roman-law texts were aerated in central Italy in the fourteenth century (p. 34). "The Insular graphic innovation of word separation was not adopted by Continental scribes until the mid-tenth century" (p. 100). Continental aerated script is a hybrid of scriptio continua and Insular practice, arranged in "hierarchical word blocks" (pp. 35–36, 100). Insular scholars on the Continent (Alcuin, Sedulius Scottus) brought true separation, "Its general acceptance there, however, had to await a range of further developments" (p. 100).
- *Why the Continent waited, and what ended the wait.* "Continental monks perceived no need to make the reading of this corpus easier. Indeed, an ambiguous text format enhanced the mystery and power of clerics, who were the sole dispensers of the Divine Word to a laity almost entirely illiterate" (p. 120). After 950 a "new corpus of protoscholastic texts" demanded technical reading: "The desirability of making reading easier became apparent to Continental scholars, and to this end the word separation by space of Insular manuscripts became an essential linguistic tool" (pp. 120–121). A second model was Arabic: Latin translations of Arabic science "brought word separation with them and formed the earliest body of writings to circulate invariably in word-separated text format" (p. 124).
- *Not yet ubiquitous before the middle decades of the eleventh century.* "Word separation prior to the middle decades of the eleventh century was still not a ubiquitous practice in western Europe"; authors of unseparated texts wrote Latin "in no way inferior to that of the authors who observed word separation" (p. 177). Yet "By the mid-eleventh century, word separation was standard and ubiquitous" (p. 176, under Sigebert of Gembloux) [page to check in a library copy]. On Adémar of Chabannes: "in the eleventh century, the absence of regular word separation was not a sign of a low level of literacy or defective Latinity" (p. 185). "Canonical separation" (a regular space between every word, bar monosyllabic prepositions) "became the norm" in the twelfth century (p. 243) [page to check in a library copy].
- *Vernaculars.* Earliest vernacular texts in Latin letters were "aerated" and written "in the same geographical regions and at approximately the same time as the acceptance of the separation of words by space", Ireland and England first, Italy last, "in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries" (p. 97). "Writing in the vernacular, like word separation, was a way of accommodating reading" (p. 97). Vernacular spacing was looser: reading one's own tongue "required fewer graphic aids" (p. 97). "Separation of function words normal for Latin, had still not been realized in 1500 for the Romance vernacular tongues" (p. 266) [page to check in a library copy]. Regions with Celtic or Teutonic vernaculars show more space than Romance-speaking ones, "where the vernacular … was not consciously recognized to be a distinct language" (p. 41).
- *Greek, Byzantine, other scripts.* Separation of Greek "originated in manuscripts copied by Irish scribes in the early Middle Ages and became a frequent practice in Renaissance Italy and France well before it became normal in Byzantium in the second half of the sixteenth century"; Cyrillic by "the beginning of the seventeenth century"; for Sanskrit-derived scripts, "only in the modern period" (p. 13) [page to check in a library copy]. "In Byzantium, aerated Greek manuscripts were normal from the ninth century to the sixteenth" (p. 34). Hebrew: the Masoretic script, with its added vowels, was "probably modeled, directly or indirectly, on" Carolingian aerated writing (p. 10). Semitic scripts without vowels were separated throughout (p. 10).
- *Universal when?* No single date. Latin: normal from the twelfth to thirteenth century in northern Europe (p. 243 [page to check in a library copy]; "the norm of scholastic culture", p. 13), later in Italy (p. 34). Romance vernaculars: separation of function words unfinished in 1500 (p. 266 [page to check in a library copy]). Greek: second half of the sixteenth century (p. 13). Cyrillic: c. 1600 (p. 13 [page to check in a library copy]).

**Who did it and why (Saenger's account).**
- *Irish and Anglo-Saxon scribes and teachers.* "The origins of rapid, silent reading lie in the scribal techniques and grammatical teachings that developed in Ireland and England in the seventh and eighth centuries" (p. 83). Aldhelm, Bede, Tatwine and Boniface turn the *figura* of a word from an abstraction into "the outline of the block of letters on the page, where space separated one pars, or part, from another" (p. 85). The Irish context: "In contrast to the way in which young Romans learned Greek, Irish- [text lost; the sentence says Irish and Anglo-Saxon pupils learned Latin from word-separated grammars and glossaries]" (p. 91, OCR lost the middle of the sentence; I quote only the clean fragment and paraphrase the rest). Alphabetical glossaries were "an aid to those readers of Latin for whom the vernacular offered no phonic clues for comprehension" (p. 91); the earliest substantial alphabetical glossaries were bilingual (p. 91, paraphrased because the OCR is damaged there). So the foreign-language-learner motive is Saenger's too, with a second strand, explicit grammatical instruction about words (pp. 83–89).
- *Intention.* For the Irish lettered tradition he writes: "Irish scribes were not seeking to establish a text free of barbarisms, but rather … one that was unambiguous and easy to read" (pp. 94–95; merged page head) [page to check in a library copy]. Aeration he reads as "an unconscious quest for a mode of textual display that enhanced the reader's exploitation of his hitherto untapped neurophysiological faculties" (p. 33). So the intention to ease reading is stated for the Insular scribes, and inferred (not documented) for the earliest aerated work.
- *Who the aid was for.* Bede's diastole corrects "a scribe's omission of the word space that ordinarily guided the inexperienced reader" (p. 86). Separation gave "shortcuts for achieving the reading skills that an elite among the ancients had mastered only through a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (p. 85) [page to check in a library copy].
- *Why Rome did not.* Saenger gives a social explanation (pp. 10–12): "the ancient world did not possess the desire, characteristic of the modern age, to make reading easier and swifter because the advantages that modern readers perceive as accruing from ease of reading were seldom viewed as advantages by the ancients" (p. 11). Those advantages "include the effective retrieval of information in reference consultation, the ability to read with minimum difficulty a great many technical, logical, and scientific texts, and the greater diffusion of literacy throughout all social strata of the population" (p. 11). Reading was "oral and rhetorical by physiological necessity as well as by taste", the canon was small, and oralisation compensated; "the notion that the greater portion of the population should be autonomous and self-motivated readers was entirely foreign to the elitist literate mentality of the ancient world"; the labour of reading went to "skilled slaves, who acted as professional readers and scribes" (p. 11). Seneca (*Epist.* 40.11) reads the interpunct as a sign of Latin's "slower cadence", not a speed aid (p. 12).
- *Why the Continent did.* The protoscholastic and Arabic-science demand described above (pp. 120–124), plus a heresy argument: "errors in reading could have potentially grave consequences by breaching the subtle distinctions separating orthodoxy from heterodoxy" (p. 123).

**Other changes on the page alongside spacing (Saenger).**
- *Word order.* "Word order in medieval Latin affected the neurophysiology of reading in a way that was complementary to word separation, and together they laid the foundation for rapid, silent reading" (p. 14). Insular authors imposed fixed word orders from the sixth century (p. 89); Tatwine made Donatus's order of the parts of speech the order of a sentence (p. 90).
- *Punctuation.* The restructuring of written language "was a necessary prerequisite for the introduction of consistent signs of syntactic punctuation" (p. 15). New emblematic punctuation "emerged in the British Isles in manuscripts written with word separation" (p. 73). The question mark, "unknown in antiquity", appears in aerated Continental script (p. 36). Construction notes (Insular, ninth century), tie notes, résumé notes (pp. 69–79).
- *Accents and small signs.* Apices for vowel length, the diastole, the hyphen and the *trait d'union*, terminal forms, monolexic abbreviations (pp. 32, 40–41, 84–86).
- *Script and capitals.* Minuscule matters "insofar as it contributed, in conjunction with word separation, to giving each word a distinct image" (the "Bouma shape", pp. 18–19). Without an upper/lower-case distinction, Roman interpuncts lost "much of the potential difference in the Bouma shape" (pp. 28–29).
- *Musical notation.* Neumes spread in ninth- and tenth-century aerated texts (pp. 36–37).
- *Genres.* Five new Insular genres: alphabetical glossaries, interlinear translations, pocket Gospel books, personal prayer books, cartularies, and vernacular books (pp. 90–98). (He says "five" and lists six headings; I count six sections.)
- *Page apparatus.* The codex: "originated in the first century A.D. in Rome", with early codices "separated by points" (p. 77). Foliation in Latin codices begins in tenth-century aerated books and becomes current "after 1200" (p. 77). Schematic diagrams from the late tenth century at Reims, Fleury and Chartres (p. 79). Chapters and *distinctiones*, "tables of chapter headings, alphabetical tables by subject, and running headings became standard features of the scholastic codex" (p. 259); the coloured paragraph mark from the early thirteenth century (p. 259); indexes and tables in lay aristocratic books after 1350 (p. 270 area; locator approximate, OCR head missing).
- *Responsibility.* "Clearly, during the course of the nine centuries following Rome's fall, the task of separating written text, which had been for half a millennium a cognitive function of the reader, became instead the task of the scribe" (p. 13); Hugh's "presumption that it was the scribe's responsibility to prepare the text for the reader exemplified the transformed mentality that the previous century had wrought" (p. 245).

**Readers before and after (Saenger).** Before: the *lector* and the professional reader ("skilled slaves", p. 11); the elite reader trained through "a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (p. 85) and the *praelectio* (p. 8); "inexperienced readers" with reader-added marks (p. 72). Insular: Latin learners with a foreign mother tongue (pp. 91, 97). Carolingian Continent: monks reading a liturgical, patristic and grammatical corpus, mostly aloud (pp. 39, 120). Eleventh to thirteenth century: protoscholastic clerics, schoolmen, students (pp. 120–123, 259–260). Fourteenth and fifteenth century: princes, aristocrats, urban merchants (pp. 265–266, 273–276). The laity as a whole stays outside until late: before 1350 French kings "rarely read themselves" (p. 265).

**Bearing.**
- H-cost: **supports**, strongly, in the form "moved parsing work from reader to scribe and page" (pp. 13, 85, 245) and "lowered the cost" (pp. 7, 13). It also supports "the audience widened step by step": Insular learners, then clerical scholars, then lay elites.
- H-cause: **complicates, and in the author's strong form refutes.** Saenger disclaims a general history of literacy (p. x), and lists "the greater diffusion of literacy throughout all social strata" among the advantages of ease of reading that the modern age values (p. 11). His strongest causal claims concern scholasticism, privacy and dissent among readers already literate in Latin, and each comes centuries after the innovation. Silent reading itself existed (p. 8). The adoption story runs from demand to script as much as the reverse (p. 120).
- Problem 4: **bundle** (word order, punctuation, notes, accents, glossaries, tables, running heads, foliation, minuscule, capitals; pp. 14–15, 36, 69–79, 90–98, 259–260).
- Problem 1: **supports** the worry. The Continent took about 260 years to adopt it (c. 690 to the mid-tenth century, p. 100: 950 − 690 = 260) and about 350 years to make it common (c. 690 to the middle decades of the eleventh century, p. 177, taking c. 1040 as the middle: 1040 − 690 = 350), and mass literacy is outside the book's scope.

**Cautions on his evidence.** Every cognitive claim rests on 1970s–80s experiments with English-speaking readers, cited in the endnotes (not examined here); the "more than twice the normal quantity of fixations" figure (p. 7) is stated without a source in the text. Facet D should test it. His dates for individual codices come from his own 1984–85 survey (Preface, p. x), described in the chapters I did not read.

## (c) Synthesis

### 1. The chronology, as far as the record I could read carries it

On the question of dated manuscripts, I found no work that tests Saenger's chronology independently of Saenger in the sources I could open (rows 13–15). Tunbridge (read in part, card) and Parkes (snippets) agree on the Insular origin and the date range, but Tunbridge was supervised by Parkes and takes the earliest dates from Brown and Lowe (p. 194, n. 3), so her thesis adds no independent test of the chronology (P2). Bronner et al. may be the nearest test.

### 2. Who did it and why

Insular scribes and teachers did it, in a Latin-learning setting where Latin was a foreign language. Saenger (pp. 83–97), Tunbridge (thesis, card) and Parkes's snippet agree on that. Two strands appear: an ease-of-reading motive (Parkes p. 1; Tunbridge, p. 98 and Long Abstract p. 7; Saenger pp. 94–95 [page to check in a library copy]) and a grammatical-teaching strand about word shape (Saenger pp. 83–89; Tunbridge). Parkes's "grammar of legibility" is Parkes's name for the second, wider complex.

The intention is documented for the Anglo-Saxon grammarians, who wrote about it. For the first aerated books, "intention" is Saenger's inference ("an unconscious quest", p. 33). Whether the scribes aimed at *novices* or at *foreign-language learners* is not the same thing, and the sources I read point to learners of Latin, not to the general public.

The Continental adoption was by a different set of actors and for a different reason: clerics and scholars who needed to read hard dialectical and scientific texts (Saenger pp. 120–124). The ancient non-adoption, in Saenger's account, was also a design choice by those who held the power to make it: an elite that handed reading to slaves and valued oral delivery (p. 11).

### 3. Consequences, per Saenger

See the table in A1. In short: his strongest, least hedged claims are physiological ("freed the intellectual faculties", p. 13) and scholastic ("without these tools would not have been possible", p. 122). His claims about private reading, heresy and erotic and devotional reading are causal in verb ("sparked", "encouraged", "stimulated", "emboldened") but rest on case evidence from elite individuals and books. The Reformation claim is explicitly hedged ("may have fostered", p. 276). He offers no evidence that spacing raised the number of readers; on p. 11 he lists "the greater diffusion of literacy throughout all social strata of the population" among the advantages of ease of reading that the modern age values.

### 4. One move or a bundle?

A bundle, on Saenger's own text and on Parkes's framing. Spacing arrived with, and in some cases after, word order conventions (pp. 14–15, 89–90), accents and small signs (pp. 84–86), syntactic punctuation (p. 15, 73), construction notes (pp. 69–71), glossaries and other genres (pp. 90–98), then tables, chapters, foliation and running heads (pp. 77, 259), and minuscule script with capitals (pp. 18–19). Saenger himself writes that "word order and word separation are thus intimately linked" and that he concentrates on separation for narrative reasons (p. 17) [page to check in a library copy]. Two further points weigh against "one move": (i) the order of arrival is mixed, since foliation and diagrams come after spacing and word-order conventions come earlier, (ii) the strongest consequence claim (scholasticism) is stated for spacing "combined with conventions for word order" (p. 122). The record cannot isolate the contribution of spacing alone.

### 5. Readers before and after

The readership widened in stages, and the stages are not one population. Ancient: lectors, slaves, trained elites. Insular: Latin learners. Continental monks: unchanged for three centuries. After 950: clerical scholars. After 1300: princes and urban elites. The record I read has nothing on peasants, women outside convents, or the broad urban population. That is a result: the evidence for "who could read because of spacing" stops at lay elites.

### What the record supports

- **H-cost: supported.** The page-level change moved word-boundary work from the reader to the scribe (Saenger pp. 13, 245). It lowered the cost (pp. 7, 13, 85) and widened the group in steps. It was motivated, at the Insular start, by ease of reading for learners of a foreign language (Parkes p. 1, Tunbridge p. 98, Saenger pp. 94–95 [page to check in a library copy]).
- **H-cause: not supported in its strong form, with one qualification.** Saenger calls his book "not a general history of literacy" (p. x). On p. 11 he lists "the greater diffusion of literacy throughout all social strata of the population" among the "advantages that modern readers perceive as accruing from ease of reading", which the ancients "seldom viewed as advantages". He attaches this to ease of reading in general and presents it as a modern value; he offers no evidence that spacing raised the number of readers. The sources I could read give no measure of numbers of readers. He does claim that spacing was a cause of rapid silent reading, reference reading, scholastic thought and private devotion among Latin readers, and these claims carry the "cause" language ("indispensable", "sparked"). Those are the claims the paper can use for H-cause, and they come with three limits: they rest on a cognitive model that facet B and D must test; the adoption story runs from demand to script as well as the reverse (p. 120); and they unfold over six hundred years.
- **Problem 4: a bundle.** See section 4.
- **Problem 1:** the record confirms that "ignited literacy" outruns the evidence in hand (adoption took about 260 to 350 years on the Continent, Saenger pp. 83, 100, 177: c. 690 to c. 950 is about 260 years, c. 690 to c. 1040 about 350; the book disclaims a general history of literacy).

### Claims in the author's framing that the record contradicts or qualifies

1. **"Converting scriptura continua to spaces between words … ignited literacy."** Saenger's book "is not a general history of literacy" (p. x). The nearest claim is that "even readers of modest intellectual capacity could read more swiftly" (p. 13), which is about speed among Latin readers. The record gives no evidence for a spread of literacy. (On p. 11 Saenger lists diffusion of literacy among the advantages of ease of reading that the modern age values; he gives no evidence that spacing produced it.)
2. **"A critical UX move … ignited" as a single, dated move.** The Irish innovation dates to the late seventh century; Continental adoption began in the mid-tenth century (p. 100) and was not general before the middle decades of the eleventh century (p. 177; St Gall, "middle to late eleventh century", p. 105, is a regional exception); canonical separation was the norm in the twelfth century (p. 243 [page to check in a library copy]); Romance vernaculars lagged to 1500 for function words (p. 266 [page to check in a library copy]); Greek and Cyrillic to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (p. 13).
3. **"Word separation did not train the populace to read *scriptio continua*; it changed the interface so that reading no longer required the training" (draft.md:263).** Partly borne out and partly not. Saenger says separation supplied "shortcuts" for skills that "an elite" had acquired through "a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (p. 85 [page to check in a library copy]), and that the work moved to the scribe (p. 13). But the scribes needed grammatical training and made characteristic errors where teaching on word boundaries was missing (Tunbridge, pp. 124–150); skilled readers of unspaced text adapt (Saenger p. 7, conceding, but maintaining that the cost remains [page to check in a library copy]; Mirault et al. in facet E). "Populace" does not fit: the readers were learners of Latin, then clerics, then lay elites.
4. **The move as a designed intervention for the many.** The record shows scribes serving a learner readership (Insular) and then scholars (Continental). Rome's elite chose not to simplify; there is no sign of anyone designing for "the populace".
5. **Silent reading as the product.** Saenger concedes that Ambrose read silently (p. 8) and allows that letters and documents may have been read "silently in the strict sense, or more probably, quietly with suppressed voice" (pp. 8–9). His claim is about *rapid* and *reference* silent reading. He says it was not possible on the Continent in the Carolingian aerated script (p. 39).
6. **Facet E's reading of Saenger p. 69.** E took "the late second or third century A.D." as a date for the Roman adoption of scriptio continua. The audit finds that p. 69 does date both the hyphen's entry into Latin and the Roman adoption of scriptura continua "in emulation of the Greeks" to "the late second or third century A.D.", so E's use is not a misreading; pp. 10 and 29 give "the second century A.D." and p. 12 gives "a century after Seneca's death in A.D. 65". Saenger's dates differ by passage. E's quotation "the ancient world had no desire to make reading easier and swifter" is a paraphrase; the sentence is "the ancient world did not possess the desire, characteristic of the modern age, to make reading easier and swifter" (p. 11).
7. **The scribe-as-designer intent.** For the first aerated books Saenger calls the motive "unconscious" (p. 33). Treat "scribes designed spacing to ease reading" as documented for the Anglo-Saxon grammarians and as inference for the earliest Irish scribes.
