# H — Candidate moves: what could be the space between words for algorithmacy

*Corrected on 2026-10-12 against `../audit/AUDIT_03.md`: 38 factual fixes; verbatim quotation of the Nadella post cut to 13 words.*

*Facet H of the DXD foundation paper. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-12 (Sonnet 5.5, worktree `wt-dxd-foundation`). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Nine families of interface and system-design interventions that could play the role spacing played for the scribes in a coordination that runs through an algorithmic third party: (1) declared operating envelopes, behaviour descriptions and stable rules; (2) seams; (3) steering and control surfaces; (4) structural separation of what the mediator shows each party, with strategy-proof matching as the formal case; (5) defaults, standard forms and pre-segmented disclosures; (6) counter-delegation; (7) structural refusal, exit and contestability; (8) experiments in genuinely triadic settings; (9) the leads in the three Gemini essays. For each I report the measured evidence, who the participants were, and whether the setting had two humans and a mediator. Following the author's binding constraint, I sort every candidate by what it does with the work: **(i)** removes a task from the person by restructuring the artefact; **(ii)** hands the person a new reading, monitoring or steering task; **(iii)** hands the task to another agent on the person's behalf; **(iv)** removes the person's exposure (refusal, exit). The scribes' move is type (i). Transparency and explanation results enter only as evidence about when predictability holds (Ruling 1).

**The question the synthesis answers.** Which candidate, if any, takes work off an ordinary person the way spacing took segmentation off the reader; what measured evidence supports it; and what nobody has yet tested.

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text this session, or the entry reuses a repo card or earlier facet entry built from full text (named). *Read in part* = I opened the text and read named sections. *Abstract only* = a database abstract (Crossref, OpenAlex, Consensus) and nothing else; every number comes from that abstract. *Metadata only* = Crossref confirmed the record; I read no abstract. *Not accessible* = no text. **Second pass (2026-10-12).** I obtained an open copy of fifteen of the sources the synthesis leans on (ten read in part, five downloaded but read only to the abstract; see (d)) and recorded located quotations for the numbers in them. Every such copy is a working-paper, preprint or author version, not the version of record, and I say which. I opened each whole text, searched it for the figures and design statements, and read the passages I cite; I did not read any of them end to end, so I label them *read in part* and name the sections. None is *read in full*. Open copies obtained: Li 2017 (MPRA working paper), Rees-Jones and Skowronek 2018 (arXiv preprint), Arteaga et al. (NBER), Kling et al. (NBER), Mejia and Parker (SSRN preprint via the Indiana University figshare copy), Guess et al. 2023 (UNC repository), Zhu et al. 2026 (arXiv), Cabrera et al. 2023 (arXiv), Harper et al. 2015 (GroupLens author copy), Ehsan et al. 2024 (arXiv v2). **No open copy was found, so these stay abstract-only:** Hassidim et al. 2017 and 2021, Fradkin et al. 2021, Vaccaro et al. 2018, Moon et al. 2025, Seira et al. 2017 (the AEA PDF returned a bot wall). **Inman and Ribes 2019** was not opened (ACM bot wall, no author copy found); it rests on the repo card. ### B1. Declared envelopes, behaviour descriptions, stable rules

**cabrera2023improving** — Cabrera, Á. A., Perer, A., & Hong, J. I. (2023). Improving human-AI collaboration with descriptions of AI behavior. *PACM HCI, 7*(CSCW1), 1-21. https://doi.org/10.1145/3579612. *Verification:* Crossref; arXiv:2301.06937. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/cabrera2023improving.md; arXiv v1).
- *Design.* "3x3 between-subjects study, 25 participants per condition, 225 participants total" across three tasks (§4, Fig. 2). "Of the 225 participants, we removed 13 for failing the attention check. Additionally, we removed three other participants ... who had an accuracy of less than 10%" (§4.1), which leaves 209 analysed; the degrees of freedom of the three omnibus tests imply 67, 70 and 68 analysed (205 in all), and the paper does not reconcile the two. The AI was a "Wizard-of-Oz" system with set accuracies: 95% on 20 instances and 40% and 20% on two subsets of five (Fig. 2). Pay was $2 for about 15 minutes.
- *Results.* Reviews task: "The only significant pairwise difference was between the No AI and AI + BD conditions (p < 0.001, 95% C.I. = [0.06, 0.22]). While the AI by itself did not significantly improve the accuracy of the participants, AI supplemented with behavior descriptions led to significantly higher team accuracy" (§5, F(2,64) = 9.18). Satellite task: "no significant difference between conditions (F(2,67) = 0.63, p = 0.534)". "Despite the increased performance, there was no human-AI complementarity" (reviews). Birds (p = 0.048 in facet B) is confirmed. Most of the gain came from relying more on the AI where it was right, not from catching its errors.
- *Correction to facet B and the first pass:* 225 is the number recruited, not the number analysed; 209 were analysed by the text (205 by the degrees of freedom). *Move type:* (ii) with a (i) flavour (a short statement of where the system fails, which the person reads). Dyadic. Best evidence that a declared envelope can raise performance, mostly through heavier reliance on the AI where it was right; hand-set accuracies, so it tests the display, not a real system's envelope.

**bansal2019updates** — Bansal, G., Nushi, B., Kamar, E., Weld, D. S., Lasecki, W. S., & Horvitz, E. (2019). Updates in human-AI teams: Understanding and addressing the performance/compatibility tradeoff. *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, 33*(1), 2429-2437. https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v33i01.33012429
- *Verification:* Crossref (title, authors, pages); OpenAlex abstract. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/bansal2019updates.md).
- *What it establishes:* "updates that increase AI performance may actually hurt team performance." The authors define compatibility of an update with prior user experience and show that "current machine learning algorithms do not produce compatible updates" on three high-stakes classification tasks; a retraining objective that penalises new errors recovers compatibility at some cost in accuracy. Participant numbers: 25 workers per condition in the mental-model studies, with the bottom quartile of scorers removed as likely spammers (p. 2433); the group sizes for the update study are not stated.
- *Move type:* (i), applied to the mediator rather than the interface: freezing the error pattern removes the task of re-learning where the system fails. Dyadic (one human, one classifier). *Bearing:* the one controlled experiment on backward-compatible updates. The crowd participants played only the abstract CAJA game; the recidivism, mortality and credit-risk work used classifiers alone, with no people. It is not a platform where a rule change reaches workers.

**chen2024chatgpt** — Chen, L., Zaharia, M., & Zou, J. (2024). How is ChatGPT's behavior changing over time? *Harvard Data Science Review, 6*(2). https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.5317da47
- *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/chen2024chatgpt.md).
- *What it establishes:* The "same" LLM service changed between March and June 2023: GPT-4 prime-versus-composite accuracy fell from 84% to 51%; GPT-4 grew less willing to answer sensitive questions; code-formatting errors rose. Measures the model, not people. The cleanest evidence is for instruction-following drift, which the authors offer as a partial explanation, not a proven one.
- *Move type:* n/a (motivates (i) stability). *Bearing:* shows that silent updates are a real, measured property of the mediator. It supplies no evidence that stability helps people.

**allouah2026what** — Allouah, A., Besbes, O., Figueroa, J. D., Kanoria, Y., & Kumar, A. (2026). What is your AI agent buying? Evaluation, biases, model dependence, and emerging implications for agentic e-commerce. *Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2026* (Crossref pages 8697-8700, a four-page item; the findings quoted here come from the longer arXiv:2508.02630 v3 abstract). https://doi.org/10.1145/3774904.3792943
- *Verification:* Crossref; arXiv 2508.02630v3. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/allouah2026what.md).
- *What it establishes:* Shopping agents concentrate demand on a few "modal" products; "model updates can drastically reshuffle market shares"; randomised trials show position biases that vary by provider and model version and persist in text-only interfaces; agents penalise sponsored tags and reward platform endorsements; a seller-side agent that tweaks descriptions gains market share, but not generally: the gain occurs in 33% of category-model pairs and sometimes reverses (p. 22). The authors also report that basic rationality has become near-perfect (p. 13); the instability is in preferences and position, not competence. The headless replication used three August-generation models only.
- *Move type:* evidence on (iii). *Triadic?* Partly: consumer agent, platform, seller, with no human in the loop during the choice. *Bearing:* it is the strongest evidence that moving the task to an agent moves the legibility problem to the agent: instability across model updates and position bias are, on the project's inference, properties the person could no longer see (no person appears in the study).

**rahman2021invisible** — Rahman, H. A. (2021). The invisible cage: Workers' reactivity to opaque algorithmic evaluations. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 66*(4), 945-988. https://doi.org/10.1177/00018392211010118
- *Verification:* Crossref (volume 66, issue 4, pages 945-988). *Read status:* abstract only.
- *What it establishes:* Three years of interviews, archives and participant observation on a freelance platform (n of interviews not in the abstract). "the criteria for success and changes to those criteria are unpredictable." Workers experimented with the criteria or withdrew activity; reactions depended on prior success and platform dependence.
- *Move type:* n/a. *Triadic?* Yes in structure (freelancer, client, rating algorithm), qualitative. *Bearing:* the best account of what unannounced criteria change does to ordinary workers. It motivates stability and notice but measures no intervention.

**Also in this family, qualitative only:** bucher2020pacifying (12,294 scraped freelancer comments; "anticipatory compliance" with an assumed algorithm), mendonca2022disconnecting (one UK courier case; unilateral intra-platform changes fragment collective action). Abstract only for both. Neither tests an intervention.

**mitchell2019model** — Mitchell, M., Wu, S., Zaldivar, A., Barnes, P., Vasserman, L., Hutchinson, B., Spitzer, E., Raji, I. D., & Gebru, T. (2019). Model cards for model reporting. *FAT\* '19*, 220-229. https://doi.org/10.1145/3287560.3287596
- *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/mitchell2019model.md). *What it establishes:* a proposal for a documentation format. *Move type:* (ii). *Bearing:* a proposal, not a measured effect.

**crisan2022interactive** — Crisan, A., Drouhard, M., Vig, J., & Rajani, N. (2022). Interactive model cards: A human-centered approach to model documentation. *FAccT '22*, 427-439. https://doi.org/10.1145/3531146.3533108
- *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract; arXiv 2205.02894. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/crisan2022interactive.md).
- *What it establishes:* A think-aloud and interview study with 30 participants (experts, then non-expert analysts who use NLP models) comparing standard and interactive model cards. The findings are thematic ("understandability", "sensemaking & skepticism", "trust & safety"). No performance measure; the authors do not test the interactive card's effect on understanding the way they count comprehension for the standard card.
- *Move type:* (ii). Dyadic. *Bearing:* the only user study of model cards with non-experts that I found; it measures reactions, not accuracy or workload.

**Driving automation, the nearest measured "declared envelope" outside LLMs** (Singer, Boelhouwer and Matsuo read in full from cards; Zhou abstract only; all dyadic: driver and system):
- **singer2022driver** — Singer, J., Tefft, B. C., Benson, A., Jenness, J. W., & Horrey, W. J. (2022). Driver expectations of a partial driving automation system in relation to branding and training. *Human Factors, 66*(5), 1531-1544. https://doi.org/10.1177/00187208221143024. 90 drivers (read in full; card: library/singer2022driver.md), randomly assigned to a name and training that stressed capabilities (AutonoDrive) or limitations (DriveAssist), and to three training modes. Capability framing raised expectations of crash avoidance "in a variety of driving scenarios, including many in which the system would not work", and willingness to take more risk; most differences persisted after driving. "Accuracy of technical information does not guarantee understanding." Move type (ii).
- **zhou2019effect** — Zhou, H. P., Itoh, M., & Kitazaki, S. (2019). Effect of instructing system limitations on the intervening behavior of drivers in partial driving automation. *Cognition, Technology & Work, 22*(2), 321-334. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10111-019-00568-1. A 2 x 2 simulator study with 24 novice drivers per condition. A description of limits in terms of scenes (not functions) gave 95% safe interventions and 2.0 s reaction time against 88% and 2.3 s. Move type (ii).
- **boelhouwer2019should** — Boelhouwer, A., van den Beukel, A. P., van der Voort, M. C., & Martens, M. H. (2019). Should I take over? *Transportation Research Part F, 60*, 669-684. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2018.11.016. 28 participants, video simulator. "the system information did not appear to support the participants in correctly deciding whether to take over"; the authors write that the mental models "did not seem to (sufficiently) change" (abstract), inferred from decision accuracy and comments; they did not elicit the models. Group 2 was nominally higher, and the information group learned less over the session. Move type (ii). Null (14 per group, so a failure to detect).
- **matsuo2026educational** — Matsuo, R., Liu, H., Hiraoka, T., & Wada, T. (2026). An educational human machine interface providing request-to-intervene trigger and reason explanation. *IEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems, 56*(3), 650-661. https://doi.org/10.1109/thms.2026.3679748. Between-group simulator study, N = 45 (15 per group). Comprehension: cue plus reason beat no cue (adjusted p = 0.001) and cue only (p = 0.017). Collisions in the final failure trial: 7 of 15 with no cue, 6 of 15 with the cue only, 1 of 15 with cue and reason (Fisher p = 0.044). The cue alone gave a small, non-significant timing change; the reason carried the result. Move type (ii).
- *Bearing of the group.* Telling drivers the limits has mixed effects: it helps when framed concretely (Zhou), backfires when the framing flatters (Singer), and fails when delivered as a manual (Boelhouwer); in Matsuo the reason, not the cue, carried the result. The envelope *as an announcement* is a reading task. An envelope enforced by the artefact (the vehicle refuses outside its ODD) is a different object, and I found no study that compares announced and enforced envelopes. The SAE J3016 term is held in the dossier; I did not open the standard.

**ehsan2024seamful** — Ehsan, U., Liao, Q. V., Passi, S., Riedl, M. O., & Daumé III, H. (2024). Seamful XAI: Operationalizing seamful design in explainable AI. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 8*(CSCW1), 1-29. https://doi.org/10.1145/3637396

- *What it establishes:* Participants were 43 people, "AI practitioners and real end-users", who worked through a scenario-based co-design activity set in lending (§4); the 258 sign-up submissions were screened to 43 and 2,796 minutes of session video were transcribed (§4.3-4.4). - *Move type:* (ii), squarely: seams hand the person something to read and act on. Dyadic. *Bearing on Ruling 1:* a legibility remedy by another name; the evidence for it is a qualitative design study.

### B3. Steering and control surfaces

**harper2015putting** — Harper, F. M., Xu, F., Kaur, H., Condiff, K., Chang, S., & Terveen, L. (2015). Putting users in control of their recommendations. *RecSys '15*, 3-10. https://doi.org/10.1145/2792838.2800179
- *Verification:* Crossref (six authors). *Read status:* read in full (card: library/harper2015putting.md) from the GroupLens author copy (8 pp.). The ACM version was not read.

### B4. Structural separation and strategy-proof design

This family is the closest formal analogue of type (i) in a coordination with a mediator, and it needs care. A strategy-proof mechanism makes truthful reporting a dominant strategy, so the participant no longer has to model what others will report. The task "guess the counterpart and the mediator" is removed by the rule, not taught.

**li2017obviously** — Li, S. (2017). Obviously strategy-proof mechanisms. *American Economic Review, 107*(11), 3257-3287. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160425
- *Verification:* Crossref (single author). Open copy: MPRA paper no. 78930, "This version: 17 April 2017", 62 pp., http://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/78930/. *Read status:* read in part (card: library/li2017obviously.md) from that working paper; proofs and appendix tables skimmed. The AER version was not read; check that the experiment survives there.
- *What it establishes (theory).* "A mechanism is obviously strategy-proof (OSP) if it has an equilibrium in obviously dominant strategies" (Abstract). Introduction: "choosing when to quit in an ascending clock auction is the same as choosing a bid in a second-price sealed-bid auction ... Nonetheless, laboratory subjects are substantially more likely to play the dominant strategy under a clock auction than under sealed bids (Kagel et al., 1987)."
- *What it establishes (experiment).* "I conduct a laboratory experiment to test the theory. In the experiment, I compare three pairs of mechanisms. In each pair, both mechanisms implement the same choice rule. One mechanism is obviously strategy-proof. The other mechanism is strategy-proof, but not obviously strategy-proof ... subjects play the dominant strategy at significantly higher rates under the OSP mechanism ... This effect occurs for all three pairs of mechanisms, and persists even after playing each mechanism five times with feedback" (Introduction). Design (§5.1): across-subjects, "144 subjects in 36 groups of 4 (with 9 groups in each treatment)", recruited from the student population at the Ohio State University Experimental Economics Laboratory, August 2015; subjects "made $37.54" on average. Results (§5.2 for the auction pair; the serial-dictatorship figures are in §5.3 and compare only those two games, since the auction pairs use a different measure): "An auction is 31 percentage points more likely to have a closing price within $2.00 of the equilibrium price under AC (OSP) compared to 2P (SP)"; in the random-serial-dictatorship pair, "Under SP-RSD, 36% of games do not end in the dominant strategy outcome. Under OSP-RSD, 7% of games do not end in the dominant strategy outcome ... In SP-RSD, 29% of submitted rank-order lists contain errors."
- *Move type:* (i), and the only source here that states the design move as removing a cognitive task. Participants: university students in stylised games, not novices in a real market. **Correction:** my first pass said Li reports no experiment of its own. That was an inference from the abstract and was wrong.

Standardizing privacy notices: An online study of the nutrition label approach. *CHI '10*, 1573-1582. https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753561. *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract. *Read status:* abstract only. *What it establishes:* online study, 764 participants; a standardised table, two short variants, full-text policies and layered policies compared; "standardized privacy policy presentations can have significant positive effects on accuracy and speed of information finding and on reader enjoyment." **kling2012comparison** — Kling, J. R., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E., Vermeulen, L. C., & Wrobel, M. V. (2012). Comparison friction: Experimental evidence from Medicare drug plans. *Quarterly Journal of Economics, 127*(1), 199-235. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr055
- *Verification:* Crossref (five authors). *Read status:* read in full (card: library/kling2012comparison.md) from the NBER working paper 17410 (Sept 2011). The QJE text was not read (a PMC copy exists, PMC3314343; the Europe PMC render failed).
- *Design.* "One group of seniors—the intervention group—was presented with personalized price information created by entering their drug data into Medicare's Plan Finder website ... A comparison group was given only the address of this website" (Introduction). "Patients of the University of Wisconsin Hospital system age 65 and over made up the sample frame. Letters of invitation were mailed to 5,873 subjects ... leading to a baseline sample size of 451"; "406 people completed the baseline survey, were randomly assigned in the experiment, and completed the 2007 follow-up survey—forming our main analytic sample" (Data). - *Results.* "The intervention group switched plans 28 percent of the time, whereas the comparison group only did so 17 percent of the time" (Introduction); the "difference of 11.5 percentage points" is in Table III; "average cost savings of the intervention—across the entire intervention group including non-switchers—was about $100 per year, or about 5 percent of the average predicted cost" (Introduction).
- *Move type:* (i) for compare-and-compute, but the letter was a bundle (a personalised computation, a recommended default, a statement of benefit and a deadline) and the authors say further work "could unbundle these effects" (§VIII); the person still has to switch. Participants: patients aged 65 and over at one US hospital system, n = 406. *Correction:* the first pass called this a field experiment on Medicare Part D enrollees without a sample size.

**seira2017are** — Seira, E., Elizondo, A., & Laguna-Müggenburg, E. (2017). Are information disclosures effective? Evidence from the credit card market. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 9*(1), 277-307. https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20140404. Read in full (card: library/seira2017are.md). A randomised trial on indebted Mexican cardholders: "providing salient interest rate disclosures had no effects, while comparisons and de-biasing messages had only modest effects at best." *Bearing:* the rate disclosure (a reading task) did nothing. The months-to-pay disclosure did not change debt and raised delinquency among frequent interest payers and high-debt clients by 0.011 points in April, 7.4% of the mean (p. 295), which cuts against reading disclosure as merely inert. The authors say the rate message "gave no benchmark for comparing the interest rate to that of other banks" and cite Kling et al. as showing that comparison frictions matter (p. 303): the missing ingredient is the benchmark, not the reading. The contrast with Kling is therefore suggestive, not a clean sign that restructuring beats informing, and Kling's letter was a bundle.

**poursabzi2021manipulating** — Poursabzi-Sangdeh, F., Goldstein, D. G., Hofman, J. M., Wortman Vaughan, J., & Wallach, H. (2021). Manipulating and measuring model interpretability. *CHI '21*, 1-52. https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445315. *Verification:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract; arXiv 1802.07810 v5. *Read status:* read in full (card: library/poursabzi2021manipulating.md). *What it establishes:* "pre-registered experiments (N = 3,800)" varying the number of features and whether model internals were visible. Participants "who saw a clear model with few features could better simulate the model's predictions. However, we did not find that participants more closely followed its predictions. Furthermore, showing participants a clear model meant that they were less able to detect and correct for the model's sizable mistakes, seemingly due to information overload." The mistake-detection result is conditional: it holds in Exps. 1, 2 and 4 (without the message) and not in Exp. 3, where people made an initial guess before seeing the model; the Exp. 2 replication holds for one of the two apartments only (apartment 12; apartment 11, p = 0.069); and the worst-simulation, least-following result for the clear eight-feature model is post hoc. A citation of the abstract's sentence without these conditions would overstate it. *Bearing:* evidence about when predictability does not come from transparency (Ruling 1). It also bears on the Gemini "information budget" lead (B9).

**fogliato2022who** — Fogliato, R., Chappidi, S., Lungren, M., et al. (2022). Who goes first? Influences of human-AI workflow on decision making in clinical imaging. *FAccT '22*, 1362-1374. https://doi.org/10.1145/3531146.3533193. Read in full (card: library/fogliato2022who.md). 19 veterinary radiologists; "participants who are asked to register provisional responses in advance of reviewing AI inferences are less likely to agree with the AI regardless of whether the advice is accurate" and the order did not lengthen time. The anchoring effect was small in absolute terms (two to six points) and minimal for critical findings, and several key tests would miss the 0.05 level (the authors use 0.1). *Move type:* (ii)/(i): a sequencing rule. Dyadic. Relevant to "pacing".

### B6. Counter-delegation

**zhu2026choose** — Zhu, K., Thain, N., Tsai, V., Wexler, J., & Qian, C. (2026). Choose your agent: Tradeoffs in adopting AI advisors, coaches, and delegates in multi-party negotiation. arXiv:2602.12089v3 (27 Jun 2026). *Verification:* arXiv PDF in hand; Consensus record (listed as arXiv, a 2025-26 preprint; I found no venue). *Read status:* read in part (card: library/zhu2026choose.md; main text read in full, appendices skimmed). Peer review: none found.
- *Design.* Online experiment, N = 243 Prolific participants (324 recruited; §3, Methods), three-person bargaining games, "three multi-turn bargaining games in groups of three", each with one LLM assistance mode: Advisor (AI proposes, human decides), Coach (human proposes, AI critiques) or Delegate (AI acts). Within-subjects, counterbalanced; Table 4 reports "N = 243 active individuals nested in 81 groups; N = 216 baseline individuals nested in 72 groups"; the all-human baseline (N = 216) is a *historical control* from an earlier study with identical rules (§6.2). All three modes run on one model that outperformed humans.
- *Results.* "participants strongly prefer the higher-control Advisor (44%) over the Delegate (19%), yet groups only significantly increase collective surplus under Delegate access." The abstract's "significantly" overstates the tables: the group-level Delegate effect is p = .033 raw and p = .100 after Holm–Bonferroni correction, and the authors call it a "suggestive trend" (Table 1, p. 0:11). Preferences: Advisor 107, Coach 37, Delegate 47, none 52 (Table 2). Joint surplus per accepted trade: AI-assisted proposals beat manual ones in every mode, but only the Advisor gap is significant (Advisor +0.523, p < 0.01; Coach +0.154; Delegate +0.180). The mechanism is a "human filter": users "modify, override, or ignore" AI proposals and revert to human-baseline trades. "The Delegate advantage arises not from a different AI capability but from bypassing this filtering step altogether." The non-adopter spillover (+21.6%) "did not survive multiple-comparison correction (p adj = 0.16)" and the authors warn of selection bias; I do not use it.
- *Limits the authors state.* A "stylized bargaining game"; single session; the human filter "may serve a vital welfare-preserving function by maintaining social norms"; SUTVA is violated by design; the baseline is historical.
- *Move type:* the direct experimental contrast of (ii) (Advisor, Coach) against (iii) (Delegate). *Triadic?* Multi-party and strategically interdependent, **but the third party is two other human players, not an algorithmic mediator that reads both sides.** The AI is each player's own agent, so this is a test of counter-delegation inside a multi-party game, not of a mediated triad.
- *Bearing.* It is the only controlled experiment I found in which handing a task to an agent showed an advantage over handing the person an advisory reading task, in a setting where outcomes depend on others; the advantage is a suggestive trend (p = .033 raw, .100 corrected), not a corrected-significant result. It also shows the preference-performance gap that Bansal and Buçinca documented in dyads (Zhu cites both). It cuts both ways for the thesis: the delegation gain comes from removing the person's judgement, which the author's "engagement layer" ruling (the user reads and rewrites the mediator) would resist.

**zhu2025automated** — Zhu, S., Sun, J., Nian, Y., South, T., Pentland, A., & Pei, J. (2025). The automated but risky game: Modeling agent-to-agent negotiations and transactions in consumer markets. *Proceedings of the Natural Legal Language Processing Workshop 2025*. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.nllp-1.2 (arXiv 2506.00073; PDF in hand, abstract-level). Abstract only. "deal-making with LLM agents in consumer settings is an inherently imbalanced game: different AI agents have large disparities in obtaining the best deals for their users", and anomalies "might lead to financial loss for both consumers and merchants". **The earlier dossier called this a preprint; Crossref now shows an ACL-anthology workshop paper (peer-reviewed at workshop level).** No human subjects; an agent benchmark.

**terrucha2025humans** — Terrucha, I., et al. (2025). **vaccaro2026advancing** — Vaccaro, M., Caosun, M., Ju, H., Aral, S., & Curhan, J. R. (2026). **alavi2026when** — Alavi, S., & Nozari, S. (2026). When agents shop for you: Role coherence in AI-mediated markets. arXiv:2604.26220. Preprint; read in full (card: library/alavi2026when.md). **hua2026passive** — Hua, W., Huang, Z., Payne, T., Yousefi, S., Amershi, S., & Celikyilmaz, A. (2026). **calacci2022bargaining** — Calacci, D., & Pentland, A. (2022). **irani2013turkopticon** — Irani, L. C., & Silberman, M. S. (2013). Turkopticon: Interrupting worker invisibility in Amazon Mechanical Turk. *CHI '13*. https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470742. **dietvorst2018overcoming** — Dietvorst, B. J., Simmons, J. P., & Massey, C. (2018). ### B8. The triadic setting itself

**midkiff1993identification** (and **pritchett1994variations**, **hansman1995party**) — see the repo cards `midkiff1993.md`, `pritchett1994.md`, `hansman1995.md` and `triadic_hf_search_notes.md`. ## (c) Synthesis

### (i) Candidates by type, strength of evidence, and setting

Strength key: **S** = randomised field or large preregistered experiment on the move, with a performance or behaviour outcome; **M** = controlled lab or online experiment, performance outcome; **P** = perceptions or satisfaction only; **Q** = qualitative or design study; **T** = theory or proposal only; **0** = nothing found. ### (ii) Which candidate best fits "takes the work off the person the way spacing took segmentation off the reader"?

**Best fit: a mediator rule that removes a strategic task from participants and does so visibly. The model case is strategy-proof matching, extended by Li's obvious strategy-proofness; the field analogues are simultaneous reveal and withheld cues.** Spacing moved a task from the reader to the page. A dominant-strategy rule moves "guess what the mediator and the others will do" from the participant to the mechanism. It is type (i), it is formal, and it was built for non-experts (school applicants) [unverified: see AUDIT_03]. The evidence also shows what the analogy would have to carry.

The evidence does not let me say "any single candidate deserves the name." Three results cut against it:

1. **The task removal is incomplete.** 23% of the 1,714 students who completed the survey and passed the exclusion criteria misreport in an analogous task (Rees-Jones; the 1,714 are about 51.9% of the students contacted), roughly half of the observed truth-telling under TTC and DA in their school-allocation lab setting is naive (Guillén & Veszteg, abstract), and no stable mechanism is obviously strategy-proof (Ashlagi & Gonczarowski). The removal succeeds for those who follow the default and fails for those who do not.
2. **Presentation, not only structure, matters.** Li's own lab experiment shows it with formally equivalent pairs: in random serial dictatorship, "Under SP-RSD, 36% of games do not end in the dominant strategy outcome. Under OSP-RSD, 7%" (§5.3; 144 subjects in the whole experiment, 18 groups per RSD arm; a one-shot variant gave 40% against 8%, §5.4). Iterative deferred acceptance beat standard deferred acceptance (Bó & Hakimov, abstract), and menu descriptions are being designed to expose strategy-proofness (Gonczarowski et al.; the version on file is theory only, with no experiment). The lesson that carries over from spacing is that the same logical structure helps more when its form lets the person see that the work is gone. This also says that a type (i) move depends on a type (ii)-looking display.
3. **The removed task relocates.** In Chile and New Haven, deferred acceptance left the search and stopping decisions to families. In Chile (a regression discontinuity at a 30% predicted non-placement risk), 21.6% of warned applicants added schools, and for those compliers risk fell by 15.5 percentage points, 58% of the below-threshold mean, against 3.3 points (13%) for all warned applicants (Arteaga et al., Introduction; pp. 26–27). Kling's letter (a bundle of computed comparison, default and deadline) removed comparison and left action (switching 28% against 17%, n = 406 patients of one hospital system); Seira's TILA-type rate disclosure removed nothing and had no effect, and its months-to-pay disclosure raised delinquency among some clients.

A second claimant is the delegate. In Zhu K. et al., only the Delegate arm showed a positive group-surplus estimate against a historical baseline (p = .033 unadjusted; .10 after Holm); Advisor and Coach did not differ from baseline. The authors call the Delegate result a "suggestive trend"; it does not survive correction. The authors' account is that the delegate bypassed the person's filter, a mechanism analysis rather than a tested contrast. It fits the form "the person no longer has to do it". It does not fit "the person can do it without the training", because the person must now trust an agent whose behaviour is unstable across model updates and biased in ranking (Allouah), can leak preference (Alavi), and carries precision errors that cannot be corrected mid-game (Terrucha). It trades one competence (interpret the mediator) for another (specify and supervise the agent). It is also not the spacing pattern: spacing reallocated work to a passive artefact that carries no agency, and a delegate is an active party.

**Three-part answer to problem 4 of the plan (one move or a complex).** The measured evidence points to a complex. What reduced work in the triadic field experiments was changing what the mediator exposes to each party and when (4b), plus a pre-computed comparison where a choice remains (5b). What reduced work in the matching literature was a rule that makes the report dominant (4a), plus choice support for the tasks left outside it (Arteaga). Parkes's "complex of conventions" fits better than a single mark.

### (iii) Gaps: experiments nobody has run (a research agenda the paper could propose)

1. **A mediated-triad test of the type (i) versus type (ii) contrast.** Zhu et al. compare advisor, coach and delegate among three humans. I found no study that ran the same contrast (Consensus search only) with a mediator that reads both sides, e.g. a simulated ride-hail or marketplace matcher with two human parties, where one arm restructures what the mediator commits (a rule, a stable envelope), one arm gives a reading aid (explanation or description), one arm gives a delegate. Outcome: joint surplus and each party's regret, with novices and experienced users sampled separately.
2. **Enforced versus announced envelopes.** Cabrera tells people where the model fails; the driving studies announce limits. I found no study that compares an announced envelope with one the artefact enforces (refusal outside the domain, bounded output), with the same underlying system.
3. **Stability under update, measured on people.** Bansal et al. test compatibility in an abstract game with crowd workers, and on classifiers alone for three high-stakes tasks. I found no test of what a silent rule change does to worker or seller outcomes in a controlled setting, nor whether advance notice or a frozen interface changes recovery time. Rahman documents the harm qualitatively.
4. **Task relocation after a structural removal.** Arteaga et al. show it for school choice. The paper could propose measuring, for each type (i) intervention, which task the removal exposes (search, timing, intent specification) and what it costs novices.
5. **Counter-delegation against a platform's own agent with human subjects.** I found no peer-reviewed human-subject evidence (B6; Consensus search only). A first design: a buyer's personal agent against a platform ranker, with the user's information retained or leaked, tested on novices.
6. **Uptake and effect of platform control and exit options.** I found no measured uptake of the DSA's non-profiled feed option or of platform feed controls in the field (rows 5, 13). Alqabandi and Hsu measure expectations and satisfaction; I found no one who measures decisions made with and without a control, in a setting with a counterpart.
7. **Novice-versus-expert effects for every candidate.** The studies seldom split by expertise (Moon splits by literacy; Rees-Jones and Hassidim report cognitive-ability correlates; most others do not split). The author's claim is about novices; the evidence is mostly pooled.

### (iv) Where the candidates conflict with the standing rulings

- **Ruling 1 (not legibility).** Seams (B2), model cards, standard disclosure formats (Kelley), behaviour descriptions (Cabrera), driving limit announcements and "why am I seeing this" controls all hand the person something to read or tune; by the four-type sort they are type (ii). Cabrera and Zhou show they can work. Under the ruling they may enter only as evidence about when predictability holds; they cannot be offered as the move.
- **The "engagement layer" lens** (`ENGAGEMENT_LAYER.md`: the user reads and rewrites the shared mediator). It conflicts with the two best-supported candidates. Strategy-proof rules and withheld cues work because the participant does *not* have to read or rewrite; Zhu et al.'s delegate, on the authors' account, gains (a suggestive trend) because the person's judgement is bypassed. It also conflicts with stability: the envelope and compatible-update candidate asks the mediator to hold still while the lens says steering is ordinary use. Part 1 lists "bounded envelopes" and "backward-compatible updates" next to "counter-delegation" and "refusal"; the evidence says these items do not travel together.
- **Part 1 line 146** claims a costless exit "makes the parties substitutable, collapsing an irreducible triadic mediator back into a separable, manageable dyad." That is an information-theoretic argument, not an empirical result, and Gauthier shows persistent effects of prior exposure, while Guess shows the exit changed exposure and activity but not attitudes. I do not rule on the formal claim.
- **Part 1 line 144** cites Dietvorst et al. for the modification right as restoring willingness to decide through an algorithm. The control-surface studies show that perceived control and satisfaction rise (Harper, Knijnenburg, Moon), sometimes even for placebo controls (Vaccaro), but none measures decision quality. Willingness is not performance.
- **Ruling 3 (analogy, not mechanism).** The author left this ruling open for this paper on 2026-10-12 (`RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §3), so what follows is a reading, not a binding constraint. The finding that the task removal is incomplete (Rees-Jones; Guillén & Veszteg) and relocates (Arteaga) matches the plan's H-cost reading (a design precedent that lowered a cost, not a cause) more than H-cause. I do not use the history here; I note that nothing in the H evidence needs an "ignition" claim.
- **Ruling 4 (full-text depth).** At the first pass no entry reached full-text depth. Since 2026-10-13 most entries named in the final section have full-text cards; the entries that remain abstract-only or metadata-only are not yet quotable in the paper, and the open-copy sources remain working-paper or preprint versions, not versions of record.

### (v) Relation to Nadella's seven principles, from the captured text

I read the whole capture (P1-P21; the capturer's paragraph numbers) in this pass and redo the mapping from it. My first-pass table was built from the principle names alone, and one row misread the text (see Verifiability). Three features of the text bear on every row. First, the principal is the enterprise: the post worries about models that hold the organisation's most sensitive data and take mission-critical actions for it (P2), and it holds that responsibility for what the models do cannot be outsourced to the provider (P3). The post names no counterpart party as one of two parties in the coordination (P3 names a model provider and P20 names those affected, the nearest), so none of the seven principles is addressed to a person who is one of two parties to a mediated coordination. Second, the post does not rest on transparency alone: it says chain-of-thought transparency on its own is "not sufficient or dependable" (P9), and it warns that using models to check models can stack opaque systems into nested black boxes (P10). Third, its structural device is a rule placed outside the intermediary: the controls on a model's access and actions belong outside the model (P11), and the model is kept apart from the harness that runs its tasks and from its action space (P12).

### (vi) Claims in the author's framing the sources contradict, or do not support

**Line numbers.** The first pass gave Part 1 line numbers that were off by two to eight lines because I read the file without numbers. 1. **Explanation and agency (Part 1 line 136).** Repo sentence: "Providing explanations increases user awareness of an algorithm's presence while leaving user agency flat or diminished." The control-surface studies report a different sign on *perceived* control (Knijnenburg: "inspectability and control indeed increase users' perceived understanding of and control over the system"; Lukoff: agency supported by search and playlists). 2. **Moon et al. 2025: the two sentences side by side.**
   - *Repo (Part 1 line 138):* "Moon et al. (2025) found that algorithmic literacy did not benefit users unless both explainability and direct control mechanisms were present simultaneously; absent control, explainability widened the perception gap between sophisticated and unsophisticated users."
   - *Source (abstract of doi:10.1089/cyber.2024.0525; full text not accessible):* "When neither feature was present, algorithmic literacy had no significant impact.
