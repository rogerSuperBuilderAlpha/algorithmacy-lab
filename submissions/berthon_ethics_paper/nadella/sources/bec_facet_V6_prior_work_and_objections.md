## What this facet covers

The brief (`../../v4/RESEARCH_BRIEF.md`) and the author's v4 instruction (`../../v4/SOURCE_v4.md`) set the thesis: Nadella's post of 10 October 2026 treats disclosure (specific disclosures, readable records, design for legibility) as sufficient ethical design, and so repeats three mistakes of the oral-to-written transition: (a) placing the burden on the person who must read; (b) keeping that person in a literacy-based experience when the medium has changed; (c) not scaffolding the person's growth to the new competence (algorithmacy). This facet asks five things.

1. **Gap search.** Has anyone argued (a) that disclosure or transparency is a literacy-based intervention unsuited to algorithmic mediation, (b) that AI ethics repeats the ethical failures of the oral-to-written transition, or (c) that firms owe users scaffolding toward a new competence?
2. **"AI as a return to orality."** Ong's secondary orality applied to chatbots and LLMs, and 2023–2026 claims that conversational AI is oral, not literate. This is the counterargument the paper must meet.
3. **The BEQ 2023 special issue on organized immaturity**, Martin (2019) in JBE, and Berthon's own informing/guiding/restricting.
4. **Algorithmic literacy, algorithmic competence, algorithmacy**: definitions and where algorithmacy sits.
5. **Objections**: paternalism; disclosure respects autonomy; scaffolding is itself responsibilization; the post addresses enterprises, not consumers; the history is analogy only.

### Findings (Q2)

**Ong (1982), `ong1982orality` (existing key; repo card in `submissions/algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/ong1982.md`). Part** (the secondary-orality passages; archive.org djvu text of a Routledge printing with New Accents pagination, saved at `pdfs/V6/ong_djvu.txt`). Ong defines secondary orality three times, and each time ties it to writing:
- p. 2 (this copy; Heyd cites p. 3): "The electronic age is also an age of 'secondary orality', the orality of telephones, radio, and television, which depends on writing and print for its existence."
- pp. 10–11: primary orality is "'primary' by contrast with the 'secondary orality' of present-day high-technology culture, in which a new orality is sustained by telephone, radio, television, and other electronic devices that depend for their existence and functioning on writing and print."
- p. 133: "This new orality has striking resemblances to the old in its participatory mystique, its fostering of a communal sense, its concentration on the present moment, and even its use of formulas ... But it is essentially a more deliberate and self-conscious orality, based permanently on the use of writing and print, which are essential for the manufacture and operation of the equipment and for its use as well."
- p. 133, on the computer: "the sequential processing and spatializing of the word, initiated by writing and raised to a new order of intensity by print, is further intensified by the computer, which maximizes commitment of the word to space and to (electronic) local motion and optimizes analytic sequentiality by making it virtually instantaneous."
- pp. 133–134: "Secondary orality is both remarkably like and remarkably unlike primary orality"; "We plan our happenings carefully to be sure that they are thoroughly spontaneous."
*Bearing:* **narrows the counterargument, and so supports the thesis.** The counterargument says chat is oral, so a literacy frame is the wrong frame for the critique. Ong's own concept answers it: the orality of electronic media rides on writing, and the computer intensifies the commitment of the word to space. A chat interface can be conversational on the surface while its operation (logs, prompts, system instructions, evidence trails) stays textual, which is the v4 line. The paper must not borrow Ong's cognitive "great divide" (contested; see the repo card's Caution: Street, Finnegan, Halverson, Scribner and Cole).

**Heyd (2021), `heyd2021tertiary`. Full** (*Anglistik* 32(2): 131–147; Winter OA PDF, `pdfs/V6/heyd2021tertiary.pdf`). The nearest peer-reviewed work that extends Ong to talking machines. "Through technologies such as speech-to-text and text-to-speech recognition, and through the rise of digital assistants and artefacts, we are increasingly talking not just through, but with machines" (p. 131). She proposes "tertiary orality" for spoken CMC in which "boundaries between orality/scripturality and human/nonhuman agency become contested or irrelevant" (p. 139), and treats digital assistants such as Alexa and Siri as the clearest case: "users speak to them, and the devices speak back" (p. 138). On algorithms she adds that YouTube routines are "oral discourse created by humans, but optimized and structured toward being read and recommended by a machine" (p. 142). Footnote 5: "The term tertiary orality has been suggested before, but never fully theorized" (p. 136). LLM chat post-dates the article (2021); it does not discuss ethics, governance or disclosure.
She also records the field consensus that typed CMC is "conceptual orality in a medium-based scripturality setting" in Koch and Oesterreicher's terms: studies "all echo Murray's initial observation of written text displaying certain spoken-like features" (p. 133), and she argues that genuinely spoken (medium-based) digital orality is the under-theorized case (pp. 133–134).
*Bearing:* **against, in its strongest scholarly form.** It is the best citation for the claim that human-machine interaction is becoming oral. It also hands the paper its distinction: typed chat with an LLM is conceptually oral but medially written (p. 133), and only voice agents are oral in the medium. It also narrows the counterargument from inside: even Heyd's oral case is "structured toward being read ... by a machine" (p. 142), so the machine side stays a reader of text.

**Arató and Balázs (2024), `arato2024communicative`. Full** (*Magyar Nyelvőr* 148(5): 601–617, English; OA PDF). They classify generated text as writing, not speech: "Computer-generated text is a new form of literacy, tertiary literacy. In 2023, chatGPT ... bursts into the public consciousness" (p. 607), and set out a stratification in which "in the 21st century, today, artificial intelligence is leading to the emergence of tertiary orality and tertiary literacy" (p. 609). They discuss AI in the Hungarian judiciary (section 6) but make no argument about disclosure.
*Bearing:* **narrows the counterargument.** A peer-reviewed linguistics paper places ChatGPT output under literacy, with voice interfaces as a separate "tertiary verbality".

**Marz (2026), `marz2026return`. Full** (*The Drift*, Issue 17, 20 April 2026; essay, not refereed; HTML saved at `pdfs/V6/marz2026return.html`). The clearest published statement of the "return to orality" claim and a reply to it. Marz reports Derek Thompson's "handoff from a culture of literacy to a culture of orality", Joe Weisenthal's warning that "the return to orality" will "fundamentally rewire the logic engine of the human brain", and James Marriott's newsletter. Her reply: "to use Ong to claim that we risk a 'return' to 'pre-literate' orality is to ignore the main thrust of his work. Ong took pains to emphasize that literacy and orality have been inextricably linked since writing began." She reports Ong's 1996 interview: online writing "comes not in oral-aural format but through the eye, and thus is not directly oral at all", and his proposed term "secondary literacy". On LLMs: they "not only commodify text but entail a new type of writing that comes in the form of the prompt. You could call LLMs oral, because the chatbots simulate conversations with us. But such a claim, like all insistence that digital text is actually something else, would be, to borrow Brandt's phrase, 'a holdover from the social arrangements of a disintegrating print culture.'" Her close: literacy "hasn't been killed but taken hostage." The 1996 Ong interview is quoted second-hand; I did not find it.
*Bearing:* **narrows the counterargument; supports (b).** Marz names the popular form of the objection (Thompson, Weisenthal, Marriott) and answers it with Ong and with Brandt, whom the paper already cites (`brandt1998sponsors`). Her "the prompt" as "a new type of writing" is the sharpest available sentence for the paper's reply. It is an essay; cite it as a statement of the debate, not as evidence.

**Popular and grey items (not cited; logged).** Bashir (2026, Substack, 3 Feb) reports that Jasmine Sun's essay "The post-literate society" "uses Ong to argue that chat interfaces succeed because they leverage 'thousands of years of honed evolutionary instinct'" (Bashir's words; Sun's essay not opened). Clark (2024, blog) says AI combines text, speech and image and so blurs Ong's division. Ryu (2023, HCII) uses "tertiary digital orality" for VR avatars. None is refereed scholarship on LLMs.

**What I did not find.** I found no peer-reviewed work, 2023–2026, that argues at length that LLM chat is oral rather than literate, in Crossref, OpenAlex, Consensus and web search (rows 1–7). The claim lives in essays and newsletters (Thompson, Weisenthal, Marriott, Sun, as reported by Marz and Bashir). The scholarly items either extend Ong to voice assistants (Heyd 2021) or class generated text as a new literacy (Arató and Balázs 2024). Colon (2026, SSRN, title only) and Cánovas (2025, chapter, title only) were not opened and could change this.

**Answer to Q2, for the drafter.** The counterargument has a real basis: Heyd shows talking-with-machines is a recognised linguistic trend, and voice agents are oral in the medium sense. The paper can meet it in three moves, each sourced: (1) Ong's secondary orality is by definition "based permanently on the use of writing and print" (p. 133), so even an oral interface leaves the governance layer textual; (2) the post's safeguards are texts (P9 chain-of-thought, P15 "human readable evidence", P20 disclosure), so whatever the interface, the remedy asks people to read; (3) Marz (2026) and Arató and Balázs (2024) class the prompt and generated text as writing. The concession the paper owes: if agents increasingly speak, a literacy-based safeguard is doubly misfit, which strengthens wrong (b) rather than refuting it.

### Findings (Q1)

**(a) Disclosure as a literacy-based intervention unsuited to algorithmic mediation.**

- **Rohlfing et al. (2021), `rohlfing2021explanation`. Part** (IEEE *TCDS* 13(3): 717–728, CC BY; pp. 717–718, 723, 726 read). The XAI literature they criticise assumes "that 2) the mere availability of information will suffice to 3) warrant an understanding that 4) enables the explainee to act further. Clearly, the explainee is 'conceptualized as an independent actor, who makes autonomous decisions on the basis of information made available to them through transparency'" (p. 718, quoting Felzmann et al. 2019). Their first named limitation is the "passive explainee" (p. 717). Their alternative: explanation as a "social practice in which explainer and explainee co-construct understanding", built on "monitoring and scaffolding" (p. 717), and "Explaining Is Scaffolding" (§IV-B, p. 723): "The goal in scaffolding is to allow a less competent partner to participate in an interaction and to contribute to a task ... an important feature of scaffolds is that they are temporary." They warn: "We must be careful, however, to not add the empowerment of persons to a number of already existing burdens [86] in legal or ethical requirements" (p. 726).
  *Bearing:* **already says part of (a) and (c), and narrows the novelty claim.** The XAI literature has named the information-suffices assumption and proposed scaffolded, interactive explanation in its place. What Rohlfing et al. do not say: that the assumption is a *literacy* assumption, that it repeats the oral-to-written transition, or that the firm owes the scaffolding as a duty. Their p. 726 burden warning is also the responsibilization objection (Q5) stated from inside the scaffolding camp.
- **Ananny and Crawford (2018), `ananny2018seeing`** (existing key, Abstract): "Being able to see a system is sometimes equated with being able to know how it works and govern it"; they list ten limits of the transparency ideal (abstract). The canonical critique; it does not frame transparency as a literacy demand.
- **Moon et al. (2025), `moon2025effects`. Abstract.** In a 2×2×2 experiment (n = 240), "When neither feature was present, algorithmic literacy had no significant impact. However, when at least one feature was present, literacy significantly influenced the dependent variables", so that "literacy creates a new dimension of the digital divide, where transparency benefits are unequally experienced" (abstract). *Bearing:* **for (a)**, with evidence: the benefit of a disclosure depends on the reader's literacy. Single experiment, perceptions only (perceived transparency, legitimacy, satisfaction).
- **Ostinelli, Bonezzi and Lisjak (2024), `ostinelli2024unintended`. Abstract.** "Merely believing that one can access an explanation of how an algorithm works can foster an illusory sense of understanding the algorithm, even when people do not actually access and read the explanation", yielding "unfounded reliance on algorithmic determinations" (abstract). *Bearing:* **for (a)**: a disclosure that is not read still changes behaviour, in the discloser's favour.
- **Grote and Paulo (2025), `grote2025minimalist`. Abstract** (Springer blocked). Against Vredenburgh, explanations for informed self-advocacy "are likely to be intellectually over-demanding since their correct interpretation will require a combination of domain-knowledge and statistical proficiency that cannot be presumed for laypersons", and "shifting the burden to detect inadequacies to laypeople makes scrutinizing explanations very costly for them"; they would replace the goal with "enabling public trust" (abstract). *Bearing:* **for (a) — wrong (a) stated in moral philosophy, 2025**: the burden argument exists. They do not call it a literacy burden or invoke history.
- **Green (2022), `green2022flaws`** (existing key; M2 read in part; local PDF p. 1). Human-oversight policies "rest on an uninterrogated assumption: that people are able to effectively oversee algorithmic decision-making" and "provide a false sense of security" (abstract, p. 1). *Bearing:* **for (a) on the staff side.** The post's "us" is the enterprise; its "human readable evidence" (P15) asks staff to read and oversee. Green's survey of 41 policies is the evidence that such reading fails. Scope: government algorithms.
- **Dembeck (2023), `dembeck2023flow`** (dxd card, Full, German). Current fake-news and critical-media-literacy education take the book-bound "Beitrag" as its starting point, and "es könnte aber sein, dass das an der Sache völlig vorbeigeht" (p. 255). *Bearing:* **for (b)**: the nearest printed statement that a book-bound literacy frame may not fit the digital. Hedged and short; no algorithmic third party.

**(b) AI ethics repeats the ethical failures of the oral-to-written transition.**

- **The Phaedrus-and-AI genre.** Calderón and Herrera (2025, `calderon2025and`, Part: abstract and §§3–4 headings, pp. 1–5 skimmed) use "Plato's myth of Theuth and Thamus" to ask whether chatbots can improve science, and note that "literature has found analogical similarities between some issues involved in Chatbots and that of Plato criticisms of writing, contained in the Phaedrus. However, the research area has been neglected" (abstract, p. 1). Their remedies are authorship rules and "Advertising transparency" with "formal responsibility for all contributions made using chatbots" (PDF p. 8; the call for "advertising transparency, accountability or responsibility" is at p. 6), i.e. disclosure. Kitzinger (2024, `kitzinger2024openai`, Abstract) argues the opposite use: "Plato's dialogue Phaedrus is often used as a cautionary tale about the futility of distrusting new communicative technologies. But in the context of LLMs ... the Phaedrus analogy is substantially misplaced", and "risks justifying the swift adoption of a commercial tool" (abstract). Ess (2026) and Richards (2023) are further entries (Records). *Bearing:* **narrows (b).** An oral-to-written comparison applied to AI ethics exists, but it is Plato's epistemic critique of writing (memory, wisdom, authorship), used either to warn about or to excuse the new tool. None I found treats the transition's *ethical failures toward people* (who bore the burden of the new medium, who was left out of the record, who was taught) as the template.
- **The literacy-transition comparisons** already logged: Kamperman and Graff 2026 (overselling; L2), Posada et al. 2021 (print and power), Qadri et al. 2026 (analogy method), Hänska 2021 (unread). See N5 §2.
- **Gap statement (b).** I found no work that argues that AI-ethics remedies repeat the ethical failures of the transition from oral to written proof (Clanchy's shift from memory to written record, the colonial treaty record) in Crossref, OpenAlex (until rate-limited), Consensus and web search (rows 9–20 here; N5 rows 5–11, 22–24; Z rows 7–36). The Phaedrus-and-AI genre uses the same transition for epistemic, not distributive, claims.

**(c) Firms owe users scaffolding toward a new competence.**

- **Scherer, Neesham, Schoeneborn and Scholz (2023), `scherer2023guest`. Part** (pp. 409–433 of the local OA PDF; N5 read pp. 409–416, 424–427, 432; I add pp. 421–423 and 428–431). Under "The Role of Business Organizations": "business organizations also have a responsibility to contribute to organizing individual and collective maturity" (p. 428), including "2) providing social rights by informing and training citizens in the appropriate use of social media and the pitfalls of manipulative algorithms; and 3) operating as a channel for political rights, whereby individuals are provided with opportunities and training in argumentation processes and collective will formation" (p. 429). *Bearing:* **already says (c), in BEQ.** The claim that firms owe people training toward a new competence is in the guest editors' introduction of the field's main special issue on AI and immaturity. The v4 paper cannot claim (c) as new; it can claim the historical argument for it and the distinction between training as CSR and scaffolding as a design duty.
- **Vredenburgh (2022), `vredenburgh2022right`. Part** (LSE accepted manuscript; AAM pp. 5, 18–22). The right to explanation is grounded in "informed self-advocacy", "a cluster of abilities to represent one's interests and values to decision-makers and to further those interests and values within an institution" (AAM p. 5). Explanations alone are not enough: "It is neither realistic nor desirable to expect individuals to use explanations to engage in informed self-advocacy within all the institutions that affect them, absent support ... Individuals require epistemic support in order to engage in tolerably costly informed self-advocacy" (AAM p. 18), so "Rights holders should be provided with access to free experts and advocates" (AAM p. 19), and "free experts could be AI systems designed to generate explanations in response to user queries" (AAM p. 21). Conclusion: "states and companies may need to invest more resources in providing individuals with free experts – human or algorithmic" (AAM p. 22). *Bearing:* **narrows (c) and answers part of (a).** A leading defender of explanation already says explanation is insufficient without supported interpretation, and puts the cost on decision-makers. Her remedy is an intermediary expert, not the person's own competence; the paper's "scaffolding growth" differs there.
- **Helberger, Pierson and Poell (2018), `helberger2018governing`** (existing key; N4, Part). "Platforms have an obligation to create the conditions that allow individual users to comply with their responsibilities" (p. 3), with Fahlquist's ordering principle (p. 3). *Bearing:* **narrows (c)**; an earlier statement of a platform duty to enable users.
- **The EU AI Act, Art. 4** (Z, R1–R2). Providers and deployers must take measures for the AI literacy of "their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf"; the 2026 Omnibus weakened this to "support the development of" literacy with no guaranteed level (Regulation 2026/1744, Art. 1(5)). *Bearing:* **narrows (c) and supports the enterprise reading.** A legal duty to scaffold literacy exists, but it runs to the firm's own staff, not to the people the systems act on, and it was softened in July 2026.
- **Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2023/2025), `oeldorf2023what`** (preprint, Part, N3/M2). Their first research direction is "balancing users' expectations of algorithmic literacy with developers' responsibility for algorithmic transparency" (preprint p. 2), and "the current reality is that individuals must endure most of effort in improving literacy" (p. 23). *Bearing:* **for (a) and (c)**: the literacy field names the allocation as unresolved.
- **Gap statement (c).** I found no work that grounds a firm's duty to scaffold users' growth toward a new competence in the record of a past literacy transition, in the sources above. The duty itself is stated (Scherer et al. 2023, p. 429; Vredenburgh 2022, AAM p. 22; Helberger et al. 2018, p. 3; AI Act Art. 4 for staff).

**Gap statement (a).** I found no work that names disclosure or transparency as a *literacy-based* intervention, i.e. one that presupposes a reader, and argues it is misfit to algorithmic mediation because the medium is not text, in Crossref, OpenAlex, Consensus and web search (rows 9–20). The pieces exist separately: the information-suffices assumption (Rohlfing et al. 2021, p. 718), the burden on laypeople (Grote and Paulo 2025), the literacy dependence of transparency's benefit (Moon et al. 2025), and the book-bound frame of literacy education (Dembeck 2023).

## Q3. The BEQ special issue, Martin (2019) and Berthon

**Scherer et al. (2023): what remedies, and where v4 stands.** Read across pp. 421–432 (see also N5). The guest editors define the harm as "the reduction of individual capacities for public use of reason constrained by sociotechnological systems" (p. 409, via N5). Their remedies come in two families. *Disorganizing immaturity* restores negative freedom: Lanier's disconnection, Harcourt's "combination of education, awareness building, self-help, and technical devices" for avoiding surveillance, and "digital disobedience" (pp. 425–426). *Organizing maturity* strengthens positive freedom through four actors: the state (education first, then regulation "using informed consent provisions, the right to be forgotten and effective appeal processes", data property rights, watchdog NGOs, public forums; p. 427), the media (p. 427–428), social movements that alert, inform and educate (p. 428), and business, which should provide "social rights by informing and training citizens" (p. 429). On paternalism, they write that organized immaturity "can emerge simply as an effect of more or less subtle versions of paternalism", which "remains, in a political sense, a form of interference with one's liberty to make one's own decisions" (p. 422), and they name "technological paternalism specific to data capitalism" (p. 422).
*Where v4 stands.* (1) The special issue is Kantian: education toward maturity is the remedy, so it **supports (c)** and **narrows** its novelty. (2) It does not treat disclosure as the remedy: Schade's article, as the editors summarise it, shows GDPR transparency leading "paradoxically, to intransparencies and an infantilization of users (e.g., when one has to click through myriad detailed GDPR settings before being able to access a web page)" (p. 430). That **supports (a)** from inside BEQ. (3) The issue warns that paternalism produces immaturity (p. 422), which is the strongest in-venue form of the paternalism objection to scaffolding (Q5). (4) Ruehle (2023, BEQ 33(3): 502–531) argues digital workplace nudging, "in its paternalistic impetus, runs the risk of fostering tendencies of organized immaturity" (editors' summary, p. 430). The v4 paper should place itself as accepting the special issue's diagnosis and its call for business to train, while adding what the issue lacks: a historical argument that information-first remedies fail people who do not command the medium, and a distinction between training as CSR (Docherty, Q5) and scaffolding built into the system.

**Martin (2019), `martin2019ethical`. Part** (local published layout, `martin2019_nsfpar.pdf`; printed pp. 835–836 and 844–846 read; N4 read the same sections). The argument: algorithms are value-laden and "influence the delegation of roles and responsibilities within these decisions", so "firms developing algorithms are accountable for designing how large a role individual will be permitted to take in the subsequent algorithmic decision" (abstract, p. 835). She rejects the excuse that opacity dissolves responsibility: "'It's complicated' or 'I do not know how it works' turns out to be an unsatisfying response" (p. 836), and "Creating inscrutable algorithms precludes users from taking responsibility for the ethical implications ... The design of the algorithm not only scripts what users can do but also the reasonable expectations of users to take responsibility" (p. 844). The ground: the firm "is knowledgeable as to the design decisions and is in a unique position to inscribe the algorithm with the value-laden biases as well as roles and responsibilities" (p. 844). Two passages cut both ways for v4. First, "if the role of the algorithm in a decision is minimized, by providing tools to allow users to revisit how the algorithm works, the firm would have more of a traditional handoff of a product with associated (minimal) responsibility" (p. 845): in Martin's model, giving users tools and understanding *moves accountability to the users*. Second, on transparency: "full transparency may be neither feasible nor desirable", it lets people "'game' the system", and "calls for transparency in algorithmic decision making may need to be targeted for a specific purpose or type of decision" (p. 846). Her "users" are the deploying decision-makers (judges using COMPAS, p. 844), which matches the post's enterprise reader.
*Bearing:* **supports** the design-duty premise (the designer allocates roles; the firm cannot hide behind opacity), **narrows** (c) (scaffolding, on Martin's own model, transfers accountability to the scaffolded user, which is the responsibilization objection with a JBE pedigree), and **supports** the enterprise reading of "the user".

**Berthon's own frame** (`berthon2019addictive`, card exists; L1 full). Informing means "educate and disclose" (pp. 452, 457): Berthon, Pitt and Campbell put education and disclosure in one bin. On dashboards and labels, "empirical research suggests that information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior (e.g., Worsley 2002)" (p. 457). They treat informing as the first and least controversial rung (p. 457), restricting as "The most extreme public policy intervention" (p. 459), and note that "Consumers may prefer more user control rather than blanket mandates" (p. 462, via L1). *Bearing:* **for (a)** in the reader's own words, and a caution: Berthon's ladder places education *with* disclosure, so the v4 claim that scaffolding is categorically different from disclosure departs from his framework and should say so. His "more user control rather than blanket mandates" is the paternalism objection in his own paper.

## Q4. Algorithmic literacy, algorithmic competence, algorithmacy

- **Algorithmic literacy, two working definitions** (Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum, preprint p. 6): DeVito (2021, p. 3): "the capacity and opportunity to be aware of both the presence and impact of algorithmically-driven systems on self- or collaboratively-identified goals, and the capacity and opportunity to crystalize this understanding into a strategic use of these systems to accomplish said goals"; Dogruel, Masur and Joeckel (2022 in print; online 2021, p. 4): "being aware of the use of algorithms in online applications, platforms, and services, knowing how algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making as well as having the skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations". (Both quoted via the review's preprint; I did not open DeVito or Dogruel. Dogruel et al. is *Communication Methods and Measures* 16(2): 115–133, issue 2022, which is why the brief says "2022".) Ridley and Pawlick-Potts (2021) add "Position the individual as a co-constituent in algorithmic decision-making" (Z, B9a). Gagrčin et al. (2024) review 169 papers and frame acquisition as "experiential learning cycles" (abstract).
- **"Algorithmic competence"** occurs in Crossref titles for pupils' or teachers' skill with algorithms as procedures (Z row 39, titles only). It is not an established construct for living with algorithmic systems.
- **"Algorithmacy."** Oberschelp (1995, p. 121; dxd card, Full): "we have to preserve logicacy and algorithmacy by continuously training simple mental activities without artificial tools." One use, undefined, meaning a skill of carrying out algorithmic steps unaided. No other independent use (dxd Z §18; this arm's Z rows 37–43). Cousin to distinguish: Aneesh's "algocracy" (2009; N3), rule *by* algorithm.
- **Positioning.** Every definition above makes the competence the individual's, measured by awareness, knowledge, critique and coping; none contains a second party or a binding decision (N3 on Dogruel; Z on Ridley). DeVito's "capacity *and opportunity*" is the one that puts the environment in the definition, which is where v4's scaffolding claim attaches: opportunity is something a firm can provide or withhold. The paper can position algorithmacy as (i) a successor term on the literacy–numeracy pattern that Oberschelp also used, (ii) defined by relation (coordinating with others through an algorithmic intermediary) rather than by knowledge about algorithms, and (iii) not acquired by reading, which is why disclosure cannot build it (the v4 link between wrongs (b) and (c)). It must cite Oberschelp as a prior use in a different sense and DeVito/Dogruel as the definitions it departs from.

## Q5. Objections the paper must meet, in their strongest published form

**O1. Scaffolding is paternalism.**
- *Strongest version in venue:* Scherer et al. (2023, p. 422): paternalism, "no matter how much more effective it is in deploying superior expertise to protect an individual's interests ... remains, in a political sense, a form of interference with one's liberty to make one's own decisions", and organized immaturity "can emerge simply as an effect of more or less subtle versions of paternalism". Ruehle (2023) applies this to workplace nudges (via the editors, p. 430).
- *Welfarist version:* Sunstein (2012, Storrs Lectures, `sunstein2012storrs`, Abstract): "Official action may fail to respect heterogeneity, may diminish learning and self-help, may be subject to pressures from self-interested private groups ... and may reflect the same errors that ordinary people make" (abstract). He also holds that "choice architecture, and sometimes a form of paternalism, are inevitable" (abstract), which cuts the other way.
- *In the reader's own paper:* Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019, p. 462, via L1): "Consumers may prefer more user control rather than blanket mandates."
- *Reply the record supports:* scaffolding in Rohlfing et al.'s sense is contingent, learner-led and "temporary" (p. 723), which is the opposite of a standing substitution of judgment; and Scherer et al. themselves list business "training" as a remedy for immaturity (p. 429). The paper should define scaffolding as building the person's capacity to act, withdrawn as capacity grows, and distinguish it from nudging.

**O2. Disclosure respects autonomy; it is the least paternalistic tool.**
- *Strongest statement found:* Bar-Gill (2016, `bargill2016information`, Abstract): "Disclosure mandates are often considered to be the least paternalistic of all regulatory techniques. Indeed, information provision is believed to enhance both autonomy and efficiency by facilitating more informed decisionmaking ... disclosure regulation ... is beyond reproach" (abstract; he states the received view in order to question it, and the chapter is unread).
- *Philosophical version:* Vredenburgh (2022): the right to explanation protects "informed self-advocacy" (AAM p. 5), and individuals "can reasonably reject institutional set-ups in which they cannot engage in informed self-advocacy" (AAM p. 5). Kim and Routledge (2022, in arm) ground an ex post right to explanation in informed consent.
- *Reply the record supports:* Vredenburgh herself concedes explanation is not usable "absent support" (AAM p. 18); Ben-Shahar and Schneider (card) document disclosure's failure; Steffel et al. (2016, abstract) find "disclosure does not make defaults less influential"; Ostinelli et al. (2024) find the mere prospect of an explanation breeds illusory understanding. The paper should concede that disclosure is necessary (Berthon's own "necessary, but not sufficient", p. 457) and argue only against sufficiency, which is the v4 claim.

**O3. Scaffolding is itself responsibilization.**
- *Strongest version:* Docherty and Valderrama Barragán (2026, `docherty2026civilizing`, Part: pp. 1–3, 11, 16–18 of the online-first pagination, White Rose published version). Meta's digital-literacy curricula show "the growing appropriation of digital literacy discourses by technology companies like Meta to respond to and counter the criticisms made against their services" (p. 17); "by placing on individuals the burden of living well on platforms, Meta's social media corporate curriculum arranges users and technology in relationships of responsibilized control" (p. 17); "this burdening of the user to live well on social media perpetuates existing neoliberal logics of responsibilization" (p. 11); these curricula are "a performative practice of corporate social responsibility" (p. 2). Even their alternative carries the risk: "a critical digital literacy focused on a renewed personal political awareness devoid of the collective could serve simply to redistribute, rather than challenge, the primary responsibility of the individual" (p. 18).
- *Also:* Oleart and Flores Moleón (2026, Abstract): EU use of media and AI literacy "shifts responsibility from platforms to individual citizens" and so "empowers" Big Tech. Giesler and Veresiu (2014, card): the "financially literate consumer" as responsibilization (p. 851). Martin (2019, p. 845): tools that let users revisit how the algorithm works move accountability to them. Rohlfing et al. (2021, p. 726): do "not add the empowerment of persons to a number of already existing burdens".
- *Reply the record supports:* Helberger et al. (2018, p. 3) with Fahlquist: users can be held responsible only to "the extent that institutional agents have taken their forward-looking responsibility". Scaffolding that is a firm-held duty, built into the system and paired with controls, is the institution taking its forward-looking responsibility first; a curriculum handed to users instead of design change is what Docherty describes. **This objection is the most dangerous to wrong (c)** because the v4 thesis asks firms to do what Meta already does in name. The paper must say what distinguishes scaffolding from a corporate curriculum (in the system, not beside it; owed, not voluntary; paired with the firm's own controls and an independent check).

**O4. The post addresses enterprises, not consumers.**
- *Evidence for the objection:* the post's "us" is the enterprise (SOURCE_v4 flag; DXD ruling D5). The AI Act's literacy duty also runs to "staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf" (Art. 4; Z R1), and Martin's "users" are the deploying decision-makers (p. 844).
- *Reply the record supports:* the three wrongs apply to the enterprise reader as well. Green (2022, p. 1) shows that policies resting on human oversight assume "that people are able to effectively oversee algorithmic decision-making", an "uninterrogated assumption"; that is wrong (a) for staff. The second reader, the people the systems act on, appears in the post only as the recipient of incident disclosure (P20). The paper should run the argument twice, once per reader, rather than speak of "the user".

**O5. The history is analogy only.**
- *Strongest versions:* Qadri et al. (2026, card): gestalt analogies "fail to isolate the precise technical and social properties that carry the comparison" (N5). Kitzinger (2024, abstract): the Phaedrus analogy for LLMs "is substantially misplaced" and "risks justifying the swift adoption of a commercial tool". The medium differs (Heyd 2021: we now talk "with machines", p. 131).
- *Reply the record supports:* name the property that carries the comparison (Qadri et al.), which for v4 is the allocation of the burden of a new medium's competence; Ong (p. 133) supplies the continuity claim that electronic orality rests on writing, so the governance layer is still textual; and use the history as a record of what institutions did to people (Clanchy, the treaty record), not as a claim that history repeats. Kitzinger's point also warns the paper away from the "people feared writing too" move.

## What the paper can say (notes for the drafter, not prose)

1. Ong defines secondary orality as dependent on writing and print, and says the computer intensifies the commitment of the word to space (Ong 1982, pp. 2, 133), so a conversational interface does not make the governance of AI oral.
2. The claim that LLM chat returns us to orality lives in essays and newsletters (Thompson, Weisenthal, Marriott, as reported by Marz 2026); I found no refereed 2023–2026 article that argues it at length, and the refereed linguistics either extends Ong to voice assistants (Heyd 2021) or classes generated text as a new literacy (Arató and Balázs 2024, p. 607).
3. The assumption that "the mere availability of information will suffice" to enable action has been named and criticised in explainable-AI research (Rohlfing et al. 2021, p. 718), and philosophers have argued that explanations are over-demanding for laypeople and shift the burden of scrutiny onto them (Grote and Paulo 2025).
4. Experimental work finds that transparency features benefit those already algorithmically literate (Moon et al. 2025) and that the mere prospect of an explanation produces illusory understanding (Ostinelli et al. 2024).
5. Human-oversight requirements rest on the "uninterrogated assumption" that people can oversee algorithms (Green 2022, p. 1); this is the staff-side form of wrong (a), and it applies to the post's "human readable evidence" (P15).
6. Business ethics already holds that firms should train people to deal with manipulative algorithms (Scherer et al. 2023, p. 429), and political philosophy that decision-makers should fund free experts to make explanations usable (Vredenburgh 2022, AAM pp. 18–22); the v4 contribution is the historical case for that duty and its placement in the system's design, not the duty itself.
7. Firm-run literacy can be responsibilization: Meta's curricula place "on individuals the burden of living well on platforms" (Docherty and Valderrama Barragán 2026, p. 17); the paper must distinguish scaffolding built into a system and owed by its operator from a curriculum handed to users.
8. Martin (2019) places on the designing firm the allocation of "how large a role individual will be permitted to take" (p. 835) and rejects opacity as an excuse (p. 836), but on her model tools that help users understand the algorithm shift accountability toward them (p. 845); v4 must pair scaffolding with the firm's retained accountability.
9. Oral-to-written comparisons with AI exist, through Plato's *Phaedrus* (Calderón and Herrera 2025; Kitzinger 2024), but they concern memory, wisdom and authorship; I found none that treats the transition's distribution of burden among people as the template.
10. Existing definitions of algorithmic literacy are individual competences (DeVito 2021 and Dogruel et al. 2022, via Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum, preprint p. 6); DeVito's "capacity and opportunity" is the hook for a firm's duty to provide opportunity. "Algorithmacy" appears once in Oberschelp (1995, p. 121) in a different sense.
11. Berthon's own framework puts education and disclosure together under "informing" and judges information "necessary, but not sufficient" (Berthon, Pitt and Campbell 2019, pp. 452, 457); v4 extends his "not sufficient" and departs from his pairing of education with disclosure.
