# B — Print, 1450–1800: who was shut out, and by what rule

*Facet B of the Berthon ethics course paper. Written 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The print era as a set of rules: who could print (guild monopoly, royal privilege, licensing, the charter of the London Stationers); who could read print (the English rank-and-sex limit on Bible reading of 1543; the Tridentine Index's permission regime for vernacular Bibles); what happened to dialects and minority languages when a printed vernacular became the standard (Welsh office-holding law of 1536, the French ordinance of 1539, London English and Older Scots in early printing); and what the historians of the book (Eisenstein, Johns, Innis, Darnton, Febvre and Martin) say about all this. It records history only. It draws no parallel to anything later.

**The question the synthesis answers.** In the print era, who was shut out, by what rule, on what stated ground, who objected at the time, and what ended it. A second question runs underneath: which of the usual claims about print (a "monopoly of knowledge", a ban on printing in the Ottoman lands, the Index as a continent-wide wall, print as the cause of dialect loss) survive a reading of the primary text.

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text this session. *Read in part* = I opened the text and read the passages cited, not the whole work (the entry says which). *Abstract only* = a database abstract or a publisher/search summary was all I saw. *Not accessible* = no text. ### B1. Primary sources

**arber1875transcript** — Arber, E., ed. (1875). *A Transcript of the Registers of the Company of Stationers of London, 1554–1640 A.D.*, vol. I. London: privately printed. https://archive.org/details/transcriptofregi01statuoft (OCR text: `transcriptofregi01statuoft_djvu.txt`).
- *Verification:* archive.org metadata (creator Arber, Edward, 1836–1912; "London Privately printed", 1875, volume 1).
- *Read status:* read in part: the English translation of the Stationers' charter (Introduction pp. xxviii–xxxii), the Star Chamber ordinances of 1566 (p. 322), and the Addendum on the controversy of 1643–5 (pp. 581–583). The rest of the 1,500,000-character volume was searched by keyword only.
- *Scale:* one city company with a national power of search; England and its dominions.
- *The charter (granted to the Stationers; Arber's introduction gives the date as 4 May 1556, and the charter's closing formula reads "the fourth day of May in the 3rd and 4th years of our reigns").* The ground stated for the grant, p. xxviii: "certain seditious and [h]eretical books rhymes and treatises are daily published and [p]rinted by divers scandalous malicious schismatical and heretical persons, not only moving our subjects and lieges to sedition and disobedience against us, our crown and dignity, but also to renew and move very great and detestable heresies". The monopoly, pp. xxx–xxxi: "no person within this our realm of England or the dominions of the same shall practise or exercise by himself or by his ministers, his servants or by any other person the art or mistery of printing any book or any thing for sale or traffic within this our realm of England or the dominions of the same, unless the same person at the time of his foresaid printing is or shall be one of the community of the foresaid mistery or art of Stationery of the foresaid City, or has therefore [li]cence of us" (OCR "hcence"; the clause runs on to "by the letters patent"). The power of search, p. xxxi: "make search whenever it shall [p]lease them in any place, shop, house, chamber, or building of any printer, binder or bookseller whatever within our [k]ingdom of England or the dominions of the same of or for any books or things printed, or to be printed, and to seize, take, hold, burn, or turn to the proper use of the foresaid [community], all and several those books and things which are or shall be printed contrary to the form of any statute, act, or proclamation". (OCR page footers "I. xxx" and "I. xxxi" bracket these passages; the English and Latin texts are interleaved, so the page split inside the clause is approximate.)
- *Date problem:* Arber's contents table is garbled in the OCR ("4 May 1666"); his introduction says 4 May 1556; I did not check any other source. I did not resolve the year. *(Added from AUDIT_01:)* The charter's own formula "3 & 4 Phil. & Mar." runs July 1556 to July 1557, so 4 May is 1557; Arber's introduction says 1556 in four places.
- *The Star Chamber ordinances of 1566 (p. 322; heading "Ordinances decreed for reformation of Disorders in printing and uttering of Bookes"; the date reads "xxix. daie of [I]une" with the year garbled in the OCR; the page sits between Arber's entries for 22 July 1565–22 July 1566 and 22 July 1566–22 July 1567).* Ordinance 2: "whosoeuer shall offend against the said ordinance, shall not onelie forfait all such Bookes and Copies, as shall be so printed or brought in; but also shall from [thence]foorth neuer vse or exercise, nor take benefit by anie vsing or exercising of the feate of printing, and shall susteine three moneths imprisonment, without baile or mainprise." Ordinance 5 gives the Wardens of the Company the right to search "all workehouses, shops, warehouses, and other places, of Printers, Bookesellers" and to present offenders "before the Queenes Maiesties hie Commissioners in causes Ecclesiasticall".
- *The Stationers' own papers, 1643 (Addendum).* The Company's petition to the Commons, dated in the OCR "[Ja]nu[ar]y 1643", says that "a very few Persons of the said Com[p]any, but Eleven in number, (whereas the Company consisteth of many hundred Families) labouring to acquire the Printing and profit thereof to themselves, have privately gained some promise from some of the said Divines, intending to exclude all others of the Company, from having any benefit thereby" (p. 582 in the OCR footers; the text sits just after the footer "I. 582"), and a later paragraph calls those eleven "Pattentees, and Monopolizers of Printing the most vendable Bookes of worth and quantity now in use" (p. 583). Thomason dated the Remonstrance "Aprill 1643"; Arber notes it was "By Henry Parker, Esq." Its p. 581 text says: "Muscovy professes enmity to Printing, and so does Turkie, for Learning's sake". That is a 1643 belief, not evidence (see B6, Schwartz).
- *Bearing on §4:* P1 supports (dates, places, a wrong named by insiders). P2: the Stationers' junior members and Milton named the monopoly a wrong in 1643–4. P3: England only. P4: not engaged. P5: not engaged.

**statutesrealm1817vol3** — Great Britain, Record Commission (1817). *The Statutes of the Realm*, vol. 3. London. https://archive.org/details/the-statutes-of-the-realm-3 (OCR text `The_Statutes_of_the_Realm-3_djvu.txt`; archive.org marks it public domain).
- *Verification:* archive.org metadata (creator Great Britain, Record Commission; date 1817; public-domain mark).
- *Read status:* read in part: 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1 and 27 Hen. VIII c. 26.
- *34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1 (A.D. 1542–3), the Act of 1543 on English Bibles; the OCR page markers "895" and "896" fall around the text, so the clauses lie on pp. 895–896.* It forbids the Tyndale translations and sets a licence for printed religious books ("by the King and his Clergye", with the printer's name). Then, on reading: "no maner of psone or psones after the firste daie of Octobre next ensuing, shall take upon him or them to reade preache or teache openlie to other in any churche or open assemblye within any the Kinges Domynions, the Byble or any parte of Scripture in Englishe ... oonles he be so appointed therunto by the King Majestie or by any Ordinarye". Stated ground for the second restriction: "a greate multytude of his saide subject[s], moste sp[e]ciallie of the lower sorte have so abused the same, that they have therbye growen and increased in divers naughtie and erronyous opynions, and by occasion therof fallen into greate dyvision and discencón amonges themselves, to the greate unquietnes of the Realme". The rule: "no woomen nor artificers prentises journeymen serving men of the degrees of yeomen or undre, husbandemen nor laborers shall reade within this Realme or in any other the King[s] Domynions, the Byble or Newe Testament in Englishe, to himselfe or [to] any other pryvatelie or openl[i]e, upon paine of oone monethes imprysonement for every tyme [so] offending". The exceptions: "it shalbe lawfull to everye noble man and gentleman being a householder to reade or cause to be red by any of his famylie or [servau]ntes in his house orchard or gardeyne, and to his owne famylie, any texte of the Byble or New Testament"; "everye marchaunte man being a householder and occupying the feate of merchaundyse" may "reade to himself privatelye"; and "everye noble wooman and gentlewooman maie reade to themselves alone and not to others any [Textes] of the Byble or Newe Testament". "All maner of psones other thenne woomen artificers prentyses journeymen serving men of the degrees of yeomen or undre, husbandemen and laborers maie reade to themselves and to none other any text of the Byble". Informers took half the fines: "the oone moytie of all the penalties and forfaictures ... shalbe to the King ... and the other moytie therof to suche psone or psones as will detecte and sue".
- *Whom the rule hit:* women below the rank of noblewoman or gentlewoman; apprentices, journeymen, servants below yeoman, husbandmen and labourers (men of those ranks were barred too). It barred reading, in this clause, not literacy as such; the Act is silent on whether the barred could read other texts. Of the barred groups, the Act does not say how many could read; I found no count.
- *27 Hen. VIII c. 26 (A.D. 1535–6), "An Acte for Lawes & Justice to be ministred in Wales in like fourme as it is in this Realme" (the Welsh Act of Union; OCR page markers near the clause read "566" and "560"; I could not fix the page).* This is not a print measure; I include it because it fixes a language bar in the print era. The stated ground, in the preamble: "by cause that the people of the same Dominion have and do daily use a speche nothing like ne consonaunt to the naturall mother tonge used within this Realme, sodme rude and ignorant people have made distinccion and diVsitie betwene the Kinges Subjectes of this Realme and hys Subjettes of the said Dominion and Principalitie of Wales, wherby greate discorde variaunce debate dyvysion murmur and sedicion hath growen". The rule: "all Justices Comissioners Shireves Coroners Eschetours Stewardes and thir Lieuten[a]ntes, and all other Officers and Ministers of the Lawe, shall pclayme and kepe the Sessions Courtes Hundred[es] Letes Shireves Courtes and all other Courtes, in the Englisshe Tonge"; and "no psonne or psones that use the Welsshe speche or langage shall have or enjoy any [maner] office or fees within the Realme of Englond Wales or other the Kinges Dominions, upon peyn of forfaiting the same offices or fees, onles he or they use and e[xer]cise the speche or langage of Englisshe." The legislation.gov.uk record for the Act reads "Laws in Wales Act 1535 (repealed 21.12.1993)"; that is the date the whole Act went, and I did not find when this clause was first repealed.
- *Scale:* the Welsh clause covers Wales and the Crown's dominions; the Bible clause covers England and the King's dominions.
- *Bearing:* P1 supports (a dated rank-and-sex bar on reading; a dated language bar on office). P2: the ground is stated in the preamble; no contemporary objector found in this volume. P3: single realm. P4: the 1543 bar is on a medium (the vernacular text) and the Welsh clause is on a language; neither is a bar on a coordination. P5: the Act presupposes that vernacular Bible reading changes opinion; the Welsh preamble presupposes language difference causes discord; neither promise is tested.

- *Read status:* read in part: 14 Car. II c. 33 (the Licensing of the Press Act, A.D. 1662; the contents table gives its first page as 428; the "twenty master printers" clause lies between OCR page markers 430 and 432) and the three later continuing Acts (chapters VII, VII and IV in three separate sessions; the OCR text of their running heads is garbled, so I do not give dates).
- *Content:* the title and preamble: "An Act for preventing the frequent Abuses in printing seditious treasonable and unlicensed Bookes and Pamphlets and for regulating of Printing and Printing Presses"; the ground: "no surer meanes can be ad[v]ised then by reducing and limiting the number of Printing Presses and by ordering and set[t]ing the said Art or Mystery of Printing by Act of Parliament". Licensing: "no private person or persons whatsoever shall att any time hereafter print or cause to be printed any Booke or Pamphlet whatsoever unlesse the same Booke and Pamphlet ... be first entred in the Booke of the Register of the Company of Stationers of London ... and unlesse the same ... shall be first lawfully licensed and authorized to be printed by such person and persons only as shall be constituted and appointed to license the same". Divinity, "Phisick, Philosophy or whatsoever other Science or Art" went to the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Bishop of London or the university chancellors; "all Books of History concerning the State of this Realm" to the Secretaries of State; common-law books to the Lord Chancellor and chief justices. The number of printers: "no man shall be admitted to ... be a Master Printer untill they who are now actually Master Printers shall be by death or otherwise reduced to the number of Twenty and from thence forth the number of Twenty Master Printers shall be continued and no more besides the Kings Printers and the Printers allowed for the Universities" (and "but four Master Founders of Letters"). Presses: "none of the said Master Printers ... shall keep above Two Printing Presses at once unlesse he hath been Master or Upper Warden of the Company who are hereby allowed to keepe Three Presses". Duration: "this A[c]t shall continue and be in force for two yeares to commence from the Tenth of June One thousand six hundred sixty and two and no longer". The later Acts (chapters VII, VII, IV) each continue it "untill the end of the next Session of Parlyament" or "the First Session of the next Parlyament".
- *Scale:* England.
- *Bearing:* P1 supports: a statute that fixes the number of masters at twenty and names who may license each class of book. P2: the preamble's ground is public order and religion; the contemporary objection is Milton's in 1644 (B1, below), and the Stationers' own 1643 petition. I did not find an account of who was refused entry after 1662. P4: a bar on a medium. P5: not engaged.

**manuzio1564index** — *Index librorum prohibitorum, cum regulis confectis per patres a Tridentina Synodo delectos, auctoritate sanctiss. D.N. Pii 4., Pont. Max. comprobatus* (1564). Venice/Rome: Paolo Manuzio (Aldine; the place is not in the metadata I read). https://archive.org/details/ita-bnc-ald-00000309-001 (copy from the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, via Early European Books).
- *Verification:* archive.org metadata (title, Manuzio, Paolo, 1564).
- *Read status:* read in part: Regulae Indicis, rules IV, VI and X (Latin OCR with long-s misread as "f"; I normalise "f" to "s" and "u"/"v" without changing letters). Page numbers are inferred from the printed running heads: rule IV falls on p. 15, rule X on pp. 18–19.
- *Rule IV, "Regula Quarta":* "si sacra Biblia uulgari lingua passim sine discrimine permittantur, plus inde, ob hominum temeritatem, detrimenti, quam utilitatis oriri; hac in parte iudicio Episcopi, aut Inquisitoris stetur; ut cum consilio Parochi, uel Confessarii Bibliorum, a Catholicis auctoribus uersorum, lectionem in uulgari lingua eis concedere possint, quos intellexerint ex huiusmodi lectione non damnum, sed fidei, atque pietatis augmentum capere posse." Then: "Qui autem absque tali facultate ea legere, seu habere praesumpserit; nisi prius Bibliis Ordinario redditis, peccatorum absolutionem percipere non possit." Booksellers who sold such Bibles "non habenti" the licence lost the price of the books and could be punished at the bishop's discretion ("poenis pro delicti qualitate eiusdem Episcopi arbitrio"). (OCR damage inside the words "facultate", "habere", "absolutionem" is normalised; the Latin is otherwise as printed.)
- *Rule VI* extends the same treatment to vernacular books on controversy. *Rule X* carries the licensing rule for printing: in Rome a book is to be examined first "per Vicarium summi Pontificis, & sacri Palatii Magistrum, uel per personas a Sanctissimo Domino nostro deputandas"; elsewhere by the bishop or a deputy and the inquisitor, "eius approbatio & examen pertineat".
- *Scale:* the Catholic church in principle; enforcement varied by state (B2, Johns p. 119).
- *Bearing:* P1 supports: a dated, text-quoted permission rule for reading vernacular Bibles. P2: the stated ground is that unrestricted vernacular reading does "more harm than good" because of "hominum temeritatem". Milton objected to the Index and to licensing in 1644 (below), though he did not name Rule IV. P3: church-wide, not world. P4: a bar on a medium. P5: not engaged.

**milton1644areopagitica** — Milton, J. (1644). *Areopagitica: A Speech for the Liberty of Unlicensed Printing*. Project Gutenberg eBook no. 608. https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/608/pg608.txt
- *Verification:* Gutenberg header ("The Project Gutenberg eBook of Areopagitica"); I did not collate against a first edition and the file has no page numbers. I cite by passage.
- *Read status:* read in part (searched for licensing, monopoly, Index and Trent passages and read each in context).
- *What it argues and the passages:* the history of licensing runs from the Popes to Trent: "the Council of Trent and the Spanish Inquisition engendering together brought forth, or perfected, those Catalogues and expurging Indexes, that rake through the entrails of many an old good author"; the Italian licensing statements are mocked. On who gained from the 1643 Order: "it may be doubted there was in it the fraud of some old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of bookselling; who under pretence of the poor in their Company not to be defrauded, and the just retaining of each man his several copy, which God forbid should be gainsaid, brought divers glossing colours to the House, which were indeed but colours, and serving to no end except it be to exercise a superiority over their neighbours". And: "what magistrate may not be misinformed, and much the sooner, if liberty of printing be reduced into the power of a few?" And: "Truth and understanding are not such wares as to be monopolized and traded in by tickets and statutes and standards."
- *Scale:* England, the Long Parliament, 1644; Milton speaks of Rome and Spain as the model.
- *Bearing:* P2 supports strongly: a contemporary who named the licensing-and-monopoly regime a wrong, on stated grounds, and named the beneficiaries. P3: single author, single country. P1/P4/P5: not engaged.

### B2. Secondary sources read in full text

**johns2002how** — Johns, A. (2002). How to acknowledge a revolution. *American Historical Review*, 107(1), 106–125. https://doi.org/10.1086/ahr/107.1.106
- *Verification:* Crossref (title, volume, issue, pages, author match). Text from the OUP-typeset PDF on the ENS course site; page numbers from the running heads (pp. 106–125).
- *Read status:* read in part: the passages cited here (pp. 109, 112, 119–120, 122, 124); the repo card records a full read (S2-verified 2026-09-18) and I did not repeat that.
- *What it argues:* the book historian's reply to Eisenstein: print has no single "culture"; fixity and credit were made by local communities of printers, booksellers, readers and censors.
- *Quotations:*
  - On the thesis of a unitary print culture, p. 109: "What really stands to lose its acknowledgement is something much more specific: the particular concept of a revolutionary shift to a unitary print culture."
  - On Eisenstein and censorship, p. 119 (Johns's paraphrase): "She asserts that censorship created a sweeping asymmetry in European culture". Johns's reply, same page: "Authorities differed widely in the rigor with which they policed the press-in France, for example, the Index was effectively ignored." And: "I can think of no Protestant state before 1695 that had no regulatory regime governing its press". And: "Contemporaries themselves saw substantial differences between licensing and other procedures that constrained authorship. Not least, licensing was part and parcel of the regimes of privilege and registration, which were designed to protect good works as well as to hinder bad."
  - On the defence of privilege, p. 112 (Johns quoting his 1998 book): "Supporters of the patents regime, on the other hand, retorted that the security associated with privileges enabled the production of worthwhile texts that would never appear at all in other circumstances."
  - On the date of the idea, p. 122: "the specific concept of a printing revolution came about late in the day, arriving around 1800 and achieving the status of an orthodoxy only in the mid-nineteenth century."
  - On the end of licensing, p. 124: "the end of licensing in 1695".
- *Scale:* England and Europe at large, argued about London; Johns concedes his book's "geographical focus is far too restricted" (p. 109) to replace Eisenstein's.
- *Bearing:* P1 complicates: Johns gives no figures on exclusion and says the history of licensing must be separated from "censorship". P2: documents what contemporaries called licensing and privilege. P3: complicates (locality). P4/P5: not engaged.

## (c) Synthesis

### Failure 1. Exclusion from the press by corporate monopoly (England, charter of 4 May 1556 on Arber's dating, 1557 by the regnal formula, to the 1695 lapse)

- *What was done.* The Crown granted the London Stationers the exclusive right to print: no one in the realm could print for sale unless a member of the Company or licensed by royal patent (charter, Arber I pp. xxx–xxxi). The Company could enter any printer's, binder's or bookseller's premises and "seize, take, hold, burn" books printed contrary to law (p. xxxi). Star Chamber in 1566 added loss of the right to print for ever and three months' imprisonment without bail for printing against law (Arber I p. 322). In 1662 Parliament fixed the number of master printers at twenty, plus the King's printers and the universities', and four letter-founders; no master could keep more than two presses (three for past masters or wardens) (Statutes of the Realm vol. 5, 14 Car. II c. 33). All printed books needed a licence from named officers by subject (same Act).
- *To whom.* Anyone outside the Company: printers, would-be printers, authors who wanted to print. The Company's own junior members were also shut out of the Bible-with-notes trade in 1643 by eleven patentees (Arber I pp. 582–583).
- *Where and when.* London, and the kingdom, from the charter of 4 May (1556 in Arber; 1557 by the regnal year) to the lapse of licensing (Johns p. 124: 1695; Innis p. 26: 1694).
- *Stated ground.* Sedition and heresy (charter p. xxviii); "the well-government and regulating of Printers and Printing Presses is matter of Publique care" (1662 preamble). The defenders of privilege also argued it paid for expensive books: "the security associated with privileges enabled the production of worthwhile texts that would never appear at all in other circumstances" (Johns p. 112, quoting his own 1998 summary of the patents regime's supporters).
- *Who objected at the time.* Milton, 1644: "old patentees and monopolizers in the trade of bookselling" brought "glossing colours" to Parliament, and "liberty of printing" should not be "reduced into the power of a few" (*Areopagitica*). The junior Stationers, 1643: eleven men "labouring to acquire the Printing and profit thereof to themselves ... intending to exclude all others of the Company" (Arber I p. 582).
- *What ended it.* Non-renewal of the licensing Acts; Johns gives "the end of licensing in 1695" (p. 124) and Innis "the lapse of the Licensing Act in 1694" (p. 26). I did not find the lapse in the statute. Johns adds that the trade did not collapse because contemporaries feared it would, and that this produced the first copyright law in 1710 (p. 124, citing Feather); I did not open Feather.
- *Evidence depth.* Statute and charter text: full text of the passages quoted. The count of people excluded: not found.

### Failure 2. Bar on reading the English Bible by rank and sex (England and dominions, 1543)

- *What was done.* 34 & 35 Hen. VIII c. 1 forbade "woomen" (except noble women and gentlewomen, who could read to themselves alone), "artificers prentises journeymen serving men of the degrees of yeomen or undre, husbandemen" and "laborers" to read the Bible or New Testament in English, privately or openly, on pain of one month's imprisonment, and left noblemen and gentlemen who were householders free to read, or have read, to their families, and merchant householders free to read privately. Informers shared the fines (Statutes of the Realm vol. 3, pp. 895–896).
- *To whom.* Women below the gentry; apprentices, journeymen and serving men of yeoman rank or below; husbandmen and labourers.
- *Where and when.* England and "the King's Dominions", from "the firste daie of Julye next cooming" (the Act's own start date for this clause; the Act belongs to the session of 1542–3, and search-result summaries of Wikipedia give royal assent as 12 May 1543, which I did not check against an official copy).
- *Stated ground.* "a greate multytude of his saide subject[s], moste sp[e]ciallie of the lower sorte have so abused the same" that they "growen and increased in divers naughtie and erronyous opynions".
- *Who objected at the time.* Not found. I did not locate a contemporary text naming the 1543 clause a wrong. The standard by which it counts as a wrong here would have to be stated by the author.
- *What ended it.* Search-result summaries say the Act was repealed in 1547; unverified against the statute.
- *Evidence depth.* Statute text read in full for the clauses quoted. Any figure for how many people it affected or how many could read: not found.

### Failure 3. Permission to read the vernacular Bible, and pre-publication licensing, under the Tridentine Index (Catholic Europe, 1564)

- *What was done.* Rule IV made reading Catholic vernacular Bibles depend on the written permission of bishop or inquisitor, on the advice of the parish priest or confessor; a reader who read or held one without it could not be absolved until he handed it in, and a bookseller who sold to a reader without the permission lost the price of the books and could be punished (1564 Index, p. 15). Rule X required prior examination and approval of any book before printing (pp. 18–19).
- *To whom.* Lay readers of vernacular Bibles in Catholic jurisdictions; booksellers.
- *Stated ground.* Experience shows that unrestricted reading brings "plus ... detrimenti, quam utilitatis" because of "hominum temeritatem".
- *Who objected at the time.* Milton (1644), who attacks the Index system and the Roman and Spanish licensing as the model for the English Order; he does not name Rule IV.
- *What ended it.* Not found.
- *Contested scope.* Johns, p. 119: "in France, for example, the Index was effectively ignored", and, against Eisenstein's contrast between censored and uncensored Europe (as Johns reports it), Johns writes that "the contrastive appeal to censorship involves an appeal to a null set" because no Protestant state before 1695 lacked a regulatory regime. Innis, p. 26, shows suppression of printing in France by other, ecclesiastical and state powers, so the claims do not collide directly; but a reader should not treat the Index as a uniform continent-wide rule. Full text of Rule IV: read; evidence of enforcement: not found.

### Failure 4. Language bars at the point of law and the standardisation of one vernacular (Wales 1536; France 1539; London English and Older Scots in printing, 1471–1510)

- *Wales, 1536.* The Act of Union required English in all courts and barred anyone who "use[s] the Welsshe speche or langage" from any office or fees unless he used English (27 Hen. VIII c. 26). The preamble's ground: a speech "nothing like ne consonaunt to the naturall mother tonge" had caused "distinccion and diVsitie" and "sedicion". Who objected: not found. It is not a print measure; the text I read does not mention print.
- *France, 1539.* Articles 110–111 required judicial acts to be given "en langage maternel françois et non autrement". Whether this excluded Occitan and other languages is disputed: Brunot reads the phrase as the king's French only, Peyre as any language of a native subject; Martel says the first is now the majority view and that written Occitan retreated from the end of the Middle Ages (Martel, sections on Brunot and Brun). Evidence depth: Lexilogos transcription (secondary) and Martel in part.
- *Printing itself.* In London and Edinburgh, the early printers favoured a London English or a prestige Older Scots over other dialects (Sánchez-Martí pp. 92–93, 97), and Welsh was printed late, in London in 1546 and in Wales in 1585 (p. 97). The same pages say the printers made no concerted effort to standardise.
- *Counter-evidence.* Sasaki's 171-group study finds that press adoption predicts a vernacular dictionary; the study measures who standardised, not who lost a dialect. Suggett and White (abstract only) argue Welsh survived because it had a print culture.
- *Scale.* A kingdom, a city press, a continent-wide sample; no world scale.

### Claims the sources contradict, or leave open

1. **A Turkish or Ottoman ban on printing.** The Stationers' 1643 Remonstrance states it (Arber I p. 581). Schwartz's abstract says the sources "do not support the notion that the sultans banned printing" and traces the claim to European writers. A print-era "ban" on the Ottoman side therefore cannot be asserted from my sources.
2. **The Index as a continent-wide wall.** Johns p. 119: in France "the Index was effectively ignored". The Index text itself is a rule; enforcement is a separate fact I did not find.
3. **Date of the lapse of licensing.** Johns p. 124: 1695. Innis p. 26: 1694. Unresolved.
4. **Date of the Stationers' charter.** Arber's introduction: 4 May 1556; his contents-table OCR: garbled. Other literature not checked. Unresolved.
5. **Print as the cause of dialect loss.** Sánchez-Martí says it "contributed" (p. 97) and that printers did not set out to standardise (p. 92); Sasaki finds press adoption goes with standardisation of minority tongues; Suggett and White (abstract) say Welsh print culture helped Welsh survive; Martel says Villers-Cotterêts was a deliberate exclusion. These do not agree on cause or on whether anyone was wronged.
6. **Villers-Cotterêts, scope.** Brunot (king's French only) against Peyre (any native language), per Martel.
7. **A "printing revolution."** Johns (p. 122) dates the concept to around 1800; Eisenstein holds the revolution thesis. This is the historiography the paper's print half would have to take a side on, and my sources do not let me weigh Eisenstein directly.
8. **Innis's "monopoly of knowledge."** It describes an organisation of a medium (monasteries, copyist guilds, the printing press), not a bar on named persons. The repo's draft use of it as a wrong done to people goes beyond what Innis wrote on the pages I read.
