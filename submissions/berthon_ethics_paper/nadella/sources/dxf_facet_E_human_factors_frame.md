# E — The human-factors frame: spacing as reallocation of work from reader to artefact

*Corrected on 2026-10-12 against `../audit/AUDIT_02.md`: 14 factual fixes.*

*Facet E of the foundation paper for Decision Experience Design. 2026-10-12. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The human-factors and cognitive-science vocabulary for a move in which a task leaves the human and enters the artefact: the representational effect (Larkin and Simon; Zhang and Norman), cognitive artefacts and external cognition (Norman, Hutchins, Scaife and Rogers, Kirsh), cognitive offloading (Risko and Gilbert), function allocation and its critics (Fitts, Jordan, Dekker and Woods, Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens), ecological interface design and display principles (Vicente and Rasmussen, Wickens and Carswell, Woods), design versus training (hierarchy of controls, expertise reversal, desirable difficulties), the contrast case of monitoring (Bainbridge, only as far as the line needs), HCI and document-design uses of writing history, and representations that coordinate people through a system (Erickson and Kellogg, Heath and Luff, Gutwin and Greenberg).

**The question the synthesis answers.** (i) What is the best-supported name for the scribes' move, with the definition quoted and located? (ii) Is "the artefact absorbs the task" versus "the human is asked to read or monitor more" a principled line, and which source is strongest for and against? (iii) Whom does such a move help, novice or expert? (iv) Where does the analogy from page to coordination break? (v) Which claims in the author's framing do the sources contradict? This is problem 2 in `RESEARCH_PLAN.md`; the answer to (iv) also feeds problem 4.

### 1. Representation and external cognition

**larkin1987why** — Larkin, J. H., & Simon, H. A. (1987). Why a diagram is (sometimes) worth ten thousand words. *Cognitive Science, 11*(1), 65-100. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6708.1987.tb00863.x
- *Verification:* Crossref title and volume match; UCSD course copy carries journal pagination (pp. 65-99 in the header).
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/larkin1987why.md).** OCR text is noisy.
- *What it argues:* Two representations can hold the same information and still differ in how cheaply a given processor can use it, because they differ in search, recognition and inference.
- *Quotations:*
  - p. 67: "Two representations are informationally equivalent if all of the information in the one is also inferable from the other, and vice versa." And: "Two representations are computationally equivalent if they are informationally equivalent and, in addition, any inference that can be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the one can also be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the other, and vice versa."
  - p. 65 (abstract): "Sentential representations are sequential, like the propositions in a text. Diagrammatic representations are indexed by location in a plane."
  - pp. 69-70, on recognition: "Human abilities to recognize information are highly sensitive to the exact form (representation) in which the information is presented to the senses (or to memory)."
  - p. 91, on a geometry problem: "In the sentential representation, the perceptual work of recognition is explicit and extensive; in the diagrammatic representation, it is automatic and easy."
- *Bearing:* **Supports H-cost and problem 2.** Spaced and unspaced text fit the definition of informationally equivalent strings with unequal computational cost (the paper's definition of computational equivalence includes the informational condition and turns on "easily and quickly", which the authors call "not precise terms", p. 67). The sentence at p. 91 states the scribes' gain in the authors' own terms: the perceptual work moves from the reader's recognition process to the drawn form. One caution is mine, not the authors': where an unspaced string has two lawful parses, the spaced text commits to one and is no longer informationally equivalent to its source. The authors' "diagram" is a spatial index over a plane, not a line of text, so the match to spacing is by function, not by kind.

**zhang1994representations** — Zhang, J., & Norman, D. A. (1994). Representations in distributed cognitive tasks. *Cognitive Science, 18*(1), 87-122. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1801_3
- *Verification:* Crossref; UCSD course copy with journal pagination.
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/zhang1994representations.md).**
- *What it argues:* The representational system of a task is the set of its internal and external representations; isomorphic representations of one formal structure produce different behaviour, because they activate different processes.
- *Quotations:*
  - p. 88: "The representational effect refers to the phenomenon that different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors." The authors' example is Arabic against Roman numerals for multiplication (73 x 27 against LXXIII x XXVII), also p. 88.
  - p. 118: "different processes are activated by different representations, but not vice versa. For example, perceptual processes are activated by external representations, while cognitive processes are usually activated by internal representations."
  - p. 118: "External objects are not just peripheral aids to cognition, they provide a different form of representation."
  - p. 118, property 2: external representations "can provide information that can be directly perceived and used without being interpreted and formulated explicitly"; in the physically constrained version of the puzzle, "When the subjects were asked to explicitly formulate the rules after the games, few could do it."
  - p. 119, property 4, citing Norman (1991): "From the system's view (internal + external representations), external representations can make a task easier; from the person's view (internal representations only), external representations change the nature of the task."
  - p. 110: "the more rules externalized, the easier the problem. In addition, external rules seem to be error proof."
- *Bearing:* **The core source for the vocabulary (problem 2).** Spacing is a change of external representation that leaves the formal task intact and moves word-boundary work from a cognitive process in the reader to a perceptual process on the page. Two features cut against the author's wording. First, the page-119 sentence says the task changes for the person; it does not say it disappears. Second, the p. 118 finding that subjects followed rules they could not state is a cost the paper's framing should face: knowledge embedded in the artefact is used without being held, so it is also not inspectable or contestable by the user. The experiments are four laboratory studies on Tower of Hanoi isomorphs (N = 24, 24, 18, 36 undergraduates); the paper is silent on text, reading and writing (card).

**hutchins1995cockpit** — Hutchins, E. (1995). How a cockpit remembers its speeds. *Cognitive Science, 19*(3), 265-288. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1903_1
- *Verification:* Crossref; Berkeley course copy with journal pagination.
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/hutchins1995cockpit.md).**
- *What it argues:* The unit that remembers landing speeds is the cockpit, not the pilot. Speed cards and airspeed-indicator "bugs" re-represent the same speeds so that a different, cheaper set of processes can do the job.
- *Quotations:*
  - p. 282: "Adding speed bugs to the system does nothing to alter the memory of the pilots, but it does permit a different set of processes to be assembled into a functional system that achieves the same results as the system without speed bugs."
  - p. 282: "Individual pilot memory has not been enhanced; rather, the memory function has now become a property of a larger system in which the individual engages in a different sort of cognitive behavior." And, p. 283: "To call speed bugs a 'memory aide' for the pilots is to mistake the cognitive properties of the reorganized functional system for the cognitive properties of one of its human components."
  - p. 283, on the final approach: "a memory and scale reading task is transformed into a judgment of spatial adjacency."
  - p. 280: "Setting any single speed bug to a particular value requires the pilot to hold the target speed in memory, read speed scale, locate the target speed on the speed scale ... and then, manually, move the speed bug to the scale position."
  - p. 277: "The external speed bug settings capture a regularity in the environment that is of a shorter time scale than the weight/speed correspondences that are represented in the speed card booklet. The speed bug settings are a memory that is malleable, and that fits a particular period of time."
- *Bearing:* **Supports H-cost and the vocabulary; sharpest source on what "moves."** Hutchins's phrase "reorganized functional system" is the nearest human-factors name for the scribes' move, and his "transformed into a judgment of spatial adjacency" is the located statement that a task becomes a perceptual one. Two further points. The work is relocated in time and person: someone must set the bugs with working-memory cost (p. 280), as someone must space the text (facet A). And Hutchins says the speed bugs "capture a regularity in the environment that is of a shorter time scale" than the speed card, a memory "that is malleable" (p. 277); the page-to-coordination analogy fails exactly where the regularity drifts (see (c)(iv)). The bugs do not take the monitoring away: the flying pilot must still monitor airspeed, glide path and runway alignment together (p. 283), and the pilot not flying watches airspeed and calls deviations (p. 285), so both pilots keep reading the instruments (card). All Hutchins quotations here come from the 1995 *Cognitive Science* article, pp. 277-283, and are cited to it. The repo's card for the 1995 book *Cognition in the Wild* is marked unverified and I did not reopen it; no quotation here is cited to the book.

**kirshmaglio1994distinguishing** — Kirsh, D., & Maglio, P. (1994). On distinguishing epistemic from pragmatic action. *Cognitive Science, 18*(4), 513-549. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1804_1
- *Verification:* Crossref; UCSD author copy. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/kirshmaglio1994distinguishing.md).**
- *What it argues:* Tetris players rotate and translate pieces to change the world so that a perceptual or computational problem gets easier, which the authors call epistemic action.
- *Quotation:* pp. 513 and 515 (the epistemic definition is on p. 513, "own computational state" on p. 514, and the pragmatic-action definition, "closer to his or her physical goal", on p. 515): "we distinguish pragmatic actions—actions performed to bring one physically closer to a goal—from epistemic actions—actions performed to uncover information that is hidden or hard to compute mentally."
- *Bearing:* **Complicates the vocabulary.** Epistemic action is something the agent does to its own task. Unspaced readers have such actions available (reading aloud and pointing are plausible cases; that link is my inference, not Kirsh and Maglio's, and the evidence is in facet A). Spacing by a scribe is a change made by a third person before the reader acts. The term fits the reader's workarounds, not the scribes' move.

**kirsh1995intelligent** — Kirsh, D. (1995). The intelligent use of space. *Artificial Intelligence, 73*(1-2), 31-68. https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(94)00017-U
- *Verification:* Crossref; revised author version (HTML) at interactivity.ucsd.edu/articles/Space/AIJ1.html. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/kirsh1995intelligent.md).** No page numbers in the HTML; locators are section names.
- *Quotation (section "Spatial Arrangements That Simplify Perception"):* "Perhaps the most obvious way of simplifying perception is to arrange objects in space so that they form equivalence classes, or partitions, that reflect relevant preconditions, or properties that are useful to track, notice, or exploit." And: "By segregating the two groups we highlight their differences."
- *Bearing:* **Supports (vocabulary, problem 2).** Spatial partitioning to ease categorisation is the closest cognitive-science description of what a space between words does. Kirsh's focus is the expert agent arranging its own workspace, but his text is wider than that: he says arrangements cue "cognitive events or processes in himself or herself, or another agent", his example is supermarket managers who bias "consumer choice" with displays, and his fourth assumption has work environments pre-structured by others (section "How to Think About Everyday Activity"). An arrangement made by one party for another is therefore inside his frame; the supermarket case is one where the arranger's interest differs from the shopper's, which Kirsh does not draw out (card).

**kirsh1996adapting** — Kirsh, D. (1996). Adapting the environment instead of oneself. *Adaptive Behavior, 4*(3-4), 415-452. https://doi.org/10.1177/105971239600400307
- *Verification:* Crossref (via Consensus record, title and DOI); *Read status:* **abstract only** (the Philpapers PDF was behind a bot wall).
- *What it argues (abstract):* "creatures with the capacity to redesign their environments have an adaptive advantage over those who can adapt only passively," and one major method is to "change its cognitive congeniality," changes that "leave the state space formally intact but reduce the number and cost of mental operations needed for task success."
- *Bearing:* **Supports (vocabulary).** The title is the cleanest statement I found of the contrast between redesigning the environment and adapting the human, but it describes an agent redesigning its own environment. Read the full text before relying on it; a human with library access should start here.

**scaife1996external** — Scaife, M., & Rogers, Y. (1996). External cognition: How do graphical representations work? *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 45*(2), 185-213. https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1996.0048
- *Verification:* Crossref; Dourish course copy with journal pagination. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/scaife1996external.md).**
- *Quotations:*
  - p. 188: "Computational offloading. This refers to the extent to which differential external representations reduce the amount of cognitive effort required to solve informationally equivalent problems."
  - p. 189: "Re-representation. This refers to how different external representations, that have the same abstract structure, make problem-solving easier or more difficult."
  - p. 210: "Many of the presumed benefits of good-old fashioned graphical representations (i.e. static diagrams) were considered to be due to years of practice of perceptual processing of visual stimuli and the learning of graphical conventions." And: "Similarly we have even less understanding of how (and if) computational offloading works in such dynamic environments."
- *Bearing:* **Supports the vocabulary and states the limit asked for in item 8.** "Computational offloading" and "re-representation" are defined for representations, not for acts, so they fit the scribes' move better than Risko and Gilbert's term. The authors also say the benefit of static representations rests on practice with conventions, which weakens "no training needed," and they say the offloading story is not understood for dynamic settings.

**risko2016cognitive** — Risko, E. F., & Gilbert, S. J. (2016). Cognitive offloading. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 20*(9), 676-688. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002
- *Verification:* Crossref; UCL Discovery author manuscript. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/risko2016cognitive.md).**
- *Quotation:* ms. pp. 2-3: "cognitive offloading: the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task in order to reduce cognitive demand." Box 3: "In many cases the decision to offload is not made by the individual. Rather, offloading is 'built-in' to the task environment by design." Costs named there: spatial-memory impairment with GPS, impaired memory for photographed objects, automation complacency and bias, and "skill decay."
- *Bearing:* **Complicates.** The definition says physical action, which points at the person's own act, but Box 3 counts offloading that a designer builds into the task environment as an instance (ms. p. 33), so the review also covers designer-built cases and the definition does not by itself exclude a scribe's spacing; the text never mentions writing, reading or conventions, so applying it to spacing is the paper's own step. Box 3 ties the built-in case to costs that appear when a task is absorbed by design. Use "offloading" for the reader's own moves; use "re-representation" for the scribe's.

**dekker2002maba** — Dekker, S. W. A., & Woods, D. D. (2002). MABA-MABA or abracadabra? Progress on human-automation co-ordination. *Cognition, Technology & Work, 4*(4), 240-244. https://doi.org/10.1007/s101110200022

**parasuraman2000types** (reused) — Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T. B., & Wickens, C. D. (2000). A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation. *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Part A, 30*(3), 286-297. https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354
- *Read status:* **read in full** per repo card `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/parasuraman2000.md` (page-verified in an S2 pass); not reopened.
- *Per the card:* automation "is not all or none" but applies at a level to each of four functions: information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, and action implementation (p. 287); "high levels should be implemented only for low-risk situations" for decision and action (p. 293); adaptive automation is set aside (p. 289).
- *Bearing:* **Complicates.** The model is built for automation, which acts. A static page does not act, so "moving parsing from reader to page" is not automation in this model's sense. It is nearest to the information-acquisition and analysis stages, delivered without an agent. The same model would class a log or audit trail as automation of acquisition that leaves analysis with the human, a point for (c)(ii).

### 3. Display design principles

**vicente1992ecological** (key reused from the repo bib) — Vicente, K. J., & Rasmussen, J. (1992). Ecological interface design: Theoretical foundations. *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, 22*(4), 589-606. https://doi.org/10.1109/21.156574
- *Verification:* Crossref; DTU accepted manuscript (no journal pagination; locators are section headings). *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/vicente1992ecological.md).**
- *Quotations:*
  - IV.A: "In general, perceptual processing is fast, effortless, and proceeds in parallel, whereas analytical problem solving is slow, laborious, and proceeds in a serial fashion." And: "It is important to note, however, that the lower levels can only be activated in familiar situations because they require that the operator be attuned to the perceptual features of the environment."
  - IV.A, why perceptual design suits process control: "operators of such systems are highly skilled and have extensive experience in controlling the system" and "interface design for complex systems consists of specifying an interface for a single, specific application."
  - V.B: "the approach will only succeed to the extent that designers understand the system they are building." And on generality: "the generalizability of the EID framework remains an open question."
  - Abstract (as retrieved): "The basic goals of EID are not to force processing to a higher level than the demands of the task require ... an EID interface should not contribute to the difficulty of the task."
- *Bearing:* **Supports in principle; sets three limits that matter for item 8.** Spacing fits the EID aim of making a task perceptual instead of analytical, and a space between words is plausibly skill-based support by perceptual means; that application is mine, since the paper is about process control and never treats text. ## (c) Synthesis

### (i) The best-supported vocabulary for the scribes' move

Ranked by how well the located definition fits.

1. **Re-representation, with the representational effect as its finding.** Zhang and Norman (1994, p. 88): "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors." Scaife and Rogers (1996, p. 189): re-representation is "how different external representations, that have the same abstract structure, make problem-solving easier or more difficult." Larkin and Simon (1987, p. 67) give the cleaner formal pair: informationally equivalent, computationally unequal (my gloss; their computational equivalence includes the informational condition and rests on "easily and quickly", which they call "not precise terms"). This family names an artefact property, which suits a page.
2. **Perceptual for cognitive substitution; computational offloading.** Larkin and Simon (p. 91): the recognition work is "explicit and extensive" in one form and "automatic and easy" in the other. Zhang and Norman (p. 118): "perceptual processes are activated by external representations, while cognitive processes are usually activated by internal representations." Casner and Larkin (1989, a DTIC technical report) call it substituting "less effortful visual operators for more effortful logical operators" (abstract only). Scaife and Rogers (p. 188) call the saving computational offloading.
3. **Reorganised functional system; task transformation.** Hutchins (1995, "How a cockpit remembers its speeds", *Cognitive Science* 19, p. 283): "a memory and scale reading task is transformed into a judgment of spatial adjacency," and "Individual pilot memory has not been enhanced" (p. 282). This is the best phrase for the claim that the author wants and the one that protects it from the "training" reading. Norman's personal and system views (via Zhang and Norman, p. 119) and Pea's amplifier-versus-reorganizer contrast (abstract only) say the same.
4. **Design versus training as a ranked choice: elimination or engineering control above administrative control.** NIOSH (read through a WebFetch extraction) ranks controls that work "without significant human interaction" above work-process training. Erickson and Kellogg (2000, pp. 61-62) give a worked case of an artefact change that makes a posted instruction unnecessary: a glass window replaces the sign "Please Open Slowly", by making people visible to one another. Their account is of visibility and accountability, not of design versus training. The NIOSH ranking is the recognised form of the distinction the author draws, and it comes from safety engineering.
5. **Environmental redesign.** Kirsh (1996), abstract only: adapt the environment "instead of oneself," and change its "cognitive congeniality." Strong title, unread text.

Terms that do not fit, with the reason. *Cognitive offloading* in Risko and Gilbert's definition is the use of physical action (ms. pp. 2-3), which points at the person's own act; their Box 3 also counts designer-built offloading (ms. p. 33), so the term is narrower than the scribe's move but not strictly excluded. *Epistemic action* (Kirsh and Maglio 1994, pp. 513-514) likewise describes an agent changing its own task; both fit the unspaced reader's workarounds, not the scribe's move. *Function allocation* presumes two agents; PSW's four stages and levels describe automation that acts, and a page does not. *Affordance* is used loosely in Zhang and Norman (p. 118, citing Gibson) and I would avoid it. *Ecological interface design* fits only as a family resemblance: EID is built for process-control operators, and the sentence "a space is skill-based support by perceptual means" is my application, not a claim from Vicente and Rasmussen.

Does "moving parsing from reader to page" count as function allocation? Not in the Fitts-to-PSW sense, because the page is not an agent. It does count as a change of who bears a task. The cost went to the scribe (who must now decide boundaries, as the setter of a speed bug must hold and place a value, Hutchins p. 280) and the benefit went to every later reader. That distribution of work across people and time, with an artefact as the carrier, is what Hutchins's cockpit shows. The substitution myth (Dekker and Woods) is the likely warning here, with the stated caveat that I could not read the paper: the work changes hands and changes shape, and new errors and new dependencies appear. For spacing, the new dependency is that the reader inherits one parse chosen by someone else, and may not be able to recover another. That is my inference, not a sourced claim, and it links to facet A.

### (ii) Is "artefact absorbs the task" versus "human reads more" a principled line?

**Yes, in three separate literatures, and none of them states it in the author's words.** NIOSH's ranking is the field-wide backing for the line: change what the person meets so the instruction is unnecessary, and do not ask the person to do more (that wording is not any source's). Erickson and Kellogg (2000, pp. 61-62) give a worked case of an artefact change that makes a posted instruction unnecessary, by making people visible to one another; their account is of visibility and accountability, not of design versus training. Hutchins (1995, "How a cockpit remembers its speeds", *Cognitive Science* 19, p. 283; the article, not *Cognition in the Wild*) gives the cognitive account of why that works. The strongest source for the line from the other side, the monitoring case, is Bainbridge (1983, p. 776): when the machine decides on more criteria than a human can follow in real time, "The human monitor has been given an impossible task." That is an argument that "observe more" does not scale, which supports the author's contrast with Nadella's seven principles.

**The line breaks in three places, and each is a source against the wording "legibility is rejected."**

1. *Making things visible is mainstream artefact-side design.* Ecological interface design is a programme of making constraints visible so that perception does the reasoning (Vicente and Rasmussen 1992; Vicente 2002), and social translucence is a programme of making participants visible to one another (Erickson and Kellogg 2000). If the paper rejects "legibility" as a category, the human-factors literature will read it as rejecting its own best examples. The principled cut is different: an artefact change counts on the author's side when it turns the user's task into a perceptual one at a load the user can carry in real time, and counts on Nadella's side when it leaves the user's task analytical and raises its volume. By that cut, "observe everything" and "human readable evidence" fall on the monitoring side, while a display that lets a coordinating user see at a glance what the mediator is doing could fall on the author's side. Parkes's "grammar of legibility" and the ruling's "legibility" are two senses of one word: graphic legibility of a script, and interpretability of an opaque system. The first is a perceptual aid; the second is a demand to read.
2. *Containment is on the artefact side.* In the hierarchy of controls, engineering controls rank above administrative ones because they do not depend on the person. Several of Nadella's principles that I take from the repo's summary (controls outside the model, containment, independent controls) are of that kind, whatever his seventh "observe everything." Facet G should test this; it bears on problem 3.
3. *Absorbing the task has costs the sources name.* Risko and Gilbert (Box 3, ms. pp. 33-34): offloading "built-in" by design can bring complacency, automation bias and "skill decay" (the source says "could"). Moritz et al. (2020, abstract only): users who controlled a transformation fell back to the level of an untrained baseline in Experiments 1 and 2, but not in a near-transfer task. Eisma et al. (2020, abstract only): an air-traffic display that handed over the answer made the task subjectively easier and conflict detection better, with side effects of visual tunnelling and misunderstanding. Dekker and Woods (unread) warn that reallocation changes the work. The author's sentence that reading "no longer required the training" is the claim these sources push against.

**Strongest sources for:** NIOSH; Erickson and Kellogg (as a visibility case, not design versus training); Hutchins; Zhang and Norman; NIOSH. **Strongest against:** Bainbridge supports the contrast but Risko and Gilbert, Moritz, Eisma, and Dekker and Woods qualify the claim that the task "disappears."

### (iii) Who benefits, novice or expert

The expertise-reversal literature predicts that aids tuned to novices can hurt experts (Kalyuga et al. 2003, abstract). The spacing data point the other way for word spacing itself, and the same data show that adding further cues hurts.

- Thai adults read marginally faster with spaces in one small study (Kohsom and Gobet 1997; n = 8, p < .08, conference paper); Winskel et al. (2009) found them slightly slower. Second-language learners of Chinese read best with word spaces, and their reading times differed between groups with Chinese reading experience (Shen et al. 2012, abstract only). The second result shows that experience still matters.
- For adult readers of ordinary spaced text, extra segmentation cues slowed word identification even after training (Termuende et al. 2025, preprint, sub-word units). The aid that works is the one already integrated into the reader's skill.
- Evidence on the cost of removing spaces from habituated readers sits in facet D and in the repo's Part 2 facet E (Rayner et al. 1998; Mirault et al. 2019) and I did not reopen it.

The prediction for the paper: a stable, conventional aid that every reader meets from the start can serve novices and experts, and a bolt-on aid layered over expert practice is the case the expertise-reversal effect warns about. Vicente and Rasmussen's EID cuts the same way: it presupposes trained operators who attune to a fixed display (IV.A), so it shows how experts gain, not novices. Bjork's framework adds that an aid that raises performance may fail to produce learning (Soderstrom and Bjork 2015, pp. 192-193), and Moritz et al. (2020, abstract only) found no carry-over: participants returned to the untrained baseline.

### (iv) Where the analogy from page to coordination breaks

The sources support the author's caution ("Traditional media hold still; an algorithmic intermediary does not") and give four located reasons.

1. *Regularity and time scale.* Hutchins's bugs "capture a regularity in the environment that is of a shorter time scale" than the card, a memory "that is malleable" (p. 277); the argument that static media hold still needs a different source. Scaife and Rogers (p. 210) say the offloading story is not understood for "dynamic environments." Vicente and Rasmussen (V.B): the approach "will only succeed to the extent that designers understand the system they are building." An adaptive mediator has no stable regularity for a representation to freeze, and its designer may not know its constraints.
2. *The user and the application.* EID assumes skilled operators on one known application (IV.A). A lay party using many platforms is the reverse. Vicente's 2002 review also reports that no full-fidelity test of EID with professional workers had yet been done (abstract).
3. *A third party with its own objective.* Every study above puts one user and one artefact (or several humans who share a goal) in the frame. Erickson and Kellogg make participants visible to each other, and their accountability argument works because all parties see each other and the system is assumed to be a neutral medium (card). A mediator that reads both parties and commits determinations neither controls is outside what these papers model. The repo's literature finds the same absence: no classic HF automation-trust source models a second human party the automation also serves, and the CPDLC party-line studies are the one field case with two humans and a mediator.
4. *Where the hidden thing sits.* Word boundaries are a property of the signal that the reader could recover with effort. In a coordination, what is hidden is the mediator's state and aims, which the user cannot recover by effort. Heath and Luff (abstract only) describe control rooms that coordinate by tacit practice and peripheral awareness; nothing I read shows that a static display can replace that when the mediator adapts.

### (v) Claims in the author's framing the sources contradict or qualify

- **"it changed the interface so that reading no longer required the training"** (`draft.md:263`). Hutchins (pp. 282-283), Zhang and Norman (p. 119) and Scaife and Rogers (p. 210) speak of a task transformed, not removed, and of benefits resting on practice with conventions. Shen et al. (2012) found that groups differed in reading time with Chinese experience, whatever the spacing. Soften to "lowered the training the task required" or keep the sentence and flag it. Per the standing rule, I flag and do not rewrite.
- **"Algorithmacy is not legibility"** (`README.md:38-43`). The human-factors literature treats making constraints visible as a core artefact-side move (Vicente and Rasmussen; Erickson and Kellogg). The ruling needs a narrower statement, such as "not more reading load placed on the user," to survive a human-factors reader.
- **Course statement: "I reject that novices should get abstracted heuristics while only experts engage structural variation"** (`phd1750_topic_and_rationale.md`). The expertise-reversal literature predicts that guidance should differ by expertise (Kalyuga et al. 2003, 2001, abstracts only), and the spacing data show the exception for a stable convention. The paper can argue that its aid is of the conventional kind, but a human-factors reader will expect the argument.
- **"The history is an analogy, not a mechanism"** (Part 2). Consistent with the sources: none of them shows that word spacing caused anything beyond a reduction in reading cost, and the one HCI paper that cites Saenger on causation (Termuende et al.) states the causal claim in its introduction without testing it, and hedges it in section 9.1. This facet gives H-cause no support.
- **"Traditional media hold still"** (Part 1, line 118). Supported by Hutchins, Scaife and Rogers, and Vicente and Rasmussen, as in (iv).
