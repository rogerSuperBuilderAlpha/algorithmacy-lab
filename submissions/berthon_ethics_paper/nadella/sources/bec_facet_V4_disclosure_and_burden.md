# Facet V4: disclosure, transparency and the burden on the reader (2026-10-14)

Facet V4, research round 7 (v4), 2026-10-14. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

The v4 thesis (v4/SOURCE_v4.md) holds that Nadella's post of 10 October 2026 treats disclosure (specific disclosures, readable records, design for legibility) as sufficient ethical design, and that this repeats three wrongs of the oral-to-written transition: (a) placing the burden on the person who must read; (b) keeping the person in a literacy-based experience; (c) not scaffolding growth to algorithmacy. This facet asks what the record says about disclosure and transparency as ethical design, and about shifting the burden onto the reader, in five bodies of work:

1. Mandated disclosure and notice-and-choice (Ben-Shahar and Schneider; Loewenstein, Sunstein and Golman; McDonald and Cranor; Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch; Solove).
2. The transparency critique in algorithmic governance (Ananny and Crawford; Edwards and Veale; Kaminski; Burrell; Selbst and Barocas; Nissenbaum).
3. Experiments on explanations and human oversight of AI (Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al.; Bansal et al.; Buçinca et al.; Vasconcelos et al.; Lee et al. 2025; Bainbridge 1983; the meta-analyses of Schemmer et al. and Vaccaro et al.).
4. Responsibilization (Shamir; Giesler and Veresiu; notice-and-choice as burden-shifting).
5. The counter-literature that defends disclosure and transparency (informed-consent ethics; disclosure as respect for autonomy; Kim and Routledge; Calo; Fung, Graham and Weil).

Findings are sorted at the end under **for**, **narrows** and **against** the thesis.

## 1. Mandated disclosure and notice-and-choice

**Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011), `benshahar2011failure`.** *Part*, card (verified). Mandated disclosure is "the single most common technique for protecting personal autonomy in modern society" (p. 647), and "it chronically fails to accomplish its purpose. Even where it seems to succeed, its costs in money, effort, and time generally swamp its benefits" (p. 651). On boilerplate, roughly 1 in 1,000 shoppers opens the terms, for a median 29 seconds (p. 671, n. 127, Bakos et al.); Marotta-Wurgler: "No matter how prominently [end-user license agreements] are disclosed, they are almost always ignored" (p. 672, n. 130). Disclosure is inequitable: it serves the sophisticated more than the poor (p. 742). The sister arm's full OCR read adds: "disclosees often do not read disclosed information, do not understand it when they read it, and do not use it even if they understand it" (p. 665); "Financial literacy education is an important component of the disclosure and informed consent paradigm, and much evidence now shows that this effort has largely failed" (p. 667); "complexity cannot be explained simply" (p. 713); and a concession that brief symbolic signals (Los Angeles restaurant grades) may work, hedged "There is little real evidence" (p. 743) (sister facet L, entry 1; OCR of an offprint, not yet checked against the journal).

**Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2014), *More Than You Wanted to Know*, `benshahar2014more`.** *Record* (Princeton University Press; Crossref book DOI 10.2307/j.ctt5hhrqj per sister facet L, not re-resolved here). I found no open review. Cite the 2011 article for every quoted claim.

**Loewenstein, Sunstein and Golman (2014), `loewenstein2014disclosure`.** *Full*, sister card (S2-verified). "In many cases, disclosure does not much affect the recipients of the information but does significantly affect the behavior of the providers" (abstract, p. 391); "A pervasive theme is that disclosure may have little effect on recipients but large effects on providers" (p. 398); fewer than 3% read website privacy disclosures (p. 399); "making disclosure simple and accessible may provide insufficient help to consumers who lack the tools to process that information" (p. 408); policy makers must guard against disclosure "implemented as a substitute for other, often more effective, regulatory interventions" (p. 412); and disclosure requirements "appear to have been less effective in changing recipient behavior than their proponents seem to assume" (p. 413). Against the thesis: simplified, standardised, comparative and personalised disclosure sometimes changed choices (Kling et al.'s Medicare letter, 28% against 17% switching, p. 407), and §4.6 proposes intermediaries who read for the consumer (p. 411). The authors call themselves proponents of disclosure (p. 395).

**McDonald and Cranor (2008), `mcdonald2008cost`.** *Part* (author's pre-press, reread this session: abstract p. 1, conclusion p. 19; the journal is *I/S* 4(3), 543–568). A modelled estimate, not observed behaviour: "if website users were to read the privacy policy for each site they visit just once a year, what would their time be worth?" (p. 1). Reading "carries costs in time of approximately 201 hours a year, worth about $3,534 annually per American Internet user. Nationally ... about $781 billion annually" (pre-press p. 19). The point estimate of 244 hours a year equals "an average of 40 minutes a day" (pre-press p. 18; journal p. 563, per sister facet L). The authors set this against an online advertising market of about $21 billion in 2007: users "should give up an estimated $781 billion of their time to protect themselves from an industry worth substantially less" (pre-press p. 20). Solove (2013, p. 1889, n. 41) cites the $781 billion figure to journal p. 564.

**Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2020), `obar2018biggest`.** *Record* for the article (Crossref DOI 10.1080/1369118X.2018.1486870; *ICS* 23(1), 128–147). Its results, **as restated by the same authors** in their 2022 article (p. 4784), which I read: an experimental survey of undergraduates (N = 543) joining a fictitious network, NameDrop; "Seventy-four percent" took the clickwrap and skipped the privacy policy; the policy "should have taken about 30 minutes to read" and those who opened it averaged 76 seconds (median 14 s); the terms of service needed 16 minutes and drew an average 51 seconds (median 14 s); "Ninety-seven percent of participants agreed to the PP and 93% to the TOS, with 98% missing the gotcha clauses" (data sharing with the NSA; a first-born child as payment). The 2020 article's own phrase, quoted in 2022: participants aimed to "pursue the ends of digital production, without being inhibited by the means" (2020, p. 128, via 2022 p. 4784). Read the 2020 article before quoting its numbers from it directly.

**Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2022), `obar2022older`.** *Part* (open-access PDF; abstract, pp. 4780, 4784, 4795–4796). Replication with adults 50+ (N = 500): "77.6% chose the clickwrap"; average reading time about 70 seconds for the privacy policy and 81.4 seconds for the terms; "91.4% of participants accepted the NameDrop PP, which included data collection/sharing 'gotcha' clauses, while 83.4% accepted the TOS, including an extreme clause requiring users to provide a kidney or other 'redundant organ' in exchange for service" (abstract, p. 4779). The authors frame the result as burden: "a primary flaw of the data privacy transparency efforts of digital service providers is the impossible burden placed on the individual (McDonald & Cranor, 2008; Solove, 2012)", who stands "as both perpetual data subject and overwhelmed privacy savior" (p. 4780). Their remedy is placed on providers and policymakers, not readers: they "must do more to address the burden placed on individuals through deceptive interface designs like the clickwrap, and via TOS and privacy policies that are long and complicated" (p. 4796; see also p. 4795, "overburdened privacy savior").

**Solove (2013), `solove2013introduction`.** *Full* (HLR PDF, pp. 1880–1903). The most direct statement of the burden claim in the privacy literature.
- The regime: "rights to notice, access, and consent" that he names "privacy self-management", and which "is being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities" (p. 1880).
- The cognitive chain: "(1) people do not read privacy policies; (2) if people read them, they do not understand them; (3) if people read and understand them, they often lack enough background knowledge to make an informed choice; and (4) if people read them, understand them, and can make an informed choice, their choice might be skewed by various decisionmaking difficulties" (p. 1888).
- The structural problem survives perfect readers: "Even assuming that people are fully informed and rational ... privacy self-management also faces serious structural problems" (p. 1888); "even if all companies provided notice and adequate choices, this data management problem would persist; the average person just does not have enough time or resources to manage all the entities that hold her data" (p. 1889). The image is the student whose five or six professors each assign a reasonable night's reading (p. 1889).
- Simplification trades against meaning: "making it simple and easy to understand conflicts with fully informing people about the consequences of giving up data" (p. 1885); education efforts "fail to address a deeper problem — privacy is quite complicated" (p. 1886).
- The burden in so many words: "Privacy self-management cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals" (p. 1893).
- Opt-in will fail: firms "will have the sophistication and motivation to find ways to generate high opt-in rates"; the iTunes agreement "often extends to more than fifty screens" (p. 1898); affirmative consent leads "to more buttons to click and more forms to sign, but not to more meaningful privacy protection" (pp. 1898–1899).
- **Against a strong reading of the thesis:** "For all its flaws, privacy self-management should not be abandoned. Providing people with notice, access, and the ability to control their data is key to facilitating some autonomy" (p. 1899). Its main effect may fall on firms: "perhaps the greatest practical impact of privacy self-management is not in informing individuals ... but in informing the companies that are collecting and using the data" (p. 1900). Self-management "is currently asked to bear far more weight than it can support" (p. 1900). Remedies: partial self-management, regulating downstream uses "at the time they are proposed", perhaps agency review (p. 1902), and "Moving Toward Substance over Neutrality", since "Consent often becomes a convenient way to reach outcomes without confronting the central values at stake" (pp. 1902–1903).

**Nissenbaum (2011), `nissenbaum2011contextual`.** *Full*, algorithmacy-library card (`algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/nissenbaum2011.md`, read twice against two copies). "There is considerable agreement that transparency-and-choice has failed" (p. 35); the transparency paradox, "transparency of textual meaning and transparency of practice conflict in all but rare instances" (p. 36); medical consent works only inside "a framework of supporting assurances" — "It is not the consent form itself that draws our signature ... but rather our faith in the system", whereas "In the online environment, by contrast, individual consent agreements must carry the entire weight of expectation" (p. 36); "burdening individuals with the full weight of protecting their privacy online through notice-and-choice is unlikely to yield success" (p. 43). Her remedy is substantive context norms embedded "in law and other specialized codes" (p. 45).

## 2. The transparency critique in algorithmic governance

**Ananny and Crawford (2018), `ananny2018seeing`.** *Abstract* (Crossref abstract verified on the algorithmacy card) plus two passages reached through citing works I read or that sit on verified cards. Abstract: "Being able to see a system is sometimes equated with being able to know how it works and govern it ... But can 'black boxes' ever be opened, and if so, would that ever be sufficient?"; the article presents "10 limitations" and "an alternative typology of algorithmic accountability". Via Selbst and Barocas (2018, p. 1094, n. 48, citing A&C at 974): "Transparency concerns are commonly driven by a certain chain of logic: observation produces insights which create the knowledge required to govern and hold systems accountable." Via Edwards and Veale (2017, p. 67, verified card): transparency places "a tremendous burden on individuals ... only then to find out they have little power to change things anyway, being 'disconnected from power.'" *Caution:* the "tremendous burden" wording is Edwards and Veale's paraphrase around a quoted phrase ("disconnected from power"); I could not check which words are Ananny and Crawford's. The ten limitations are unread.

**Selbst and Barocas (2018), `selbst2018intuitive`.** *Part* (Fordham PDF, local; p. 1094 and its notes reread). **This is the cleanest located statement that transparency puts the work of understanding on the observer**: "The process of moving from observation to knowledge to accountability cannot be assumed; in many cases, the ability to leverage observations for accountability requires preexisting knowledge that allows observers to appreciate the significance of a disclosure" (p. 1094; n. 49 cites Burrell at 4). The same page: "Transparency of systems of decision-making is important, but incomplete", and, against making this an algorithm-only problem, "the problem of expertise—like the problem of secrecy—is not unique to algorithms" (p. 1094). Abstract (algorithmacy card): existing law addresses inscrutability, but this "may not help if the goal is to assess whether the basis for decision-making is normatively defensible"; one "must seek explanations of the process behind a model's development, not just explanations of the model itself" (p. 1086).

**Burrell (2016), `burrell2016machine`.** *Full*, algorithmacy-library card (`burrell2016.md`, S2-checked against the Wayback copy of the version of record). Three opacities: secrecy (remediable by disclosure or audit), "technical illiteracy" (remediable by education), and the mismatch "between mathematical optimization in high-dimensionality ... and the demands of human-scale reasoning". End-user explanations listing top-weighted criteria "provide an understanding that is at best incomplete and at worst false reassurance". Her remedies include external audit and, "perhaps surprisingly", not using machine learning in some domains. The HTML has no page numbers; cite by section.

**Edwards and Veale (2017), `edwards2017slave`.** *Full* (verified card). The search for a right to explanation "may be at best distracting, and at worst nurture a new kind of 'transparency fallacy'" (abstract); "Individuals are mostly too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise to meaningfully make use of these individual rights" (p. 67); individual rights "rely too much on individual rights for what are too often group harms" (p. 81); they prefer "creating better systems, with less opacity, clearer audit trails, well and holistically trained designers, and input from concerned publics" (p. 82).

**Kaminski (2019), `kaminski2019right`.** *Part* (preprint reread this session, pp. 18–19, 23). **The direct reply to Edwards and Veale, and it narrows the thesis.** "To summarize the right to explanation and accompanying transparency measures, as some have, as a 'transparency fallacy'—palliative measures requiring mere icons or simplistic explanations—is to both misrepresent their actual substance and mischaracterize the GDPR's overall transparency regime" (p. 19). The GDPR approximates Pasquale's "qualified transparency": "targeted revelations of different degrees of depth and scope, aimed at different recipients", including "internal company oversight, oversight by regulators, oversight by third parties, and communications to affected individuals" (p. 19). "Individuals might not have access to source code or data sets, but other parties do" (p. 23). Earlier rounds (N2) recorded her own caveat: reliance on "collaborative governance in the absence of significant public or third-party oversight is not a known recipe for success" (p. 25).

**Kaminski and Urban (2021), `kaminski2021right`.** *Part* (verified card). A right to contest private-sector AI "is unlikely to include a neutral arbiter, at least at first bite. ... interested actors make the decisions" (p. 2039). Bearing: the reviewer of a disclosure-based safeguard is the deploying organisation, which fits the post's "us".

## 3. Experiments on explanation and human oversight of AI

All entries in this section rest on S2-verified cards in the sister arm's library (`/Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/wt-dxd-foundation/submissions/dxd_foundation/literature/library/<key>.md`) or in the algorithmacy library, except where marked. **The direction of the evidence is mixed.** Explanations rarely improve accuracy over the AI's advice alone; they often raise acceptance of wrong advice; but some designs do lower overreliance, and every study is one person and one machine.

**Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (2021), `poursabzi2021manipulating`.** *Full* (sister card; N = 3,800 over four pre-registered MTurk experiments). A clear two-feature model let people simulate its predictions better (Exp. 1: t(994) = −12.06), but people did not follow it more than a black box (t(994) = 0.67, p = 0.5), and those shown the clear model were *less* able to detect its large mistake on an unusual apartment (Exp. 1: F(1, 994) = 8.81, p = 0.003; replicated for one of two apartments in Exp. 2; absent in Exp. 3, where people guessed first; removed by an "outlier focus" message in Exp. 4). The authors' rule: "testing, not intuition" (p. 26). *Bearing:* seeing inside raised prediction of the model, not detection of its failures; the fix that worked was a flag the system raised, not more to read.

**Bansal et al. (2021), `bansal2021whole`.** *Full* (sister card; 1,626 MTurk users, AI accuracy matched to human). Confidence alone produced complementary performance (Beer: team 0.89 against AI 0.84 and humans 0.82); no explanation condition beat it (0.89 against 0.88, z = −1.18, p = .24); explanations "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness" (abstract), better when the AI was right and worse when it was wrong (§5.1, Fig. 4B). *Narrows:* with immediate feedback after each item, some workers learned when to rely on the AI (§5.3), which is learning by acting, not by reading.

**Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021), `bucinca2021trust`.** *Full* (sister card; arXiv v1, N = 199). Cognitive forcing functions (AI advice on request, after one's own decision, or after a wait) beat simple explanations only on the six wrong-AI questions (correct source picked: no AI .49, explanation .08, forcing .27) and never reached the no-AI level; the authors found no support that forcing functions improve team performance overall (p. 188:16). People "performed best in conditions that they preferred and trusted the least" (p. 188:16), and benefits went mainly to high need-for-cognition participants, which the authors name "intervention-generated inequalities" (p. 188:12). *Bearing:* the design that helped adds friction and reorders the task; it does not add text. Its gains fall unevenly.

**Vasconcelos et al. (2023), `vasconcelos2023explanations`.** *Full* (sister card; five studies, N = 731, maze task). **Against a blanket "explanations fail":** a highlighted-path explanation lowered overreliance in the hard task (50×50 maze; estimate 1.74, CI 0.89 to 2.76) though not in easy or medium mazes; a written step-list carrying the same content did no better than no explanation (Study 2); a salient explanation that coloured the path where it crossed a wall averaged 0% overreliance (Study 3, exploratory, p. 129:21); higher pay lowered overreliance (Study 4). Conclusion: "overreliance is not merely an immutable inevitability of cognition but at least, in part, a strategic choice" (p. 129:32). *Bearing:* explanations help when they make verification cheaper than doing the task, which is a change of form, not more reading; a text with the same content did nothing.

**Schemmer et al. (2022), `schemmer2022meta`.** *Full* (sister card). Explanation over AI advice alone: SMD 0.07, 95% CI −0.15 to 0.30, 11 studies (p. 620); explanation-plus-AI over humans alone: 0.59 [0.39, 0.79], 33 studies (p. 620). "Just one article reports some form of appropriate reliance" (p. 624); pure acceptance increase "can be understood as a form of manipulating people to blindly accept AI advice" (p. 625). Quote both numbers together.

**Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (2024), `vaccaro2024when`.** *Full* (sister card; 106 experiments, 370 effect sizes). Human–AI combinations did worse than the better of human or AI alone (g = −0.23), worst in decision tasks (g = −0.27); explanation and confidence were not significant moderators (p. 2295); researchers "may wish to de-emphasize this line of inquiry" (p. 2297). A between-study null, not proof of no effect.

**Lee et al. (2025), `lee2025impact`.** *Full* (sister card; survey of 319 knowledge workers, 936 tasks; self-report, cross-sectional). Confidence in the AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking (β = −0.69), self-confidence with more (β = 0.26); critical thinking shifts "from information gathering to information verification" and toward "task stewardship" (§5.2); GenAI "may also reduce critical engagement, particularly in routine or lower-stakes tasks in which users simply rely on AI" (§1, p. 2). *Against the thesis on remedy:* the authors' own design advice is partly explanatory ("providing explanations of AI reasoning, suggesting areas for user refinement, or offering guided critiques", §6.1.2). "Our analysis does not establish causation" (§6.1.1).

**Simkute et al. (2024), `simkute2024ironies`.** *Full* (sister card; preprint, narrative synthesis). With GenAI, "users' roles have shifted from producing output to evaluating it, often with little contextual information and situational awareness" (p. 2). Its first remedy is feedback "tied to carefully designed explainability features" (p. 13), so it documents the reading burden and then prescribes more legible output.

**Bainbridge (1983), `bainbridge1983ironies`.** *Full* (Pergamon PDF reread this session, pp. 775–777; also the S2-verified algorithmacy-library card `algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/bainbridge1983.md`). The designer "still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate" (p. 775); vigilance cannot be sustained "for more than about half an hour" (p. 776); where the computer uses finer criteria than a human can follow, "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly ... The human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776); the job is "very boring but very responsible, yet there is no opportunity to aquire [sic] or maintain the qualities required to handle the responsibility" (p. 776). Remedies: the computer should decide "using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow" (p. 777); "hands-on control for a short period in each shift" (p. 777); systems "should fail obviously" (p. 777); and "it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training" (p. 777). *Bearing on wrong (c):* Bainbridge's remedy is scaffolded practice, not readable records.

**Green (2022), `green2022flaws`.** *Full* (sister card; 41 policy documents). Oversight rules "rest on an uninterrogated assumption: that people are able to effectively oversee algorithmic decision-making" (abstract, p. 1); "Algorithmic transparency similarly reduces people's ability to detect and correct model errors" (p. 8, citing Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al.); the overseer becomes the "scapegoat" (p. 10); "The default assumption should be that human oversight is likely to be ineffective, unless proven otherwise" (p. 15). Green leaves better design "an open empirical question" (p. 11) and moves oversight to institutions (pp. 12–14).

**Elish (2019), `elish2019moral`.** *Full* (sister card). The moral crumple zone: responsibility "may be misattributed to a human actor who had limited control over the behavior of an automated or autonomous system", protecting "the integrity of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human operator" (abstract, p. 40); "While control has been effectively distributed, responsibility has not scaled accordingly" (p. 49). At Three Mile Island "the operators made incorrect decisions because they had incorrect information" (p. 44).

**Crootof, Kaminski and Price (2023), `crootof2023humans`.** *Full* (sister card). The "MABA-MABA trap", "an assumption that is intuitive, wrong, and dangerous: that human-machine systems represent the best of both worlds" (p. 438); the "tautological challenge of relying on humans to monitor the performance of systems designed to improve on human performance" (p. 469); a human may be held responsible legitimately only "if the human in the loop has the power, information, judgment, and time to make the final decision" (pp. 482–83).

*Bearing on the post.* The post's P15 asks that "Every meaningful model action must leave tamper-proof human readable evidence" and its P9 calls chain-of-thought transparency "non-negotiable" while saying it is not sufficient alone. This section's studies concern a reader of AI output or explanation at decision time; none tests after-the-fact audit of agent logs by trained staff (the sister card on Green says the same). The transfer is by analogy.

## 4. Responsibilization and notice-and-choice as burden-shifting

**Giesler and Veresiu (2014), `giesler2014creating`.** *Full* (card, verified: corrected). Responsibilization proceeds through "four distinct but interrelated processes through which responsibility is shifted away from the state and corporations and the responsible consumer subject is shaped" (p. 841); personalization moves a problem "from the systemic to the level of individual decision making" (p. 843); the fourth case is "the financially literate consumer" (p. 851), for whom the WEF "shifted the problem of financial debt away from the level of better financial regulation to a need for more conscious individual financial decision makers" (p. 851).

**Shamir (2008), `shamir2008age`.** *Record* (Economy and Society 37(1), 1–19; no open copy). Known through Giesler and Veresiu: responsibilization is a "technique of the self that sets into action a reflexive subjectivity deemed suitable to partake in the deployment of horizontal authority and one which willingly bears the consequences of its actions" (Shamir 2008, p. 4, via Giesler and Veresiu p. 841; facet N4); and Shamir "describes corporations as one target of responsibilization" (p. 841). Do not quote Shamir directly until read.

**Notice-and-choice named as burden-shifting.** The privacy literature says it in plain terms, which spares the paper an inference: Solove, privacy self-management "cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals" (2013, p. 1893); Nissenbaum, "burdening individuals with the full weight of protecting their privacy online through notice-and-choice is unlikely to yield success" (2011, p. 43); Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch, "the impossible burden placed on the individual", the user as "overwhelmed privacy savior" (2022, p. 4780); Edwards and Veale, individuals "too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise" (2017, p. 67). Ben-Shahar and Schneider pair disclosure with literacy education and report the education "has largely failed" (2011, p. 667, via sister OCR read). Giesler and Veresiu supply the sociological name, responsibilization, and the literacy case (financial literacy). **I found no work that applies the responsibilization frame to AI or algorithmic *disclosure* specifically** in the sources this facet and facets M4 and N4 searched (Crossref and web searches recorded there; M4 found only an unopened arXiv title on "AI literacy" and power-knowledge).

## 5. The counter-literature: disclosure and transparency defended

**Calo (2012), `calo2012against`.** *Part* (Notre Dame production file, journal pagination; pp. 1027–1035, 1047–1050, 1069–1072). The strongest defence of notice I read, and it cuts two ways. Its thesis: "experience as a form of privacy disclosure is worthy of further study before we give in to calls to abandon notice as a regulatory strategy" (p. 1027). Notice has a legitimating role in law (Fuller's "internal morality", Hayek on the rule of law, p. 1028); "Notice purports to respect the basic autonomy of the consumer or citizen by arming her with information and placing the ultimate decision in her hands" (p. 1049); and "notice is the worst regulatory mechanism, except for all of the alternatives" (p. 1047), since substitutes are "perceived to be invasive, ineffective, expensive, or arbitrary" (p. 1047). He concedes the textual case: "Studies show only marginal improvement in consumer understanding where privacy policies get expressed as tables, icons, or labels, assuming the consumer even reads them" (p. 1033). **His remedy leaves text behind:** "visceral" notice "does not rely exclusively on language or its symbolic equivalent. Rather, it is 'visceral,' in the sense of changing the consumers understanding by leveraging the very experience of a product or service" (p. 1033); a camera that must make a shutter sound is his first example (p. 1027). He keeps it a notice strategy, whose purpose is "to ensure consumers have the correct mental model of the product or service and can make decisions accordingly" (p. 1070). Conclusion: "Emerging strategies that do not necessarily rely on words or symbols to convey salient information to consumers may not be susceptible to the same withering critiques as more traditional notice" (pp. 1071–1072). *Bearing:* against the claim that disclosure is ethically empty (notice respects autonomy, and its alternatives have costs), and **for** wrong (b): the most cited defender of notice saves it only by moving it out of the text and into the experience of using the product.

**Solove (2013).** *Full* (see §1). Defends keeping self-management for autonomy (p. 1899) and warns that the alternative, paternalism, "even more directly denies people the freedom to make consensual choices about their data" (pp. 1881–1882). His "consent dilemma" is the objection the paper must meet: if disclosure is not sufficient, the obvious replacement removes choice.

**Kim and Routledge (2022), `kim2022why`.** *Part* (card, verified). Informed consent reconceived "as an assurance of trust for incomplete transactions" grounds "a right to ex post explanation" (p. 75); data subjects can trust firms "only when the relationship is predicated on the companies' readiness/commitment to be accountable" (p. 88). Their own limit: "We do not believe that a right to ex post explanation can fundamentally solve such problems" (p. 101). *Bearing:* a business-ethics argument that disclosure (here, ex post explanation) is owed to the person as a matter of trust, independent of whether it improves her decisions. The post's P20 ("timely disclosure to those affected") fits their ex post model.

**Kaminski (2019).** *Part* (see §2). Calling the GDPR's explanation right a "transparency fallacy" misrepresents a layered regime of "targeted revelations ... aimed at different recipients" (p. 19). *Bearing:* disclosure regimes need not put the whole load on the individual reader; the reading can be distributed to regulators, auditors and internal reviewers.

**Fung, Graham and Weil (2007), `fung2007full`.** *Part*, via the sister arm's facet L (entry 3; read in part from the Ash Center PDF, printed pagination). "Transparency systems alter decisions only when they provide pertinent information that enables people to substantially improve their choices without imposing significant additional costs" (p. 55); effective disclosure is "doubly embedded" in the user's and the discloser's routines (p. 65); grade cards "convert data and expert interpretation into simple normative signals such as stars or letter grades" (p. 57). *Bearing:* the targeted-transparency school places the success condition in the design of the disclosure, not in the reader's skill, which narrows the thesis from "disclosure fails" to "disclosure designed as text for a reader fails".

**Loewenstein, Sunstein and Golman (2014)** and **Solove (2013, p. 1900)** both record that disclosure duties act on the discloser (the "telltale heart" effect; notices that force firms "to inspect their own practices"). *Bearing:* a disclosure regime can be ethically useful even when no one reads it, because it disciplines the organisation that writes it. That fits the post's P15 reading of evidence as an internal control.

**Disclosure that changes behaviour.** Sister facet L (entries 24, 26, 27) records studies in which a single statement of an algorithm's rule changed how people used it: Cen et al. (2024; read in part there), likes used 1.9 times more when users were told the algorithm attends to likes; Jhaver et al. (2019; abstract only there), removal explanations reduced later removals; Tyler et al. (abstract only there), a procedural-justice message reduced violations. Facet L's summary: "disclosure changes behaviour, and often in the wrong direction or on the wrong party" (§(c) Q5). I did not reread these; cite facet L.

**Informed-consent ethics.** O'Neill (2003), "Some limits of informed consent", *J Med Ethics* 29(1), 4–7 (`oneill2003some`): *Record* (Crossref). The PMC copy returned a bot challenge and I did not work around it. It is the standard medical-ethics source for the view that consent is a limited waiver rather than full autonomous choice and belongs in the objections section once read. Nissenbaum's medical-consent passage (2011, p. 36, §1) already supplies the point the paper needs: consent forms work in medicine because a "framework of supporting assurances" carries most of the weight.

## Sorted: for, narrows, against

### For the thesis
1. **(a) Burden on the reader.** The privacy and disclosure literatures state it in so many words: Solove (privacy self-management "cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals", p. 1893; it "is being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities", p. 1880); Nissenbaum ("burdening individuals with the full weight", p. 43); Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch ("the impossible burden placed on the individual", 2022, p. 4780); Edwards and Veale ("too time-poor, resource-poor, and lacking in the necessary expertise", p. 67); McDonald and Cranor (a modelled 201–244 hours a year; $781 billion nationally, pre-press pp. 18–19).
2. **(a) The burden survives a perfect reader.** Solove's structural argument: even "fully informed and rational" people cannot manage scale, aggregation and future harm (pp. 1888–1891).
3. **(a) Transparency assumes a knowing observer.** Selbst and Barocas: moving "from observation to knowledge to accountability cannot be assumed", and needs "preexisting knowledge that allows observers to appreciate the significance of a disclosure" (p. 1094), restating Ananny and Crawford's "chain of logic" (via p. 1094, A&C at 974).
4. **(a) People do not read.** 74% skipped the policy and 98% missed the gotcha clauses (Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch 2020, as restated in 2022, p. 4784); 77.6% and 91.4%/83.4% among adults 50+ (2022, p. 4779); about 1 in 1,000 open boilerplate (Ben-Shahar and Schneider, p. 671).
5. **(a) The burden moves blame to the person.** Elish's moral crumple zone (p. 40), Green's "scapegoat" (p. 10), Crootof et al.'s "liability sponge" test (pp. 482–83).
6. **(b) Text is the weak form.** Solove: simplification "conflicts with fully informing people" (p. 1885); Ben-Shahar and Schneider: "complexity cannot be explained simply" (p. 713, sister OCR); Calo: tables, icons and labels give "only marginal improvement" (p. 1033), and his rescue of notice leaves "language or its symbolic equivalent" behind (p. 1033); Vasconcelos et al.: a written step-list did nothing where a visual highlight helped (Study 2).
7. **(b) Seeing inside does not help one catch failures.** Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (clear model, worse error detection); Bansal et al. (explanations raise acceptance regardless of correctness); Schemmer et al. (0.07 over AI advice alone); Vaccaro et al. (explanation not a moderator); Burrell ("at best incomplete and at worst false reassurance").
8. **(c) No scaffolding, and literacy education as the failed complement.** Ben-Shahar and Schneider: financial-literacy education "has largely failed" (p. 667, sister OCR); Giesler and Veresiu: financial literacy as responsibilization that moves debt "away from the level of better financial regulation" (p. 851). Bainbridge: the monitor gets "no opportunity to aquire [sic] or maintain the qualities required" (p. 776); "the most successful automated systems ... may need the greatest investment in human operator training" (p. 777).

### Narrows the thesis
1. **The post's charge must be "necessary but treated as enough", not "disclosure is worthless".** Solove keeps self-management for autonomy (p. 1899); Calo calls notice the worst mechanism "except for all of the alternatives" (p. 1047); Kim and Routledge ground ex post explanation in trust (pp. 75, 88).
2. **Disclosure works on the discloser.** Loewenstein et al. (pp. 391, 398, 403) and Solove (p. 1900): the main effect may be on the firm that must write the disclosure. P15's readable evidence may be valuable as internal discipline even if the affected people never read it.
3. **Disclosure can be layered across readers.** Kaminski (2019, pp. 19, 23): regulators, auditors and internal reviewers read deeper than the individual. Nadella's P15 audience is the enterprise, not the end user (SOURCE_v4 note), so "burden on the user" applies most to the organisation's staff as overseers (Bainbridge, Green, Elish) and least to the people the systems act on, who appear only at P20.
4. **Form, not disclosure as such, decides.** Fung et al. (simple signals embedded in routines, pp. 55–65), Vasconcelos et al. (cheap-to-verify explanations reduce overreliance), Calo (experiential notice), Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (an outlier flag fixed the detection loss). These support wrong (b) more than wrong (a): the problem is the reading form, and some non-textual disclosures help.
5. **The scaffolding remedy has its own costs.** Buçinca et al.: forcing functions helped on wrong-AI items only, were disliked, and benefited high-NFC people most ("intervention-generated inequalities", p. 188:12). Fernandes et al. (via sister facet L): financial-literacy interventions explain 0.1% of variance in behaviour and decay. A paper that demands scaffolding to algorithmacy must say why it will not repeat literacy education's weak record.
6. **The AI evidence is dyadic and at decision time.** None of the studies tests after-the-fact audit of agent logs, or a mediated triad; transfer to P15 is analogy.

### Against the thesis
1. **Some explanations help.** Vasconcelos et al. (hard task; salient explanation at 0% overreliance); Bansal et al. (feedback let some workers learn when to rely); explanation-plus-AI beats humans alone (Schemmer et al., 0.59). Lee et al. recommend explanations and guided critiques as remedies (§6.1.2).
2. **Disclosure changes behaviour.** Cen et al. (1.9×), Jhaver et al., Tyler et al. (sister facet L). "Disclosure does nothing" is false; "disclosure does not reliably help the person" is the defensible claim.
3. **Abandoning disclosure has a cost to autonomy.** Solove's consent dilemma (pp. 1881–1882, 1894) and Calo (p. 1049): the alternatives to notice decide for people. The paper must say what replaces the reader's choice without removing it.
4. **The post itself says CoT transparency is not sufficient** (P9) and puts most weight on controls (SOURCE_v4 note). The literature's burden critique targets regimes that rely on the individual reader; the post's reader is mainly the enterprise's own staff. The charge that the post treats disclosure as *sufficient* needs that qualification.

## What the paper can say

(Claims the record supports, with sources. Notes for the drafter, not prose.)

1. Mandated disclosure has been called "the single most common technique for protecting personal autonomy in modern society", and its critics find that it "chronically fails" (Ben-Shahar and Schneider 2011, pp. 647, 651).
2. Privacy law's notice-and-consent regime "cedes substantial responsibility for preserving privacy to individuals" and "is being tasked with doing work beyond its capabilities" (Solove 2013, pp. 1893, 1880); the same critique holds even for "fully informed and rational" readers, because of scale and aggregation (pp. 1888–1891).
3. Reading every privacy policy once a year would cost an estimated 201–244 hours per person and about $781 billion nationally, a modelled figure (McDonald and Cranor 2008, pre-press pp. 18–19).
4. In a controlled sign-up, 74% skipped the privacy policy and 98% missed clauses giving data to the NSA and a first-born child as payment (Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch 2020, as restated in 2022, p. 4784); among adults over 50, 83.4% accepted terms requiring a kidney (2022, p. 4779).
5. Transparency presupposes an observer who already knows enough to see what a disclosure means (Selbst and Barocas 2018, p. 1094), which is the literacy precondition the oral-to-written analogy needs.
6. Human-factors research since Bainbridge (1983, pp. 776–777) holds that a person asked to monitor a system faster and finer than she is has "an impossible task", and that her competence has to be built by practice, not by records; this is the oldest statement of wrong (c).
7. When oversight fails, blame settles on the nearest human (Elish 2019, p. 40; Green 2022, p. 10), so a regime of readable evidence can shift responsibility as well as work.
8. In AI-assisted decisions, adding an explanation to the AI's advice did not improve accuracy on average (Schemmer et al. 2022, SMD 0.07; Vaccaro et al. 2024) and often raised acceptance of wrong advice (Bansal et al. 2021); explanations helped where they made checking cheaper than the task (Vasconcelos et al. 2023).
9. Literacy-style remedies have a weak record: financial-literacy education "has largely failed" (Ben-Shahar and Schneider 2011, p. 667) and is a vehicle of responsibilization (Giesler and Veresiu 2014, pp. 841, 851).
10. Defenders of notice concede the textual case and save notice by moving it into the experience of the product (Calo 2012, pp. 1033, 1071–1072), which is the move from a literacy-based experience the thesis asks for.
11. The paper should state the objection fairly: disclosure respects autonomy (Calo 2012, p. 1049), disciplines the discloser (Loewenstein et al. 2014, p. 398; Solove 2013, p. 1900), can be layered across expert readers (Kaminski 2019, pp. 19, 23), and the alternative to choice is paternalism (Solove's consent dilemma, p. 1894).
