# B — The dispute over Saenger's thesis: silent reading, word separation and what survives

*Corrected on 2026-10-12 against `../audit/AUDIT_01.md`: 11 factual fixes.*

*Facet B of the foundation-paper literature run. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-12. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The reception of Paul Saenger's thesis (*Space Between Words*, 1997) by classicists, medievalists and book historians: Knox 1968, Gavrilov and Burnyeat 1997, Johnson 2000 and 2010, Vatri 2012, McCutcheon 2015, Heilmann (2020, 2021), the published reviews of Saenger's book, and the dissenters who side with Saenger (Flory, Mollon, Svenbro in part). Facet A covers what Saenger and Parkes say themselves; I quote them here only as the critics report them.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What do the critics reject in Saenger (the claim about antiquity, the physiological mechanism, the causal link from spacing to silent reading, or the medieval story), what do they leave standing, and what does that do to H-cost and H-cause in `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §3?

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text this session from a legitimate open copy. *Read in part* = I read the pages named. *Abstract only* = Crossref abstract, publisher preview or database abstract. *Second-hand* = I know the work only through a named reviewer or critic who describes it; no quotation from the work itself is claimed. *Not accessible* = no text and no abstract. Page locators for the open-access PDFs I downloaded are the journal's own printed pagination (Knox, Heilmann) or the review's own pages (Garrett). Where a quotation comes from another scholar's quotation, the entry says so.

**Headline, before the detail.** (1) The sources I could read agree that silent reading existed in antiquity, and that Knox (1968) showed it for documents and short texts; none of them says literary reading was *normally* silent. (2) The sharpest attack on Saenger is on the *mechanism* (Vatri 2012, as reported by Heilmann 2020), not on the medieval manuscript survey. (3) The one classicist review of Saenger I could read in full (O'Donnell 2000) praises the survey and flags exactly the causal step H-cause needs ("necessary and sufficient condition") as the part some would reject. (4) I found no source, pro or con, that measures when silent reading of literary texts became typical; the late-medieval dates are Saenger's own inference from manuscript layout. (5) My depth on Gavrilov, Burnyeat, Johnson (2000) and Vatri is the **opening paragraph or abstract only, plus what Johnson 2000 and Heilmann 2020 say about them**. None of those four was read in full. Every statement below about their arguments carries that limit.

### B1. Background: the old view and the first correction

**balogh1927voces** — Balogh, J. (1927). Voces paginarum: Beiträge zur Geschichte des lauten Lesens und Schreibens. *Philologus, 82*, 84–109, 202–240.
- *Verification:* the citation is confirmed in Knox 1968, p. 421 n. 1, and in the bibliography of Heilmann 2020 (p. 192, "Philologus 82, 84–109.202–240"). No DOI.
- *Read status:* **second-hand** (Knox, in full; Heilmann). Not opened.
- *What it argues, per Knox:* Balogh's aim (p. 87 of the article) was to prove "dass das Altertum alles, was wir heute stumm für uns lesen, stets laut las" (Knox p. 421 n. 3). Knox: "Since the publication of Josef Balogh's article 'Voces Paginarum,' it has become standard doctrine that silent reading (and writing) was, if not completely unknown in the ancient world, at least so rare that whenever it was observed, it aroused astonishment, even suspicion" (p. 421). Heilmann (2020, p. 179) quotes Balogh: "Der Mensch des Altertums las und schrieb in der Regel laut; das Gegenteil war zwar nicht unerhört, doch immer eine Ausnahme" (Balogh 1927, 220).
- *Bearing:* the target of the whole classicist reply. It is the position Parkes (1992, p. 9, per facet E) still repeats.

**knox1968silent** — Knox, B. M. W. (1968). Silent reading in antiquity. *Greek, Roman, and Byzantine Studies, 9*(4), 421–435. https://grbs.library.duke.edu/index.php/grbs/article/view/10731
- *Verification:* journal page and OpenAlex (W record, diamond OA, 242 citations); the PDF is the journal's own and carries the original pagination 421–435. (O'Donnell 2000 cites 421–436; the PDF's last printed page is 435.)
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/knox1968silent.md)** (re-read this session; the card confirms the quotations, pages 421, 422, 427, 434–435, and that Knox never mentions word separation).
- *What it argues:* Balogh's evidence supports reading aloud of *literary* texts and no more. "The evidence assembled by Balogh (which should be supplemented by that of G. L. Hendrickson) does indeed make it perfectly clear that the normal way to read a literary text (non-literary texts are a different matter and will be dealt with later) was out loud, whether before an audience, in the company of friends or alone. But Balogh's insistence that silent reading was not just unusual but almost unheard of seems to go too far" (p. 421). The positive evidence: Cicero, *Tusc.* 5.116 on the deaf reading songs, with "multo maiorem percipi posse legendis his quam audiendis voluptatem" ("Cicero could not possibly have written the concluding phrase if silent reading of poetic texts had been impossible or even a nine-day wonder", p. 427); Antiphanes' *Sappho* riddle, which shows "silent reading of a letter in the presence of others was taken completely for granted" in fourth-century Athens (pp. 432–433); Euripides *Hipp.* 856ff and Aristophanes *Knights* 115ff, both silent reading on stage (p. 433); and the conclusion "These three passages … clearly demonstrate for fifth and fourth century Athens that silent reading of letters and oracles (and consequently of any short document) was taken completely for granted" (p. 434). Final: "Ancient books were normally read aloud, but there is nothing to show that silent reading of books was anything extraordinary except the famous passage from Augustine's *Confessions*, and that is countered by the phrase of Cicero" (p. 435).
- *Augustine and Ambrose:* Augustine's surprise is the surprise of "an African provincial from a poor family" at a man who had been "consular prefect" and educated at Rome; "there is no reason to suppose that Ambrose's silent reading would have excited such comment in the Italian imperial circles" (p. 422). Augustine "was quite capable of adopting it": at *Conf.* 8.12 he reads the Epistle to the Romans "in silentio" (p. 422).
- *Metaphor point:* Balogh's "acoustic" evidence (*pagina loquitur* and similar) is read literally; Theseus's letter "sings", "speaks" and "shouts aloud" in Euripides, "of course it does nothing of the sort; the audience has watched Theseus read the letter, and he did so in silence" (pp. 434–435).
- *What Knox does not do:* he never mentions word separation, spacing or *scriptio continua*; 1968 predates Saenger (1982, 1997). He also does not date when silent reading of books became ordinary. His "except the famous passage" concedes Augustine's report is one piece of evidence that silent reading of books *did* surprise someone, then discounts it by class and provenance.
- *Bearing:* **Refutes** "made possible" (supports the weak form of H-cost, not H-cause). **Complicates** any "made ordinary" wording: Knox's own conclusion is that *books* were normally read aloud (p. 435), so for literary reading "ordinary" silent reading is not shown for antiquity either way.

### B2. The 1997 correction and its aftermath

**gavrilov1997techniques** — Gavrilov, A. K. (1997). Techniques of reading in classical antiquity. *The Classical Quarterly, 47*(1), 56–73. https://doi.org/10.1093/cq/47.1.56
- *Verification:* Crossref record (title, container, pages 56–73, issued May 1997); Cambridge Core landing page (WebFetch). Cited as "Gavrilov, Aleksandr K. (1997) … CQ 47, 56–73" in Heilmann 2020's bibliography.
- *Read status:* **abstract only** (Crossref abstract, which is the paper's opening). Unpaywall: closed; OpenAlex: JSTOR 639597 only. **Not read in full.**
- *What the abstract says (verbatim from Crossref):* "It has gradually become accepted among historians of ancient culture that the Greeks and Romans always, or nearly always, read aloud. They did not read to themselves, silently, save in rare and special cases. Either they were not able to read silently, or they felt no need to do so, or they did not enjoy doing it even when they were alone." The paper then attacks that view; the abstract does not state the attack. What the paper argues is known to me only from Johnson 2000, p. 593 (below): Gavrilov and Burnyeat "improved the evidential base, by refining interpretation (especially Gavrilov on Augustine) … and by adding observations from cognitive psychology."
- *Bearing:* supports silent reading existing. **I cannot say whether Gavrilov addresses word separation or Saenger.** Johnson's "observations from cognitive psychology" may bear on it; the claim is untested here.

**johnson2000toward** — Johnson, W. A. (2000). Toward a sociology of reading in classical antiquity. *American Journal of Philology, 121*(4), 593–627. https://doi.org/10.1353/ajp.2000.0053
- *Verification:* Crossref; MUSE record; Heilmann 2020 bibliography (593–627).
- *Read status:* **opening paragraph only** (verbatim via Consensus; the MUSE page was a verification wall and the article is closed everywhere). **Not read in full.** Heilmann 2020 names Johnson 1994, 2000, 2004 and 2009 at pp. 177 and 190 (n. 82, n. 85) as holding that reader's aids served reading aloud and that line length served performance; he argues against that position (see below).
- *What it says in the opening paragraph (p. 593; OCR hyphenation removed):* "In the last century, scholarly debate on ancient reading has largely revolved around the question 'Did the ancient Greeks and Romans read aloud or silently?' Given the recent work of Gavrilov and Burnyeat, which has set the debate on new, seemingly firmer, footing, the question is at first glance easily answered. Without hesitation we can now assert that there was no cognitive difficulty when fully literate ancient readers wished to read silently to themselves, and that the cognitive act of silent reading was neither extraordinary nor noticeably unusual in antiquity. This conclusion has been known to careful readers since at least 1968, when Bernard Knox demonstrated beyond reasonable doubt that the silent reading of ancient documentary texts, including letters, is accepted by ancient witnesses as an ordinary event. Gavrilov and Burnyeat have improved the evidential base, by refining interpretation (especially Gavrilov on Augustine), by focusing on neglected but important evidence (Burnyeat on Ptolemy), and by adding observations from cognitive psychology. The resulting clarity is salutary. Yet I suspect many will be dissatisfied with the terms in which the debate has been couched."
- *Two things the paragraph does and does not do.* (i) It is the source of the "closed" characterisation in `REUSE.md`; the word "closed" is the plan's, Johnson says "easily answered" and "seemingly firmer". (ii) It names *documentary* texts for Knox ("silent reading of ancient documentary texts, including letters"), which matches Knox's own limit. Johnson does not say literary reading was silent.
- *Johnson's pivot (second-hand, via reviewers of his 2010 book):* the question should be social, "the negotiated construction of meaning within a particular sociocultural context" (Reis 2011, quoting Johnson 2010, p. 12; Ker 2012 gives it as p. 12, emphasis in the original).
- *Bearing:* **Supports** that "impossible" is gone as a position among the scholars Johnson cites. **Complicates** the plan's "closed question": Heilmann 2020 (B3) says the opposite about the field, ten years later (see §c, Q1).

**johnson2010readers** — Johnson, W. A. (2010). *Readers and reading culture in the High Roman Empire: A study of elite communities*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195176407.001.0001
- *Verification:* Crossref (book and chapter records: ch. 1 "Reading as a Sociocultural System" pp. 3–16; ch. 2 "Pragmatics of Reading" pp. 17–31); three published reviews (below).
- *Read status:* **second-hand only.** No full text and no free chapter found. Chapter 2 is the one that bears on *scriptio continua*, and I have it only through the reviewers.
- *What it argues, per the reviewers:*
  - Ker (*CJ* 2012): chapter 2 shows "that certain general characteristics of ancient reading and ancient bookrolls, such as the absence of spaces between words (scriptio continua), are not signs of a technological lack, but are consistent with an elite culture in which reading from a bookroll with full articulation and comprehension is a marker of education and status, and often an act of connoisseurship." Johnson's purpose in treating Ambrose "is less to prove that silent reading is known much earlier … than to draw attention to Augustine's expectations and the reasons for his surprise at Ambrose's habit."
  - Reis (*Religious Studies Review* 2011): "Johnson shows that the scriptio continua of the bookroll and its costly aesthetic features ensured that the consumers of texts were limited. Yet restricting the book's accessibility was precisely the point for elites who used reading to create and reaffirm the values of high society."
  - Mannering (BMCR 2012.11.37): "The considerable effort in apprehending scriptio continua with only minimal punctuation is interpreted by Johnson as a hallmark of the exclusivity of literary culture as social institution," and the bookroll needed "years of training required for its proper use." Chapter 1 moves "beyond speculation on the neurophysiology of reading, the cognitive act having been settled by scholarly consensus as essentially the same for the ancients as for us today."
- *Bearing:* **Supports** the social half of Saenger's own line, "the ancient world did not possess the desire, characteristic of the modern age, to make reading easier and swifter" (Saenger 1997, p. 11), and so Q4's answer. It **complicates** H-cost's premise that spacing *lowered* a cost: the reviewers describe Johnson as treating the cost as real (Mannering: "considerable effort", "years of training") and valued. That is nearer the author's sentence ("the interface … required the training") than anything in the physiological critics. All of this rests on three reviewers and must be checked against chapter 2.

**vatri2012physiology** — Vatri, A. (2012). The physiology of ancient Greek reading. *The Classical Quarterly, 62*(2), 633–647. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0009838812000213
- *Verification:* Crossref (no abstract field); Heilmann 2020 bibliography; JSTOR 23470128.
- *Read status:* **opening paragraphs only** (verbatim via Consensus), **plus what Heilmann 2020 quotes from pp. 638–639 and 646–647**. **Not read in full.** The one open-looking PDF is reachable only through another library's proxy; I did not use it.
- *What the opening paragraphs say (p. 633):* "In the 1980s and 1990s, the medievalist Paul Saenger argued in several publications that it was physiologically impossible for the ancient Greeks and Romans to read silently. His contributions revived an age-old controversy, which was first raised by Balogh in the early twentieth century and seemed to have been settled in 1968 by Knox, who concluded that silent reading was possible and not even unusual in antiquity. Unlike his predecessors, however, Saenger did not base his arguments on the interpretation of the literary sources for ancient reading, but tackled the question from the point of view of cognitive psychology. He developed a detailed model of the processes involved in reading ancient scriptura continua, claiming that the very lack of word separation made vocal rehearsal necessary for the readers to recognize the words. Classical sources hinting at silent reading, in his view, might rather refer to silent, or sub-vocal, oral reading – a sort of mumbling. Since their first appearance, Saenger's ideas have been met with partial acceptance or more or less harsh criticism by classicists. Svenbro, for example, acknowledges that scriptura continua hindered silent reading. However, he also maintains that the Greeks were able to practise it because of a change of attitude towards the written word."
- *What Heilmann 2020 reports Vatri as concluding (quotations are Heilmann's, p. 181, from Vatri pp. 638–639 and 646–647):* "the readers' deep-rooted habits play a major role, and this must also have applied to the ancient readers of scriptura continua. […] [T]here is no reason to assume that reading unspaced text is a particularly demanding cognitive task in itself, and Saenger's model must be rejected." And: "No physiological constraints prevent the Greeks from reading silently." Heilmann also reports that Vatri rests on "recent cross-cultural comparative studies" (Kajii et al. 2001; Sainio et al. 2007; Bai et al. 2008) showing "that the addition of spaces between words is redundant and can even disrupt the reading process" (this sentence is Heilmann's own summary of those studies, p. 181, n. 22, citing Vatri 2012, 638f. and others; it is not a quotation from Vatri), on Thai eye movements ("exactly the same as those of English readers", Winskel et al. 2009, via Vatri p. 639), and on Vatri's own frequency analysis of Greek word beginnings and endings (p. 181).
- *Bearing:* **Refutes** the physiological mechanism (the "made possible" version of H-cause, and the strong form of H-cost), on the account I can verify only second-hand. **Complicates** my own use: Vatri frames Saenger's claim as "physiologically impossible"; Saenger's book (per facet E, p. 8) says Ambrose read silently but not rapidly. Vatri's opening also states Saenger's real claim as *vocal rehearsal*, "a sort of mumbling", i.e. a claim about a mode of reading, not a prohibition.

**mccutcheon2015silent** — McCutcheon, R. W. (2015). Silent reading in antiquity and the future history of the book. *Book History, 18*(1), 1–32. https://doi.org/10.1353/bh.2015.0011
- *Verification:* Crossref; MUSE record.
- *Read status:* **abstract only** (Crossref). A PDF exists on a personal website (found by WebSearch); I did not open it (unauthorised copy).
- *What the abstract says (verbatim):* "Book historians have long insisted that silent reading was a rare or nonexistent practice in classical antiquity. In this paper, I offer a critical review of the evidence and the scholarship concerning reading in antiquity before offering an explanation for these conflicting views on the subject. **heilmann2020reading** — Heilmann, J. (2020). Reading early New Testament manuscripts: *Scriptio continua*, "reading aids", and other characteristic features. In *Material aspects of reading in ancient and medieval cultures* (pp. 177–196). De Gruyter. https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110639247-011 (CC BY-NC-ND; read from the LMU Munich repository copy)
- *Verification:* Crossref (title, container, pages 177–196, 5 May 2020, De Gruyter); Unpaywall open locations; the PDF's own running heads (the part on *scriptio continua* is pp. 178–183).
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/heilmann2020reading.md)** for the *scriptio continua* section (pp. 178–184) and its notes, and the conclusions (pp. 190–191); the rest (diacritics, paragraphoi, nomina sacra) was read by search, not line by line. The card confirms the page locators used here (pp. 178–183, 190). Addition from the card: Heilmann relies on Vatri for the Thai result and does not cite the 2009 study's sentence-level slowing, which Chantavarin and Leung (2025, `chantavarin2025effects`) report.
- *Why it matters:* it is the only post-2012 source I could read in full that argues the case against Saenger's mechanism in print, and it supplies the Latin-side evidence (interpuncts, Vindolanda) that bears on Q4. Caveat: Heilmann is writing here on New Testament manuscripts and arguing his own thesis (that Christian papyri's reader's aids were not for public reading), so he has a stake in dismantling the "needs reading aloud" premise. His treatment of Saenger is a few pages, in service of that.
- *What it argues (all quotations are from the pages cited):*
  - Against Saenger's proof structure, p. 178: "The most elaborated defense of this view can be found in P. Saenger's book 'Space between Words: The Origins of Silent Reading,' and it is a view commonly accepted in Classical Studies as well as New Testament Studies. However, the arguments of P. On the contrary, they presuppose precisely what must be proved."
  - On the state of the field, p. 179: reading aloud is "still taken for granted today"; "Well-argued counterproposals (Knox 1968; Gavrilov 1997; Burnyeat 1997; Svenbro 1999, 2002, 2005; Burfeind 2002) have not been accepted against this standard view, which has become a quasi-dogma." (Counterproposals named in n. 13; the list of those who accept the standard view is n. 8, pp. 178.)
  - On evidence generally, p. 180: "the sources do not allow for statistical generalizations as one can find them as the basis of the common opinion outlined above. In unmarked cases, one cannot clearly decide if someone read with or without his voice." And a distinction (p. 179) between "use of the voice" and "volume/acoustic perceptibility", so that the silent/aloud binary is "too narrow".
  - On the mechanism, pp. 180–181: Heilmann concedes the premise, "average readers socialized in modern 'western' scripts read English texts in scriptio continua slower and … the PVL shifts from the middle of the word to the beginning of the word" (p. 180), then calls the inference "methodologically problematic in that this research data is based exclusively on test subjects that have been socialized in modern 'western' scripts and untrained in reading scriptio continua" (p. 180). He cites Epelboim et al. 1994/1997 as showing "one can train the eye to read scriptio continua and that spaces between words are not the primary factor controlling eye movement" (pp. 180–181). **He also lists, in n. 20, "the controversy between Epelboim/Booth/Steinman 1996 … and Rayner/Pollatsek 1996"** (Rayner and Pollatsek's reply is titled "Reading Unspaced Text Is Not Easy"). So the eye-movement literature is itself contested, and Heilmann's presentation is one side of it; facet D should settle it.
  - On the ancient sources, pp. 181–182: those cited to prove difficulty "should rather be interpreted within the context of the process of learning to read and/or improving reading ability" (Dion. Hal. *Comp.* 25; Quint. *Inst.* 1.1.34; Lucian *Adv. ind.* 2; Petron. *Sat.* 75.4, n. 28), or discourses about illiteracy (Gell. 13.31); "some … refer to Latin texts, which were not written in scriptio continua until the early imperial period" (p. 182); scribal errors "do not prove that reading scriptio continua would have been particularly demanding" (p. 182).
  - On the Roman change, p. 182: "Moreover, the fact that the Romans replaced their habit of writing scriptio discontinua with scriptio continua in the second century CE shows that scriptio continua is not a characteristic of deficient writing systems." He cites Oliver (1951) for noticing the late introduction in Latin and Wingo (1972), quoting Wingo p. 15: "The word-divider is regularly found on all good inscriptions, in papyri, on wax tablets, and even in graffiti from the earliest Republican times through the Golden Age and well into the Second Century." (Oliver's full reference is not in Heilmann's bibliography; I could not find it.)
  - Spacing in Roman use, n. 37 (pp. 182–183): "The transition from writing with frequent word separations to scriptio continua is documented impressively by the heterogeneous evidence of the Vindolanda tablets (around the turn from 1st/2nd century). … in T. Vindol. II 297; 315; 323; 345 almost all words are separated from each other with a middle dot, in many other tablets the words are separated with spaces [!] (e.g. T. Vindol. II 296; 299; 301; 316; 343); only a few (e.g. T. Vindol. II 292) are in scriptio continua." Heilmann's own bracketed [!] marks the surprise. I could not check the tablets.
  - On attitude, p. 183: Suetonius (*Aug.* 87.3) "finds it peculiar that Augustus did not separate individual words" ("non dividit verba"); Seneca (*Ep.* 40.10–11) "associates Greek not with the difficulties of deciphering scriptio continua, but with speed." Summary, p. 183: "there is no need to assume that reading unspaced scripts was any more cognitively challenging for ancient readers than reading spaced scripts is for us. We should be careful not to project our difficulties with reading scriptio continua onto ancient readers."
  - Conclusion on aids, p. 190: the diacritics etc. "all functioned to assist in reading without vocalization."
- *Weak points I can see:* (i) The inference from the second-century Roman shift to "scriptio continua is not a characteristic of deficient writing systems" does not refute Saenger's *social* explanation; a community can drop a useful aid for reasons of status. Heilmann argues against a physiological deficiency, which is not the same claim. (ii) Heilmann nowhere shows that spaces would not have lowered the cost for *beginners*; his own n. 28 reads the difficulty evidence as about learners, which is the cost-for-novices claim H-cost makes. (iii) His n. 8 list of works that accept the standard view mixes authors who hold only Knox's position (literary reading mostly aloud) with those who hold Saenger's mechanism, and he does not separate them. That is my reading of the note, not his statement.
- *Bearing:* **Refutes** (on his account) the physiological premise of Saenger's thesis, so **refutes the strong form of H-cause and strong H-cost**. **Complicates** H-cost's weak form: he concedes slowing for untrained moderns and reads the ancient difficulty-sources as about learners. **Complicates Problem 1** by showing the field has not settled: the consensus Johnson announced in 2000 is not what Heilmann sees in 2020.

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. Did ancient readers read silently, and how ordinary was it? What is the consensus and who dissents?

**What the sources I could read support.**
- Silent reading of *short documents and letters* was taken for granted in fifth- and fourth-century Athens (Knox 1968, pp. 432–434) and is "accepted by ancient witnesses as an ordinary event" (Johnson 2000, p. 593, summarising Knox).
- Silent reading of *poetry* by at least one reader (Cicero) was thinkable (Knox, p. 427). Augustine reads silently himself at *Conf.* 8.12 (Knox, p. 422).
- Ambrose's silence is not an index of general practice (Knox, p. 422).
- Johnson, summarising the field after Gavrilov and Burnyeat, writes that they "improved the evidential base" and concludes there was "no cognitive difficulty" for fully literate readers (Johnson, p. 593; I did not read either).

**What the sources I read do not support.** None says that literary reading was *normally* silent. Knox says the opposite: "Ancient books were normally read aloud" (p. 435). Heilmann (2020, pp. 179–180) argues the silent/aloud binary is too crude and the evidence cannot support "statistical generalizations", which pushes against *both* "usually aloud" and "usually silent".

**Where the "consensus" claim fails.** The plan's phrase "closed the question for classicists" rests on Johnson's opening paragraph (p. 593), which says the question is "at first glance easily answered" and that the conclusion "has been known to careful readers since at least 1968." Three later or wider statements cut against a flat "closed":
1. Heilmann 2020 (p. 178–179): the reading-aloud view "is commonly accepted in Classical Studies as well as New Testament Studies", "has become a quasi-dogma", and "Well-argued counterproposals have not been accepted." His n. 8 (p. 178) lists eighteen works from 1990 to 2015 that accept it.
2. Feldt 2023 (CR 73.2, p. 688, first paragraph): "In research on ancient reading it has been common knowledge that ancient literature was read aloud."
3. McCutcheon 2015 (abstract): book historians "have long insisted" that silent reading was rare or nonexistent; this "runs counter to much research in the field of Classics."

These are consistent once three claims are separated. (i) *Silent reading was possible and occurred*: settled among those who have engaged with Knox, Gavrilov, Burnyeat; (ii) *Literary texts were normally read aloud*: accepted by Knox himself, by Flory/Mollon-type dissenters on stronger grounds, and challenged only by Heilmann; (iii) *Unspaced script forced vocalisation*: rejected by Vatri and Heilmann, held by Saenger, partly by Svenbro. The disagreement about "the consensus" is a disagreement about which of the three is meant.

**Dissent from the Knox line.** Flory (2007, abstract only, paraphrase) reads Knox's and Johnson's passages as subvocalisation; Mollon (2001, abstract) accepts that silent reading was "unknown" and "not practised in the West until the mediaeval period"; Svenbro grants the cost premise and gives a different cause (Vatri p. 633). O'Donnell (2000) states as "clear" that silent reading "did not become commonplace until long after" Augustine.

**Problem 1 bearing.** The plan's "ignited literacy" has no support in this literature. The dispute is about *mode* of reading (aloud, murmured, silent) among people who could read, not about how many could read. No source here connects spacing to the size of the reading public.

### Q2. What exactly do the critics reject in Saenger, and what do they leave standing?

Three observations.
1. The critics' target is A and B. **The medievalist half (D) is not contested in anything I could read.** I cannot say whether the reviews I could not open (Hanna, Richter, Walker, Mulchahey, Bäuml) attack it. "Exasperating" (Aronoff) shows at least one reviewer was irritated.
2. C is the step H-cause needs, and the only reader of Saenger I could read in full who names it (O'Donnell) predicts dissent but does not supply it. Since B, its physiological bridge, is the part Vatri and Heilmann say is wrong, **C loses its mechanism**; what remains is a correlation of timing (spacing arrives; silent reading becomes common) plus social explanation.
3. Heilmann's own concession (p. 180) that untrained moderns read unspaced English slower, and his reading of the Dionysius, Quintilian and Lucian passages as about learners (n. 28, pp. 181–182), leave a smaller thesis standing: spacing cuts the cost for beginners and untrained readers. That is H-cost's weak form, and nobody in this facet's reading denies it.

### Q3. Does anyone measure or date when silent reading of literary texts became typical?

I found no such measurement. What exists:
- **Saenger's own dates** (7th-century Irish spacing; Continental late tenth century; 7th–13th c. survey) are dates of *word separation in manuscripts*, not of silent reading (Aronoff abstract; [unverified: see AUDIT_01] the book's publisher abstract, which the audit could not find). The inference from layout to practice is the contested step.
- **O'Donnell (2000, ¶2)** asserts that silent reading "did not become commonplace until long after" Augustine, without evidence in the review, and calls it "clear".
- **Knox (1968)** gives no date; his "normally read aloud" (p. 435) covers antiquity as a whole.
- **Johnson (2000, 2010)** reframes the question; he is not trying to date a shift.
- **McCutcheon (2015)** explains why book history insists on the late date; it does not give one (abstract only).
- Possible dating studies I could not reach: Saenger (1982), the Parkes and Saenger chapters in Cavallo & Chartier (1999), Coleman (1996) on public reading in late medieval England and France (title only; not read).

So: **a typical-date for silent reading of literary texts is not measured in the sources I could read.** Any sentence in the paper that says "by X, silent reading was usual" would rest on Saenger's inference or on O'Donnell's unsupported "clear".

### Q4. Why did the Romans drop interpuncts if separation helps, and what does that do to a story in which spacing is an obvious improvement waiting to be found?

**What the sources say.**
- Heilmann 2020 (p. 182, citing Oliver 1951 and Wingo 1972, p. 15): Latin used word-dividers "from the earliest Republican times through the Golden Age and well into the Second Century", then "replaced" them by *scriptio continua* "in the second century CE". Heilmann's n. 37 (pp. 182–183) reports the Vindolanda tablets (c. 100 CE) showing three practices at once: middle dots, plain *spaces* between words, and a few in *scriptio continua*. If that report holds, spacing was in Roman use before and during the move to continuous script. I could not check the tablets.
- Heilmann (p. 183) reports Suetonius, *Aug.* 87.3, calling it odd that Augustus did not separate his words, and Seneca, *Ep.* 40, linking Greek continuous script with speed, Latin word-division with restraint. Both are attitudes to the practice, not engineering judgements.
- Saenger: the ancient world "did not possess the desire, characteristic of the modern age, to make reading easier and swifter" (1997, p. 11), and interpuncts "were ineffective aids to the physiological process of reading" when compared to spaces (p. 28 [page to check in a library copy]; as quoted in facet E, snippets). So Saenger's own answer is *social*: the improvement was available (or would have been recognised) and unwanted.
- Ker (reviewing Johnson 2010) writes that for Johnson continuous script is "consistent with an elite culture" in which fluent reading from a bookroll "is a marker of education and status"; Mannering writes that Johnson interprets the effort of reading it as "a hallmark of the exclusivity of literary culture". These quoted phrases are the reviewers' words, not Johnson's. On their account Johnson gives a social explanation with no physiological premise.
- Heilmann's own inference (p. 182): the change "shows that scriptio continua is not a characteristic of deficient writing systems." This disproves a *deficiency* story; it is silent on whether spacing would have lowered cost.

**What this does to the "obvious improvement" story.** Three results, none of which needs the other:
1. **The improvement was not waiting to be found.** Romans used dividers for centuries and, per Heilmann's n. 37, spaces too; they then moved to continuous script at the time of the elite reading communities Johnson studies (Trajan to Marcus Aurelius, per Reis). The device was known and set aside.
2. **Both the critic and the defender point to social choice.** Saenger (no desire) and Johnson (elite exclusivity) both give a social account of why spacing was not adopted, and they differ on whether unspaced text was hard; the point survives whichever side is right about physiology. That is the strongest finding in this facet for the paper: *the history cannot be told as "a good UX move discovered late"*. It has to be told as a move that became attractive when the readers it served became a different group (Saenger's Irish scribes reading Latin as a foreign language, per facet E on Parkes p. 1).
3. **Heilmann's version supports a stronger and different claim: continuous script was not a handicap.** That sits against H-cost: if trained readers lose nothing, spacing moved work only for novices.

**Caveats.** The interpunct was a mark within continuous lines, not space; Saenger and Parkes distinguish them and the plan's author should read their texts (facet A). The Vindolanda reading is Heilmann's and not checked. The Roman change is dated by Heilmann to "the second century CE" and by Saenger (via facet E snippets) to before the end of the second century with adoption "late second or third century"; facet A should confirm.

### Q5. Verdict for H-cost and H-cause, in the critics' terms

**H-cost** (word separation moved parsing work from the reader to the page; lowered the cost of reading; widened the group who could read without long training; did not create silent reading).

- *Survives in the form:* "spacing lowers the cost of reading unspaced script for untrained readers." Supports: Heilmann concedes the slowing for untrained readers (p. 180) and reads the ancient difficulty-passages as about learners (n. 28); Mannering reports that, for Johnson, the bookroll needed "years of training"; O'Donnell's summary of Saenger's answer: spacing made visual reading "so easy as to become widespread" (¶3). The critics who reject Saenger's mechanism (Vatri, Heilmann) reject it for *trained* readers; they do not deny that learners found continuous script harder.
- *Does not survive in the form:* "unspaced script carried a cost large enough to determine how people read." Vatri via Heilmann (p. 181): "there is no reason to assume that reading unspaced text is a particularly demanding cognitive task in itself"; Heilmann (p. 181), citing Vatri 638 f. and others, writes that spaces are "redundant and can even disrupt the reading process." Facet D must say whether the eye-movement literature backs Vatri or Rayner and Pollatsek (Heilmann's n. 20 names the 1996 exchange).
- *"Widened the group … without long training"* is not tested in this facet. The critics' framing (Johnson) is that long training was kept on purpose. This is a statement about the *ancient* choice; the *medieval* widening (Irish scribes and later) rests on Saenger and Parkes and on O'Donnell's reading.
- *"Did not create silent reading"*: supported (Knox p. 434–435; Johnson p. 593; Vatri p. 633; Heilmann). **Verdict: H-cost is the safer hypothesis, in its weak form only, and only if the paper says "for untrained readers" and does not claim a mechanism.**

**H-cause** (word separation was a cause of the spread of silent reading and, through it, of wider literacy).

- *Silent reading:* no source I read in full proves it; the physiological bridge is rejected by Vatri and Heilmann; the dating inference is Saenger's; O'Donnell expects some dissent from the "necessary and sufficient" claim (¶3) and calls the physiology step "the most venturesome and vulnerable part of the book" (¶4). Svenbro's rival (attitude) and Johnson's rival (social setting) are non-physiological causes the field takes seriously. **H-cause for silent reading is unsupported as a mechanism and unmeasured as a date.**
- *Wider literacy:* untouched by anything in this facet. **Not supported, not tested.** `C_spacing_to_literacy` must carry it.
- **Verdict: H-cause cannot be defended from the dispute literature.** It can be stated as Saenger's thesis, dated, with the critics' rejection; it should not be adopted as the paper's claim.

**Where this leaves the author's sentence** (`dial_response_algorithmacy/draft.md:263`: "Word separation did not train the populace to read *scriptio continua*; it changed the interface so that reading no longer required the training."). From the critics' side: the first clause is untouched; the second is a claim H-cost makes in a weak form only for untrained readers, and the training Johnson describes was an elite practice kept long on purpose. The word "populace" and "no longer required" go further than anything I found.

### Claims the sources contradict or qualify

1. **"Johnson (2000) closed the question for classicists."** Johnson says "easily answered" and "seemingly firmer". Heilmann (2020, pp. 178–179) says the opposite view is a "quasi-dogma"; Feldt (2023, p. 688) calls reading-aloud of literature "common knowledge"; McCutcheon's abstract says the split persists.
2. **"Critics show silent reading was common/ordinary in antiquity."** Knox's own conclusion is that books were "normally read aloud" (p. 435); his silent reading is for documents, letters and short texts, plus the Cicero inference for verse.
3. **"Saenger says the ancients could not read silently."** Vatri reports "physiologically impossible"; the book, per facet E, concedes Ambrose read silently but not rapidly. Vatri himself notes Saenger's reading of the sources as "silent, or sub-vocal, oral reading – a sort of mumbling." The strong form is the critics' framing.
4. **"Spacing is obviously better and was waiting to be found."** The Romans used dividers and, per Heilmann (n. 37), spaces, then moved to continuous script; Saenger and Johnson both give social accounts of non-adoption and differ on whether unspaced text was hard.
5. **"Continuous script was a handicap."** Vatri and Heilmann dispute it for trained readers; Heilmann concedes it for untrained modern readers.
6. **"The medievalists' reviews attack the thesis."** The two I read in full (O'Donnell, Garrett) praise it; O'Donnell calls the survey a "cathedral" and expects the debate over the physiology-to-practice step to be resolved in Saenger's favour. Vatri (2012) and Heilmann (2020) reject the physiology O'Donnell expected to be vindicated, and the eye-movement literature is itself contested (Heilmann n. 20); that does not make the survey wrong.
7. **"Spacing ignited literacy."** No source here speaks to literacy rates.
8. **"Parkes and Saenger are one view."** A 1994 reviewer (O'Donnell on Parkes) treats Saenger's work as missing and needing a bridge. Also, per facet E, Parkes (1992, p. 9) repeats the older view.
