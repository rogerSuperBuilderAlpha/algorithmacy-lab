# V2 — Oracy to literacy: the ethical failures of the move from oral to written standing, proof and agreement

*Facet V2 of research round 7 (version 4 of the Berthon ethics course paper). Agent-drafted on 2026-10-14. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Six cases from the transition from oral to written modes of standing, proof and agreement, read for the v4 thesis: that treating disclosure as sufficient ethical design (a) puts the burden on the person who must read, (b) keeps that person in a literacy-based procedure after the medium has changed, and (c) builds no capacity in them. The cases: (1) medieval England's shift from memory to written record (Clanchy; *quo warranto*; the Warenne sword; forgery; seals); (2) benefit of clergy and the neck verse; (3) the Statute of Frauds 1677; (4) the Requerimiento; (5) the Numbered Treaties of Canada (Treaty 6, Treaty 8); (6) Plato's *Phaedrus* and the scholarly cautions against the "great divide". For each case I record who was bound by writing, whether the text was read or explained to them, whether that was treated as sufficient, who bore the burden, and whether anyone built the hearers' capacity to read.

**Builds on, does not redo.** Facet A (`A_documents_and_standing.md`) already holds Clanchy 3rd ed. snippets, Harrison 2012 on Barnet, Blackstone on benefit of clergy, the Statute of Frauds s. 1, *Delgamuukw* and *Marshall*, and the Waitangi report. N3 and E hold the great-divide records (Goody and Watt 1963, Halverson 1992, Ong 1982, Collins 1995, Finnegan 2012). V6 holds the gap search on oral-to-written analogies for AI and the Phaedrus-and-AI genre; I cite it and do not repeat it. The sister arm's verified card on *Phaedrus* (`wt-dxd-foundation/.../library/plato1925phaedrus.md`) and the algorithmacy arm's verified Havelock card (`algorithmacy_design_ethics/literature/library/havelock1963.md`) are reused for quotations.

**Read-depth marks.** *Full* = I read the whole text or the whole operative passage this session. *Part* = I read the pages cited. *Snippet* is a kind of *Part*: archive.org full-text-search highlights of about 20 words from a lending-only scan, with no page shown; I give a page only where an index entry, a running head or a page-cited secondary source fixes it, and I say which. *Abstract* = abstract only. *Record* = catalogue or database record only.

**Spelling.** Spanish quotations keep the 1853 and 1876 printed spelling. The djvu OCR of Oviedo renders *ç* as *g* ("parésgeme"); I restored *ç* and changed nothing else. English statute and treatise quotations keep their spelling.

## (b) Findings, case by case

### Case 1. Memory to written record in England, 1066–1307

**Clanchy, *From Memory to Written Record* (1st ed., Harvard UP 1979), `clanchy1979memory`. Part (Snippet).** The 1979 edition is the one Ong, Hunnisett and Robertson paginate. I add it as a key beside the arm's `clanchy2013memory` because the locators below are 1979 pages.

- *The Warenne story and Clanchy's caution (1979, pp. 21–22; page from the book's index entry "Walter of Guisborough, 21" and from Ong 1982 p. 95, who cites "1979, pp. 21–2").* The story "appears in one version of the chronicle of Walter of Guisborough". The earl "produced in their midst an ancient and rusty sword and said: 'Look at this, my lords, this is my warrant! [F]or my ancestors came with William the Bastard and conquered their lands with the sword, and by the sword I will defend them from anyone intending to seize them.'" (OCR prints "Kor"; I read "For".) Clanchy's caution, in his words: "The story is certainly inaccurate in details, but it has a value comparable with the myths Fitz Neal recorded a century earlier"; "Some historians have found the Warenne story as unacceptable as Edward's judges may have done, because it seems so primitive and ill-founded by literate [standards]" (the last word is cut off); "a rusty sword was not going to help his cause at law"; "The story seems to be a desperate reassertion of the primacy of oral tradition over recorded history and of non-literate forms [of proof]" (cut off); the sword's exhibition was "an archaic method of proof … which had been superseded in Edward I's courts by written evidence and book-learned law"; "The sword was rusty; it was a relic, not a practical weapon." Ong reports the caution as "somewhat questionable because of certain inconsistencies" (Ong p. 95, read this session).
- *The writing rule in quo warranto (snippet, no page).* "the king's attorneys were arguing in many of the quo warranto prosecutions against the magnates that the only sufficient warrant for a privilege was a written one and that in the form of a specific statement in a charter" (the 3rd-ed. snippet in facet A, PP18, continues: "Memory … if unsupported by clear written evidence, was ruled out of court"). "the quo warranto prosecutions threatened to disfranchise nearly all the magnates."
- *The retreat (snippet, no page).* "In 1290 by the quo warranto statute a compromise was made. In subsequent case law the king was understood to have conceded …"; "Although the quo warranto cases were rapidly suspended in the 1290s and the king's government had to concede that tenure 'from time out of mind' was a legitimate claim, the principle had been established for the future"; had the Crown's maxim held ("'time does not run against the king'"), "nearly all owners of property in England would have been disfranchised". Hunnisett (1981, p. 68, Full) adds that "the establishment of 1189 as the legal limit of memory" was "an early recognition of the value of the archival work" of Hubert Walter.
- *Forgery (1979, p. 249 per Ong p. 96; snippets).* "monks, who were the traditional experts in writing, were also the greatest forgers. The more powerful and ancient the house, the more likely it was that its documents would be forged in a professional [manner]"; "In the monastic community archivists and forgers shared a common aim in documenting a house's titles"; "Forgers recreated the past in an acceptable literate form. They are best understood not as occasional deviants on the peripheries [of legal practice]". Of "the 164 documents written in the name of Edward the Confessor, 44 are likely to be spurious and there are doubts about a further 56." **Correction for any draft that follows Ong:** Ong (p. 96, read) says "44 are certainly forged, only 64 certainly authentic"; Clanchy's own words are "likely to be spurious" with doubts about 56 more. Hunnisett (1981, p. 68, Full): Clanchy shows "how the change from memory to written record, and the quo warranto proceedings, made inevitable the large-scale forgery of documents, such forgeries having a greater chance of acceptance than had genuine Anglo-Saxon charters". Robertson (1980, p. 612, Full): "Forgery is described as a product of the gradual transition from oral to written records."
- *Using writing without reading it: seals (1979, pp. 31, 36, 184 per the index entry "statute of Exeter &"; snippets).* "the statute of Exeter of 1285 actually required 'bondsmen' to have seals to authenticate their written evi[dence]"; "even some serfs probably had them"; "in Edward I's reign even serfs were required by statute to have them." Hunnisett (p. 67, Full) summarises Clanchy's account of "the use of seals in lieu of signatures" and of reading as hearing: "in the middle ages 'reading' normally meant 'hearing' and 'writing' meant 'dictating' … it was therefore not essential for kings or for such officials as sheriffs to be literate in the modern sense (p. 219)". Robertson (p. 612): Clanchy treats "the tendency to 'hear' documents when they were used rather than to read them silently".
- *Documents distrusted (1979, p. 230, via Ong p. 94, read).* "'documents did not immediately inspire trust' (Clanchy 1979, p. 230). People had to be persuaded that writing improved the old oral methods sufficiently to warrant all the expense and troublesome techniques it involved." Ong (p. 94): "Witnesses were prima facie more credible than texts because they could be challenged and made to defend their statements, whereas texts could not" (citing Clanchy pp. 235–6). The Sandwich jury of 1127 swore from what "'I have received from my ancestors, and I have seen and heard from my youth'" (Clanchy pp. 232–3, via Ong p. 94).
- *Breadth.* Hunnisett (p. 67): the growth of royal documents "made it ever more necessary for the king's subjects to have at least a nodding acquaintance with letters, not excluding peasant smallholders who by 1300 were making grants by charter in large numbers", and the book argues "its main result was the gradual spread of literacy from a few monasteries to all ranks of society". Hunnisett warns that "the extent and, even more, the depth of lay literacy in 1300 must remain conjectural, and Dr. Clanchy is not completely consistent about this". Robertson (p. 613): Clanchy "may be overly optimistic about the literacy of manorial reeves, whose accounts to their stewards were often prepared and delivered by local clerks."

*Case summary.* **Bound by writing:** magnates holding franchises (quo warranto); all free landholders as written title displaced remembered title; serfs required to own seals. **Read or explained:** documents were heard, not read (reading meant hearing). **Treated as sufficient:** the Crown's attorneys argued that only a written warrant sufficed; the Crown retreated in 1290 and conceded tenure from time out of mind. **Burden:** on the holder to produce a written warrant. **Capacity built:** none by the Crown; literacy spread "to all ranks" as a by-product (Hunnisett's summary), and lords answered peasant charters by seizing them (facet A, Harrison pp. 21–22). **Who gained:** the writing experts, the monasteries, who were "also the greatest forgers".

### Case 2. Benefit of clergy and the neck verse

**Statute 4 Hen. VII c. 13, *Statutes of the Realm* vol. 2 (1816), p. 538, `statute1489clergy`. Full (the chapter).** Running head "A.D.1488-9. 4° Hen.VII." Title: "An Acte to take awaye the benefytt of Clergye from certayne psons." Preamble: "WHERE AS upon trust of privilege of the Churche div[er]s psones lettred hath ben the more bold to cõmitte murdre rape robbery thefte and all othre myschevous dedys, bicause they have ben continuelly admitted to the beneffice of the Clergie as ofte as they did offend". Enacting words: every person "not being within orders, whiche onys hath ben admytted to the benefice of his Clergie, eftsonys arayned of eny suche offence, be not admitted to have the benefice or privilege of his Clergie"; each to be "marked with a M. upon the brawne of the lefte thumbe" for murder and "a T." for other felony, "by the Gaillour openly in the Courte before the Jugge". **Narrows the brief's framing.** The 1489 statute does not create the reading test. It presupposes it ("psones lettred"), limits a lay reader to one use and brands him so the court can see he has used it. **Date:** the volume dates the session 1488–9; Cross (1917, p. 552) prints "in 1487". The brief's "1489" matches the volume's end year.

**Cross, "The English Criminal Law and Benefit of Clergy during the Eighteenth and Early Nineteenth Century", *AHR* 22(3), 1917, pp. 544–565, `cross1917english`. Part (pp. 551–556 read closely, the rest skimmed); public-domain JSTOR Early Journal Content.**
- p. 552: after a 1350 ordinance the privilege "was apparently interpreted to mean all who could read; at any rate such grew to be the usage of the courts. A verse in the Psalter was commonly selected (usually the 51st Psalm) which came to be known as the 'neck-verse', and if the accused was able to 'read like a clerk' he was handed over to the ordinary, though it was an indictable offense at common law to teach a felon to read that he might claim his clergy." (Cross gives no source for the last clause in the text I read.)
- p. 553: peers were to be treated as clerks convict "'though he cannot read'" (1 Edw. VI c. 12, quoted from Pike); n. 47 quotes Pike: "a Peer who could or who could not read might still have robbed one church or committed one highway robbery with impunity, though an ignorant peasant would have been hanged."
- p. 554: the petition of Osmund Gibbs, yeoman, 1640 (Hist. MSS. Comm., Report IV): falsely charged by a justice who coveted his copyhold, he "was found guilty and put to read for his life. Petitioner desired to read the Psalm of Mercy, but Farwell so incensed the judge against him, that there was not only a clear bar made to prevent promptings, but the judge turned him unto one of the hardest verses to read, which by God's grace he was enabled to do, and so escaped hanging, but was burnt in the hand."
- p. 555: Jeaffreson's Middlesex figures: "In the last four years of the reign of Edward VI., 8.54 per cent. out of 117 convicted of capital offenses could read and pleaded their clergy: in the reign of James I. there was an amazing increase to 38.83 per cent. out of 1725 cases." Cross then: "At length Parliament came to realize the rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered criminals, so in the reign of Anne the reading test was taken away", quoting the Act: a person "'shall not be required to read, but without any reading shall be allowed, taken and reported to be and punished as a clerk convict.'" n. 55: the Act is "6 Anne, c. 9, s. 4" in *Statutes of the Realm* "though the statute is usually cited as 5 Anne, c. 6".

**Gabel, *Benefit of Clergy in England in the Later Middle Ages* (Smith College Studies in History 14, 1928–29; reprint Octagon 1969), `gabel1928benefit`. Part (Snippet; index "'Neck-verse,' 72").**
- "Although any passage might be chosen at random, the first verse of the fifty-first Psalm came to be known as the 'neck-verse,' no doubt because of its common use for the purpose."
- A prisoner "could neither read nor 'sillabicare,' yet seemed to know certain passages by rote. At this point the secular judge gave him the book upside down, but the prisoner 'read' as before, in nowise disturbed by the altered circumstance. When the Ordinary refused to claim him, John was given another chance to plead as a layman, but stood fast by his claim of clergy."
- A prisoner "had improved his last days or hours by a diligent study of the 'neck-verse'"; another "may have learned to 'read' while awaiting the day of execution"; one "was discovered to have been a layman and illiterate at the time of his arrest, but had been taught what he knew by two boys from Appleby, who had been admitted to the gaol through [—]" (cut off).
- "Towards the close of the century the reading-test was commonly administered by the court".

**Blackstone, *Commentaries* IV ch. 28, `blackstonendcommentaries`.** Reused from facet A (Part): "those men, who could not read, if under the degree of peerage, were hanged"; the reading condition was removed because learning was "no extenuations of guilt" and death was "too severe for the ignorant also".

**Criminal Law Act 1827 (7 & 8 Geo. IV c. 28), `statute1827criminal`. Part (vLex transcription via a fetch tool).** "VI. And be it enacted, That Benefit of Clergy, with respect to Persons convicted of Felony, shall be abolished"; s. VII: no person convicted of felony "shall suffer Death, unless it be for some Felony which was excluded from the Benefit of Clergy before or on the First Day of the present Session of Parliament, or which hath been or shall be made punishable with Death by some Statute". Dated "21st June 1827". Re-check against a printed Statutes at Large before quoting.

*Case summary.* **Bound by the text:** every lay commoner convicted of a clergyable felony; peers and (later) women exempt from reading (Cross pp. 553–554). **Read or explained:** the reverse of disclosure — the court handed the accused a text and made his life depend on reading it. **Treated as sufficient:** a performance of reading, even by rote, was treated as proof of clergy until judges tested it (book upside down; a bar against prompting; "the hardest verses"). **Burden:** wholly on the accused, and the judge could raise it at will (the Gibbs petition). **Capacity built:** none by the state; Cross reports that teaching a felon to read for this purpose was itself indictable, and Gabel records prisoners taught in gaol by outsiders. **Ended by:** Parliament removing the reading condition under Anne on a ground Cross calls "the rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered", then abolishing the privilege in 1827.

### Case 3. The Statute of Frauds 1677

**An Act for prevention of Frauds and Perjuryes, 29 Car. II c. 3, *Statutes of the Realm* vol. 5, pp. 839–841, `statute1677frauds`. Full (ss. 1–5, 17, 19; local `B/statutes5.txt`).** Facet A read s. 1. Added here:
- Preamble (p. 839): "For prevention of many fraudulent Practices which are commonly endeavoured to be upheld by Perjury and Subornation of Perjury".
- s. 4 (p. 840): "noe Action shall be brought whereby to charge any Executor or Administrator upon any speciall promise to answere damages out of his owne Estate or whereby to charge the Defendant upon any speciall promise to answere for the debt default or miscarriages of another person or to charge any person upon any agreement made upon consideration of Marriage or upon any Contract or Sale of Lands … or upon any Agreement that is not to be performed within the space of one yeare from the makeing thereof unlesse the Agreement … or some Memorandum or Note thereof shall be in Writeing and signed by the partie to be charged therewith or some other person thereunto by him lawfully authorized."
- s. 17 (p. 841): no contract for the sale of goods "for the price of ten pounds Sterling or upwards shall be allowed to be good except the Buyer shall accept part of the Goods soe sold and actually receive the same or give some thing in earnest to bind the bargaine or in part of payment, or that some Note or Memorandum in writeing … be made and signed".
- s. 19 (p. 841): "for prevention of fraudulent Practices in setting up Nuncupative Wills", no oral will is good above "thirty pounds" unless "proved by the Oathes of three Wittnesses (at the least)".
- **Narrows.** The statute is not a reading test. An agent may sign; s. 17 keeps oral routes (part delivery, earnest money); s. 19 keeps oral wills under £30 and above it with three witnesses; s. 3 exempts copyhold and customary interests (facet A).

**Stollery, "Statute of Frauds", *Alberta Law Review* 14 (1976), from p. 222, `stollery1976statute`. Part (pp. 222–223, 254).**
- The stated ground, explained (p. 222): "In 1677, parties to an action, their husbands or wives, and persons with an interest in the result of the action could not be witnesses"; quoting Lord Bowen, "the merchant whose name was forged to a bill of exchange had to sit by, silent and unheard". Statutes of 1844–1854 let litigants testify, "removing this rationale". Pp. 222–223: jury verdicts were "practically unappealable"; "Subordination and perjury evidently were rife."
- Who bore the cost (p. 254, quoting the Law Revision Committee's Sixth Interim Report, Cmd. 5449, 1937, at 7): "'promotes more frauds than it prevents.' True, it shuts out perjury; but it also and more frequently shuts out the truth. It strikes impartially at the perjurer and the honest man who has omitted a precaution, sealing the lips of both." Fitzjames Stephen, as quoted there: "'in the vast majority of cases its operation is simply to enable a man to break a promise with impunity, because he did not write it down with sufficient formality.'" And: "A and B contract: A has signed a sufficient note or memorandum, but B has not. In these circumstances, B can enforce the contract against A, but A cannot enforce it against B."

**Law Reform (Enforcement of Contracts) Act 1954, 2 & 3 Eliz. 2 c. 34, `statute1954lawreform`. Full (2 pp., OCR).** s. 1 repeals, within s. 4 of the 1677 Act, the executor clause, the marriage clause and the one-year clause "in relation to any promise or agreement, whether made before or after the commencement of this Act"; s. 2 repeals s. 4 of the Sale of Goods Act 1893. The Act states no ground.

*Case summary.* **Bound:** parties to the listed classes of oral promise. **Read or explained:** not applicable; the statute requires a signed note, not a reading. **Treated as sufficient:** the signed writing; its absence defeats the promise. **Burden:** on the person who relied on a spoken promise and did not get the other side to sign (the "honest man who has omitted a precaution"). **Capacity built:** none; the law presumes parties can get a writing made. **Stated ground:** perjury, at a time when parties could not testify. **Who bore the cost:** I found no source that names a social class (the poor, the unlettered) as the losers; the 1937 Committee names the honest promisee. Most of s. 4 was repealed in 1954.

### Case 4. The Requerimiento

**Las Casas, *Historia de las Indias*, Book III, chs. 57–59 and 67, in t. IV (ed. Marqués de la Fuensanta del Valle and José Sancho Rayón, Madrid: Imprenta de Miguel Ginesta, 1876), pp. 154–167, 198–199, `lascasas1876historia`. Full (the four chapters).**
- *The text* (ch. 57, pp. 154–156), "la forma y orden que Pedrárias habia de tener en requerir á los indios … el cual se envió después á todas las Indias" (p. 154). It opens "De parte del rey D. Fernando, y de la Reina doña Joana, su hija … nos, sus criados, os notificamos y hacemos saber como mejor podemos" (p. 154).
- *Disclosure by reference to writings the hearer may inspect* (p. 155): the papal donation is "según se contiene en ciertas escripturas que sobre ello pasaron, según dicho es, que podéis ver si quisiéredes".
- *The demand to understand* (pp. 155–156): "vos rogamos y requerimos que entendáis bien ésto que os decimos, y toméis para entenderlo y deliberar sobre ello el tiempo que fuere justo".
- *The burden clause* (p. 156): "y protestamos que las muertes y daños que dello se recrecieren sea á vuestra culpa y no de Sus Altezas, ni nuestra, ni destos caballeros que con nosotros vienen: y de como lo decimos y requerimos pedimos al presente escribano que nos lo dé por testimonio signado, y á los presentes rogamos que dello nos sean testigos".
- *Authorship* (pp. 156–157): "Este requerimiento ordenó el venerable doctor Palacios Rubios, bien mi amigo".
- *Condemnation* (ch. 58, pp. 158–163). He argues first on the supposition "ya que los indios entendieran nuestra lengua" (p. 158) that the content was unprovable to them; then (p. 162) calls it "injusto, impío, escandaloso, irracional y absurdo", and "cosa es de reir, ó de llorar por mejor decir"; then (pp. 162–163): "¿qué podrá alguno decir en excusa de los que formaron aquel requerimiento y de los que á ejecutallo iban, haciéndolo á quien ni palabra del entendían, más que si fuera en latin referido ó en algarabía?; y ya saben los que estudiaron derechos, qué valor ó momento tiene el mando ó precepto, ó requerimiento, que se hace á gente que la lengua en que se dice no entiende".
- *Its falsity on the record* (p. 162): the text's claim that other islands submitted on being informed is false, "porque no es verdad que les notificasen é informasen de cosa dello á ninguna isla".
- *In use* (ch. 59, p. 167): "creer que entendieron ellos cosa del requerimiento es falsísimo, porque no sabían más de nuestra lengua que de la latina".
- *Read to the trees* (ch. 67, pp. 198–199): raiding parties stopped a league or half a league from a village, "y entre sí leian el requerimiento á los árboles diciendo: «Caciques é indios de tal pueblo, hacemos saber …»", and the captain asked the notary for "testimonio autorizado … de como se habia requerido á los Caciques é indios de aquel pueblo … pero que no habian querido venir á dar la obediencia"; then at dawn they fired the houses.

**Oviedo, *Historia general y natural de las Indias*, Lib. XXIX cap. VII, in t. III (Madrid: Real Academia de la Historia, 1853), pp. 27–32, `oviedo1853historia`. Full (the chapter's account of the Requerimiento).** Oviedo was the expedition's *veedor* and carried the text himself.
- pp. 27–28: Pedrarias "mandó … que yo llevasse el requirimiento in scriptis que se avia de hacer á los indios, é me lo dió de su mano, como si yo entendiera á los indios, para se lo leer, ó tuviéramos allí quien se lo diera á entender, queriéndolo ellos oyr: pues mostrarles el papel, en que estaba escripto, poco hacia al caso".
- p. 29: the friars "que el requirimiento diçe … se quedaron en los navios hasta ver cómo subçedian las cosas"; the reading was on 14 June 1514.
- p. 31: to Pedrarias, before the captains: "Señor: parésçeme que estos indios no quieren escuchar la teología deste requirimiento, ni vos tenés quien se la dé á entender: mande vuestra merçed guardalle, hasta que tengamos algún indio destos en una jaula, para que despaçio lo aprenda é el señor obispo se lo dé á entender." Pedrarias "lo tomó con mucha risa".
- p. 31: "Quisiera yo que aquel requirimiento se les hiçiera entender primero; pero como cosa excusada ó por demás, no se tractó dello".
- pp. 31–32: in 1516 Oviedo asked Palacios Rubios "si quedaba satisfecha la conçiençia de los chripstianos con aquel requirimiento; é díxome que sí, si se hiçiesse como el requirimiento lo diçe. Mas parésçeme que se reía muchas veçes" — and Oviedo could laugh more at him "si pensaba que lo que diçe aquel requirimiento lo avían de entender los indios, sin discurso de años é tiempo."
- p. 32: on the promised "tiempo que fuere justo": "Adelante se dirá el tiempo que los capitanes les daban, atando los indios después de salteados, y en tanto leyéndoles toda aquella capitulaçion del requirimiento."

**Helps, *The Spanish Conquest in America*, vol. 1 (London: J. W. Parker, 1855), pp. 379–383, 387, `helps1855spanish`. Part.** English translation, used for the English of the burden clause (pp. 382): "we protest that the deaths and losses which shall accrue from this are your fault, and not that of their Highnesses, or ours, nor of these cavaliers who come with us. And that we have said this to you and made this Requisition, we request the notary here present to give us his testimony in writing". The writings clause (p. 380): "as is contained in certain writings which passed upon the subject as aforesaid, which you can see if you wish." The understanding clause (p. 381): "that you take the time that shall be necessary to understand and deliberate upon it". Helps's own comment (p. 383): "the very terms 'Church,' 'privileges,' 'vassalage,' 'exemptions,' are such as require a knowledge of Christianity and of the peculiar civilization of Europe for any one to understand … we may fancy what ideas the reading of the document, even when it was read, conveyed to a number of Indians sitting in a circle". Helps translates Oviedo (p. 387): the Governor "gave it to me from his own hand, as if I understood the Indians, or as if we should find any one there who would make them understand it, even if they were willing to listen." Note that Helps renders "el tiempo que fuere justo" as "the time that shall be necessary"; "just" is closer.

**Israeli, "The *Requerimiento* in the Old World", *Law and History Review* 40(1), 2022, pp. 37–62, doi:10.1017/S0738248021000602, `israeli2022requerimiento`. Part (abstract; pp. 39–40, 46–47).** The procedure was ordinary Castilian law before it was colonial: "a demand or warning to an addressee and its official recording" (p. 39). Its effect (p. 46): "Once a requerimiento had been conducted, the addressee could not pretend to be ignorant of the demand, and could be considered as having been given a fair opportunity to make amends." The record mattered as much as the speech: "the lack of a testimonio could render the entire procedure meaningless" (p. 46). In Castile it had "an important dialogical component": the addressee "was requested to declare whether or not they were willing to fulfil it", and "the notary was obliged to document the addressee's response" (p. 47). Las Casas and others argued that "the conditions needed for the requerimiento to function as a performative speech act in fact never existed in the New World, both because in many cases the indios could not understand the text recited to them and because they did not receive the proper opportunity to respond" (p. 47, summarising Faudree). Seed's hypothesis of an Islamic origin is reported at p. 39.

**Faudree, "How to Say Things with Wars", *J. Linguistic Anthropology* 22(3), 2012, pp. 182–200, doi:10.1111/j.1548-1395.2012.01152.x, `faudree2012how`. Abstract.** The text "presupposes and simultaneously instantiates social hierarchies by establishing participant roles"; "Competing interpretations of those participant roles are made possible by the text's strategic indeterminacy and the temporal dynamics of its circulation."

*Case summary.* **Bound:** the Indigenous peoples addressed, who were made liable for the war. **Read or explained:** read aloud in Spanish, sometimes not even within earshot (Las Casas ch. 67); no interpreter required (Oviedo pp. 27–28). **Treated as sufficient:** yes — the notary's sworn record that it had been read was the legal product (Las Casas p. 156, p. 199; Israeli p. 46). **Burden:** placed in words on the hearers: deaths and losses "á vuestra culpa". **Capacity built:** none; the text promised "el tiempo que fuere justo" to understand, and Oviedo reports the time given was the time it took to read it to captives already tied (p. 32). Oviedo's proposal to teach it to an Indian "en una jaula" is the only capacity-building in the record, and it is a jest. **Contemporaries who named it a wrong:** Las Casas (pp. 158–163, 167, 198–199) and Oviedo (pp. 27–32). The legal principle Las Casas invokes — a command in a language the hearer does not understand has no force (p. 163) — is a standard of the time, not a present-day one.

### Case 5. The Numbered Treaties of Canada

**Morris, *The Treaties of Canada with the Indians of Manitoba and the North-West Territories* (Toronto: Willing & Williamson, 1880), `morris1880treaties`. Part (pp. 185–186, 217–219, 351–357).** Morris was the Treaty 6 commissioner; the book prints his reports, the negotiation record and the treaty texts.
- *Read and explained* (p. 357): Treaty 6 was "Signed by the Chiefs within named in the presence of the following witnesses, the same having been first read and explained by Peter Erasmus, Peter Ballendine and the Rev. John McKay". At the Fort Carlton conference (p. 185) "the interpreter, Peter Erasmus, would read what changes they desired in the terms of our offer".
- *What the Cree asked for* (p. 185): "an ox and a cow each family … provisions for the poor, unfortunate, blind and lame; to be provided with missionaries and school teachers … a free supply of medicines". Morris's report on the same page: "They were anxious to learn to support themselves by agriculture, but felt too ignorant to do so".
- *The oral promise against the written clause.* Morris's spoken reply (p. 218): "A medicine chest will be kept at the house of each Indian agent, in case of sickness amongst you." The written text (p. 355): "That a medicine chest shall be kept at the house of each Indian Agent for the use and benefit of the Indians, at the discretion of such Agent". The written text adds the agent's discretion; the spoken promise ties it to "sickness amongst you".
- *Schools* (p. 353): "Her Majesty agrees to maintain schools for instruction in such reserves hereby made, as to her Government of the Dominion of Canada may seem advisable, whenever the Indians of the reserve shall desire it". The Crown wrote capacity-building into the treaty; the arm's TRC card (`trc2015honouring`) records what Canada's schooling became.

**R. v. Badger, [1996] 1 S.C.R. 771 (Treaty 8, 1899), `badger1996`. Full (¶¶ 51–55 read; the rest skimmed).** Cory J. for the majority, ¶ 52: "The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and they did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement" (citing Morris 1880 pp. 338–42). "The treaties were drafted in English by representatives of the Canadian government who, it should be assumed, were familiar with common law doctrines. Yet, the treaties were not translated in written form into the languages (here Cree and Dene) of the various Indian nations who were signatories. Even if they had been, it is unlikely that the Indians, who had a history of communicating only orally, would have understood them any differently." Hence the words "must be interpreted in the sense that they would naturally have been understood by the Indians at the time of the signing". ¶ 55: "The Indian people made their agreements orally and recorded their history orally. Thus, the verbal promises made on behalf of the federal government at the times the treaties were concluded are of great significance in their interpretation."

*Case summary.* **Bound:** the Cree, Dene and other signatory nations. **Read or explained:** yes, by named interpreters (Morris p. 357). **Treated as sufficient:** by the Crown at the time; not by the Supreme Court in 1996, which holds the written text an incomplete record of an oral agreement (¶ 52) and gives the oral promises interpretive weight (¶ 55). **Burden:** on the signatories, until the courts shifted interpretation toward their understanding. **Capacity built:** the Cree asked for school teachers (p. 185) and the treaty promised schools "as … may seem advisable" to the government (p. 353); what followed is outside this facet. **Caution for the paper:** *Badger* ¶ 52's clause "who had a history of communicating only orally, would [not] have understood them any differently" is a court's own great-divide assumption; cite the holding, not that clause.

### Case 6. *Phaedrus* 274c–275b and the cautions against the "great divide"

**Plato, *Phaedrus* (Fowler trans.), sister-arm card `plato1925phaedrus` (verified S2, `wt-dxd-foundation/.../literature/library/plato1925phaedrus.md`).** Quotations taken from the verified card: Thamus says letters "will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory" (275a); "you offer your pupils the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom, for they will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things, when they are for the most part ignorant and hard to get along with, since they are not wise, but only appear wise" (275a–b). Socrates continues: a written word "knows not to whom to speak or not to speak" and "always needs its father to help it" (275e). The card records that the 1925/vol. 9 citation is not supported by the Perseus copy (header: Fowler 1914, vol. 1) and that Phaedrus doubts the myth (275b). Ong (p. 78, read this session) lists Plato's objections, including that "a written text is basically unresponsive". **Use:** two phrases carry the v4 thesis without any cognitive claim — "read many things without instruction" (no scaffolding) and a text that cannot answer its reader (disclosure without dialogue).

**Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982), existing card `ong1982orality` (V6; pp. 2–3, 132–134).** I read further pages in the archive.org PDF (`pdfs/V2/ong1982.pdf`): p. 3, the book "treats rather the differences in 'mentality' between oral and writing cultures" and treats social, economic, political and religious structures as "only indirect concerns"; p. 56, "Nor must we imagine that orally based thought is 'prelogical' or 'illogical' in any simplistic sense", yet "they cannot organize elaborate concatenations of causes in the analytic kind of linear sequences which can only be set up with the help of texts"; p. 55, "Writing has to be personally interiorized to affect thinking processes". **These are the cognitive claims v4 must not adopt.** Ong pp. 94–96 is the Clanchy summary used in Case 1; p. 133, secondary orality is "essentially a more deliberate and self-conscious orality, based permanently on the use of writing and print" (also on the V6 card).

**Finnegan, "Literacy versus Non-literacy: The Great Divide?", in Horton and Finnegan (eds.), *Modes of Thought* (London: Faber, 1973), from p. 112, `finnegan1973literacy`. Part (Snippet).** She opens on UNESCO's director-general: "of UNESCO, René Maheu, mankind can be divided into two main groups, the difference between them being apparently directly attri[butable]" (cut off; the note cites the World Congress of Ministers of Education on the Eradication of Illiteracy, Teheran 1965, *Speeches and Messages*, UNESCO 1966, p. 29). She asks: "Is it true that the label 'non-literate' necessarily implies 'without literature'?" and whether "individuals in non-literate (or largely non-literate) cultures are ipso facto less creative, thoughtful, self-aware or individu[ally …]". **Use:** the great-divide claim was a literacy-campaign premise as well as an academic one; Finnegan's target includes UNESCO's own framing.

**Goody's later qualifications. `goody1977domestication` (Part, Snippet; index "Great Divide, the, 8, 151, 164") and `goody1987interface` (Part, Snippet).** 1977: "To argue this way is not to subscribe to a 'great divide' theory"; "This is not a great-divide theory. It sees some changes as more important than others"; he grants that his work on "the consequences of literacy can be considered a 'great-divide theory', since we treat 'literacy' as a variable". 1987: "the Greek achievement has been interpreted as a Great Divide theory (Finnegan 1973)"; "On the contrary, we were attempting to avoid the binary divisions current in much scholarship"; "we should not think of a simple binary shift between oral and literate, but a whole sequence [of changes]"; literacy "is never a question of crossing a single frontier, a simple binary shift". **Use:** even the divide's chief proponent disowned the binary; v4 can cite Goody for the institutional consequences of writing (the file, the law — `goody1986logic`, Record) without the cognitive thesis.

**Street 2003, card `street2003whats` (verified).** The autonomous model holds that literacy in itself has effects "regardless of the social and economic conditions" (p. 77, per the card); the ideological model treats literacy as a social practice rooted in power. v4's three wrongs are claims about institutions (who bears the burden, what procedure is kept, who builds capacity) and so sit inside Street's ideological model.

**Havelock, card `havelock1963` (algorithmacy arm, verified S2).** From the card, *Preface to Plato* p. 39: public inscription is "craft literacy, in which the public inscription is composed as a source of referral for officials and as a check upon arbitrary interpretations"; "The clue to the whole problem lies not in the use of written characters and writing materials … but upon the supply of readers, and this depended on a universalisation of letters. The reading trauma, to use a modern term, had to be imposed at the primary level of schooling". **Use:** a record made for officials, published to a public that cannot read it, is Havelock's "craft literacy"; his remedy is a supply of readers, built by schooling. The card also carries Havelock's cognitive claim ("an oral state of mind", p. 41), which v4 must leave aside.

**Already held (Record or Abstract):** Goody and Watt 1963 (`goody1963consequences`, the divide's founding text, opening paragraph only), Halverson 1992 (`halverson1992goody`, Record), Collins 1995 (`collins1995literacy`, Abstract: universalist claims "cannot withstand criticism"), Scribner and Cole (sister-arm card `scribner1981psychology`: literacy's effects are practice-specific; general cognitive gains follow schooling; 1978 article pp. 453, 457).

## (c) For, narrows, against

### For the thesis

1. **The Requerimiento is the paradigm of disclosure treated as sufficient, with the burden on the hearer.** It required the hearers to understand ("que entendáis bien ésto que os decimos", pp. 155–156), referred them to writings they could inspect "si quisiéredes" (p. 155), declared deaths and losses "á vuestra culpa" (p. 156), and made the notary's record of the reading the legal product (p. 156; p. 199; Israeli p. 46). Two contemporaries named the wrong: Las Casas, on the force of a command given in a language the hearer does not understand (pp. 162–163), and Oviedo, who carried the paper and said showing it to them "poco hacia al caso" (pp. 27–28). Wrongs (a) and (c) both hold.
2. **Wrong (b) has a documented mechanism.** Israeli (pp. 46–47) shows the requerimiento was an ordinary Castilian procedure built for addressees who understood the language, could answer, and whose answers the notary recorded. Transplanted to people who could do none of these things, the procedure kept its form and lost its conditions. That is wrong (b): the literate procedure stays when the situation that made it fair has gone.
3. **The neck verse shows a literacy test deciding standing, with the burden adjustable by the tester and capacity-building forbidden.** The judge could bar prompting and choose "the hardest verses" (Cross p. 554); a prisoner who knew passages by rote was handed the book upside down (Gabel); teaching a felon to read for the purpose was, on Cross's account, indictable (p. 552). Peers were excused from reading (Cross p. 553). Parliament ended the reading condition on the ground Cross gives as "the rank injustice of discriminating between lettered and unlettered" (p. 555).
4. **Written title made writing experts powerful and invited forgery.** Monks, "the traditional experts in writing, were also the greatest forgers" (Clanchy 1979, snippet); 44 of 164 Confessor charters likely spurious (Clanchy, snippet; Ong p. 96 overstates as "certainly"). Hunnisett (p. 68): the shift and quo warranto "made inevitable the large-scale forgery of documents". The party holding the writing skill gained from a rule that put the burden on the other side.
5. **The written treaty diverged from the oral promise, and a court said so.** Treaty 6's medicine chest is "in case of sickness amongst you" when spoken (Morris p. 218) and "at the discretion of such Agent" when written (p. 355). *Badger* ¶ 52: the written treaties "did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement" and were not translated into Cree or Dene.
6. **Thamus states wrong (c) in a phrase:** readers "will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things" (275a–b, verified card); and the written word cannot answer its reader (275e).

### Narrows the thesis

1. **The reading in the Requerimiento was a ritual for the record, not a failed attempt to inform.** Its legal function was the notary's *testimonio* (Israeli p. 46; Las Casas p. 199). This sharpens rather than weakens the parallel, but it changes the charge: the wrong was not that a disclosure was badly designed; it was that the disclosure's purpose was to discharge the discloser. v4 should say that, and should check whether Nadella's "tamper-proof human readable evidence" (P15) is evidence for the organisation's own record in the same sense.
2. **4 Hen. VII c. 13 did not create the reading test** (Statutes vol. 2 p. 538). It limited lay readers to one use and branded them. The test came from court practice after 1350 (Cross p. 552; Gabel: "commonly administered by the court" by the century's close). Cite the statute for branding, Cross and Gabel for the test. Benefit of clergy was a mercy that literacy unlocked, not a penalty on illiteracy (facet A); the injustice was the unequal mercy.
3. **The Statute of Frauds is not a literacy test.** Agents may sign; part delivery or earnest money satisfies s. 17; oral wills survive under £30 or with three witnesses (pp. 840–841). Its ground was evidential in a system where parties could not testify (Stollery p. 222). Its cost fell on "the honest man who has omitted a precaution" (1937 Committee via Stollery p. 254), not on a named class of the unlettered. I found no source that names the poor or the illiterate as its losers. Use it, if at all, for the narrower point that a writing rule allocates the cost of a missing formality to the party who trusted speech.
4. **Medieval England's losers were often the powerful, and the Crown retreated.** The quo warranto writing rule "threatened to disfranchise nearly all the magnates"; in 1290 "a compromise was made" and tenure "from time out of mind" was conceded (Clanchy, snippets). Clanchy says the Warenne story is "certainly inaccurate in details". Serfs used charters and were required to hold seals (Clanchy; Hunnisett p. 67). The case shows a writing rule pushed and partly withdrawn, not the unlettered dispossessed.
5. **The Treaty 6 record shows interpreters reading and explaining, and the Cree asking for schools.** Disclosure was oral and mediated (Morris pp. 185, 357), and capacity-building (schools) was in the text (p. 353). The failure is the divergence of the written clause and the later reading of it, not an absence of explanation.
6. **Ong's account is a cognitive one** (p. 3, "differences in 'mentality'"; p. 56). v4 may cite Ong for secondary orality and the Clanchy summary, not for what oral peoples could think.

### Against the thesis

1. **Literacy also widened standing.** Benefit of clergy saved readers from the gallows and then, once the test went, saved everyone convicted of a first clergyable felony (Cross p. 555; Blackstone in facet A). Hunnisett (p. 67) reads Clanchy's main result as "the gradual spread of literacy from a few monasteries to all ranks of society". A literacy transition can enlarge the class that has standing; the paper cannot present it as only a story of exclusion.
2. **Contemporaries of the transition did not always demand capacity.** The 1489 statute, the Statute of Frauds and the quo warranto rule all assume the burden can be met by hiring a clerk, an agent or a notary (agents sign in 1677, clerks prepare reeves' accounts per Robertson p. 613). That is an intermediary model, not a scaffolding model, and it was how literate societies actually spread written proof. The paper must argue why intermediation is not enough for algorithmacy, not assume it.
3. **"Read and explained" was in places done.** The Treaty 6 record names three interpreters (Morris p. 357), and the Cree negotiated changes read back to them (p. 185). Where disclosure was oral, mediated and negotiated, the record does not support a charge that the hearers were left to read alone.

## (e) What the paper can say

Notes for the drafter, each a claim the record supports.

1. The Requerimiento, drafted by Palacios Rubios for Pedrarias Dávila's expedition and read by that expedition at Santa Marta, by Oviedo's account, on 14 June 1514 (Oviedo 1853 t. III, cap. VII, p. 29), made Indigenous hearers responsible for a war on the strength of a Spanish text read aloud: "las muertes y daños que dello se recrecieren sea á vuestra culpa" (Las Casas, *Historia* III.57, t. IV p. 156; Helps 1855 p. 382 for the English).
2. Its legal product was the notary's sworn record that it had been read, not the hearers' understanding (Las Casas p. 156 and p. 199; Israeli 2022 p. 46: once performed, "the addressee could not pretend to be ignorant of the demand").
3. Two men who were there or knew its author condemned it in its own century: Oviedo, who carried it and wrote that showing the Indians the paper "poco hacia al caso" (Oviedo 1853 t. III pp. 27–28), and Las Casas, who held that a command in a language the hearer does not understand has no force (pp. 162–163).
4. The procedure came from Castilian practice where addressees understood, answered and had their answers recorded; it failed when moved to people who could do none of these (Israeli 2022 p. 47). This is the clearest historical instance of v4's wrong (b).
5. The text promised the hearers "el tiempo que fuere justo" to understand; Oviedo reports that captains read it to people already captured and tied (p. 32), and his only proposal to teach it was to put an Indian "en una jaula" (p. 31). No one built the hearers' capacity.
6. In English criminal law a reading test decided who hanged: a lay commoner who could read a Psalter verse, usually Psalm 51, was branded and released; one who could not, unless a peer, was hanged (Cross 1917 pp. 552–553; Blackstone IV.28). The judge controlled the test's difficulty (Cross p. 554), and Cross reports that teaching a felon to read for the purpose was indictable (p. 552).
7. Parliament removed the reading condition under Anne and abolished the privilege in 1827 (Cross p. 555; 7 & 8 Geo. IV c. 28 s. VI); the 1489 statute (4 Hen. VII c. 13, Statutes vol. 2 p. 538) only branded readers and limited them to one use.
8. In medieval England the Crown argued under Edward I that only a written charter warranted a franchise, then conceded tenure "from time out of mind" in 1290 (Clanchy 1979, snippets; facet A for the 3rd-ed. passage); written proof also made forgery by the writing experts, the monasteries, routine (Clanchy, p. 249 per Ong; Hunnisett 1981 p. 68).
9. The Numbered Treaties were read and explained by interpreters (Morris 1880 p. 357), yet the written text could narrow what was said, as Treaty 6's medicine chest did (p. 218 spoken; p. 355 written), and the Supreme Court of Canada holds the written treaties an incomplete record of oral agreements (*Badger* ¶¶ 52, 55).
10. The Statute of Frauds put the cost of a missing writing on the party who trusted a spoken promise, "the honest man who has omitted a precaution" (1937 Law Revision Committee, quoted in Stollery 1976 p. 254); it was not a literacy test, and most of s. 4 was repealed in 1954.
11. Plato's Thamus gives the oldest statement of the scaffolding objection: readers "will read many things without instruction" and a written word cannot answer its reader (*Phaedrus* 275a–b, 275e, verified sister-arm card).
12. The paper should state its claims as claims about institutions, not minds: Finnegan (1973) and Street (2003, p. 77) reject the great divide, Goody himself disowned the binary ("not … a simple binary shift", 1987), and Ong's account is explicitly about "mentality" (Ong p. 3) and so is not the paper's ground.

**Gap statements.**
- I found no work that reads the Requerimiento, the neck verse, the Statute of Frauds or the Numbered Treaties as precedents for disclosure-based AI governance, in OpenAlex (five queries, log row 12) or in the arm's facets; V6 documents the wider gap for oral-to-written analogies.
- I found no source that names a social class as the losers under the Statute of Frauds, in the statute, Stollery 1976 or facet A's sources.
- I found no court text on Treaty 6's medicine chest in the sources I could open (*Dreaver* 1935 and *Johnston* 1966 not reached).
