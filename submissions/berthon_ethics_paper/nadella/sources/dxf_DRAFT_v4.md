# The Space Between Decisions: Word Separation as a Guide to Designing for Algorithmacy

> **Version 4, 2026-10-15. Claude/Fable-drafted, not the author's text, except the abstract, which is the author's, verbatim.** The author's read-aloud is the last gate.

## Abstract

Human–machine interaction has moved from signals and screens to extended reality (Mourtzis & Tsakalos, 2026) and human–machine integration (Grahn, Wang, Wang, & Wang, 2026). Yet, whether on a factory headset or in an enterprise audit log (Nadella, 2026), the system still sends a person something to read so they can check the machine. That approach assumes a dyad: an operator paired with a tool (Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000). On digital platforms, though, counterparts—a driver and a rider, or a buyer and a seller—coordinate through an algorithmic mediator that binds and optimizes, forming an irreducible algorithmic triad. Mandated disclosure (Ben-Shahar & Schneider, 2011) does not spare them from having to game the mediator to coordinate with the other person. What these settings beg for instead is the kind of cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) seventh-century Irish scribes achieved when they broke up scriptio continua by adding spaces between words (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997). Unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line just to hear where each word ended, scaffolding literacy with oracy; word separation freed literacy by moving boundary detection from the voice to the page, exploiting a representational effect (Zhang & Norman, 1994) that enabled readers to internalize text. I argue we should look for a similar "smoking gun" design change that would free algorithmacy from literacy—enabling people to decide within irreducible triads through epistemic action (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994), strategic offloading (Li, 2017), and ecological feedback immediacy (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009).

## 1. Introduction

Human–machine interaction keeps changing what it hands a person and keeps the person's job the same. Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) tell the industrial version of the story: levers, then buttons, then graphical interfaces that moved the human "from operators to overseers," then dashboards, augmented reality and decision support "designed to assist humans in understanding machine behavior," and now extended reality, whose interface "provides the human with the necessary information to perform tasks and make decisions in real-time" (pp. 2–3, 10). Grahn et al. (2026) tell the same story at a different scale, four levels of coupling ordered by how much it costs to leave, and concede that the classical models "were developed for supervisory control settings in which a human operator, clearly separated from the machine, allocates functions and monitors discrete levels of automation through external displays" (p. 2). The enterprise version is Nadella's (2026) proposal to treat frontier models as insider risks: every meaningful model action is to leave "tamper-proof human readable evidence" (P15), and "an authorized person" is to hold a brake (P19). A headset, a dashboard and an audit log differ in everything except the task they set. Each sends a person something to read so the person can check the machine.

That task comes from a particular picture of who is in the room. Parasuraman et al. (2000) allocate functions between "the human operator" and "automation," which they define as a device that does something "previously, or conceivably could be, carried out ... by a human operator" (p. 287). The automation is an aid to the operator's task, and the operator reads it to confirm that the aid is working. The pair is the wrong picture for a digital platform. A rider and a driver, a buyer and a seller, a host and a guest do not each operate a tool. They coordinate with each other through a third party that matches them, prices them, ranks them, and does so to serve an objective of its own. Stark and Pais (2020) call the relation "typically three-sided" (p. 53); Vallas and Schor (2020) call the platform Simmel's *tertius gaudens*, the third who rejoices. The abstract calls it an irreducible algorithmic triad. The design question it raises is not how a person checks a machine but how two people decide through one.

The standing answer is disclosure: publish the parameters, explain the decision, show the log. Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011) showed that mandated disclosure fails its recipients across consumer law, and the platform statutes that followed copy its form while limiting its content. What the platform studies show is that a published rule changes how people strategize and does not remove the strategizing, because the task the rule leaves in place is reaching the other person through the mediator. Disclosure hands the person a text. It is, in the terms this paper adopts, a literacy-based intervention, and the capacity the triad calls for is not literacy.

The paper's move is to look for a design change of a different kind, and to take its model from the history of reading. Seventh-century Irish scribes, copying Latin that was not their language, put space between its words (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997). The change disclosed nothing about the text; it parsed it, marking boundaries with space and with points and altered letter forms (Tunbridge, 1992, pp. 165–175). It trained no reader to parse, though it presupposed a trained scribe and a convention reader and scribe shared. It moved a piece of work, finding where one word ends and the next begins, from the reader to the page: offloading "built-in" to the task environment (Risko & Gilbert, 2016, Box 3), and a representational effect, the same formal structure in a different external form with "dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (Zhang & Norman, 1994, p. 88). I argue that designing for algorithmacy, the capacity to decide well inside an algorithmic triad, should look for a change of that kind, and I name three places to look: epistemic action (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994), the removal of strategic work by the mediator's own rule (Li, 2017), and feedback that is both valid and immediate (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009).

This is a framework paper in the sense this journal names: it reviews literatures that HCI has mostly left to others, builds a set of orienting claims, and ends in a research agenda. It reports no study. It makes three contributions. It states, with the field's own sources, why the read-to-check role survives every change of medium and cannot be the role of a counterpart in a triad. It gives the scribal precedent a human-factors reading that survives the historians' dispute, by separating what word separation did to the page from what it has been claimed to do to literacy. And it specifies what a "smoking gun" design change for the triad would have to do and what evidence would count as having found one. The sections follow the abstract's order, from the read-to-check role (§2) and the dyad (§3) through the triad (§4), disclosure (§5), the precedent (§6) and the three means (§7) to candidate changes and their test (§8), limits (§9) and a conclusion (§10).

## 2. Reading to Check

### 2.1 From signals and screens to headsets and integration

The 2026 reviews agree about where human–machine interaction has gone and about what the human still does there. Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) reviewed 87 engineering publications from 2021 to 2026 and found most of the sensor work still at technology readiness levels 3 to 4, "with only a single case among forty showing TRL 5" (p. 8); in their collaborative model the robot senses and acts and the XR interface "provide[s] instructions and support[s] decision-making for humans" (p. 11). Grahn et al. (2026) rank four levels of integration by exit cost and place "work allocation on labor platforms" at Level 4b, where a person "cannot decline the system without forgoing an economic or social role" (Table 2, p. 8). Beside override and opt-out rights (p. 13), their remedy at most levels is a reader's remedy: outputs that are "transparent and interpretable to human operators" (Table 1, p. 5), explainable AI as "essential for maintaining the principle of human agency" (p. 16), and "civic AI literacy" in the education row (p. 13).
The factory headset is the clearest case, and the case where the abstract's claim needs its narrowest form. De Moura Costa et al. (2022) reviewed 32 papers on augmented reality for human–robot collaboration and found that the headset's job is to put information into the operator's field of view; safety features were the most common application, most were "for informational purposes only," 13 of 20 safety systems were passive, and most were "not certified" (pp. 27–29). Quandt and Freitag (2021) read 33 papers on industrial AR acceptance and found "no widespread adoption" (p. 14). What the headset sends is mostly instructions, warnings and status; checking the robot, through a trajectory preview or a digital twin, is one use among several. The abstract's "something to read so they can check the machine" therefore describes the audit log better than the headset, where the field's own purpose is to tell the worker what to do next. In both cases the worker reads.

### 2.2 The audit log and the overseer

The enterprise audit log is where the read-to-check role is written into law and into the leading statement of human-centered AI. Shneiderman (2020) prescribes "high levels of human control AND high levels of automation" (p. 1), rests reliability on "audit trails and analysis tools to review failures & near misses" (pp. 3–4), and rejects the teammate metaphor outright (p. 5). The European Union's Article 14 asks the person assigned to oversight to understand, monitor, "correctly interpret" the system's output, override it and stop it (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Enqvist (2023) reads that duty as one performed "through human intermediaries as interpreters of the data produced by the operating algorithms," observes that without transparency "the human overseers would have nothing substantive to review" (pp. 517–518), and notes that the Act leaves "a great deal of room for the providers to determine the detail on what human overseers will be presented with" (p. 525). Koulu (2020) explains why the role is adopted at all: "through digital traces and metadata we can easily ascertain that the oversight requirement has been fulfilled," at the risk of "turning human decision-makers into rubberstamps" (False promise; Conclusion).

Nadella's (2026) post belongs here, read as an enterprise-security proposal rather than as a statement about people who meet a platform's mediator. "We" in the post is the deploying organization throughout; the words "user," "customer" and "employee" do not occur. The remedy is containment: "separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it" (P5), keep every control outside the model (P11), and surround it with seven principles of observability, of which two give a person anything to do. Observe everything, with "tamper-proof human readable evidence" for every meaningful action (P15), gives a person a record to read; containment, with "an authorized person" able to stop a model "like an emergency brake" (P19), gives a person a brake. Model diversity (P14) and independent controls (P17) disclose nothing, and chain-of-thought transparency is called "not sufficient or dependable" (P9), so the post is not a disclosure proposal in the narrow sense. What it shares with the headset and the dashboard is the human seat: a reader of records after the fact and a holder of a stop. The people the model acts upon appear once, as recipients of incident disclosure (P20).

### 2.3 What the dyadic literature already knows about checking by reading

Human factors has measured this seat for forty years and has not liked what it found. Bainbridge (1983) gave the problem its name: the operator "may be expected to monitor that the automatic system is operating correctly," yet attention to a source "on which very little happens" lasts about half an hour, and "the human monitor has been given an impossible task" (pp. 775–776). Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) report detection of automation failures at 33% under constant reliability against 82% under variable reliability in multitask conditions, and 75% of pilots following a wrong automated checklist against 25% with paper. Dismukes and Berman (2010), observing 60 airline flights, logged 899 deviations and found crews trapped 35.4% of deviations in primary procedures, 14.4% in checklists and 5.6% in monitoring; pilots "do not receive feedback on the effectiveness of their monitoring" (pp. 23–24).
The same literature already knows the one lever that helps, and it is not more to read. Onnasch et al. (2014) meta-analyzed 18 experiments and found that return-to-manual performance fell with the degree of automation (tau −.34) and collapsed once action selection was automated (tau −.90 across six studies); they attribute the protection of keeping the choice to the generation effect and recommend that operators "should be kept involved at least to some extent in decision and action selection" (p. 486). O'Neill et al. (2022) reviewed 76 human–autonomy teaming studies and report that transparency, more of the agent's reasoning to read, is "mixed" and "may create a level of complacency that leads to less vigilance." Lai et al. (2023) surveyed over 100 human–AI decision-making studies and found the dominant paradigm "to have models providing predictions, then users can choose to follow or ignore" (§4.2.4, p. 20), with the agency-preserving alternatives in a minority. The protective factor the dyad has found is that the person keep deciding. That finding is the abstract's "epistemic action" in the dyad's own terms, and §7 picks it up. Before that, the paper has to say what the dyad is and where it stops.

## 3. The Dyadic Assumption

### 3.1 One operator, one aid

The supervisory-control lineage models two parties from its first page. Sheridan and Verplank's (1978) scale and the ten levels that Parasuraman et al. (2000) adopt describe the computer's autonomy "over human action" (p. 287): at level 4 "the computer suggests one decision alternative, but the human retains authority"; at level 6 "the system gives the human only a limited time for a veto." Wiener and Curry's (1980) Figure 1 has two dimensions, the operator monitoring the computer and the computer monitoring the operator (p. 13). Every one of Onnasch et al.'s (2014) 18 experiments is an operator and an aid. Even where the model's own example has several people in it, the unit of analysis stays the pair: Parasuraman et al.'s air-traffic case has a controller issue "a verbal clearance to a pilot" that "may be a computer choice" uplinked to the aircraft (p. 293), and the model analyzes each operator's relation to the automation separately. The automation has no objective. It helps the operator do the operator's task, and the operator's reading of it is a check that the help is sound.

### 3.2 Where the lineage admits teams, and why the machine stays a tool

Three branches of the field admit more than two parties, and in none of them does the machine acquire an interest against the people it faces. Cognitive systems engineering takes the joint system as the unit: Rayo et al. (2023) write that cognition "resides not only in the head of an individual, but in components of the system and their interactions" (p. 184), and Woods calls the levels-of-automation proposals "obsolete, narrow, and/or counterproductive" (p. 185). Klein et al. (2004) ask automation to be a "team player" that enters a Basic Compact, "a commitment to some degree of goal alignment" (p. 91); the machine has goals, and the design aim is to align them with the team's. O'Neill et al. (2022) require of a human–autonomy team "a common goal as a collective"; of their 76 studies 45 pair one human with one agent, and the authors describe the evidence base as "dyads." Oversight law adds a chain, provider to deployer to overseer to system, with affected persons as rights-holders who have no interface (Enqvist, 2023, pp. 518–530); even there, the act of oversight is one reader facing one system's output, and the subject coordinates with no one through the system. The lineage admits teams and chains. It treats the machine as a tool, a component or an aligned teammate. The interested mediator between two counterparts is absent from it.

### 3.3 Shneiderman's choice

One qualification to the abstract's wording belongs here. For the leading statement of human-centered AI, the dyad is not an assumption but a position. Shneiderman (2020) keeps the pair on purpose: computers "are not teammates" (p. 5), the person should operate a controllable device, and organizations should read the records. The argument against the pair therefore has to be made against a choice, and the ground for making it is empirical: there are settings in which the machine is not the person's device at all. A rider cannot operate Uber's dispatch; a seller cannot operate Amazon's Buy Box. The person is on one side of a system whose other side is another person and whose middle has a purpose of its own.

## 4. The Irreducible Algorithmic Triad

### 4.1 Three parties, one of them optimizing

Three literatures model platform coordination as three parties, with the platform as a party of its own. Economic sociology states it directly. Stark and Pais (2020) write that the relationship "is typically three-sided: on one side, the platform owner; on the second side, the provider ...; and on the third side, the user-customer" (p. 53), and that what the algorithmic-management literature misses "is that this triangular geometry ... and the power asymmetries along its various axes are key" (p. 54). Their mechanism is co-optation: the owner enrolls the other two sides' ratings "without managerial authority having been delegated to them" (p. 47), so that feedback loops are "twisted by the triadic relations of owner-provider-user" (p. 54). Vallas and Schor (2020) supply the ancestor: platforms "govern the service triangle that links employers, workers, and customers" and play "the role of tertius gaudens—'the third who rejoices'—as in Simmel's classic theory of the triad" (16.10). Simmel (1950) is the source of the concept. The third member makes possible mediation and arbitration (pp. 145–154), exploitation as *tertius gaudens* (pp. 154–162) and *divide et impera* (pp. 162–169), forms that "are impossible if there are only two elements" (p. 145). The abstract's "binds and optimizes" fuses Simmel's arbitrator, who "ends up by taking sides" (p. 148), with his self-interested third (p. 154): the platform both settles the match and gains from how it settles it.

The economics of two-sided markets makes the platform a profit maximizer by construction. Rochet and Tirole (2006) define a market as two-sided when "the volume of transactions between end-users depends on the structure and not only on the overall level of the fees charged by the platform" (pp. 2–3), and derive the platform's price level and structure from its own profit (pp. 17, 24). Hagiu and Wright (2015) define a multi-sided platform by "direct interactions" in which the sides "retain control over the key terms" plus "affiliation," and set the platform's fees to maximize its profit (pp. 5, 12–13). The management literature dissents. Kellogg et al. (2020) read algorithms at work through labor-process theory, with the algorithm as the employer's instrument and customers as raters, so their frame is a dyad with an instrument. Stark and Pais themselves bound the triad: where the platform employs directly, as in "Amazon's enormous warehouses," there is "no triangular relationship" (p. 54, n. 13). The triad is a property of the marketplace form, not of algorithms as such, and the paper's claim sides with the sociologists against the labor-process frame for marketplaces only.

### 4.2 Binds and optimizes

The documented cases show both verbs, and show that the strength of the binding varies. On the rider side of Uber, Cohen et al. (2016) describe a take-it-or-leave-it surge multiplier, rounded to 1.2x, 1.3x and upward with no 1.1x and never below 1.0x, whose stated goal is to clear local supply and demand and allocate rides to the riders who value them most (p. 7); business rules override the algorithm in 11.5% of sessions, and rider purchase rates fall from 62% at base price to 39% at high surge. Castillo et al. (2025) model the dispatch: an idle driver is sent on every request, the price is set per geofence every two minutes (p. 18), and a static price would sit at the 92nd percentile of dynamic prices (p. 32). Garg and Nazerzadeh (2022) show that the platform sets the rider's price and the driver's payment separately, "decoupled pricing" (p. 8), that multiplicative surge makes it optimal for a driver to reject short surge trips and long non-surge trips, and that additive surge removes much of the gain, worth about $15 per hour for short trips at high surge in their RideAustin estimate (p. 22).
On Amazon, Chen et al. (2016) found that the Buy Box, through which 82% of sales pass, chooses the default seller by undocumented weights, and that 543 sellers repricing by algorithm won it more often at every rank but the top (pp. 2, 7, 10). Farronato et al. (2023) found Amazon's own brands ranked higher in search. On Airbnb, Haldar et al. (2019) describe a ranking model trained to put booked listings high and shipped on A/B conversion gains, whose engineers could not recover stable feature importances (pp. 1, 7–8). On remote freelance platforms, Wood et al. (2019) found that clients rate and "the platforms' algorithmic ranking of workers within search results" allocates, with control sitting "at the end of the labour process rather than during it" (Findings).

Two boundaries follow. First, binding varies by platform: Uber sets the match and both prices; Amazon picks a default seller; Airbnb decides visibility while the host still sets the price; freelance platforms rank and filter. By Hagiu and Wright's (2015) own definition, a mediator that takes control of the key terms has moved from a pure platform toward a reseller or an integrated firm (pp. 5, 21). The triad this paper means is that hybrid, not the textbook two-sided market. Second, the binding is not total for everyone. Vallas and Schor (2020) note that supplemental earners "can refuse low-paying tasks" (16.8), and Cruz and Gameiro (2023) describe workers who "agree to allocate tasks directly without the need for platform intermediation" (p. 3). Where the match survives the mediator, the triad was reducible for that pair.

### 4.3 What "irreducible" can mean

The word carries the argument and needs a definition the sources can bear. Three senses came out of the reading, and the paper adopts a fourth as its gauge. Simmel's sense is formal: the triad's forms belong to the configuration and not to any pair, and adding a third "completely changes" a dyad while a fourth does not change the triad (1950, pp. 138, 141, 145). Rochet and Tirole's sense is price-structure non-neutrality: the market is two-sided exactly when the two end-users cannot bargain the mediator's choice away (2006, pp. 6, 8, 11). Castillo et al.'s sense is system feedback: trip supply depends on both sides through pickup time, a rider's request removes an idle driver and lengthens every other rider's wait, and the resulting failure mode, the "wild goose chase," is one no party in either dyad can see or cause (2025, pp. 5–8, 13–14). Against all three, Hagiu and Wright's (2015) model decomposes into N professional–platform relations plus a spillover term (pp. 9–14), which is how an economist resists the word.

The fourth sense is the one the author's program uses: integrated information, Φ, as the gauge of whether a given triad's causal structure can be cut into parts without loss (Hunt, 2026). Φ is the right kind of instrument for the question, because it is defined over the whole system's transition structure rather than over any pair's payoffs, and it returns a quantity rather than a verdict by example. What the record shows is that it has not yet been applied to a mediated triad. Engel and Malone (2018) computed observational estimates of Φ on who-is-active data from four-person peer groups and found a correlation with collective intelligence of r = .370 (p. 11); their groups had no mediator with an objective of its own, their lag was tuned to maximize Φ, and they use the number as a predictor of performance rather than as a test of reducibility. No source outside the author's program proposes Φ for a platform triad, and none computes it. The paper therefore says "irreducible" in the senses the platform cases support, system feedback and non-neutrality, and points to Φ as the instrument that would make the word measurable on a modeled triad, with its measure, coding, lag and partition fixed before computation. §8 returns to it.

### 4.4 Why HCI has not modeled it

The nearest HCI field defines its mediator so that the triad falls outside it. Hancock et al. (2020) define AI-mediated communication as communication "in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages" (p. 1); the agent's autonomy "is similar to the principal–agent relationship," and "content ranking, recommendation and classification algorithms ... we therefore exclude" (p. 4). The experiments in that tradition randomize a delegate's availability to one or both partners (Hohenstein et al., 2023), and what moves is the partner's suspicion, not any property of a third party with interests. AI-MC is a dyad with a delegate. Terveen and McDonald (2005), whose framework-and-agenda form this paper borrows, did describe a system that stands between two people, matching them, introducing them and taking feedback on the match; their matching system has no objective beyond good matches, and their own caution is that "it has not been established that social network diagrams help users select others with whom to interact" (p. 418). Özçetin and Wiltse (2025), in this journal, come closest to the problem and propose the remedy the paper argues against: "revealing design," which would make platform relations "legible and actionable at the level of interface" (§6) and which the authors expect "would need to be mandated at the level of legislation" (§6). They agree that terms of service fail, judging the usability fixes "notably limited" (§2.1); the disagreement is whether the fix is more for the person to see and understand.

## 5. Why Disclosure Does Not Spare the Counterparts

### 5.1 Mandated disclosure and its platform descendants

Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011) defined their target narrowly, a technique "in which a lawmaker requires a discloser to give a disclosee a standard disclosure" (p. 651), and found it failing in three steps: disclosees "do not read disclosed information, do not understand it when they read it, and do not use it even if they understand it" (p. 665). Two features of their evidence limit what the abstract can take from them. Their cases are consumer and patient disclosures to individuals, before platforms and outside any triad, and their own remedies, symbols instead of sentences, advice and ratings instead of data, defaults and prohibition (pp. 743–749), all sit on the discloser's side of the relation. Loewenstein et al. (2014) add that disclosure "may have little effect on recipients but large effects on providers" (p. 398). What the article establishes is that handing a person a text is a weak way to change what the person does. It does not by itself establish anything about gaming a mediator.

Platform regulation copied the form and limited the content. Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 requires "the main parameters determining ranking and the reasons for the relative importance" (Art. 5(1)) and says providers are "not required to disclose algorithms" (Art. 5(6)); the Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Art. 27) and the Platform Work Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, Art. 9) use the same formula. The Commission's guidelines on ranking transparency say what the disclosure is for: it "aims to improve predictability and help users improve the presentation of their goods and services" (European Commission, 2020, pts 5, 15), weights and algorithms need not be disclosed (pt 46), "an excess of information can mean that, in effect, no meaningful information is provided" (pt 25), and the point is to let businesses decide "how to invest in 'ranking strategies'" (pt 94). The regulator's own statement of purpose is the strongest support the abstract has: the disclosure exists so that the disclosee can strategize better.

### 5.2 A disclosed rule redirects strategizing

The founding models of strategic classification assume disclosure and ask the designer to survive it. Hardt et al. (2016) note that "concerns of gaming and manipulation are often used as a reason for keeping classification mechanisms secret," and reply that "secrecy is not a robust solution" because outsiders learn the rule "from classification outcomes" (p. 2). Kleinberg and Raghavan (2020) add that secrecy "can create inequalities between insiders ... and outsiders" (p. 2). Perdomo et al. (2020) show that the rule is itself a response to the population it scores (§5), so the loop runs whether or not the rule is disclosed, and Ghalme et al. (2021) show that hiding the rule does not stop gaming; people game a guessed rule at a "price of opacity."

Where disclosure was manipulated, strategizing rose or moved. Björkegren et al. (2020) showed participants the scoring rule and found them moving "closer to the direction incentivized by the algorithm," at a cost of about 23% of predictive performance for a standard rule (pp. 5, 29). Tasnim et al. (2025) found truthful ranking fall from 80.14% with no probabilities shown to 57.45% and 40.43% when probabilities were shown, and aggressive manipulation rise from 5.67% to 39.01% when it was shown to succeed 90% of the time (Table 2, p. 2474). Eslami et al. (2019) told Yelp users that their reviews had been filtered; six of fifteen said they would "write for the algorithm," seven that they would use Yelp less or leave (p. 11). Danz et al. (2022), in a belief-elicitation rule that was truthful by construction, found that showing the exact payoff table doubled false reports, 41.5% against 20.6% with no quantitative information (Table 2, pp. 12–17). Hakimov and Kübler (2021) report that 85% truthful play under top trading cycles fell to 31% once subjects were told others might misreport (pp. 444–449).

The models disagree about what the extra strategizing does, and the disagreement narrows the claim. Wang et al. (2023) find "a broad set of conditions under which making the algorithm transparent is beneficial for the firm," because gaming a disclosed cheap feature pushes people onto the costly causal one, while "the agents are not always better off" (p. 4). Cofone and Strandburg (2019) argue that gaming requires weak proxies and cheap modification and that "blanket claims that disclosure will lead to gaming are over-blown" (p. 623; the prerequisites are on pp. 662–663). The abstract's claim therefore holds for ranking rules built on cheap, mutable signals, reviews, engagement, response time, acceptance rate, which is the platform case, and not for every disclosed rule.

### 5.3 Gaming as the cost of reaching the other person

Platforms give the anti-gaming reason for opacity in their own words, and partial disclosure then produces gaming by guesswork. Upwork's help page said that revealing the calculation "would make it easier for some users to artificially boost their scores" (Rahman, 2019, p. 89); Airbnb's researchers wrote that "there needs to be some degree of uncertainty ... or otherwise, some hosts would inevitably game the system" (Jhaver et al., 2018, Discussion); the Commission's guidelines license it, since fraud mechanisms "cannot be revealed in detail" (European Commission, 2020, pt 73). The result is what Rahman (2019) calls "superstitious reactivity": a score that dropped "from 100% to 82%" after closing an idle contract (p. 93), updates at unknown times (p. 94), and freelancers "trying various methods to take apart and understand the algorithm" (p. 117). Bucher et al. (2020) record Upwork workers' "20/1 rule" and avoidance of "gray area words"; Bishop (2019) records YouTube creators' "algorithmic gossip" behind $75–$500 guild fees; Jhaver et al. (2018) record a host who changed his title daily because "lots of blogs" said to (P-15).

Every platform study reads this work as aimed at the other person. Curchod et al. (2020) found 41 of 77 high-rated eBay sellers describing "working around the algorithm" to prevent buyer negatives, in a relation the authors call triadic. Bucher et al. (2020) describe freelancers forgoing proposals and underpricing work to keep a score that lets them reach clients, "extra work" in a "triadic relationship between algorithm, client and worker." Jhaver et al. (2018) call the host's position a "double negotiation," an appeal to guests and to algorithms at once that conflicts when declining short bookings costs acceptance rate. Luca and Zervas (2016) found about 16% of 316,415 Boston Yelp reviews filtered as suspicious, with businesses caught soliciting fake reviews averaging 2.6 stars and 18 reviews against 3.5 and 86 (pp. 6, 19, accepted manuscript). Lee et al.'s (2015) driver toggled the app at traffic lights to avoid distant requests (p. 1607). Bambauer and Zarsky (2018) name the cost: "third-order inefficiencies—the costs of the system itself," including "the persistent worry and vigilance" (p. 32).

Three qualifications keep this claim honest. None of these studies isolates a coordination cost from a competition or reputation cost, and none measures what the other party loses, so "to coordinate with the other person" is supported by description and not by a test. "Gaming" is the platform's moral label: Petre et al. (2019) find the line between manipulation and legitimate strategy "nebulous and largely reflective of their material interests," and record Facebook advising calls-to-action and then demoting them as "engagement bait" in the same year. And the counterparts can ally against the mediator: Rahman's freelancers formed "online alliances with clients to game the performance evaluation system" (Vallas & Schor, 2020, 16.7), which means coordinating with the other person can itself be the way the mediator is gamed.

### 5.4 Where disclosure helped

The counter-evidence is real and is of a particular shape. Kizilcec (2016) found that a procedural explanation of an adjusted peer grade removed the trust gap between learners whose expectations were violated and met (3.72 against 3.70), where no explanation (3.02 against 4.18) and full outcome data (2.77 against 3.89) did not; the outcome is self-reported trust in 103 learners (p. 2392). Masuda et al. (2022) raised truth-telling by a net 23.8 points by announcing strategy-proofness in a Vickrey auction, and Gonczarowski et al. (2024) found that a short statement of a mechanism's property helped where teaching the algorithm did not. Matsuo et al. (2026) cut simulator collisions to 1 of 15 with a warning cue and its reason, against 7 of 15 with no cue, in a person-and-machine setting. Tasnim et al. (2025) read their own data as a case for disclosing risk, since parents shifted to aggressive strategies only at a 90% stated success chance while simulations put real gains at 1–2% of students (p. 2477).

Two things are true of this list together. Where disclosure helped, it was a short statement of a property, a reason attached to a cue, or advice computed for the person, not a published rule or a log; and none of it is a triad with a behavioral outcome. The author's ruling that disclosure is a literacy-based intervention stands in this form: handing a counterpart a description of the mediator has not been shown to change how well the counterpart reaches the other person, and in the measured cases it changed how the counterpart strategized. The two documented changes that removed a strategic task altogether were not disclosures. Uber's move from multiplicative to additive surge removed the incentive to cherry-pick, and Garg and Nazerzadeh (2022) record Uber disclosing destinations only afterwards: "Incentive compatible pricing is an important stepping stone to showing this information" (p. 9, n. 7). Compelled pooling in US insurance and credit-card law, Bambauer and Zarsky (2018) write, "renders gaming efforts ... unnecessary" (p. 47). Both changed the rule rather than the reader. The question is what kind of change that is, and the history of reading offers a model.

## 6. The Scribal Precedent

### 6.1 What the Insular scribes did

The earliest surviving Irish manuscripts "already contain some kind of separation" (Tunbridge, 1992, p. 193). Tunbridge's table runs from erratic wax tablets and syntactic-unit spacing through word-based separation in the St Gall Isidore of the second quarter of the seventh century to the Bangor Antiphonary (c. 680–691) and the Schaffhausen Adomnán (before 713) (pp. 214–215). Her explanation is linguistic: "Irish scribes when copying Latin began to abandon scriptio continua in favour of a layout which clarified the syntax and morphology of the language and hence met the needs of the non-native Latin speaker" (Long Abstract, p. 4). Saenger (1997) gives the chronology the field uses: Insular origin in the seventh and eighth centuries, Continental adoption from the mid-tenth century, standard and ubiquitous by the mid-eleventh (chs. 4, 6, 9; pp. 83, 100, 176–177). Three facts narrow the picture. The same scribes wrote Old Irish in stress-groups rather than words: Thurneysen's rule, quoted by Doyle and McCrae (2025), is that "words which are grouped round a single chief stress and have a close syntactic connexion with each other are written as one" (p. 24). Separation came as a bundle with points, hyphens, tags on final letters and variant letter forms (Tunbridge, 1992, pp. 165–175), and Saenger's own sentence names four devices, "word separation, the diastole, the hyphen, and eventually the trait d'union" (p. 85). And Latin had used interpuncts before the second century AD and dropped them (Heilmann, 2020, pp. 182–183), so separation was a return rather than an invention. The scribes' move was a learner's analysis of a foreign language, carried out on the page, and it was the central move in a complex of conventions.

### 6.2 What the voice did and did not do

The abstract's account of the reader before spacing is Saenger's, and it is contested at every load-bearing point. Saenger's (1997) thesis correlates oral reading with the "threshold in the duration of cognitive activity needed to achieve lexical access" in a script (p. 2), and holds that the Insular devices "provided shortcuts for achieving the reading skills that an elite among the ancients had mastered only through a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (p. 85). O'Donnell (2000), a sympathetic reviewer, called the stretch from the physiology of reading to the theory of reading practice "the most venturesome and vulnerable part of the book" (¶4). The classicists then tested it. Knox (1968) had already shown that ancient books were "normally read aloud" while silent reading of letters was "taken completely for granted" in fifth- and fourth-century Athens (pp. 434–435), on unspaced text. Heilmann (2020) calls the reading-aloud consensus a "quasi-dogma," says Saenger's arguments "presuppose precisely what must be proved" (pp. 178–179), reads the ancient complaints about laborious reading as being about learners (p. 181, n. 28), and concludes: "We should be careful not to project our difficulties with reading scriptio continua onto ancient readers" (p. 183). The founders of the oralization thesis cut the other way too. Balogh (1927) explained the difficulty of *scriptio continua* by whole-word recognition and long practice (pp. 227–228) and credited the printing press, not the scribes, with regular word boundaries (p. 236); Hendrickson (1929) found unspaced script "only strange, not necessarily difficult" (p. 186).

Reading science settles what the voice does. Readers of spaced scripts lose a third to a half of their speed when the spaces are removed: 33% for English monolinguals and 45% for Thai–English bilinguals reading English (Winskel et al., 2009, p. 344), and 69% for Spanish readers of one-color unspaced text (Perea et al., 2015, Table 2). Readers of unspaced scripts do not pay that cost. Forty-six Chinese readers segmented silently by lexical knowledge with the same word-based eye-movement signature as German readers (Li et al., 2014); skilled Chinese adults read word-spaced and unspaced text in the same time, 5,239 against 5,221 ms (Bai et al., 2008); Thai children target word centers from Grade 6 at 88–93% comprehension, silently (Kasisopa et al., 2016, pp. 12–13). The voice is not the boundary detector even when it is used: in reading aloud the eye leads the voice by 16.2 letters at fixation onset, about 561 ms, so words are found before they are said (Laubrock & Kliegl, 2015). Speech itself does not mark word ends; "continuous speech lacks directly accessible cues to word boundaries" and pauses "are rare and occur only inconsistently" (Matzinger et al., 2021, Introduction). The word boundary is partly a literate idea: 60 American adults disagreed with written convention about where words end in proportion to their reading level (Barton & Hamilton, 1980, Tables 1–3). Nor did the voice leave reading when spaces arrived. Leinenger's (2014) review shows skilled silent readers of spaced text generating phonological codes within 80–125 ms of a word, before fixation through preview, and later as an inner voice that rises with difficulty.

What survives of the abstract's sentence is a narrower claim, and it is the claim the design argument needs. For an unpracticed reader of a foreign language, voicing an unspaced line gave a second channel: a sounded string could be matched against words already known by ear (Busch, 2002, p. 20), as Scribner and Cole (1981) describe Vai readers resolving an unspaced syllabary by trial pronunciation. That is a learner's scaffold, not a physiological necessity, and "oracy," which none of the sources read here uses for voiced reading, is a new sense of the word for it; the sources say "reading aloud," "voiced reading" or "oralization." Word separation put the boundary on the page, where any salient mark will serve: alternating color recovers all but 4.7% of the cost of removing spaces (Perea et al., 2015, p. 12). It removed a segmentation task from the reader. It did not remove the inner voice from reading, and the sentence "enabled readers to internalize text" has no source behind it in either sense the phrase might carry.

### 6.3 Offloading by design and the representational effect

Read as a change to the task rather than to the reader, the scribes' move has an exact description in human factors. Risko and Gilbert (2016) define cognitive offloading as "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task in order to reduce cognitive demand" (p. 2), which names the reader's own act, and then extend it: "In many cases the decision to offload is not made by the individual. Rather, offloading is 'built-in' to the task environment by design" (Box 3, p. 33). Word separation is offloading built in by design: another person's act on the page before the reader acts. Hutchins's (1995) speed bugs are the same move. Without bugs a pilot reads, remembers and compares speed values; with them "a memory and scale reading task is transformed into a judgment of spatial adjacency" (p. 283), and "individual pilot memory has not been enhanced; rather, the memory function has now become a property of a larger system" (p. 282). Larkin and Simon (1987) give the formal version: two representations can be informationally equivalent and computationally unequal, so that the pulley problem costs 138 element-considerations in sentences and essentially the seven inferences themselves in a diagram (Table 2, p. 77), and whoever draws the diagram "makes these new inferences which are then displayed explicitly" (p. 70).
Zhang and Norman (1994) supply the construct the abstract names. The representational effect holds when "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (p. 88); in their Tower of Hanoi isomorphs, solution times differed by "as large as a ratio of one to ten" (p. 107), and rules carried by the physical setup were "error proof," since no subject broke one (p. 110). Two conditions in their experiments bear on a page. The external rule is enforced either by physics or by shared cultural knowledge, the coffee-cup rule being "an external rule grounded in cultural knowledge" (p. 106), and Zhang and Patel (2006) later name that case a "cognitive affordance ... provided by cultural conventions" (p. 339). A word space is a cultural affordance of this kind: it works because readers share the convention, and it can be withheld by whoever controls the page. And the effect is defined over isomorphs that carry identical information. Where an unspaced string has one lawful parse, spacing is isomorphic and the effect is representational; where it has two, the space adds information as well.
The precedent also carries the costs that re-representation brings. What is offloaded is practiced less and retained less: more offloading meant faster copying, 49.3 against 55.1 s, and worse memory for the offloaded layout, 5.91 against 7.07 bindings of 12 (Grinschgl et al., 2021, Table 1); statements believed saved were recalled at 0.19–0.22 against 0.29–0.31 for statements believed erased (Sparrow et al., 2011, pp. 777–778). People over-rely on the store, d > 0.8 (Gilbert et al., 2020), and "adapt by becoming dependent on those tools" (Kirsh, 2010, p. 450). Against that, the same group reports forgetting of about 45% unaided against about 5% with reminders and calls fears of long-term harm "overstated" (Gilbert et al., 2023). The reader of spaced text who can no longer read *scriptio continua* has paid the dependence cost; Winskel et al.'s 33–45% is its measure.

### 6.4 What the precedent teaches, and what it does not

The precedent teaches the shape of an intervention that is neither disclosure nor training. The letters were the same before and after, so nothing was revealed. The reader was the same before and after, so nobody was taught. A piece of inferential work moved from the person to the artifact, and it stayed there because the convention was shared. That shape is the model this paper carries forward.

The precedent does not teach that word separation spread literacy, and the paper does not claim it. Saenger's book "is not a general history of literacy" (1997, p. x), and no historian of literacy read for this paper credits spacing with raising the number of readers; Balogh (1927, p. 236) and Hendrickson (1929, pp. 193–194) date the end of voiced reading to the press, half a millennium later. Nor does the precedent teach that one move did the work alone. The author's ruling is that spacing was the critical move within a complex, and the record supports a complex (Tunbridge, 1992; O'Donnell, 2000, ¶4) with one device, the visible word boundary, carrying the segmentation cost that modern readers still pay without it. Finally, the precedent is a guide to the move from one capacity to another and no proof of how the next move will go. The capacity it helped was literacy, a person and a page; the capacity the triad calls for is something else.

## 7. Freeing Algorithmacy from Literacy

### 7.1 Algorithmacy

I define algorithmacy as the capacity to decide well within an algorithmic triad: to reach the other party through a mediator that binds and optimizes, without first reading and understanding the mediator. The word has a prior printed use in another sense: Oberschelp (1995) wrote that "we have to preserve logicacy and algorithmacy by continuously training simple mental activities without artificial tools" (p. 121), meaning a skill of carrying out algorithmic steps by hand. The sense here is the lab's (Hunt, 2026): not executing algorithms but coordinating through one. Literacy takes in a fixed text and acts afterward; the read-to-check role of §2 is literacy applied to a machine. Algorithmacy acts and takes in the result, because the mediator is not a text, it answers, and it answers differently as the other party and the mediator's own objective move. The author's ruling is that understanding in these settings comes through deciding and quickly ingesting the results. The sources support a conditional form of that ruling, and the conditions are where the design work lies.

Two cautions set the limits of the contrast. First, the ruling's own example, that pilots do not read the situation but act, is contradicted by the aviation record: both pilots in Hutchins's (1995) cockpit keep monitoring airspeed (pp. 283, 285), and monitoring generated the largest share of the deviations Dismukes and Berman (2010) observed, 391 of 899, and the least caught, 5.6%. What that record shows is narrower and still useful: people correct what they do far more often than what they watch. Second, acting as a way of knowing has a cost that Weick (1988) stated first. "People think by acting" (p. 305), but there is "a delicate tradeoff between dangerous action which produces understanding and safe inaction which produces confusion" (p. 305), and the acts that build understanding change the thing understood. On a platform the person's acts feed an adaptive mediator. Each of the three means below answers that problem in part, and none answers it alone.

### 7.2 Epistemic action in a triad

Kirsh and Maglio (1994) distinguished pragmatic actions, which bring an agent "closer to his or her physical goal" (p. 515), from epistemic actions, "physical actions that make mental computation easier, faster, or more reliable" (p. 513). Their Tetris players rotated falling pieces in as few as 100 ms where mental rotation took 800 to 1200 ms (pp. 530–531), and "experts keep their costs lower, in part, by performing more epistemic actions" (p. 547). This is the dyadic literature's own lever stated as a theory: keep the person acting, because acting is how the person comes to know. Onnasch et al.'s (2014) generation effect and Shneiderman's (2020) "rapid, incremental, and reversible actions" (p. 13) are the same point in human factors and HCAI.

The 1994 paper also states the condition under which it works, and the condition excludes the triad as written. Epistemic rotation pays because "the local effects of an action are totally determinate. There are no hidden states, exogenous influences, or other agents to change the result of hitting the rotate key" (p. 543). A platform has all three. Kirsh (2010) supplies the bridge: for outcomes that depend on "the voting of n participants" or "certain market equilibrium problems," "it is necessary to run the process, and it is best to run the process repeatedly" (p. 453). In a triad, epistemic action is not acting to see a fixed answer but acting to see a moving one, and the one experiment that put a shared display in front of several agents and one provider shows what moving means. Kayongo et al. (2022) ran a congestion game in which players saw a chart of prior play. Players followed the chart two-thirds as often when it was public as when it was private (odds 0.67), 25–40% did not follow it even in private, and the "visualization equilibrium," at which the chart predicts the behavior it induces, lay at 0.34–0.35, between the Nash outcome (0.22) and the optimum (0.44) (pp. 1, 5–7). The provider's convention is constrained to a fixed point, and the agent's act of reading and responding moves the thing read. The defensible claim is therefore "act to know," not "act and the answer holds," and what makes acting informative in a triad is the subject of the third means.

### 7.3 Strategic offloading

The abstract's second means is the removal of strategic work from the person by the mediator's own rule. The label "strategic offloading" is the author's. Li (2017) does not use it; his term is *obviously strategy-proof*, and the phrase has a prior printed meaning in cognitive psychology, Gilbert's (2015) title for a person's own choice to put a delayed intention into the environment, in four online experiments with 1,196 participants. The paper keeps the author's label for the mechanism and attributes the mechanism, not the phrase, to Li. The idea itself runs through market design in other words. Li's own rationale is cognitive and about trust: "Participants have to see for themselves that it is incentive-compatible; the mechanism has to be simple. Simplicity bypasses the need for participants to trust the mechanism designer" (2024, p. 3). Boston's superintendent, recommending a strategy-proof school assignment in 2005, wanted "to enable families to list their true choices of schools without jeopardizing their chances," because the old rule gave "an advantage to families who have the time, resources and knowledge to conduct the necessary research" (Abdulkadiroğlu et al., 2006, p. 24).

The mechanism is real and its size is measured. Li (2017) defines a strategy as obviously dominant if, "for any deviation, at any information set where both strategies first diverge, the best outcome under the deviation is no better than the worst outcome under the dominant strategy," and shows that an ascending clock auction is obviously strategy-proof while the second-price sealed-bid auction, with the same outcome rule, is not (§1). In his serial-dictatorship experiment, "36% of games do not end in the dominant strategy outcome" when subjects submit ranked lists and 7% when they take turns picking (§5.3, p. 29). Chen and Sönmez (2006) found roughly 80% of participants misrepresenting under the Boston mechanism, 53% under top trading cycles and 36% under Gale–Shapley (Table 4, p. 217). Martínez-Marquina et al. (2019) isolated the piece of work that goes: removing uncertainty about a counterpart while keeping every contingency payoff-relevant doubled payoff-maximizing play, 19.7% to 41.5%, N = 371, and made advice effective, 72.5% against 41.5% under certainty (Table 3, pp. 15, 19, 26). The clock auction works, they argue, by resolving the lottery over counterparts before the bidder acts (pp. 30–31). That is the scribal shape: contingent reasoning, the segmentation task of a strategic setting, moved from the person into the rule.

Two findings narrow the means and point the search elsewhere. First, the obviously-strategy-proof label has not survived as the active ingredient. Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch (2022) replicated Li's auction comparison with his materials and split the move into steps: about 49% of the reduction in deviations came from showing an ascending clock after sealed bids, 12% (not significant) from live bidding on the clock, which is the step that creates obvious strategy-proofness, and almost 40% from live drop-out information; "obviousness is really a matter of what one gets to see ... not how one formally gets to decide" (pp. 3–4, 18). Bó and Hakimov (2024) found 93% truthful play inside an OSP mechanism when the dominant path only required clinching and 56% when it required passing, the latter no better than the direct mechanism (Table 4, p. 33). Li (2024) concedes both points (fnn. 5, 8). What removes the task is the shape of the decision, pick an object, clinch, and what the person sees, a clock, rivals dropping out, a step-by-step rejection. Those are the abstract's first and third means, and offloading works through them. Second, strategy-proofness removes most but not all strategizing, and the residue is systematic. In Israel's psychology match, 19.3% of 672 relevant lists ranked an unfunded contract above the funded one despite repeated advice (Hassidim et al., 2021, pp. 11–21); in Hungary, 11% of 528,172 eligible lists contained a dominated choice, more often where it was cheaper, which the authors read as ruling out cognitive limits "as a sole determinant" (Shorrer & Sóvágó, 2023, pp. 2–13); among 1,714 recent residency applicants, 23.3% submitted a suboptimal list in an incentivized replica (Rees-Jones & Skowronek, 2018). The task is removed for the person only when the person can see that it is gone, and that seeing is a representational matter.

### 7.4 Ecological feedback and immediacy

The abstract's third means pairs two sources that support two different things, and the paper states them as two conditions. Shneiderman's (1983) third principle is "rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose impact on the object of interest is immediately visible" (p. 64), with the benefit that "users can immediately see if their actions are furthering their goals" (p. 65). Hutchins et al. (1985) explain why: "the feeling of directness results from the commitment of fewer cognitive resources" (p. 317), and rapid feedback lets the user "make use of perceptual faculties in evaluating the outcome of actions" (pp. 333–334); their water-tank display, which shows a rate rather than a level the user would otherwise compute (p. 323), is the scribal move on the evaluation side of an interface. This is *immediacy*, a property of an interface's response to the user's own action. Kahneman and Klein (2009) name two necessary conditions for trustworthy intuition, "an environment of sufficiently high validity and adequate opportunity to practice the skill" (p. 520), where validity "describes the causal and statistical structure of the relevant environment" (p. 520), and summarize that learning needs "prolonged practice and feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (p. 524). This is *validity*, a property of the environment, with rapidity as one conjoined clause of the practice condition. Hogarth et al. (2015) give the fuller classification: a kind environment's feedback "links outcomes directly to the appropriate actions or judgments and is both accurate and plentiful"; in a wicked one it is "poor, misleading, or even missing" (p. 379). The two conditions coincide only where the interface is the environment, which is the dyad the abstract set aside.

Immediacy on its own is not a learning principle, and the paper does not claim it as one. Schmidt and Bjork (1992) report that feedback after every trial gave the best practice and the worst two-day retention, because "frequent feedback comes to be a part of the task" and blocks the learner's own error detection (pp. 212–213). McKay et al. (2022), across 61 papers (k = 75, N = 2,228), found the effect of reduced feedback frequency undetectable either way, g = 0.19 at acquisition and 0.01 at immediate retention, and advised scientists to "abstain from making substantive recommendations regarding feedback frequency" (pp. 32, 35). In the field, Casas-Arce et al. (2017) randomized about 800 repair professionals to weekly or monthly, detailed or aggregate customer-score feedback: monthly-detailed did best, weekly groups did no better than the control, and the mechanism was salience of the last report (pp. 23–33). Gneezy and Potters (1997) found that subjects given feedback every round bet 50.5% of their endowment against 67.4% for those given it every three rounds, and earned less (pp. 639–640). More frequent return of a counterpart's judgment through an intermediary made things worse in the one field case that resembles a rating system.

Validity is the condition the triad threatens, and the games literature says how. Bereby-Meyer and Roth (2006) ran ten-period prisoner's dilemmas in which the partner's action was always visible; with deterministic payoffs first-period cooperation rose from 0.61 to 0.93 over 20 supergames, and with the same payoffs paid as lotteries it stayed at 0.40 to 0.48 (pp. 1035–1037), because "the game provides a feedback loop between what players learn and what there is to learn" (p. 1040). Noise in what comes back is enough to stop two people learning to cooperate even when each can see what the other did. Burton-Chellew and West (2021) give the quantity a mediator controls: *influence*, the share of a person's payoff range under the person's own control (eq. 1, p. 4). With 43% influence, contributions in a black-box public-goods game fell from 46% to 15% in 16 rounds; with 11–12% they finished "at just over 50%, indicative of no learning on average" (p. 6); across 237 published games with 17,940 participants, influence predicted the rate of learning (p. 6). Hogarth and Soyer (2011) show that experiencing simulated outcomes lifted sophisticated participants on a Bayesian task from 17% correct analytically to 97% (pp. 15–16), and left "little or no difference in accuracy" between sophisticated and naïve participants who experienced them (p. 24). People learn by acting and reading the return, provided the return tracks the act. A mediator that pools counterparts and optimizes on its own account lowers the share of the return that the act explains, and Hogarth et al. (2015) place an environment that reacts to the person's own actions, or changes over time, among the wicked cases unless "the nature of the dynamic change can be inferred" (p. 382). "Ecological feedback" in a triad therefore means a visible correlation between the person's act and what comes back, which the mediator can raise or lower by design. Immediacy serves it and does not substitute for it.

## 8. Candidate Design Changes and the Test for a Smoking Gun

### 8.1 What a smoking gun would have to do

The precedent fixes four properties. The change discloses nothing new about the mediator; the rule may stay as opaque as before. It trains nobody; the counterpart does not have to learn the mediator to benefit. It moves a piece of work from the person into the artifact, and the person can see that the work is gone. And it holds, because the convention is shared or enforced rather than granted. The three means of §7 say which work can move: contingent reasoning about the other party into the rule (§7.3), the reading of a response from inference to perception (§7.2, §7.4), and the return made to track the act closely enough to learn from (§7.4). Four candidates meet at least three of the four properties.

**Candidate 1: a rule that removes the contingency and shows it gone.** Uber's additive surge is the documented case: under multiplicative surge a driver's best response depended on the rider-side demand process through surge transitions, and the additive rule made that dependence irrelevant to the choice (Garg & Nazerzadeh, 2022, pp. 10–13, 22). Boston's deferred acceptance is the other (Abdulkadiroğlu et al., 2006). Both change the rule, neither explains it, and both leave a residue when the person cannot see that the task is gone (Hassidim et al., 2021; Shorrer & Sóvágó, 2023). The design implication is the one the designers drew: "the advantages of strategy proofness would be lost if it is not effectively communicated" (Abdulkadiroğlu et al., 2006, p. 27), and what communicates it is not a description but the form of the decision.

**Candidate 2: a pick-from-a-menu form with step-by-step return.** This is where the measured gains in market design sit. Dynamic deferred acceptance raised truth-telling over the static form, a gain Hakimov and Kübler (2021) attribute to "the feedback it provides after every step ... and the possibility to re-strategize" (p. 468); posting tentative cut-offs after each step raised it further, and continuous feedback on the would-be allocation raised stable outcomes from 83% to "almost 99% of markets" (p. 469). Bó and Hakimov's (2024) "pick your best feasible object and wait to see if you can keep it" (p. 3) and Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch's (2022) clock and drop-outs are the same device. The person acts, sees the provisional result, and acts again, with the contingency resolved one step at a time. This is the scribal move applied to a sequence: the mediator writes the boundary between settled and open, and the person reads it rather than inferring it.

**Candidate 3: a return that tracks the act.** Burton-Chellew and West's (2021) influence measure names a design variable: the share of the outcome under the person's own control, which a mediator can raise by holding other inputs still long enough for the act's effect to show, or lower by pooling and re-optimizing between the act and the return. Rahman's (2019) unknown update times and hidden private inputs (pp. 94, 96–100) are the negative case; Heiland's (2025) corrected rider theories, produced by controlled variation, are the positive one. The candidate is a convention on timing and attribution, not on content: the mediator returns the result of a decision at a known time, attributable to the decision, and holds the rule still between returns. Casas-Arce et al. (2017) warn that returning it more often is not the same as returning it more attributably.

**Candidate 4: a representation that shows the strategic structure without stating it.** Chou et al. (2009) recast a two-person guessing game as placing troops on a hill and raised dominant-strategy play in a community-college pool from 0% to 46%, with "no changes in incentives, information or subject pool," while a one-sentence hint that disclosed the structure moved Caltech subjects from 46% to 87% and college subjects only from 0% to 7% (Tables 4–5, pp. 5–7, 16). Re-representation helped those whom disclosure did not. Subjects "either perceive the game form correctly or do not" (pp. 7–8), which is the strongest evidence in the record that a single presentational change can flip behavior rather than nudge it. Against it, Crosetto and Mantovani (2018) found that making payoffs less accessible in a centipede game shifted stopping by two-thirds of a node, in a direction that depends on the baseline game (pp. 5–9), and Hollan et al. (2000) record an airspeed-tape redesign that "inadvertently removed" the perceptual property pilots used (p. 7). The same kind of change can run either way, and it can be lost without notice.

### 8.2 What evidence would count

No convention of any of these four kinds has been tested in a triad with a behavioral outcome. Every measured effect above comes from a person and a system, from two players with no mediator, or from a clearinghouse with no objective of its own. That gap is the paper's main finding about the literature and the first item of its agenda. In the manner of Terveen and McDonald (2005), whose claims were "intuitions about what is most likely to be true" with research questions attached (p. 414), I state four claims so that data could count against them.

*Claim 1. In a modeled triad with an optimizing mediator, a convention that removes a contingency from the counterpart's decision (Candidate 1 or 2) will reduce the share of dominated choices more than a full description of the mediator's rule will, and the description will raise strategizing in a subsample.* The dyadic and clearinghouse evidence predicts both halves (Danz et al., 2022; Hakimov & Kübler, 2021; Chou et al., 2009). Data that would count against it: a triad in which the disclosed rule lowers dominated choices and the rule change does not. The design is a laboratory market with two populations and an experimenter-set mediator objective, the convention varied between arms and the rule held fixed (cf. Hohenstein et al., 2023).

*Claim 2. The gain from a step-by-step return (Candidate 2) will be carried by what the counterpart sees, not by the incentive property of the rule.* Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch (2022) and Bó and Hakimov (2024) predict it. Against it: an arm in which the same visible steps are shown under a manipulable rule and the gain vanishes.

*Claim 3. Learning in the triad will track the counterpart's influence over the return (Candidate 3) and not the frequency of the return.* Burton-Chellew and West (2021), Bereby-Meyer and Roth (2006) and Casas-Arce et al. (2017) predict it. Against it: a triad in which more frequent returns at fixed influence raise learning, or higher influence at fixed frequency does not.

*Claim 4. A triad's irreducibility, gauged by Φ on the modeled system with the measure, coding, lag and partition fixed in advance, will predict which conventions help: where the mediator's objective makes the system irreducible, conventions that raise influence will help more than conventions that remove contingency, because the contingency cannot be removed without changing what the mediator optimizes.* This claim joins the lab's instrument to the design question. Engel and Malone (2018) show that a Φ-family measure can be computed on coded human interaction and can track an outcome, and also show the choices a pre-specification has to close (lag chosen by maximum Φ, p. 5; low-variance nodes dropped until a valid value appeared, p. 13). Against it: a triad with high Φ in which removal of contingency helps as much as, or more than, raised influence. Φ here classifies the triad; it does not by itself show that a convention helps the people in it, and the two measures are complements.

### 8.3 Who would make the change

The last property, that the convention holds, is the one the triad makes hardest. Every strategy-proof or obviously-strategy-proof result above comes from a clearinghouse or an experimenter with no stake. Where the mediator has a stake, the picture changes. A strategy-proof direct mechanism hands an auctioneer the information to cheat, so "the first-price auction is the unique credible static mechanism" and "the ascending auction is the unique credible strategy-proof mechanism" (Akbarpour & Li, 2020, pp. 425, 428). eBay told users to "bid the absolute maximum they are willing to pay" and let proxy bidding work (Ockenfels & Roth, 2006, p. 298, n. 1), and users sniped anyway: 13% of last bids on eBay came in the last five minutes against 1% on Amazon, whose soft close removed the advantage, and experience raised late bidding on eBay and lowered it on Amazon (pp. 313–315). Google moved its display inventory to a unified first-price auction in 2019 because layered auctions had "driven our publishers and app developers to implement increasingly complicated ad monetization strategies," and told buyers and sellers that they "will need to ... adjust how they bid" (Cox, 2019). The mediator's interest decided the rule each time. A platform's own terms gave "broad, unfettered discretion" in all fourteen that Suzor (2018) read, and a European business user gets fifteen days' notice of a change (Regulation (EU) 2019/1150, Art. 3). In courts an interested judge is barred (*Tumey v. Ohio*, 1927); in dealer foreign exchange the Global FX Committee asks that substantial changes "be agreed before any transaction" (Global Foreign Exchange Committee, 2024, Principle 40), voluntarily. The paper does not name who should hold a triad's convention. What the record shows is mixed: courts and the European regulation moved the convention toward the weaker party under outside force, while Google's first-price auction, Uber's additive surge and Boston's change came on the mediator's or its designers' own initiative; the author's ruling leaves the holder open. The agenda therefore includes a second question beside the laboratory one: under what condition does an optimizing mediator adopt a convention that lowers its counterparts' strategic load, and whether the condition is credibility, as Akbarpour and Li's result suggests, statute, or collective action by the counterparts themselves.

## 9. Scope and Limits

The paper is a framework and reports no data. Its claims about the triad rest on sociological, economic and HCI studies of marketplace platforms, and on laboratory results from dyads and clearinghouses; the step from those to a triad with an optimizing mediator is the inference §8.2 is written to test. Several of the sources behind the three means were read in working-paper or preprint versions, among them Li (2017), Martínez-Marquina et al. (2019), Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch (2022) and Hogarth and Soyer (2011), and their locators are to those versions. Saenger (1997) was read through its chapter records, two reviews and earlier extracts rather than in full, and the one sentence the paper quotes from it (p. 85) should be checked against a library copy before submission. The historical argument stands on a narrower base than the abstract states: the Insular dating and the learner's motive are supported; the claim that unspaced text forced reading aloud is contested by classicists and contradicted by reading science for fluent readers; and no source credits word separation with spreading literacy. The paper therefore carries the precedent as a guide to the shape of an intervention and not as a causal history. "Irreducible" is used in the senses the platform studies support, and Φ is named as the instrument that would make the word measurable on a modeled triad; it has not been computed on one. Two sources that bear on the argument could not be opened and are listed in the notes; both should be read before submission.

## 10. Conclusion

Human–machine interaction has carried the same human role through every change of medium: the person reads what the system sends and checks the machine. The role comes from a pair, an operator and an aid, and the field's own evidence says it degrades with time, with reliability and with the amount handed over, while the one protective factor is that the person keep deciding. On a platform the pair is the wrong picture. Two people coordinate through a mediator that settles their match and gains from how it settles it. The remedy that law and HCI reach for, disclosure, hands the counterpart a text. The platform studies show that a disclosed rule redirects strategizing and does not remove it, because the work the rule leaves in place is reaching the other person; the regulator's own guidelines say the disclosure exists so that businesses can invest in ranking strategies.

Seventh-century Irish scribes made a change of a different kind. They put the word boundary on the page, where any visible mark will serve, and moved a segmentation task from the reader into the artifact without disclosing anything and without training anyone. Read in human-factors terms, that is offloading built in by design and a representational effect, and it carries the costs of dependence that every such move carries. The paper takes the shape and not the history: the record does not support the claim that the change freed literacy or ended voiced reading, and the paper does not need it to.

The capacity a triad calls for is algorithmacy, deciding well through a mediator one has not read. Three means could free it from literacy, and the evidence sorts them. Epistemic action is the dyad's own lever, and in a triad it is acting to see a moving answer. Removing strategic work by rule is real and measured, and what does the removing is the shape of the decision and what the person sees, not the incentive label. Feedback helps when it is valid, when the return tracks the act, and immediacy serves that condition rather than substituting for it. Four candidate conventions follow, and none has been tested where it matters. The next step is a modeled triad with an optimizing mediator, the conventions varied between arms, the rule held still, and Φ fixed in advance as the gauge of what the mediator has made irreducible. The field will know it has found a smoking gun when a counterpart who has never read the mediator reaches the other person better, and can see on the surface that the work has gone.

### (1) Fact flags on the abstract, each with the correct value, its source and locator

1. **"the system still sends a person something to read so they can check the machine" (factory headset).** Narrower than the field's own purpose. On the industrial headset the system mostly sends instructions, warnings and status; checking the robot is one use among several. De Moura Costa et al. (2022), pp. 27–29, 45 (`demouracosta2022augmented`); Quandt & Freitag (2021), abstract and p. 14 (`quandt2021systematic`). The audit-log half of the sentence is supported (Shneiderman 2020, pp. 3–4; Enqvist 2023, pp. 517–525; Koulu 2020; Nadella P15). Body §2.1 states the narrower form.
2. **"That approach assumes a dyad" (Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000).** Supported for the supervisory lineage and the HCI decision-support studies (`parasuraman2000types` p. 287; `wiener1980flight` p. 1; `onnasch2014human`; `lai2023towards` §4.2.4; 45 of 76 studies in `oneill2022human`). Two caveats: the model's own ATC example involves a controller and a pilot (p. 293); and for Shneiderman (2020, p. 5) the dyad is a chosen position ("not teammates"), not an unexamined assumption. Body §3.1 and §3.3.
3. **"irreducible algorithmic triad."** "Three parties, one with its own objective" is supported (`stark2020algorithmic` pp. 53–54; `vallas2020what` 16.10; `rochet2006two`; `hagiu2015multi`). "Irreducible" has no measured sense in the sources; the defensible senses are Simmel's formal one (pp. 138, 141, 145), Rochet–Tirole's non-neutrality (pp. 6, 8, 11) and Castillo et al.'s system feedback (2017 draft, pp. 5–8, 13–14). No source computes or proposes Φ on a mediated triad; Engel & Malone (2018) estimated observational Φ on peer groups with no mediator (p. 11). Counterparts can ally against or exit the mediator (`vallas2020what` 16.7; `cruz2023digital` p. 3). The triad is a property of the marketplace form, not of algorithms as such (`stark2020algorithmic` p. 54 n. 13). Body §4.1–4.3.
4. **"binds and optimizes."** "Optimizes" supported; "binds" supported in a narrower form, since binding strength varies (Uber sets match and both prices; Amazon picks a default seller; Airbnb decides visibility while the host prices; freelance platforms rank and filter), and by Hagiu & Wright's definition (pp. 5, 21) a mediator controlling key terms is a hybrid, not a pure platform. Body §4.2.
5. **"Mandated disclosure (Ben-Shahar & Schneider, 2011) does not spare them from having to game the mediator to coordinate with the other person."** Ben-Shahar & Schneider's evidence is consumer and patient disclosure to individuals, pre-platform, with no triad (`benshahar2011failure` pp. 651, 665, 743–749). Disclosure evidence is mixed in direction: null or harm in `jhaver2019suspect`, `seira2017are`, `poursabzi2021manipulating`, `danz2022belief`; gains in `kizilcec2016how` (trust, self-report), `masuda2022net` (+23.8 points), `gonczarowski2024describing`, `matsuo2026educational` (1 of 15 vs 7 of 15), `gilbert2020optimal` (p. 28); `wang2023algorithmic` finds a region where transparency Pareto-dominates; `cofone2019strategic` (pp. 655–663) shows disclosed strong proxies are not gamed. "To coordinate with the other person" is supported by description, not by a test; no study isolates a coordination cost or measures the other party's loss. "Game" is the platform's moral label (`petre2019gaming`). Counterparts can game the mediator *by* coordinating with each other (`vallas2020what` 16.7). Body §5.
6. **"seventh-century Irish scribes ... broke up scriptio continua by adding spaces between words (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997)."** Supported, with narrowings: the same scribes wrote Old Irish in stress-groups (Thurneysen 1946 p. 24, via `doyle2025assessment`; `tunbridge1992study` pp. 89–92); separation was a learner's analysis of Latin and came in a bundle with points, hyphens and letter forms (Tunbridge pp. 165–175; Saenger p. 85 names four devices); Latin had used interpuncts before the second century AD (`heilmann2020reading` pp. 182–183; `busch2002lautes` p. 19). Tunbridge was supervised by Parkes and is no independent test of Saenger's chronology. Body §6.1.
7. **"Unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line just to hear where each word ended."** Cuts against as stated. Silent reading of unspaced text is attested in classical Athens (`knox1968silent` pp. 432–435); Heilmann (2020) calls the consensus a "quasi-dogma" (p. 179) and reads the laboured-reading passages as about learners (p. 181 n. 28); Balogh (1927) locates the difficulty in whole-word recognition and practice (pp. 227–228); Hendrickson (1929): "only strange, not necessarily difficult" (p. 186). Reading science: skilled readers of unspaced Chinese and Thai segment silently (`li2014reading`; `bai2008reading` 5,239 vs 5,221 ms; `kasisopa2016child` pp. 12–13); the eye leads the voice by 9.7–16.2 letters, ~561 ms (`laubrock2015eyevoice`); speech does not mark word ends (`matzinger2021influence`); word boundaries are partly a literate convention (`barton1980awareness`). Saenger's oralization thesis is contested at every load-bearing point (`odonnell2000saenger` ¶4; Heilmann pp. 178–183). Narrower form supported: for an unpractised reader of a foreign language, voicing gave a second channel for matching a sounded string to a known word (`busch2002lautes` p. 20; Vai readers via `scribner1981psychology`). Body §6.2.
8. **"scaffolding literacy with oracy."** "Oracy" is an education term for speaking-and-listening competence; none of the sources read uses it for voiced reading (facet W Q4). The sources say "reading aloud," "voiced reading" or "oralization." The term's origin (Wilkinson 1965) was reached at abstract level only and is therefore not cited in the body.
9. **"word separation freed literacy."** No historian of literacy read for the paper credits spacing with raising the number of readers; Saenger p. x: the book "is not a general history of literacy"; Balogh (p. 236) and Hendrickson (pp. 193–194) credit the printing press with the end of voiced reading; facet C records spacing standard c. 1050 and English male literacy about 20% in 1558. Narrower form: separation removed a segmentation cost for readers of Latin as a foreign language and for any reader of a spaced script deprived of the cue (33–69%: `winskel2009eye` p. 344; `perea2015colours` Table 2). Body §6.2 and §6.4.
10. **"moving boundary detection from the voice to the page."** "To the page" supported, with the narrowing that any salient mark serves (alternating colour recovers all but 4.7% of the cost: `perea2015colours` p. 12). "From the voice" cuts against for fluent readers (sources in flag 7) and is untested for learners. Body §6.2.
11. **"exploiting a representational effect (Zhang & Norman, 1994)."** Supported as an analogy; the construct is defined over isomorphs that carry identical information (p. 88) and over rules enforced by physics or shared cultural knowledge (p. 106). Where an unspaced string has two lawful parses, the space adds information. Nothing in Zhang & Norman concerns text. Body §6.3.
12. **"that enabled readers to internalize text."** Cuts against if it means reading without inner speech: skilled silent readers of spaced text generate phonological codes within 80–125 ms and move the articulators on hard text (`leinenger2014phonological`). Cuts against if it means retention: what is offloaded is practised and retained less (`grinschgl2021consequences` Table 1; `sparrow2011google` pp. 777–778). No source defines a third sense. Body §6.2 says the sentence has no source behind it.
13. **"the kind of cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)."** The definition (p. 2) requires the person's own physical action; only Box 3 (p. 33) extends the term to offloading "built-in" by design. The body cites Box 3. Body §6.3.
14. **"epistemic action (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994)."** The 1994 paper ties the gain to actions whose local effects are "totally determinate," with no "other agents" (p. 543), which excludes a triad as written. `kirsh2010thinking` p. 453 supplies the bridge (outcomes depending on n participants must be run to be known); `kayongo2022visualization` shows that following a shared chart fell by a third once public (odds 0.67). Defensible form: "act to know," not "act and the answer holds." Body §7.2.
15. **"strategic offloading (Li, 2017)."** The phrase is the author's. Li's term is *obviously strategy-proof*; he does not use "offloading" (`li2017obviously`; `li2024designing` p. 3). "Strategic offloading" has a prior printed meaning in cognitive psychology, a person's own reminder-setting (`gilbert2015strategic`, N = 1,196). Further: the OSP label has not survived as the active ingredient (Breitmoser & Schweighofer-Kodritsch 2022: 49% / 12% n.s. / almost 40%, pp. 3–4; Bó & Hakimov 2024 Table 4: 93% vs 56%; Li 2024 fnn. 5, 8); 11–23% keep strategising under strategy-proof rules in the field (`hassidim2021limits`; `shorrer2023dominated`; `reesjones2018experimental`); every OSP result comes from a mediator with no stake, and interested mediators chose otherwise (`akbarpour2020credible` pp. 425, 428; `ockenfels2006late` pp. 298, 313–315; `cox2019simplifying`). Body §7.3 and §8.3 attribute the mechanism, not the phrase, to Li.
16. **"ecological feedback immediacy (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009)."** The two sources support two different things: Shneiderman's "immediately visible" is a property of an interface's response to the user's own action (pp. 64–65; `hutchins1985direct` pp. 317, 333–334), with predictability as a stated condition (1983 p. 62; `shneiderman1997direct` pp. 35–36); Kahneman & Klein's condition is environmental validity (p. 520) with feedback "both rapid and unequivocal" as one conjoined clause (p. 524). Immediacy as such is not a learning principle: frequent feedback helped practice and hurt retention (`schmidt1992new` pp. 212–213), the meta-analytic effect is undetectable (`mckay2022meta`, g ≈ 0.0–0.2), immediate vs delayed feedback across 38 classes was 0.002 (`fyfe2021manyclasses`), weekly feedback did worse than monthly-detailed (`casasarce2017performance` pp. 23–33), and more frequent feedback lowered bets and earnings (`gneezy1997experiment` pp. 639–640). Hogarth et al. (2015) place a reactive, nonstationary environment among the wicked cases (pp. 380, 382). The design tradition the abstract cites proposed disclosure of the model as its remedy for adaptive systems (`shneiderman1997maes` p. 58), which the author's D3 classifies as a literacy intervention. Body §7.4 states two conditions.
17. **"Human–machine integration (Grahn, Wang, Wang, & Wang, 2026)."** Supported; the review's own remedies are interpretable outputs, explicability and "civic AI literacy" (pp. 5, 13, 16), which puts it on the literacy side of the paper's distinction. Body §2.1.
18. **Nadella (2026).** Read as an enterprise-security proposal: "we" is the deployer throughout; "user," "customer," "employee" do not occur; model diversity (P14), independent controls (P17) and containment (P19) disclose nothing, and P9 calls chain-of-thought transparency "not sufficient or dependable," so "his only move is disclosure" (ruling D4) does not match the text. The critique in the body rests on the human seat (reader of records, holder of a brake) and on the post's silence about the people the model acts upon, not on a claim that people review records badly (CERT practice endorses such review; `kutasov2025shade` gives one AUC 0.65 measure). Body §2.2.

### (2) Where the body narrows a claim the abstract makes

- §2.1: the headset sends instructions, warnings and status; "to check the machine" fits the audit log better.
- §3.1–3.3: the dyad is the supervisory lineage's unit of analysis and, for Shneiderman (2020), a chosen position; the ATC example has several people.
- §4.1: the triad is a property of the marketplace form, not of algorithms as such; the labor-process frame (Kellogg et al. 2020) dissents.
- §4.2: binding varies by platform; the triad meant is the hybrid; counterparts can exit or ally.
- §4.3: "irreducible" is used in the senses the platform cases support (system feedback, non-neutrality); Φ is named as the instrument, not reported as applied.
- §5.1: Ben-Shahar & Schneider establish that handing a person a text is weak; they establish nothing about gaming a mediator.
- §5.2: the claim holds for ranking rules built on cheap, mutable signals, not for every disclosed rule (Cofone & Strandburg; Wang et al.).
- §5.3: "to coordinate with the other person" is supported by description, not by a test; "gaming" is the platform's label; coordinating with the counterpart can be the way the mediator is gamed.
- §5.4: disclosure helped where it was a short property statement, a reason attached to a cue, or computed advice; none in a triad with a behavioural outcome.
- §6.1: the scribes' move was a learner's analysis of a foreign language, one device in a bundle, and a return to an older Latin practice.
- §6.2: the "sound out the line to hear where each word ended" account is contested by classicists and contradicted by reading science for fluent readers; the narrower learner's-scaffold form is stated; "oracy" is flagged as a new sense; "internalize text" is stated to have no source behind it.
- §6.3: the representational effect is applied as an analogy with its isomorphism and cultural-convention conditions stated; offloading is cited through Box 3; the costs of offloading are reported.
- §6.4: the precedent is carried as a guide to the shape of an intervention, not as a causal history of literacy or of silent reading.
- §7.1: "pilots don't read the situation" is replaced by "people correct what they do far more often than what they watch"; Weick's trade-off is kept beside "think by acting."
- §7.2: epistemic action in a triad is "act to know," not "act and the answer holds."
- §7.3: "strategic offloading" is attributed to the author as a label; the OSP label is not the active ingredient; the residue of strategising is reported.
- §7.4: "ecological feedback immediacy" is split into validity (Kahneman & Klein; Hogarth et al.) and immediacy (Shneiderman; Hutchins et al.); immediacy is denied as a learning principle on its own; the triad is placed among the wicked cases unless the dynamics can be inferred.
- §8.2: no convention has been tested in a triad with a behavioural outcome; the claims are stated so data can count against them.
- §8.3: interested mediators have not adopted conventions that lower counterparts' strategic load; the holder of the convention is left open (ruling D8).
- §9: working-paper versions, the unread Saenger, and the two unreached sources are stated.
