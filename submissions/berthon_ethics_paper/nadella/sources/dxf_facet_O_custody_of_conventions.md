# O — Custody of conventions: who has held and enforced them, and what binds the interface owner

*Facet O, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_10.md); WRONG and OVERSTATED rows corrected in place.*

**What this facet covers.** The outline's section 2 says "scribes and readers shared mutual communicative interests" (the same idea is in `DRAFT.md:166`: "Environmental conventions like word separation spread because scribes and readers shared a common linguistic community"). Section 7 asks who can hold a convention against the party that owns the interface. Facets J and A hold courts, dealer foreign exchange, platform terms of service, the DSA and P2B in part, and the record of the page. I look for custody literatures they do not hold: (Q1) who held and enforced the written page in the Carolingian reform; (Q2) standards bodies, accessibility law, the browser padlock and mandated labels; (Q3) platform-governance scholarship, information fiduciaries and audit regimes; (Q4) statute and bargaining on algorithmic management (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, Spain's Royal Decree-Law 9/2021, German co-determination, collective agreements); (Q5) Ostrom's design principles and worker data collectives.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Which custody arrangements give the weaker party something other than information, and which of them bind the interface owner so that it cannot change the convention alone? Short answer, argued in (c): very few. The Carolingian page was held by the ruler and the church, not by a scribe-reader community, so the outline's sentence has no support in the sources I opened. Standards bodies write conventions that owners adopt or drop at will; Chrome removed its "Secure" label and HTTPS scheme in 2018 on Google's own schedule (a second change, the 2023 lock icon, rests on press only [unverified: see AUDIT_10]). Among the legal instruments, only three features bind an owner against its own wish: a mandated format (labels, DSA Arts. 25 and 38), a rule that a human takes the decision and a decision that infringes the worker's rights is rectified within two weeks or compensated (Directive 2024/2831 Arts. 10(5), 11(3); the Directive has been in force since 1 December 2024, and national transposition is due on 2 December 2026), and a works council's co-determination right with a binding arbitration body (BetrVG §87(1) no. 6 and (2)). The one study I found of works councils in platform work (a single-case thesis, abstract only; the PDF is embargoed until 1 June 2029) finds co-determination "excluded from the core of the algorithmic work organisation"; the abstract does not name § 87(1) no. 6.

### Q1. Who held and enforced the conventions of the written page

**charlemagne1900letter** — Charlemagne, letter to Abbot Baugulf of Fulda ("De litteris colendis"), c. 780–800; Boretius no. 29, p. 78; English by D. C. Munro in *Translations and Reprints from the Original Sources of European History*, vol. 6, no. 5, pp. 12–14 (Philadelphia, 1900); web text in the Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook. *Verification:* page opened; the source line names Boretius, Munro and the series. *Read status:* read in full (translation, not the Latin).
- The ruler states the aim as correct speech: "those who desire to please God by living rightly should not neglect to please him also by speaking correctly."
- The letter judges the monasteries' own writing: "we have recognized in most of these letters both correct thoughts and uncouth expressions; because what pious devotion dictated faithfully to the mind, the tongue, uneducated on account of the neglect of study, was not able to express in the letter without error."
- The stake is Scripture: "as the skill in writing was less, so also the wisdom for understanding the Holy Scriptures might be much less than it rightly ought to be."
- Enforcement is a request backed by favour: "Do not neglect, therefore, if you wish to have our favor, to send copies of this letter to all your suffragans and fellow-bishops and to all the monasteries."
- *Bearing.* The party that fixes the standard of correctness is the king, writing to institutions that are to be "the especial servants of truth". The letter says nothing of a shared interest between the writing monk and a reader. It says the king found the product wanting. Complicates the outline's sentence.

**boretius1883capitularia** — Boretius, A. (ed.) (1883). *Capitularia regum Francorum*, vol. 1 (MGH, Legum sectio II). No. 22, *Admonitio generalis* (23 March 789). *Verification:* archive.org item `capitulariaregum01bore`, OCR text; the heading "22. Admonitio generalis. 789. Mart. 23" and chapter 72 located in the OCR. *Read status:* read in part (c. 72 and the lines around it; OCR, with c/e confusions in the apparatus only).
- c. 72, p. 60 (chapter opens p. 59): "Et ut scolae legentium puerorum fiant. Psalmos, notas, cantus, compotum, grammaticam per singula monasteria vel episcopia et libros catholicos bene emendate; quia saepe, dum bene aliqui Deum rogare cupiunt, sed per inemendatos libros male rogant. Et pueros vestros non sinite eos vel legendo vel scribendo corrumpere; et si opus est euangelium, psalterium et missale scribere, perfectae aetatis homines scribant cum omni diligentia."
- My gloss (agent's, not a translation of record): the king orders schools and correct books in every monastery and bishopric, because people who want to pray well "pray badly through uncorrected books"; the boys must not spoil texts by reading or writing, and a gospel book, psalter or missal is to be copied by grown men with all care.
- *Bearing.* The text addresses the clergy as subordinates ("vestros") and names a defect (error in the book) and a victim (the person praying). The scribe appears as a worker to be supervised by age and care, not as a party with an interest of his own. Complicates the outline's sentence. The passage concerns correctness of text, not word separation; no source I opened ties it to spacing.

## (c) Synthesis by question

### Q1. Who held and enforced the conventions of the written page?

In the Carolingian sources I opened, the ruler and the church held the standard and enforced it by order, correction and hierarchy. The king's letter measures the monasteries' own letters against a standard of correct language and asks the abbot, "if you wish to have our favor", to circulate the letter. The *Admonitio generalis* (c. 72) orders schools and "libros catholicos bene emendate" and says that, if a gospel book, psalter or missal has to be copied ("si opus est"), men of full age are to copy it with all care. Alcuin's poem tells scribes to find corrected books and to mark sense units so the reader in church does not read wrongly. Piazzoni ties scripts to abbots and the court. Westwell, writing on the liturgy, finds that uniformity "from above" was not achieved and that local houses chose and edited. Nothing I opened shows scribes and readers sharing interests; the interests named are the king's (correct Scripture, correct prayer), the abbot's (a regulated house) and the church's, with the lector and the person praying as the beneficiaries. Parkes's abstract gives scribes a role: they "developed a complex of new graphic conventions" to make texts intelligible. That supports a narrower statement: the people who did the work also refined the form, inside an institution that wanted correct reading. I found no historian who describes a free exchange between scribes and readers, and I could not quote McKitterick, Ganz or Bischoff, who are the authorities (d). The sources I opened concern correct language and correct books (Charlemagne's letter; *Admonitio generalis* c. 72) and, in Alcuin's poem, the marking of sense units with cola, commata and points so that the lector in church reads correctly. None of the three primary texts or Westwell mentions word separation; only Alcuin ties the matter to reading aloud. Piazzoni describes the Maurdramnus script as having "well-spaced words", as a feature of the script and not as an aim of the reform. Parkes's abstract credits the "grammar of legibility" to Irish and Anglo-Saxon scribes who taught continental pupils; that abstract (and Parkes 1992, facet A), not the court texts, is the nearest lead on word separation.

### Q2. Standards bodies and mandated conventions

The IETF and W3C write conventions in open consensus processes. RFC 2026 calls adherence "voluntary"; the W3C says "Dissenters cannot stop a group's work simply by saying that they cannot live with a decision" (a chair may record a decision over dissent; Formal Objections go to the Advisory Board or TAG). RFC 8890, an informational document of the Internet Architecture Board, says the IAB believes IETF decisions "should favor end users" and concedes that, even when the IETF tries to engage a broader audience, end users' "participation is minimal -- until a change affects someone in a way they don't like". Neither body can bind an implementer, and the one case I opened where an interface owner rewrote a user-facing convention, Chrome's HTTPS indicator, shows the owner acting on its own schedule (the sources do not say whether anyone outside Google was consulted). Google's 2016 paper reports how the lock was read (of 733 respondents describing the green lock, 40.1% gave a connection answer and 13.4% an identity answer, a category that includes correct answers such as "valid certificate"; about 3% of the total wrongly said HTTPS vouched for trustworthiness) in a sample that skews tech-savvy, and the Chromium post of 2018 removes the "Secure" wording on Google's schedule. Standards gain force when a statute or regulation cites them: the DOJ's 2024 rule (a regulation under title II of the ADA) adopted WCAG 2.1 AA for state and local governments, and an interim final rule of 20 April 2026 moved the compliance dates by a year (to 26 April 2027 for entities of 50,000 or more, 26 April 2028 for smaller ones); the EAA gives a presumption of conformity for products and services that meet harmonised standards (Art. 15(1); the Directive itself does not mention WCAG), exempts microenterprise service providers (Art. 4(5)) and requires effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties (Art. 30(2)). Labels have measured effects on the seller as well as the buyer: reformulation (Shangguan, abstract: sodium −8.9%, based on 4 studies, 95% CI −17.3% to −0.6%; artificial trans fat −64.3%, 3 studies), though Shangguan found no significant difference between voluntary and legislative approaches. Ganderats-Fuentes (abstract) reports larger and more consistent reformulation under mandatory front-of-package schemes, and more under labels with intuitive than with numerical information. Hatton 2026 (abstract) found no substantive reformulation after brands adopted the revised Nutrition Facts label (a revision of an existing mandatory label, one supermarket chain, 2016-2019). Energy labels raised the sales proportion of efficient appliances in one Swiss field study (Stadelmann, abstract; no magnitude in the abstract), and the EU label's format can lead people to disregard actual energy consumption (Waechter, abstract). All of the effect sizes are abstract-level.

### Q3. Platform-governance scholarship

Gorwa sorts models into self-, external and co-governance and says that, under self-governance, "voluntary arrangements rely on goodwill and provide limited recourse in the case of non-compliance". Balkin proposes a duty of loyalty enforceable in court and observes that companies can "change their privacy policies more or less at will", so that relying on terms of service "may offer only very limited privacy protections". Khan and Pozen (abstract) fear that the fiduciary framework "invites an enervating complacency toward online platforms' structural power and a premature abandonment of more robust visions of public regulation". Raji and colleagues propose an audit run inside the owner, because outside auditors see only outputs. The one audit regime with statutory force, DSA Art. 37, produced in its first round audits that, on one critic's reading, skipped provisions under investigation and were "not very ambitious". Art. 37(6) lets a platform decline a recommendation if, within one month, it justifies the reasons and sets out any alternative measures. For Facebook, the auditor skipped Art. 25(1) among other provisions (Holznagel). Evidence that any of these models binds a platform: none in the sources I opened beyond the DSA's own text, whose enforcement record (fines, Art. 52) I did not search.

### Q4. Statute and bargaining on algorithmic management

Beyond information, Directive 2024/2831 gives workers: bans on processing emotional-state, private-conversation and off-duty data (Art. 7); a human decision for every restriction, suspension or termination (10(5)); a contact person with authority (11(1)); reasons by the date a decision takes effect (11(1)); correction within two weeks or compensation (11(3)); a duty, where oversight finds a high risk of discrimination or infringed rights, to take the necessary steps, "including, if appropriate, the modification" or discontinuation of the system (10(3)); consultation of the platform workers' representatives, who may be assisted by an expert whose proportionate expenses a platform with more than 250 workers in the Member State pays (13(2)-(3)). Arts. 10(5) and 11 cover every person performing platform work, but Art. 11 does not apply to business users under Regulation 2019/1150 (11(5)), and Art. 13 reaches representatives of platform workers who are employees (Art. 15 limits what representatives of the self-employed may use). Penalties for Arts. 7-11 run through the data-protection authorities and the GDPR fine ceiling (Art. 24(1)). It does not give a veto or a share in design. Consultation is under Directive 2002/14/EC. The Directive entered into force on the twentieth day after publication (Art. 31, 1 December 2024); the transposition deadline is 2 December 2026 (Art. 29(1)), so I found no measured effect. Whether any Member State has already transposed it I did not check [unverified: see AUDIT_10]. Spain's Royal Decree-Law gives the works council information on "parámetros, reglas e instrucciones" and a presumption of employment; the Glovo record shows the status rule enforced and says nothing of the information right. German co-determination is the strongest: § 87(1) no. 6 and (2) mean the employer cannot introduce a monitoring system without agreement or a board ruling. The one platform study I found (Neumann, a single-case PhD thesis, abstract only) says council activity led to improvements in shift planning, health protection and continued pay during illness, and that co-determination remained excluded from "the core of the algorithmic work organisation". Collective agreements I could confirm (Hilfr; Just Eat [unverified: see AUDIT_10]) give an explanation duty and an information right; the Hilfr firm had 38 employees and did not yet use AI.

### Q5. Commons and data collectives

Cox and colleagues find Ostrom's principles "well supported" across 91 studies, strongest for monitors accountable to the appropriators (ratio 11.7). Those studies are of local resource commons whose users monitor each other. A platform's user does not fit that description, and the review does not test interfaces. The only working case I found of a worker-side instrument is the Shipt Calculator, built by researchers with non-profit worker groups: 140 workers contributed pay data, and the authors say the data "is not 'owned' by workers". I found no passage reporting a change by the platform. Data trusts are proposals. Facet J's Seattle ordinance and the 1,400 reactivations won by a drivers' union belong in this comparison but I did not reopen them.

### Q6. Which arrangements give the weaker party more than disclosure, and which bind the owner?

| Arrangement | Gives the weaker party | Binds the owner against its will? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carolingian ruler and church | A corrected book for the reader in church | The ruler could change it; no appeal for the scribe | Primary texts; Westwell on limits of imposition |
| IETF, W3C | A seat at the table | No. Adoption voluntary; owner changes UI alone (Chrome 2018) | RFCs; Chromium post |
| WCAG via ADA rule or EAA | A legal duty for covered bodies | Yes for covered public bodies (ADA rule; dates moved a year) and for covered private suppliers (EAA; microenterprise services exempt) | 91 FR 20902; Directive 2019/882 Arts. 4, 15, 30 |
| Mandated labels | A comparable format; the seller reformulates | Yes, more under mandates | Shangguan; Ganderats-Fuentes (abstracts) |
| Information fiduciary | A court-enforced duty of loyalty (proposed) | In theory; no case found | Balkin (read in part); Khan and Pozen (abstract) |
| Internal audit (Raji) | None directly | No: the owner audits itself | Raji §2.4 |
| DSA Arts. 25, 38 | No deceptive interface (except practices covered by Directive 2005/29/EC or the GDPR, Art. 25(2); not micro or small platforms, Art. 19); a non-profiled option on very large platforms | Yes (fines to 6%, Art. 52(3); Commission fines on very large platforms, Art. 74(1)) | Official text; no enforcement record read |
| DSA Art. 37 audit | A published audit and a justification | Partly: comply or explain (37(6)) | Holznagel (blog) on first round |
| Platform Work Directive Ch. III | Human decision, rectification or compensation in two weeks where rights are infringed, bans, expert for employees' representatives | Yes once national law applies (transposition due 2 December 2026); consultation not agreement | Official text; not yet in force |
| Spanish Rider Law | Information to council; employment presumption | Status rule enforced; information right untested | BOE text; Eurofound record |
| German BetrVG § 87(1) no. 6, (2) | A veto-with-arbitration over monitoring systems | Yes | Statute; Neumann (abstract): kept out of the algorithmic core |
| Collective agreements (Hilfr, Just Eat) | Explanation duty; information right | Only by renegotiation | Secondary summaries |
| Ostrom commons | Self-rule over a shared stock | Not tested on interfaces | Cox 2010 |
| Worker-side data tool (Shipt Calculator) | Evidence for bargaining | No | Calacci 2022 (data not owned by workers) |

The pattern (the agent's inference from the rows above; none of the sources states it, and the sample is fourteen arrangements, not a test): a convention binds the owner only when a third party with a sanction stands behind it (a regulator, a conciliation board, a court), or when the convention is a mandated format the owner cannot alter. The two halves overlap, since a mandated format also depends on a regulator's sanction. Everything the owner can renegotiate, redesign or audit itself is custody in name.

## What the outline should add

*Section 2 (the scribal precedent).*

1. **Replace "scribes and readers shared mutual communicative interests" with a statement about who held the page.** Claim: in the Carolingian reform, the ruler and the church fixed and enforced the standard of correct writing, and the people named as beneficiaries were the reader in church and the person praying. Sources: Charlemagne's letter to Baugulf (read in full, translation); *Admonitio generalis* c. 72 (read in part, Latin OCR); Alcuin, carm. XCIV (read in part, OCR). Add Mostert 2016 and Ganz 1995 (abstract only) for who copied and in what numbers. The letter and c. 72 do not mention reading aloud; only Alcuin's poem does.
2. **Qualify the "reform from above" picture.** Claim: even for the liturgy, Westwell (citing McKitterick and Hen) reports local initiative and no imposition of the Roman rite from above. Source: Westwell 2018 (read in part). Note its scope is liturgical texts; it does not discuss word separation.
3. **Keep two claims apart.** (i) The sources concern correct language, correct books and (Alcuin) punctuation for the lector; no source I opened ties the reform to word separation. Piazzoni's "well-spaced words" describes a script, not a reform aim. (ii) The scribes' own practice produced new graphic conventions (Parkes 2016, abstract only, which credits Irish and Anglo-Saxon scribes with teaching the "grammar of legibility"; facet A for Parkes 1992). The outline can say scribes refined conventions; it cannot say they and readers shared a bargain.

*Section 7 (institutional custody).*

4. **Add the test "can the owner change it alone?" and apply it to cases.** Claims: Google's own staff designed and shipped new connection-security indicators (Felt et al. 2016, read in part) and removed the "Secure" label on its own schedule (Chromium 2018, read in full; the sources do not say whether anyone outside Google was consulted); a standard binds through statute or regulation (EAA Arts. 4, 15, read in part; 91 FR 20902, read in part); a works-council right with arbitration binds on paper (BetrVG § 87(1) no. 6 and (2), read in full), and Neumann's single case shows co-determination kept out of the algorithmic core. The sentence in `DRAFT.md:166`, "DXD conventions will never be granted voluntarily", needs this evidence: Google granted one, in its interest.
5. **State what the law gives beyond information, and date it.** Claim: Directive 2024/2831 Arts. 7, 10(3), 10(5), 11(3) and 13 go beyond disclosure; the Directive is in force, but its platform obligations apply only through national law due by 2 December 2026; Art. 10(3) requires "the steps necessary" and names modification or discontinuation only "if appropriate"; Art. 11(3) applies where a decision infringes the worker's rights; and consultation is not co-determination. Source: the Directive (read in part, official text). Contrast the Spanish Rider Law, whose algorithm right is information (BOE text, read in part).
6. **Add mandated formats and the labels evidence, with the disagreement.** Claim: labels change what sellers offer; Ganderats-Fuentes 2023 reports more reformulation under mandatory than voluntary front-of-package schemes, Shangguan 2019 found no significant difference between voluntary and legislative approaches, and Hatton 2026 found no substantive reformulation after the revised Nutrition Facts label (all abstract only; read the full texts before print). DSA Arts. 25 and 38 (read in part) are the interface-design versions of the same idea.
7. **Add the caution that the strongest custody arrangement was kept out of the algorithm.** Claim: platform works councils won rosters and sick pay and were excluded from the core algorithmic system. Source: Neumann 2025 (abstract only; single case; the thesis PDF is embargoed until 1 June 2029, so it cannot be opened before then). The abstract does not say which statutory right the councils used.
8. **Add a short typology and name Gorwa's three models.** Sources: Gorwa 2019 (read in part), Balkin 2016 (read in part), Khan and Pozen 2019 (abstract only), Raji 2020 (read in part), Holznagel 2025 (read in part).
9. **Say the commons evidence does not transfer.** Cox, Arnold and Villamayor Tomás 2010 (read in part): the best-supported principle is monitors accountable to the appropriators, which the platform user lacks.
10. **Cite the one working worker-side tool as information.** Calacci and Pentland 2022 (read in part): built by researchers with worker groups; the data are not owned by workers.

### Claims in the paper's position that these sources contradict or qualify

1. **"Scribes and readers shared mutual communicative interests"** (outline; `DRAFT.md:166` "shared a common linguistic community"). The sources show a ruler and abbots directing scribes to correct books; the beneficiary named is the reader in church. No source I opened shows scribe–reader mutuality. Parkes (abstract) allows scribes a role in refining conventions. Not supported as worded.
2. **"Word separation [and similar conventions] spread because scribes and readers shared a common community."** Nothing I opened tests a cause of spread; the Carolingian sources concern correction of text, not spacing.
3. **"DXD conventions will never be granted voluntarily through corporate design guidelines."** Google designed and shipped new Chrome security indicators and published its user research (Felt et al. 2016, by Google staff); in 2018 it announced removal of the "Secure" label (the Chromium post does not mention the padlock or research). No law is shown to have compelled either; whether the owner acted "voluntarily" in its own interest is the agent's inference. The claim holds where the owner's interest diverges from the user's (Hatton 2026 shows a mandated panel changed little) but "never" is too strong.
4. **"They must be institutionalized through legal frameworks or collective resistance."** The legal frameworks I opened mostly give information and consultation (Rider Law Art. 64.4 d); Directive 2024/2831 goes further but is not in force; the one arrangement that binds on paper (BetrVG § 87(1) no. 6 and (2)) sits beside a single platform case in which co-determination was excluded from the algorithmic core (the abstract does not name § 87). Collective resistance produced evidence, not a changed pay model, in the case I read.
5. **A reading of "the failure of disclosure" (section 4) that treats statute as disclosure.** Directive Arts. 10(5), 11(3) and DSA Arts. 25, 38 are not disclosure. The section-4 argument is on firmer ground against disclosure-only instruments (Nutrition Facts panel, Rider Law information right, Hilfr and Just Eat agreements).
6. **The assumption that the Carolingian reform was imposed uniformly from above.** Westwell finds local initiative in the liturgy. The reform's custodians were many, not one.
7. **That a standards body can serve as holder.** RFC 8890 (IAB) says the IETF has no unique insight into what is good for end users and that end-user participation is often minimal until a change affects them; the W3C lets a chair record a decision over dissent after the group has considered the concerns.
