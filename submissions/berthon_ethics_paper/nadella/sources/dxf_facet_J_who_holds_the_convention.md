# J — Who holds the convention: courts, foreign-exchange markets and platforms

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_05.md); WRONG, OVERSTATED and STITCHED rows corrected in place.*

*Facet J, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The author's ruling D8 (2026-10-13): "we just dont know yet. we can look at how courts and forex markets work, compare that platforms (which i think take a highly draconian position), and look for where the convention bends towards". I compare three settings in which a third party stands between two parties: (1) courts and arbitration, (2) foreign-exchange (FX) dealer markets and their settlement and benchmark infrastructure, (3) digital platforms (social media, app stores, marketplaces, ride-hail). For each I ask who writes the procedural conventions, what the losing party can do, whether the third party is interested and what holds it, what the evidence says about effects, and in whose direction the conventions have moved. The target is premise P10 of `ARGUMENT_MAP.md` (the scribes shared their readers' interest; a platform owner does not) and `T_adjudication.md` §5.1.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Do courts and FX markets hold their conventions in a way platforms do not, and does the record support "a highly draconian position" for platforms? Short answer, argued in (c): partly. Platform terms of service give the owner near-total discretion (Suzor), and until 2019 no statute I could open limited that. But the FX comparison does not flatter FX: the FX Global Code is voluntary, its last-look rule leaves "sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes" with the dealer, and five banks were fined by the FCA for control failures that allowed traders to attempt to manipulate fix rates (the Code was written later by central banks and private-sector participants, and the sources do not say these were the same firms). By one measure, the EU Platform-to-Business Regulation and the Digital Services Act now require platforms by law to give notice, reasons and a complaint route; the Code asks related things of FX dealers by voluntary principle: that the basis for rejecting requests be disclosed (Principle 9), that rejection reasons be recorded and information be available to clients on request (Principles 36, 39), that substantial changes to terms be agreed before any transaction (Principle 40, which is stronger than DSA Art. 14(2), a duty only to inform), and that concerns can be raised through a channel (Principle 7). The Code names no complaint body or sanction. I compared texts, not practice.

- Defines the actors: "We might divide our actors into those claimants who have only occasional recourse to the courts (one-shotters or OS) and repeat players (RP) who are engaged in many similar litigations over time." (p. 97)

### B3. Foreign-exchange and settlement

**globalfxc2024code** — Global Foreign Exchange Committee (footer "Updated December 2024"). *FX Global Code: A set of global principles of good practice in the foreign exchange market.* https://www.globalfxc.org/docs/fx_global.pdf. *Verification:* fetched 2026-10-13, 84 pages; the PDF footer reads "Updated December 2024" (key renamed from `globalfxc2024code`). *Read status:* read in full (card: library/globalfxc2024code.md; December 2024 edition, printed page numbers; earlier editions not read).
- Status: "The Global Code does not impose legal or regulatory obligations on Market Participants nor does it substitute for regulation, but rather it is intended to serve as a supplement to any and all local laws, rules, and regulation" (Foreword I).
- Who holds it: "The Global Code is maintained by the Global Foreign Exchange Committee (GFXC). - Adoption: "The Statement, like the Code, is voluntary" (Foreword II). "the decision of what steps should be undertaken, and in what manner, resides with each Market Participant" (Foreword II).
- Principle 17: "Market Participants employing last look should be transparent regarding its use and provide appropriate disclosures to Clients." "In the context of last look, the Market Participant has sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes, over whether the Client's trade request is accepted or not, leaving the Client with potential market risk in the event the trade request is not accepted." "Last look should not be used for purposes of information gathering with no intention to accept the Client's request to trade." The disclosure the Code asks for is general (whether price changes may affect the decision, the typical decision time, the purpose); I found no requirement to give a client a reason for an individual rejection.

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. Who writes the conventions, who can change them, with what notice?

**Courts.** Written by judges and lawyers under statute. In the federal system the Supreme Court prescribes procedural and evidence rules, which "shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right" (28 U.S.C. §2072); committees of "members of the bench and the professional bar", appointed under the Judicial Conference, recommend; they meet in public unless the committee, in open session and with a majority present, closes all or part of a meeting and states its reason (§2073(a)(2), (c)(1)); meetings need "sufficient notice" (§2073(c)(2)); and they must supply an explanatory note and a written report with minority views (§2073(d)). I found no statutory comment period in the text I opened (there is a duty of notice of meetings, §2073(c)(2)); failure to comply "does not invalidate a rule" (§2073(e)), so the process duties are not enforceable by striking a rule down; Congress's role (§2074) is unread. Arbitration procedures come from the administering body (the AAA in Colvin's data, in cases arising from "employer-promulgated arbitration procedures") or the trade association (the Liverpool Cotton Association in Bernstein 2001).

**FX.** The FX Global Code is written and maintained by the GFXC, "a forum bringing together central banks and private sector participants", established May 2017; it "does not impose legal or regulatory obligations", and adopting it, or a Statement of Commitment, is voluntary (Foreword I-II). The WM/Reuters fix is calculated by the WM Company, which widened its window to five minutes for all currency pairs on 15 February 2015 (from one minute for "traded" and two for "non-traded" currencies), after the FSB group recommended widening it and left the width to WM (FSB 2014, rec. 1; FSB 2015, §2.1). Last-look practice is for each dealer to disclose; the Code asks it to explain at a minimum whether price changes in either direction may affect the decision, the typical decision time and the purpose (Principle 17). The Code does not say how the terms are agreed. Notice of change to clients: the Code asks, voluntarily, that substantial changes to terms be "agreed before any transaction" (Principle 40, p. 42) and that a changed capacity be "agreed by both parties" (Principle 8, p. 12).

**Platforms.** Written and changed by the owner. Apple: "new apps presenting new questions may result in new rules at any time." Suzor (2016) found "broad, unfettered discretion to platform owners" in the terms of all fifteen platforms he examined; his 2018 article repeats the sentence for fourteen platforms (June 2015 data; p. 6) and finds that thirteen of them reserve termination "for any reason (or no reason)" (pp. 5-6). Statute now sets a floor for some users: P2B Art. 3(2) requires a notice period of at least 15 days for business users and voids changes that skip it (Art. 3(3)); DSA Art. 14(2) requires only that recipients be informed of "any significant change", with no period.

### Q3. Is the third party interested, and what holds it?

- **Courts.** The judge is barred by the Constitution from deciding where he has a "direct, personal, substantial pecuniary interest" (*Tumey*), and by statute where "impartiality might reasonably be questioned" (§455(a)). Arbitrators can be challenged for "evident partiality" (9 U.S.C. §10(a)(2)). What holds the court: appeal, recusal, precedent, and an outside body that can review. Galanter's point is that the repeat player still shapes rules through lobbying and litigation selection (pp. 97, 100).
- **FX.** The dealer is the counterparty. In last look "the liquidity provider owns finality of the deal" (Oomen §1), and the Code leaves the dealer "sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes", over acceptance, and bars use of last look for information gathering (Principle 17). What holds it: reputation among repeat dealers (Greif, Milgrom-North-Weingast describe the mechanism for medieval traders), competition among liquidity providers, and regulators. The 2008-2013 record (FCA) and Barclays (NYDFS, whose statements are recitals in a negotiated consent order, not findings) show reputation and competition did not prevent breaches; regulators acted afterwards. The FSB wrote that the structure "can create an opportunity and an incentive for dealers to try to influence the exchange rate – allegedly including by collusion".
- **Platforms.** The owner is an interested party. What holds it: regulation (P2B; the DSA, enforced by regulators with fines up to 6% of worldwide turnover, a complaint route and a damages claim, Arts. 52-54, 74; Seattle), litigation (I saw only news reports of *Epic v. Apple*), reputation and user exit (Klonick names government requests, media coverage and civil-society groups as the forces that move platform rules and treats exit sceptically, pp. 1629-30, 1668), and a self-built body (the Oversight Board, with Meta controlling "the OB's precedent" per Wong and Floridi).
- **Result.** In none of the three settings is the third party uninterested except the court. FX and platforms both rely on outside regulators to hold an interested party. The difference is that FX also has a voluntary code and the platform owner has none beyond its own terms.

### Q4. What does the evidence say about effects?

- **Procedure affects judgments.** In Tyler's 652 interviews, procedural justice had "a major influence on both satisfaction and evaluation" of police and courts (Tyler 1988; the weights of the criteria vary by situation, p. 131). The organisational-justice meta-analysis (Colquitt et al. 2001, 183 studies) extends this to employment.
- **It transfers to platforms.** Tyler et al. (2019) report random assignment of a procedural-justice message reducing later violations [unverified: see AUDIT_05]; Jhaver et al. (2019) find removal explanations are associated with fewer later removals, with bot explanations showing no significant disadvantage against human ones in the removal-odds models. Counter: Vaccaro et al. (2020, per repo note) found no appeal design improved perceived fairness over no appeal. All abstract only.
- **Repeat players win more, and the private forum is not a refuge.** Galanter (1974) states the argument; Colvin (2011) finds a "repeat employer effect": employee win rates and awards are "significantly lower where the employer is involved in multiple arbitration cases" (abstract); in the body, 16.9% against 31.6% (repeat against one-shot employers, p. 19) and 12.0% against 23.4% where the same employer and arbitrator recur (p. 21). Overall the employee win rate was 21.4% in AAA employment arbitration, lower than rates reported for litigation trials (the comparison is not matched, p. 7), and in 97% of cases the employer paid all fees beyond a small filing fee (abstract). Speed in arbitration was higher.
- **Law merchant works under conditions.** Milgrom-North-Weingast and Bernstein describe private judges and trade-association tribunals that work among repeat dealers; the FX record shows the same structure failing where dealers allegedly colluded (FSB 2014).
- **FX last look.** The only quantitative statement I opened is Oomen's model, which argues last look "is not an issue of fairness" and that the trader's effective transaction cost "need not be affected by" the choice of design and protocol, though execution risk differs (§4). The NYDFS order shows misuse in one bank. I found no study of customers' realised costs.
- **Platform workers.** Qualitative: unpredictable criteria (Rahman 2021), soft control (Rosenblat and Stark 2016), platform decay (Maffie and Hurtado 2025). No study I opened measures what a statute-mandated notice or appeal changed for drivers.

### Q5. Test "platforms take a highly draconian position"

Where it holds. (i) On the face of the contract: Suzor (2016) found "broad, unfettered discretion to platform owners" in the terms of all fifteen platforms he examined; his 2018 article repeats the sentence for fourteen platforms (June 2015 data; p. 6), finds that thirteen of them reserve termination "for any reason (or no reason)" (pp. 5-6) and that none sets up an internal dispute process (pp. 7-8). (ii) Rule change: Apple may add rules "at any time"; Rahman records workers facing "unpredictable" criteria. (iii) Appeals: in 2017-18 Facebook allowed an appeal only of the removal of a profile or page (Klonick, p. 1648), and Klonick concluded that "the lack of an appeals system for individual users" and unequal treatment of powerful users showed that fair participation was not a prioritized part of platform moderation (p. 1665); at YouTube and Twitter, broader actions were appealable (p. 1648). She also calls the system "impressively intricate and responsive" (p. 1664), so her complaint is lack of accountability and unequal treatment, not severity. (iv) Scale: in Q1 2022 users submitted an estimated 479,649 cases to the Oversight Board (the Board's preliminary count) and the Board decided two cases (one of them a Meta referral), selected earlier, overturning Meta in both; the ratio compares different cohorts, and Meta had already reversed itself in 14 of the 20 shortlisted user cases (p. 9).

Where it fails or needs qualification.
1. **FX is not a gentler holder.** The Code is voluntary; last look leaves "sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes" with the dealer; the Code requires disclosure of the basis on which requests may be rejected (Principle 9) and a record of the reason for each electronic rejection, shared with clients "where appropriate" and made available "upon request" (Principle 36), with handling information on request (Principle 39); I found no requirement to give a client, unasked, the reason for an individual rejection; Barclays sent clients "NACK", and an internal email said the team "generally does not share this info with the client". Five of the dealers active in the market were fined by the FCA for control failures that allowed traders to attempt to manipulate fix rates; the Code was written later by central banks and private-sector participants (Foreword I), and the sources do not say these were the same firms. Retail customers appear in the record only through regulators (CFTC/FXCM). Oomen, a dealer, argues last look is not unfair.
2. **Courts are costly and slow, and favour repeat players.** Galanter lists "cost and delay barriers" among the haves' advantages. Colvin finds arbitration faster than litigation, with a lower employee win rate and lower awards; this qualifies the case for a private forum, not for court procedure.
3. **Platforms are not uniform and are now regulated.** Apple has an appeal route and restores accounts at its discretion. In the EU, a change to terms that skips the 15-day notice period is void as against a business user (P2B Art. 3(2)-(3)), unless the business user waives the period or an Art. 3(4) exception applies. For online platforms that are not micro or small enterprises (Art. 19), the DSA requires a statement of reasons for restrictions imposed for illegal content or incompatibility with terms (Art. 17), an internal complaint system (Art. 20) and decisions taken "under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means" (Art. 20(6)). Seattle's ordinance (SMC 8.40) requires notice 14 days before most deactivations, with reasons and records, and gives the worker 90 days to challenge through the company, which decides the challenge itself within 14 days (nothing on the model form requires an independent reviewer); the OLS cannot investigate whether the reason was permissible before 1 June 2027. On notice, reasons and complaint, the EU instruments bind platforms by law; the Code asks related things of FX dealers by voluntary principle (reasons and handling information on request, Principles 9, 17, 36, 39; substantial changes to terms agreed before any transaction, Principle 40, which is stronger than DSA Art. 14(2); a channel for concerns, Principle 7). I compared texts, not practice, and did not read the rules that bind dealers by law.
4. **The statutory rights are partial.** Out-of-court DSA bodies cannot bind (Art. 21); reasons duties have exceptions: P2B Art. 4(5) (repeat infringement leading to termination of the whole service; a legal bar on disclosure) and DSA Art. 17(2) ("deceptive high-volume commercial content"; contact details unknown); P2B's internal-complaint and mediation duties do not apply to providers that are small enterprises (Arts. 11(5), 12(7)); DSA Art. 19 exempts micro and small online platforms from Arts. 20-21 unless they are very large platforms; van der Donk finds five marketplaces' terms short of the safeguards the DSA would require (2022, before it applied); Pałka et al. find unfair clauses under EU consumer law in 100 terms of service (abstracts).
5. **Evidence on effect is thin.** Appeal designs did not improve perceived fairness in Vaccaro et al.; explanations were associated with lower odds of later removal in Jhaver et al.; no study I opened measures outcomes under the new EU or Seattle rules.

In courts the convention has bent toward the weaker party through litigation about the decider's interest. In FX it bent toward clients after scandal and regulators, and less through clients themselves. In platforms it is bending toward users through statute and local law, with platform self-regulation (Board, appeals) a partial response; the statutory rights stop short of independent first-instance review (in Seattle the company decides the challenge within 14 days; DSA out-of-court bodies cannot bind), and the Board reaches few cases while Meta controls its precedent. In all three, the movers were outside the third party. I found one setting (merchant tribunals among repeat dealers) where the parties themselves write the convention, and it works because the dealers are on both sides.

### Claims the sources contradict or qualify (author's wording first)

1. **"platforms ... take a highly draconian position"** (D8). Qualified. True of terms-of-service discretion (Suzor 2016 and 2018, "broad, unfettered discretion") and, in the sense of thin appeals and lack of accountability, of 2017 practice (Klonick, pp. 1648, 1665). "Draconian" in the sense of severity is not supported by the sources I opened: neither Suzor nor Klonick uses the word, Suzor's finding is about discretion, and Klonick calls the system "impressively intricate and responsive" (p. 1664). Not true as a blanket statement: Apple's guidelines include an appeal route; the EU P2B and DSA require notice, reasons and complaint; Seattle requires notice and a 90-day challenge. Say "platform terms give the owner near-total discretion unless a statute limits it".
2. **Implication that FX markets show a convention that holds the third party** (D8, "how courts and forex markets work"). Qualified. The FX Global Code "does not impose legal or regulatory obligations" and is voluntary; last look is at the dealer's "sole discretion, based upon the validity and price check processes"; the fix was a target of attempted manipulation by traders at five fined banks (FCA 2014), and the FSB reported allegations of collusion (FSB 2014). FX shows a convention held by dealers and corrected by regulators.
3. **Implication that courts are the model for the weaker party.** Qualified. Galanter (1974) puts "cost and delay barriers" and repeat-player advantage in the courts; Colvin (2011) finds arbitration faster than litigation, with a lower employee win rate and lower awards.
4. **P10 / T §5.1: a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention.** Qualified by two sources. The FX Code can also be declined by any participant (Foreword II), although it asks, voluntarily, that substantial changes to terms be agreed before any transaction (Principle 40). Conversely, in the EU a platform that changes terms must give business users at least 15 days' notice (with exceptions) and the business user may terminate; the owner still decides the change alone. This weakens, but does not refute, "a convention the owner can withdraw alone is no convention". The Apple guidelines ("at any time") are the case where the owner can withdraw alone.
5. **Any claim that platforms lack court-like bodies.** Qualified. The Oversight Board decides cases and its decisions are "binding" (Q1 2022 report), but in Q1 2022 users submitted an estimated 479,649 cases (the Board's preliminary count) and the Board decided two cases (one a Meta referral), selected earlier, so the ratio compares different cohorts, and Meta had already reversed itself in 14 of 20 shortlisted cases before the Board ruled (p. 9); and Meta controls its precedent (Wong and Floridi, abstract).
6. **Any claim that the interested decider is unremarkable.** Contradicted by *Tumey* (1927): a judge with a "direct, personal, substantial pecuniary interest" cannot decide. The legal order already treats interest in the outcome as disqualifying.

### Inferences that are mine and have no source

1. Reading Galanter's repeat player as the platform and the driver or seller as a one-shotter. Galanter's examples are insurers, prosecutors and finance companies, and his note 4 (p. 97) says clients in continuing relationships "are not pure OSs", so the driver or seller fits only with that qualification.
2. That the court's disqualification of interested deciders (*Tumey*, §455) is the right model for the platform case. No source in this facet makes that comparison.
3. That EU platforms are bound by law to give notice, reasons and a complaint route that FX dealers are asked to give only by voluntary principle. 4. That the movers in all three settings were "outside the third party". Each row of the Q6 table is sourced; the generalisation is mine.
5. That a merchant tribunal works because "the dealers are on both sides". Milgrom-North-Weingast and Bernstein abstracts describe repeat dealing; the "both sides" reading is mine.
6. That the current (December 2024) Code's last-look text bends toward clients compared to 2015. I did not read earlier editions.
7. That the answer for the paper is to name no holder (D8). This facet does not choose.
