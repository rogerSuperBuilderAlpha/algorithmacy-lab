## Abstract

Two reviews in the first issue of *Human-Machine Interaction* state where the field stands in 2026. Grahn, Wang, Wang, and Wang (2026) move the subject from interaction to integration, and the remedies they keep are oversight, interpretable outputs, and an education in working with machines. Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) trace the interface from levers to screens to extended reality, and the human in their model still receives information in order to decide. Nadella (2026) offers the enterprise version of that remedy: every meaningful model action should leave "tamper-proof human readable evidence," an unending chain of content. This paper argues that the vision is literacy, carried into a triadic platform. What it asks for is the change that occurred when oracy gave way to literacy: one alteration of the representation that took the finding of a boundary off the ear and put it on the page. Decision Experience Design names two candidates for that change and sets out what would count as finding it.

## 1. Introduction

Grahn, Wang, Wang, and Wang (2026) and Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) review human–machine interaction as it stands. Grahn et al. treat the classical model as a separated operator who monitors discrete levels of automation through an external display, and they say that model strains once there is no display to monitor and no stable boundary between person and machine. They order the field by what it costs to leave the coupling. At an external interface the person steps away from a screen. At what they call Level 4b, institutionally mandated AI, the person cannot decline the system without declining a role, and their examples include work allocation on labor platforms. The principles they set down for that future are meaningful human control, a stated human benefit, inclusion of people who do not adopt the technology, foresight, and an education in collaborating with machines. One educational obligation they transfer to that societal level is civic AI literacy. Mourtzis and Tsakalos date the same arc to the industrial revolutions. The human went from operator, to overseer, to supervisor, and is now to become a colleague. The interfaces that carry the arc are levers, buttons, screens, dashboards, decision-support tools, and extended reality. In the collaborative model they draw, the extended-reality interface "provides the human with the necessary information to perform tasks and make decisions in real-time" (Section 5.4). Both reviews are current. Both still hand the person something to take in. §2 reads them against the transparency record.

Nadella's (2026) post is the argument this paper makes against that vision, read in §2.5. He asks organizations to "separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," to leave "tamper-proof human readable evidence" of every meaningful model action, and to let an authorized person halt the model. Containment and the brake are real. They are the oversight Grahn et al. still ask for, and the information Mourtzis and Tsakalos still route to a human, written for the firm that deploys the model. The human job the post leaves is to read a chain. The people the model acts on enter once, as readers of a notice after a failure. We are already in a triadic platform, a rider and a driver, a buyer and a seller, a creator and an audience, and the vision on offer is still literacy.

The paper is a search for the spaces-between-words moment in that situation. *Scriptio continua* only really makes sense read aloud, so the ear can find the boundary the eye cannot. The transition from oracy to literacy was the moment that work moved. The smoking gun, in this paper, is one change in the representation that takes a task off the person and puts it on the surface. On the written page, that change was the space between words. The space moved the work onto the page, so the line could be taken in by looking (Saenger, 1997; Busch, 2002). Modern readers of English deprived of the space lose about 30 to 50 percent of their reading rate (Rayner, Fischer, & Pollatsek, 1998; Oralova & Kuperman, 2021; McGowan, White, Jordan, & Paterson, 2014). The question is what the space would be.

The space is not the invention of literacy, and it was not a blank between every word from the late seventh century. Latin divided words with interpuncts until about AD 100, and continuous script after that lasted about six centuries, with learner texts still word-divided (Dickey, 2017; Busch, 2002). Irish manuscripts already show some separation in the second quarter of the seventh century; Insular scribes grouped prepositions with their nouns, and fully consistent separation is not found before the twelfth (Tunbridge, 1992). Mass literacy took centuries of institutional change beyond the space (Clanchy, 1993; Cressy, 1980). Those limits do not touch the claim. The smoking gun is the change in what the reader had to do. §3 states the history at that strength.

The paper makes that argument in three moves. First, the 2026 reviews, and the transparency record beside them, still hand people a text (§2). Second, Nadella's post is that remedy at enterprise scale: readers, a chain, and a brake held inside the firm (§2.5). Third, the change to look for is the one the transition from oracy to literacy already contains, and Decision Experience Design names two candidates and a test (§3, §5–§8). A candidate is the space only if it takes a task off the person in the triad and shows, on the surface, that the task is gone. Strategic offloading removes a tactical calculation and makes the removal plain. Ecological feedback immediacy returns a valid state as the person acts, rather than a log to be read afterward. Neither is announced here as the space.

The sections follow those three moves. §2 reads the two reviews and the HCI record that still hands people a text, and §2.5 is the reading of the post. §3 is the oracy-to-literacy change. §4 is the regulatory record of the same text. §5 names the sensibility a space would call on. §6 holds the two candidates to the test taken from the history. §7 states the condition any candidate has to meet: a space the operator can withdraw is not a space. §8 is how we would know. §9 states the scope and returns to the question.

## 2. Background: Transparency, Explanation, and Legibility in HCI

### 2.1 Where the 2026 Reviews Leave the Field

The two reviews are the context for the argument, and they do not yet contain it. Grahn et al. (2026) are explicit about the model they inherit. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens, and Endsley after them, describe "a human operator, clearly separated from the machine," who "monitors discrete levels of automation through external displays" (p. 2). Grahn et al. say that scaffolding should be extended, not presupposed. At a closed-loop neural interface "there is no discrete level of automation to select, no external display through which oversight is exercised, and no stable boundary at which the human ends and the machine begins" (p. 7). At infrastructural scale, "the classical assumption of a supervisory operator standing outside the system is least tenable" (p. 8). Between those two strains sits the case this paper is about. Their Level 4b is "institutionally mandated AI," and the examples include "work allocation on labor platforms" (Table 2). A person in that class cannot decline the system without declining the role.

What they put in place of the strained display is still something a person takes in or authorizes. At the external interface, outputs should be "transparent and interpretable to human operators" (Table 1). Agency, as they use Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven, requires that behavior be traceable to a human's reasons, plus override, opt-out, and human authorization of critical decisions (Section 4). Of the educational obligations they map onto the societal level, one transferred cell is "civic AI literacy." In the discussion, explainable AI is "essential for maintaining the principle of human agency" (p. 16). They also record that most deployed collaboration is still asymmetric: "the AI proposes and the human merely approves" (p. 10). The review is a narrative synthesis, purposive rather than exhaustive, and it does not mention a two-sided allocation or Nadella. The inference is this paper's. A field that has seen the external display fail, and that answers with interpretable outputs, authorization, and literacy, has not yet changed what the person in the triad has to calculate.

Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) supply the shop-floor history of the same habit. Across four industrial revolutions the human goes from operator, to overseer, to supervisor, to colleague (Section 2). The interfaces go with the role: levers and wheels, then buttons, then screens and graphical interfaces, then "visualization dashboards, Augmented Reality (AR), and decision-support tools" built "to assist humans in understanding machine behavior" (Section 2). Industry 4.0, on their account, makes the human's work "cognitive and supervisory," and names trust and situational awareness as what that work requires. Industry 5.0 is supposed to make the human a colleague, and extended reality is supposed to cut "manual effort and cognitive workload" (Section 2). The colleague in the model they then draw still decides from a display. The extended-reality interface "provides the human with the necessary information to perform tasks and make decisions in real-time" (Section 5.4). The conclusions give the three layers the same split: sensors let the robot perceive, "XR interfaces provide instructions and support decision-making for humans collaborating with robots," and the robot does the precision work. Their state of the art is 87 publications from 2021 to 2026, and the sensor papers in it mostly sit at technology readiness levels 3 to 4 (Section 5.1; Table 2). The review is about a worker and a machine, not a rider and a driver. What it shows is that the current interface program, even when it calls the human a colleague, still hands that human information and instructions.

The transparency record below is the same remedy, measured. Disclosure often fails. Where a text helps, it still asks for a reader inside a dyad.

### 2.2 The Transparency and Trust-Calibration Turn in HCI and CSCW

HCI's transparency turn hands people a text. For about twenty-six years, since Herlocker, Konstan, and Riedl (2000), a line of that work has treated opacity as an informational defect to be repaired by exposure. Herlocker et al. called recommender systems "black boxes, computerized oracles that give advice, but cannot be questioned" (p. 1). The remedy they proposed was an explanation. A scoping review of trust-calibration studies reports mixed and null findings (Wischnewski, Krämer, & Müller, 2023). The hope attached to the text is that an operator who can see the system's state will form an accurate mental model, calibrate trust, and decide better. Körber (2018) treats trust as a mainly affective response and notes that operators may not form a complete mental model.

Disclosure often fails, and where a text helps it still asks for a reader inside a dyad. Feature-based explanations can raise acceptance of a recommendation whether or not it is correct; example-based explanations in the same study reduced overreliance (Chen, Liao, Wortman Vaughan, & Bansal, 2023). Explanations reduce overreliance when the task is hard and the explanation is cheap to check (Vasconcelos et al., 2023). Confidence information shown with recommendations led to smaller performance decrements when the aid was wrong (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010, reviewing McGuirl & Sarter, 2006). Verification of one code assistant's suggestions took 22.4% of session time, in a lab study of 21 programmers on short tasks (Mozannar, Bansal, Fourney, & Horvitz, 2024). Knowledge workers report reduced critical engagement with generative tools (Lee et al., 2025). In each of these settings the person is still reading, and the loop is still one person and one system.

### 2.3 Situated Rationale vs. Post-Hoc Audit Trails and "Revealing Design"

Friction at the moment of choice is still, in these studies, work a person does with a text inside a dyad. Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos (2021) showed that cognitive forcing functions—such as requiring users to formulate an independent choice before viewing an AI recommendation—reduced overreliance compared with simple explanations on the questions where the AI was wrong. Disley and Khan (2026) reframe system representations as an active "design surface" and show that selectively suppressing a concept in a model can introduce friction. Horvath et al. (2022) studied annotation in browser documentation. The anchor carried context for later readers, and the annotator did not benefit (p = 0.78). Pucci, Piro, and Matera (2027) report tensions around verbosity, pacing, and control for language-model agents that fill web forms, in a study of 18 blind and visually impaired participants. The person is still working through a representation.

Özçetin and Wiltse (2025) critique how Terms of Service rely on static, legalistic text to govern dynamic, asymmetric relations. They advocate for "revealing design," an approach aimed at making platform entanglements "legible" to participants. Their later moves design an interaction surface for contestation, including what they call appropriateness-adjustment buttons, and call for legislation to require the approach. Making an entanglement legible still begins by handing someone a text. Understanding that one is entangled does not, by itself, change the actions required to accept a dispatch or price a product. Contesting a penalty is one of the actions their design is built to change.

### 2.4 From Human–Machine Dyads to Irreducible Algorithmic Triads

The reader in the studies above sits in a dyad. A triad is the situation the space would have to change, and explaining an allocation to one party does not take the tactical calculation off them. Mejia and Parker (2021), Li, Liu, and Sundararajan (2026), and Ehsan et al. (2021) already take up settings with more than one person and a system. In the classic cognitive engineering literature, interaction is modeled as a two-node loop: a human operator paired with a machine aid (Sheridan & Verplank, 1978; Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000). The pilot monitors the autopilot; the clinician reviews a diagnostic classifier; the driver takes over from an automated steering system (Matsuo et al., 2026). In a dyad, the machine's role is auxiliary. Its explicit functional purpose is to support, inform, or substitute for the human operator in executing an external task.

```
Classic HCI Dyad:
[Human Operator] <====== Interaction Loop ======> [Machine / Tool]
```

Coordination across contemporary digital platforms does not fit this schema in every market the same way. In ride-hailing and digital labor platforms, two human counterparts coordinate through a third node: an active algorithmic mediator (Lee, Kusbit, Metsky, & Dabbish, 2015; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020; Rahman, 2019). In dealer foreign-exchange markets the dealer is a counterparty and owns finality of the deal (Cartea, Jaimungal, & Walton, 2019; Oomen, 2017). Reddit moderation in the study cited above was done by human volunteers (Jhaver et al., 2019). HCI and CSCW have studied settings with more than one person and a system, including AI-mediated communication, where suggested replies change how two correspondents write and regard each other (Hohenstein et al., 2023; Mejia & Parker, 2021; Li et al., 2026; Ehsan et al., 2021).

```
                  Mediation Probe│ │Boundary Reaction
                                 │ │
            ┌────────────────────┴─┴────────────────────┐
            │                                           │
  ┌─────────┴─────────┐                       ┌─────────┴─────────┐
  │      Party A      │◄──Coordination Move──►│      Party B      │
  │ (Worker / Driver) │                       │ (Client / Rider)  │
  └───────────────────┘                       └───────────────────┘
```

This mediator is not an advisory instrument assisting one party. It reads inputs from both sides, filters information symmetrically or asymmetrically, applies an independent objective function (J_M)—such as platform profit maximization, throughput balancing, or dispute minimization—and commits an allocation that neither party directly controls. On a ride-hailing platform the driver can decline the allocation, at a cost (Lee, Kusbit, Metsky, & Dabbish, 2015). Party A's payoff depends directly on Party B's move, yet Party A can only perceive, probe, or influence Party B through the transformation imposed by M; in that sense the triad does not decompose into two independent dyads. Transplanting dyadic transparency tools into a triadic mediator yields incoherence: explaining to Party A why the algorithm made an allocation does not eliminate the tactical necessity for Party A to second-guess what Party B will do through that same mediator.

### 2.5 The Post Preserves Literacy

This is the argument against the vision the reviews leave in place. Grahn et al. (2026) and Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) still hand a person a display, an authorization, or an instruction. Nadella's (2026) post, "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era," is that remedy written for the firm that deploys the model. It is a literacy solution for a triadic situation. It is a personal essay, not a Microsoft policy document, and the "we" throughout is the deploying organization. The words user, customer, employee, and people do not occur. Paragraph numbers below are the project's.

The post opens on an attribution gap. With traditional software, "we had the tools and capability to trace behaviors to a specific code path" (P1). For frontier models, "we can't attribute model behaviors and outputs to specific inputs of training data or configurations of model weights," and organizations still give them "access to our most sensitive data" and "mission-critical actions on our behalf" (P2). The remedy he reaches for is not a change in what a person in a triad has to calculate. It is containment. "We need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it" (P5, before the seven principles). Models are to be treated as insider risks because a capable actor can make mistakes or be compromised (P7), in the tradition of insider-threat practice (CERT National Insider Threat Center, 2022).

The human task that follows is reading. Chain-of-thought transparency is "a non-negotiable," and "Neuralese" cannot excuse opaque reasoning, but the chain "alone is not sufficient or dependable" (P9). Controls over access and action "must sit outside the model" (P11), and the model is separated from its harness and action space (P12). Among the seven principles of observability, "Observe everything" requires "tamper-proof human readable evidence" for every meaningful action, so an outcome can be reconstructed without the model's own attestation (P15). Verifiability, independent controls, and independent auditability are further principles (P16–P18). Independent auditability is not the evidence sentence. The evidence is a text. Someone inside the firm has to read the chain: the chain of thought, the logs, the record that can reconstruct an outcome.

Containment is real, and it is not a space. An authorized person must be able to pause or stop a model in mid-task, "like an emergency brake" (P19). The brake is held inside the firm. Incident disclosure tells "those affected" after a failure (P20). That is the one place a party outside the deployer appears, and the party appears as a reader of a notice. The close asks for the system that "enables us to trust the model the least" (P21). Trusting the model least, on this design, means reading it more.

*Scriptio continua* asked the ear to find a boundary the eye could not see. The post asks a reader to find, in a chain, what the model did. Containment and the brake do not take that task off the person and show, on the surface, that it is gone. They add readers, and a brake held inside the firm. A rider, a driver, a buyer, a seller still has to calculate through the mediator. The post does not change that calculation. The transition from oracy to literacy is the change that did, and §3 is that change.

## 3. Re-Representation: The Scribal Precedent in Human-Factors Terms

### 3.1 The Page Before and After Word Separation

The reviews and the post leave the person with something to read. This section is the change they do not contain. The transition from oracy to literacy put the finding of a word's boundary on the page. The space between words was the smoking gun: one change in the representation that took that work off the voice.

In the main Greek tradition, texts were transcribed in *scriptio continua*, words strung together in unbroken lines. Because the line gave no spatial demarcation, Saenger (1997) argues, a reader identified words by pronouncing or subvocalizing the characters. Heilmann (2020) reports that the classical consensus follows Saenger. Busch (2002) defends reading aloud as the ancient norm and allows that fluent silent readers were an exception. *Scriptio continua* made sense read aloud, because the ear did the work the eye could not.

The device that moved the work was the space. Scribes in Ireland and Anglo-Saxon Britain learned Latin as a foreign language, not as a spoken one, and Insular copyists began grouping words on the page (Tunbridge, 1992). Saenger's statement of the effect is that word separation and related devices in Insular books "provided shortcuts for achieving the reading skills that an elite among the ancients had mastered only through a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (1997, p. 85). The space did not add a new word. By altering the layout, the scribes altered what a reader had to do.

The limits are these, and they do not touch that claim. The space is not the invention of literacy. Mass literacy required centuries of institutional change, legal documentation, and administrative centralization (Clanchy, 1993; Cressy, 1980). Latin used interpuncts until about AD 100, and continuous script after that lasted about six centuries; learner and bilingual texts kept word division (Dickey, 2017; Busch, 2002). The earliest word-based separation in the Irish record is the second quarter of the seventh century, and the practice was already in some manuscripts before a start date can be fixed (Tunbridge, 1992). Insular copyists grouped prepositions with their nouns and joined *et*; fully consistent separation into individual parts of speech is not found before the twelfth century, and on Saenger's account the Continental convention was standard by the mid-eleventh, with canonical separation in the twelfth (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997; Parkes, 1992). Silent reading is attested for letters and short texts, and for an elite, long before the space (Knox, 1968; Gavrilov, 1997; Burnyeat, 1997; Heilmann, 2020). Letter forms also changed (Tunbridge, 1992). The smoking gun is the change in what the reader had to do.

### 3.2 Cognitive Offloading, Visual Search, and Perceptual Substitution

What the reader no longer had to do is the content of the smoking gun. In the language of cognitive engineering, the space can be read as environmental cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Kirsh, 1995) that exploited the representational effect (Zhang & Norman, 1994). Those sources do not mention writing. The extension to the page is this paper's. Zhang and Norman showed that "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (p. 88). When a constraint sits in the layout, it does not have to be held in working memory.

Hutchins (1995) documented the same principle on the flight deck: pilots set movable speed bugs on the rim of the airspeed indicator, and the comparison of speeds is externalized into the spatial proximity between a needle and a marker.

```
Representational Shift on the Page:

Scriptio Continua:
[L][E][X][I][C][A][L][B][O][U][N][D][A][R][Y]
▲                                           ▲
└──────── Working Memory / Phonological ────┘
          Reconstruction Overhead

Word Spacing:
[L][E][X][I][C][A][L]   [B][O][U][N][D][A][R][Y]
          ▲          ▲          ▲
          └── Visual ┴── Eye ───┘
              Saccade Targeting
```

*Scriptio continua* left boundary detection to the reader, who had to hold ambiguous strings of sound while testing competing segmentations. The space substituted visual segmentation for that inference. No study has measured that substitution in medieval readers. What has been measured is the modern cost of taking the space away.

The visual system processes spatial structure across the field in parallel, within the limits of eccentricity and crowding (Wolfe, 2021). Under Wolfe's Guided Search model, bottom-up feature salience is one of five sources that guide selective attention. By carving white channels between character clusters, spacing can give the oculomotor system targets for the next saccade. Removing spaces from English changes where the eyes land as well as how fast words are identified (Rayner et al., 1998). Thai readers' initial landing positions were similar in spaced and unspaced text (Winskel, Radach, & Luksaneeyanawin, 2009), so saccade targeting does not depend on spaces in every script. Deprived of these markers, modern readers of English lose about 30 to 50 percent of their reading rate (Rayner et al., 1998; Winskel et al., 2009; Oralova & Kuperman, 2021). The geometry of the page did part of the work of finding the word. That is what a smoking gun does.

### 3.3 Cross-Orthographic Asymmetries and the Structural Disanalogy

The space pays where finding the boundary was the reader's job. Where a script already gives the eye another cue, inserting spaces is a different change. When spaces are removed from English, reading performance drops sharply. When spaces are inserted into Chinese or Thai, the effect is mixed (Bai et al., 2008; Winskel, Radach, & Luksaneeyanawin, 2009; Chantavarin & Leung, 2025).

Skilled readers of Chinese read word-spaced text no faster than normal unspaced text, 5,239 against 5,221 milliseconds a sentence (Bai et al., 2008). Oralova and Kuperman (2021) replicate that null and explain it: a word-level gain is cancelled by extra saccades, while spaces placed only at boundaries that raters agree on gave a small reliable speed-up of about 2 percent. Thai readers fixate a spaced word for less time, and the sentence-level slowing is marginal (Winskel et al., 2009; Chantavarin & Leung, 2025). Learners do not reliably gain. Child learners of Chinese read word-spaced text more slowly and less accurately than text spaced between every character (Bassetti & Masterson, 2012), and for adult second-language learners the difference between spaced and unspaced text was not significant (Cui, 2023). Native readers of unspaced Thai gain at the word level (Chantavarin & Leung, 2025). Colour-marked word boundaries gave young skilled Chinese readers no significant benefit and helped older readers, whom the authors see as limited by vision (Pan, Wang, Zhang, Tsang, & Yan, 2025). An ergonomic intervention is not an abstract virtue; it must match the visual search affordances of the medium to the cognitive tasks of the user.

The page and the platform are not the same surface, and the difference matters for what a later space would have to survive. A manuscript page does not act on its own. Once ink dries, it does not reorder its words when the reader blinks. Later readers did add punctuation and marks, and correctors' hands are routine (Tunbridge, 1992). The scribes who made the page worked under a discipline that was real and uneven.

Carolingian rulers and church authorities ordered some books made correct. Charlemagne's letter *De litteris colendis* measures monasteries' written Latin and study against a standard of correctness (Charlemagne, trans. 1900; Boretius, 1883, no. 29). The *Admonitio generalis* of 789, chapter 72, orders books corrected and copied with care (Boretius, 1883, no. 22). Alcuin's *Carmen* XCIV instructs scribes to mark sense units for the lector (Dümmler, 1881). Only the capitulary and the poem concern books; the letter concerns written Latin and study. Westwell (2018) cautions that uniformity imposed from above can be overstated, and authority was distributed and negotiated. None of these texts concerns word separation, which the Insular scribes had adopted earlier. What they show is who stood behind some of the page: an authority with standing over the scribe, not a single court that held every copyist.

An algorithmic intermediary is dynamic, adaptive, and governed by the commercial incentives of its owner. The platform controls the interface, observes user probes, and can rewrite its ranking rules, pricing multipliers, and allocation weights (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Lee et al., 2015; Rahman, 2019). The page, once dry, held the space. The platform can withdraw one. The reviews and the post, already read, still hand the person a text. The space is the change they do not make.

## 4. Disclosure as a Literacy-Based Intervention

### 4.1 Taxonomizing Disclosure and the Law-and-Economics Critique

Regulation's usual remedy is the same text. When algorithmic mediation produces unfair or exploitative outcomes, public regulators and legal bodies often fall back on disclosure. Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011) call it the most common technique in regulation generally. It is common, and it is not the whole of the law: the Platform Work Directive pairs disclosure with bans on some processing and with duties to modify a discriminatory system (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, Arts. 7, 10). The four targets below are ways of handing a person a document. Only the last is the category error. A one-line statement of a property, Target 3, can change what people do, and it is still a sentence to study (§6.2).

```
               └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

1. Target 1 (Presence): Notifying users that an algorithm or bot is active (Ishowo-Oloko et al., 2019).
2. Target 2 (Objective Function): Stating the objective the mediator is optimizing (e.g., platform margin maximization vs. wait-time minimization).
3. Target 3 (Formal Rule Property): Informing users of an actionable structural property of the mechanism (e.g., that sincere bidding is a dominant strategy; Masuda et al., 2022). "Obviously strategy-proof" is Li's (2017) term for a stronger property, and the second-price sealed-bid auction, which is Masuda's case, is strategy-proof and not obviously strategy-proof.
4. Target 4 (Full Textual Rule and Rationale): Providing full terms of service, technical documentation, or post-hoc feature-attribution scores explaining an individual allocation.

The category error named in this paper lies in Target 4. Target 3 behaves differently: a one-line statement of a property changes what people do (§6.2). In Masuda, Mikami, Sakai, Serizawa, and Wakayama (2022) that statement came with instructions and a seventeen-question quiz, and the gain in sincere bidding was concentrated among perfect scorers. It is not a signal with nothing to study.

The legal and behavioral critique of mandated disclosure explains why a rulebook often fails, and it does not cover the text of algorithmic rules. Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2011) reviewed disclosure regimes across finance, healthcare, and consumer contracts. They write first that its failure is inevitable (p. 651) and later that it regularly, though not inevitably, fails (p. 679), because the people it addresses do not read disclosures, do not understand them when they do, and do not use them when they understand (p. 665). Fung, Graham, and Weil (2007) find that disclosure works where it is embedded in the routines of users and disclosers alike (pp. 54–55, 65), and they rate complex chemical and toxics disclosure as only moderately effective (p. 77). Psychology explains much of the gap between what is disclosed and what is done with it (Loewenstein, Sunstein, & Golman, 2014).

The disclosures that do alter outcomes tend to be simple, low-cost signals, such as letter grades for hygiene posted in restaurant windows. They act in part on the discloser. Loewenstein, Sunstein, and Golman (2014) put the motive in the spotlight and in guilt (p. 404). Jin and Leslie (2003) put it in consumer demand: Los Angeles grade cards raised scores, made revenue sensitive to hygiene, and cut food-related hospitalisations, with inspector leniency at the A threshold. The same device can fail where the grade is not a valid measure. Ho (2012) finds San Diego grades inflated, New York scores almost uninformative, and no detectable health effect after New York grading. Dranove, Kessler, McClellan, and Satterthwaite (2003) find that cardiac-surgery report cards shifted surgery toward healthier patients and left sicker patients worse off. Ben-Shahar and Schneider hedge even the restaurant case.

### 4.2 The Empirical Record Across Finance, XAI, and Platform Governance

The empirical record is the same verdict. Disclosure often fails. Where a text helps, it still asks for a reader. In a meta-analysis of explainable-AI interventions, explanations yielded no reliable improvement over AI advice alone (Schemmer et al., 2022; standardized mean difference 0.07, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.30]). Against unaided humans the same paper reports a gain (0.59, 95% CI [0.39, 0.79]). Across 370 effect sizes the presence of an explanation was not a significant moderator of human–AI performance (Vaccaro et al., 2024). Heatmap explanations raised balanced accuracy by 7.7 points for 48 factory workers and by 4.7 points for 113 radiologists, over the same black-box score (Senoner et al., 2024). The heatmap re-represents the object, and radiologists overruled fewer wrong predictions when they had it.

In consumer finance, one field experiment shows that a disclosure can backfire. A Mexican bank sent a separate mailing in a statement-like envelope, with no statement inside (Seira et al., 2017). A message stating the interest rate changed nothing in the aggregate and reduced debt by 525 pesos, about 3% of the mean, in one subgroup. A message that stated the personalized months-to-pay and advised the cardholder to pay more than the minimum raised delinquency by 0.011 points among clients who paid interest and by 0.012 among high-debt clients, 7.4% of the mean (pp. 293–295). A two-bank follow-up found effects under 5%, none significant (p. 304). A repayment disclosure under the CARD Act moved repayment slightly in the intended direction (Agarwal, Chomsisengphet, Mahoney, & Stroebel, 2015). Disclosure can backfire. It does not always.

In machine learning interfaces, Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (2021) showed that a clear model, set against a black-box model, reduced users' ability to detect and correct the model's mistake on an unusual case in the first two experiments. The two-feature comparison is only the fourth experiment. The effect is a main effect of transparency, pooled over the two-feature and eight-feature clear models. The authors conjecture that the visible detail overloaded participants.

In platform governance, Jhaver et al. (2019) surveyed 907 Reddit users whose posts had been removed. The two coefficients come from different subsamples. Among the 526 who had noticed the rules, having read them went with judging the removal less fair (β = −.314). Among 738 respondents, receiving an explanation went with judging it fairer (β = .384). Both are cross-sectional associations from self-report, and the analysis is primarily exploratory. Rule clarity also raised fairness (β = .170) and intention to post again (β = .223). The rulebook and the situated reason pulled in opposite directions for fairness.

Nouwens, Liccardi, Veale, Karger, and Kagal (2020) showed in cookie consent interfaces that moving the reject button off the first page raised consent by 22 to 23 percentage points, in a sample of 40 participants in the United States. Users respond to the spatial architecture of the interface. The person is still choosing inside a notice.

The reading of Nadella (2026) is in §2.5. What §4 adds is the regulatory record of the same remedy: a text handed to a person.

## 5. Algorithmacy: Epistemic Action in Irreducible Triads

### 5.1 Defining Algorithmacy

Algorithmacy, as used here, is the sensibility the space would call on. It is deciding, and taking in what returns, rather than reading a record. I define it as the behavioral sensibility required to navigate irreducible algorithmic triads: the capacity to coordinate with a counterpart through an active, optimizing mediator whose internal rules shift dynamically.

Algorithmacy is distinct from dyadic "AI literacy." The latter typically refers to conceptual knowledge: understanding what neural networks are, how training data shapes bias, or how to compose structured text prompts in chat windows.

Nor does it match the one earlier printed use of the word that I have found. Oberschelp (1995), writing on informatics teaching, urges that "we have to preserve logicacy and algorithmacy by continuously training simple mental activities without artificial tools" (p. 121). He does not define the term; in context it names the skill of carrying out algorithmic steps by hand.

Algorithmacy as I use it is an active coordination competency. Just as literacy allows a reader to traverse an author's text without demanding that the reader build a printing press, algorithmacy allows an individual to formulate choices, infer hidden constraints, and protect their welfare across an algorithmic intermediary without requiring them to inspect its source code.

### 5.2 Enacted Sensemaking and Epistemic Action

In an irreducible triad, people do not come to understand a mediator by reading about it. They understand it by acting through it and observing what happens. This process is grounded in Weick's (1988) account of enacted sensemaking: in ambiguous, dynamic environments, "people think by acting" (p. 305). Actors take action, create consequences in the environment, and then look at the results to infer what kind of system they are dealing with. Weick adds a warning that belongs in any design that relies on this: action that clarifies a situation can also intensify it.

In cognitive science, exploratory moves of this kind are known as epistemic actions (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994). Kirsh and Maglio showed that in Tetris, players do not rotate and translate falling pieces only to place them (pragmatic action). They also move them to learn something sooner than they could work it out in the head. Epistemic actions are "physical actions that make mental computation easier, faster, or more reliable" (p. 513).

```
Triadic Epistemic Probing:
[Worker / User] ──── (1) Epistemic Probe: Move / Micro-bid ────► [Algorithmic Mediator]
                                                                        │
                                                                 (2) Evaluates Probe
                                                                 Against Objective J_M
                                                                        │
[Worker / User] ◄─── (3) Boundary Reaction / State Update ──────────────┘
       │
(4) Dynamic Recalibration of Strategy
```

Ride-hail drivers' strategies drew partly on the company's published general rules, and partly on what the system did in response to their own moves (Lee et al., 2015). They trade those theories in online forums (Lee et al., 2015; Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020). Workers also pool what they observe and push back together (Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2019). Upwork freelancers learn a rating algorithm from trial and error, forums, and documentation (Sutherland, Jarrahi, Dunn, & Nelson, 2020). When a decision rule is hidden, people infer it from the observed outcomes of others (Ghalme et al., 2021). Shen et al. (2021) already theorise that probing. What this definition adds is the name of the sensibility a space would call on: deciding, and taking in what returns.

Learners also settle into a false rule and stop probing (Rich & Gureckis, 2018; Lee, Li, Lee, & Hayes, 2023). Taking in what returns is not a guarantee that the return was read correctly.

### 5.3 High-Validity Learning Environments vs. Mediated Distortion

Kahneman and Klein (2009) set two conditions for the development of skilled intuition: an environment regular enough to be learned, and an opportunity to learn it through "feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (p. 524). They also write that validity and uncertainty are not incompatible, and name poker and warfare.

When feedback is delayed or noisy, calibration suffers, and not in one way. Rahmandad and Gary (2023) found that delay pushes decision-makers toward options that pay off quickly. Uncertainty was not an important predictor of bias. It cut performance in one study and not robustly in the second, and in the first study uncertainty may have helped performance when delays were long. Movement toward the middle of the strategy space comes from an increase in delay or in uncertainty, so it is not unique to noise. Learning was present across all conditions.

Algorithmic intermediaries often fail these conditions. To analyze mediated distortion, we must separate two distinct drivers:

1. Strategic Rule Secrecy: Some platforms conceal evaluation criteria, ranking parameters, and matching formulas (Rahman, 2019; Regulation (EU) 2019/1150, Arts. 5(1) and 5(6)). Rahman (2019) reports that operators justify this secrecy as necessary to prevent "gaming," and reads it as also keeping control of a stratified workforce with the platform.
2. Technical Noise and Latency: Algorithmic mediators can introduce jitter and stochastic allocations. Not all distortion is intended. The jitter Chen, Mislove, and Wilson (2015) measured in Uber's surge multipliers was, by Uber's own account, a bug: stale multipliers served to random clients. Deliberate noise does occur. LinkedIn added calibrated differential-privacy noise to the analytics marketers receive (Rogers et al., 2021). Yahoo researchers describe serving randomly selected articles to a randomly chosen bucket of visitors in order to learn (Li, Chu, Langford, & Schapire, 2010). Deliberate delay is not shown in the sources read here.

Hidden rules and noisy or delayed feedback degrade learning by acting. They do not remove it. Rahmandad and Gary (2023) find that delay and noise degrade and bias learning without abolishing it, and last-round performance still beat a random benchmark. The user who probes and receives an ambiguous response later may be unable to verify whether the action contributed (Rahman, 2019). Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey (2015) complicate the mechanism: people who watched a superior algorithm win still abandoned it for a worse human forecast. Folk theories are part of what remains. They are also what launched audits that found real bias (Shen et al., 2021). Defensive gaming is not the only residue.

## 6. Decision Experience Design (DXD): Two Candidate Conventions

### 6.1 Architectural Framing via Norman's Gulfs of Action

This section is the third move. The test is the one the space supplies. A candidate is the smoking gun only if it takes a task off the person in the triad and shows, on the surface, that the task is gone, so they do not have to read a chain to find the boundary. Decision Experience Design names two candidates and holds each to that test. The vocabulary is Norman's (1988) gulfs of execution and evaluation. Those gulfs are between a person and a device. The mediator here is a third party, and the fit is an analogy:

1. The Gulf of Execution: The cognitive distance between a user's goal and the actions permitted by the interface. Convention A (Strategic Offloading) narrows the Gulf of Execution by eliminating tactical gaming calculations from the transaction mechanism and showing on the display that they have been eliminated.
2. The Gulf of Evaluation: The cognitive distance between the system's state and the user's ability to assess that state. Convention B (Ecological Feedback Immediacy) narrows the Gulf of Evaluation by delivering valid, noise-filtered, low-latency feedback as the user formulates their choice.

### 6.2 Convention A: Strategic Offloading (Removing a Tactical Task and Showing It Is Gone)

Convention A passes the test only if the tactical calculation is taken off the person and the display shows that it is gone. A sentence that states the property is still a text. The evidence is about how far a menu does the first thing, and how far a description does not.

In classical mechanism design, an allocation mechanism is strategy-proof if reporting one's true valuation or preference is a dominant strategy: no participant can gain by lying, shading their bid, or misrepresenting their constraints (Li, 2017). Yet theoretical strategy-proofness often fails to produce sincere human behavior. Non-sincere play is frequent, on the order of a fifth to a half of reports depending on the mechanism, and it is not near-universal (Masuda et al., 2022; Li, 2017; Gonczarowski, Heffetz, Ishai, & Thomas, 2024; Artemov, Che, & He, 2023). In a laboratory Vickrey auction, only about a fifth of bids were sincere without advice (Masuda et al., 2022). In field data, 17 to 35 percent of applicants make dominated reports, and only 1 to 20 percent of those mistakes change the assignment (Artemov et al., 2023).

To address this, Li (2017) formalized obviously strategy-proof (OSP) mechanisms. A strategy is obviously dominant if, at any information set where it and a deviation first diverge, the best possible outcome under the deviation is no better than the worst possible outcome under the strategy. A mechanism is OSP if it has an equilibrium in obviously dominant strategies. The comparison is not a property of the mechanism taken apart from that information set. OSP mechanisms can be recognized as safe by agents who lack contingent reasoning capabilities, and in Li's experiments far fewer games ended off the dominant-strategy outcome under the obviously strategy-proof version of the same rule.

Convention A embeds this property in the visual interface. It removes the tactical calculation—eliminating the need to second-guess what other participants are doing—and uses the interface to make that removal obvious.

Recent experimental economics calibrates the convention and its limit. Gonczarowski, Heffetz, Ishai, and Thomas (2024; N = 542) tested several descriptions. The gain in understanding belongs to a property-only menu, not to a menu description of the algorithm. Understanding of strategy-proofness was 71% under the property-only menu (Menu-SP), 62% under a textbook statement of strategy-proofness, 58% under a menu description of deferred acceptance (Menu-DA), 56% under the traditional description, and 54% under a null. Straightforward play, measured as the share of rounds, was 59% under Menu-SP. Menu-DA was 50% and the textbook statement 53%. Menu-SP beat the null (p = 0.01) and beat Menu-DA (p = 0.04). It did not beat the traditional description.

Understanding moved play by a large margin. The description moved it by a small one. Most Menu-SP participants mixed straightforward and non-straightforward rounds: 59% of rounds were straightforward, and 19% of those participants played straightforwardly in every round. Participants who understood the property, scored above 75%, played straightforwardly in 83% of rounds, against 46% for those scored below 75%. That is a 36-point difference (Gonczarowski et al., 2024).

This is why Masuda et al. (2022) matter: advice that sincere bidding is a dominant strategy, given with instructions and a quiz, raised sincere bids from 20.6% to 46.9%, while efficiency rose by about one percentage point. The authors attribute the small efficiency effect to a symmetric design in which efficiency was already near the ceiling. Convention A must therefore pair the structural elimination of the tactical task with an interface that visibly confirms the task is gone. Designers should not treat a large change in behavior as a promise of a large change in outcomes, and they should not treat the small efficiency gap as a general law.

### 6.3 Convention B: Ecological Feedback Immediacy (Valid and Plain First, Rapid Second)

Convention B passes the test only if a valid state appears on the surface as the person acts, so they do not have to read a log afterward. Speed is not the test. The state has to be valid, and it has to be on the surface.

Convention B addresses the Gulf of Evaluation by shortening the temporal and causal distance between an actor's exploratory probe and the mediator's response. Shneiderman (1983) states the principle that the impact on the object of interest is immediately visible, and his examples run wider than classical graphical interfaces: display editors, VisiCalc, video games, CAD, and the Xerox Star, where a document icon is dragged onto a printer icon. In algorithmic mediation, that coupling is often broken. A user submits a bid, posts a job, or positions a car, and waits while the system calculates. Uber recomputed surge on a five-minute clock (Chen et al., 2015), and Last Look gives a dealer a rejection window (Cartea et al., 2019).

However, in designing Convention B, ecological validity strictly precedes speed. Frequent feedback can damage decisions when it makes losses salient. Larson, List, and Metcalfe (2016) found that professional traders shown prices second by second invested about 25% less in the risky asset and earned about 35% less than traders shown prices every four hours, a pattern they attribute to myopic loss aversion on an asset with a positive drift. The harm is from seeing losses often, not from noisy or unreliable data. With real funds over a year, and with realistic return distributions, the frequency effect vanished (Beshears, Choi, Laibson, & Madrian, 2017).

Kluger and DeNisi (1996) showed in a meta-analysis that more than one-third of feedback interventions reduce performance. Harm grew as attention moved toward the self and away from the task. Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie (2020) put the harm in low-information feedback. Validity comes first for that reason.

Convention B therefore specifies feedback that is valid and noise-filtered first, plain second, and low-latency third. It would give participants a state on the surface before they commit. The closest test is dyadic: a what-if console that let people drag inputs and watch a prediction change did not improve decisions and raised over-reliance in one task (Liu, Lai, & Tan, 2021).

```
       │
```

**Design Opening: What if...?**
What if gig drivers and marketplace sellers were provided with noise-filtered contour maps that showed, as they dragged a price or radius slider, how the platform's clearing mechanism would allocate orders across candidate locations before they committed to a shift or contract?

On the test from the history, Convention B has not passed. It is untested in a triad. The nearest sandbox, a what-if console, did not improve decisions and raised over-reliance (Liu, Lai, & Tan, 2021). A contour that has to be read as a log is another chain. Arms 4a and 4b of §8 are how the test would be run. The question stays open.

## 7. Institutional Custody: Who Can Hold a Convention?

### 7.1 The Unilateral-Modification Custody Test

A space the operator can withdraw is not a space. That is the only condition this section adds. A candidate that takes a task off the person still fails if the party that operates the interface can take the representation back.

To evaluate institutional stability, I formulate the Unilateral-Modification Custody Test:

*Can the party that operates the intermediary unilaterally alter, suspend, or distort the representational convention without the informed consent of the transacting parties?*

This test is an analogy to the economic theory of agency costs (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Their page on continuing incentives concerns divergence between managers and shareholders, and their remedy is monitoring and bonding. Applying the point to a platform and its users is this paper's step. Farronato, Fradkin, and MacKay (2023) record one measured tilt: Amazon's own brands ranked higher than observably similar products. The authors do not claim surplus capture or consumer harm.

### 7.2 Comparative Analysis Across Three Institutional Realms

To evaluate how custody functions in practice, I examine three institutional environments:

| Institutional Realm | Primary Enforcement Authority | Structural & Procedural Mechanism | Unilateral-Modification Vulnerability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Courts of Law | Constitutional oversight & statutory civil procedure | Rules Enabling Act (28 U.S.C. §§ 2072–2074); judicial disqualification (*Tumey v. Ohio*, 1927); procedural due process (*Mathews v. Eldridge*, 1976) | Low, and not airtight: a judge cannot rewrite the rules mid-trial, and failure to follow the enabling procedure does not itself invalidate a rule |
| Dealer FX Markets | Voluntary industry code; regulators after the fact | FX Global Code (GFXC, 2024): Principle 17 on last look; Principle 9 on disclosure to clients; Principle 36 on keeping reasons for rejections; Principle 40 on agreeing substantial changes to legal terms | Moderate (soft law): the Code is voluntary; control failures in G10 spot FX were fined by the FCA (2014); a 17 November 2015 NYDFS order concerns Last Look, and a 20 May 2015 order concerns benchmarks |
| Digital Platforms | Private corporate ownership | Unilateral Terms of Service (Suzor, 2018; Klonick, 2018); proprietary interface updates; advisory boards (Oversight Board, 2022) | High (unilateral): the owner sets and changes rankings, algorithms, and interface conventions, subject to statute where one applies |

In judicial systems, procedural conventions are partly insulated from unilateral tampering. The Supreme Court prescribes the federal rules (28 U.S.C. § 2072), Judicial Conference committees recommend them and notice their meetings (§ 2073), and Congress reviews them (§ 2074). Failure to comply with the committee procedure does not invalidate a rule (§ 2073(e)), and a court may make an immediate-need local rule first and take comment afterwards (§ 2071(e)). The insulation is procedural and partly unenforceable. The Supreme Court ruled in *Tumey v. Ohio* (1927) that a direct, personal, substantial pecuniary interest, in a criminal case, violates due process. The holding is not a general ban on any pecuniary interest. Repeat litigants hold systemic advantages (Galanter, 1974). Employees fare worse in arbitration than in court in Colvin's (2011) comparison, which is unmatched and draws litigation rates from other studies. A judge cannot rewrite the federal rules in the middle of a trial. The insulation is the statute, and it is partial.

In foreign-exchange dealer markets, banks act as both liquidity providers and counterparties on their own platforms. "Last look" lets a dealer hold a client's order briefly and decide whether to accept it. Paragraphs 8, 13, and 24 of the 17 November 2015 NYDFS consent order, which are stipulated settlement text and not adjudicated findings, state that one bank used the practice to reject trades that had moved against it and did not tell clients why (NYDFS, 2015). Cartea, Jaimungal, and Walton (2019) model Last Look as a defence against latency arbitrage that tightens quoted spreads, with the cost to ordinary clients being the rejection of their favourable fills. The consent order is not the whole of the practice.

The FCA's press release of 12 November 2014 records fines of £1,114,918,000 against five banks for control failures in G10 spot foreign exchange, and its finding is attempted manipulation (FCA, 2014). The Financial Stability Board's 2014 report does not report those fines. The industry's code followed. The FX Global Code asks dealers to disclose last-look practice (Principle 17). Principle 36 is a record-keeping duty, to keep reasons for rejections. Principle 40, under legal risk, says substantial changes to standard legal terms between counterparties should be agreed before any transaction. It is not a client-disclosure duty of the same kind as Principles 9 and 17. Adherence is voluntary. A soft-law convention holds as far as reputation and the threat of a regulator carry it.

Digital platforms fare worst on the custody test in the terms Suzor examined. In all fourteen sets of terms that Suzor (2018) examined, the terms "provided broad, unfettered discretion to platform owners." The fourteen are the largest English-language social media platforms by traffic, a sample Suzor calls highly western- and US-centric. It includes no matching, pricing, or dispatch platform, so the sentence does not by itself cover ride-hailing or labor dispatch. Appeals were thin at Facebook in 2016–17 when Klonick (2018) studied them, and broader at YouTube and Twitter. Case decisions of the Meta Oversight Board (2022) are binding and its policy recommendations are advisory. The narrow remit that does not reach matching, pricing, or dispatch is the point made by Wong and Floridi (2023) and by Douek (2024).

Technical standards bodies cannot fill the gap on their own. Voluntary adherence is the rule for Internet Standards in RFC 2026. End users take little part in the work Nottingham (2020) describes. Participation in the IETF from 2001 to 2023 is concentrated in a few organisations, and that dataset cannot observe end users (Zhang et al., 2025). If a platform can rewrite its matching conventions with a software update, a designer's promise of "strategic offloading" can be withdrawn whenever margins compress.

### 7.3 External Compulsion and Statutes Beyond Disclosure

Because platforms fail the custody test, Decision Experience Design conventions cannot survive through voluntary designer ethics alone. They require external legal compulsion.

First-generation platform regulations relied largely on disclosure and procedure, and not only on those. The EU Platform-to-Business Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2019/1150) requires fifteen days' notice of changes to terms (Art. 3) and a description of the main ranking parameters (Art. 5), and it states that providers are not required to disclose their algorithms (Art. 5(6)). Article 3(3) makes non-compliant terms and changes null and void. The Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) requires a statement of reasons for restrictions (Art. 17) and an internal complaint-handling system (Art. 20), gives users a complaint to a regulator and a claim for damages (Arts. 53–54), and constrains interface design (Arts. 25 and 38). Of 131 million statements of reasons under the Act, 99.8% cite terms of service, and most facts fields are templates (Kaushal et al., 2024). These measures give notice, reasons, and some recourse. They do not, taken alone, leave every owner free to set every convention. The EU Unfair Contract Terms Directive has, since 1993, treated unilateral change-of-terms clauses as presumptively unfair (Loos & Luzak, 2016). The gap is enforcement as well as the text of the rule.

Later statutes add structural duties beside disclosure. They do not replace it.

1. The EU Platform Work Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2831; in force since 1 December 2024; transposition due by 2 December 2026): bans certain processing of personal data (Art. 7); requires a system to be modified or discontinued where appropriate when there is a high risk of discrimination or of infringed rights (Art. 10(3)); requires that decisions to restrict, suspend, or terminate an account be taken by a human (Art. 10(5)); requires rectification within two weeks of the decision, and compensation only where rectification is not possible, where a decision infringes a worker's rights (Art. 11(3)). Article 13 extends an existing consultation right so that it covers decisions to introduce or substantially change automated systems, and it says representatives may be assisted by an expert. It does not establish the consultation right. The Directive also requires disclosure of main parameters (Art. 9). It gives workers no veto. It does not reach non-employee sellers.
2. The German Works Constitution Act is not recent. It dates from 1972. Section 87(1) no. 6 grants works councils co-determination over technical systems designed to monitor employee behavior or performance. Section 87(2) provides that the conciliation committee's award replaces an agreement. Later amendments add information and consultation duties that name artificial intelligence (§§ 80(3), 90(1) no. 3, 95(2a)). Those are not a veto. The Act does not reach consumer-facing platforms or non-employee sellers.

These statutes are part of the institutional setting in which a DXD convention would have to be held. They are not, by themselves, the framework that sustains it, and they do not cover the sellers in this paper's own examples. Carolingian scriptoria were not uniformly directed by one royal and ecclesiastical authority to produce correct books (Charlemagne, trans. 1900; Boretius, 1883; Westwell, 2018). Digital platforms will maintain a representational convention only where some external rule removes the power to alter it at will, and which rule can do that is not settled by the two statutes above.

## 8. A Research Agenda for Triadic Decision Design

### 8.1 Four Falsifiable Claims

The four claims and the five-arm protocol are how we would know a candidate is the gun. A result counts only if the task is gone from the person in the triad, on the surface. A result that hands them another text does not. In the manner of a framework and research agenda (Terveen & McDonald, 2005), I set out four claims, each stated so that data could count against it:

- **Claim 1 (Irreducibility).** In a triad comprising Party A, Party B, and Mediator M, a proxy measure of integrated information computed on the joint behavior of all three will exceed the same measure computed on each person–mediator pair, and the excess will shrink when M's determinations are made independent of one party's moves.
  - *Operationalization.* Integrated information (Φ) has so far been applied to human groups only by proxy: Engel and Malone (2018) computed a proxy on who was active when in a study of 68 groups, with the reported correlation (r = .37) on 61 of them, in groups with no mediator. Barrett et al. (2026) write that Φ is not well-defined for real physical systems and has not been computed on any real physical system. Niizato et al. (2020) did compute IIT 3.0 Φ on fish schools of two to five, and Φ rose with group size even with zero coupling, so a triad-versus-pairs comparison needs a size-matched uncoupled null. On human triads the study can test a proxy, not the untested quantity. I therefore fix the measure, the coding of behavior into discrete states, the time lag, and the partition in advance, validate them on small Boolean models where exact values can be computed, and treat the application to human triads as untested.
- **Claim 2 (Representational conventions outperform textual disclosure).** In a triadic allocation game where Mediator M optimizes an independent objective (J_M ≠ J_A, J_B), representing the system through Convention A (menu-based strategic offloading) produces higher joint surplus and less tactical distortion than full textual rule disclosure.
  - *Operationalization.* The closest precedent is Casella, Friedman, and Perez Archila (2026), where two participants bargain through a computer mediator that maximizes the chance of agreement and shows its rule every round. I give M a margin of its own and compare joint surplus between rulebook arms and menu arms.
- **Claim 3 (Validity governs the sign of feedback immediacy).** The effect of feedback immediacy on error correction depends on mediator noise. Under low noise, continuous feedback speeds convergence; under high noise, continuous feedback degrades choices, and discrete filtered feedback yields higher joint surplus.
  - *Operationalization.* Larson, List, and Metcalfe (2016) test how often traders see prices, under myopic loss aversion, not mediator noise. "Jitter" is this paper's variable. I compare continuous boundary feedback against discrete-round filtered feedback across high-jitter and low-jitter mediator regimes, and I do not treat a failure of the Larson result to travel as a failure of Claim 3. Beshears et al. (2017) already found that the frequency effect vanished with real funds over a year.
- **Claim 4 (Custodial drift degrades sincere play).** Conventions subject to unannounced mediator parameter drift decay into defensive, manipulative play, whereas conventions the mediator has committed not to change within a session sustain sincere coordination over repeated rounds.

### 8.2 Five-Arm Multi-Agent Experimental Protocol

To test these claims, I specify a controlled laboratory experiment. The protocol follows experimental-economics norms: no false statements to participants (Charness, Samek, & van de Ven, 2022), power calculated at the triad level (the independent unit of observation is the three-node triad, not the individual participant), and joint surplus preregistered as the primary outcome variable.

```
Experimental Architecture (5-Arm Triadic Protocol):

    [ Participant A ] <═══════> [ Mediator M ] <═══════> [ Participant B ]
                                     ║
               Varying Interface / Representation Condition:
    ═════════════════════════════════╬═════════════════════════════════
    Arm 1: Control (Black-Box Interface; Terminal Payout Only)
    Arm 2: Disclosure (Full Written Rulebook, Margin Formulas, Audit Logs)
    Arm 3: DXD Convention A (Posted-Price Menu / Strategic Offloading)
    Arm 4a: DXD Convention B (Continuous Low-Latency Boundary Contours)
    Arm 4b: Discrete Control (Identical Filtered Feedback Between Rounds)
```

1. Arm 1 (Control — Black-Box): Participants submit raw bids or demands through an unadorned interface, receiving only terminal round payoffs with no explanatory rules.
2. Arm 2 (Literacy — Full Disclosure): Participants receive complete documentation of M's objective function, matching formulas, and an itemized post-round audit log explaining how both inputs were scored. Claim 4's unannounced drift cannot be added to this arm. If the documentation says what M optimizes and M then changes without notice, the documentation becomes a false statement, which the norm cited above forbids (Charness et al., 2022). Drift without notice is defensible in arms that did not promise a complete account of M.
3. Arm 3 (DXD Convention A — Strategic Offloading): Participants interact with personalized menus where dominant strategies are pre-cleared, visually confirming that strategic undercutting has been removed.
4. Arm 4a (DXD Convention B — Continuous Immediacy): Participants receive real-time, pre-commit visual boundary contours that update as they adjust their sliders, showing clearing odds before commitment.
5. Arm 4b (Discrete Control for Convention B): Participants receive identical noise-filtered boundary data, delivered only at discrete intervals between rounds, isolating the effect of temporal immediacy from informational content.

## 9. Conclusion and Scope Boundaries

### 9.1 Synthesis

The argument has three moves. Grahn et al. (2026) and Mourtzis and Tsakalos (2026) state the field, and the person in both reviews still takes something in. Nadella's post keeps the chain and the brake, and leaves the person in the triad to read. The space between words was the change the transition from oracy to literacy already contains, because it changed what the reader had to do (Zhang & Norman, 1994; Hutchins, 1995; Tunbridge, 1992). Vicente and Rasmussen (1992) write that no level of representation is globally superior. The two conventions are candidates for a change of the same kind. Neither has passed the test.

Faced with opaque algorithmic intermediaries, a common response has been to demand more literacy: more documentation, more transparency dashboards, more explainability cards, and more post-hoc audit logs (Ben-Shahar & Schneider, 2011; Regulation (EU) 2019/1150, Art. 5; Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Arts. 14 and 27). It is not the only response. The Digital Services Act already constrains interface design (Arts. 25, 38, and 40).

This is a category error. Rulebooks and rationales treat human beings as readers of algorithms rather than deciders within mediated environments. Navigating irreducible triads requires algorithmacy—an enacted sensibility that operates through epistemic probing and the integration of valid feedback.

Decision Experience Design (DXD) is the name of the search. Strategic offloading and ecological feedback immediacy are two candidates for the space between the words. Either is that moment only if it takes a task off the person in the triad and shows, on the surface, that the task is gone. Whether either one does that is untested. The paper's question stands: what could the spaces-between-words moment be?

### 9.2 Explicit Scope Boundaries

To ensure theoretical precision, this framework establishes five explicit scope boundaries:

1. Analytical Guide, Not Historical Determinism: Word separation is used as an ergonomic guide for one change in what a reader had to do, and not as an explanation of historical literacy (Street, 2003).
2. Asymmetric Empirical Maturity: Convention A rests on experiments in mechanism design that show a real but partial effect (Li, 2017; Gonczarowski et al., 2024). Convention B and the measurement of triadic integrated information remain hypotheses awaiting the five-arm protocol.
3. Choice Architecture Boundary: DXD is a neighbor of nudging and boosting and must be placed against them (Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff, 2017). A nudge alters how choices are presented within an unchanged game; DXD changes what the mechanism requires a person to calculate and shows that it has. The nudge literature classes re-presentation of information with informational interventions, the weaker kind (Mertens, Herberz, Hahnel, & Brosch, 2022), and DXD's claim to differ rests on Claim 2.
4. Theoretical Status of Working Disclosures: The argument that working disclosures succeed insofar as they function as simple perceptual signals is this paper's theoretical claim, not an established consensus in prior literature.
5. Institutional Boundary: Interaction conventions cannot withstand commercial pressure without some external constraint, and the two statutes in §7.3 do not yet supply it for the sellers and riders in the examples. The Carolingian page was not sustained by one church and one court (Westwell, 2018). What a modern convention requires is a rule that removes the operator's power to alter the representation at will. Which rule can do that is open.
