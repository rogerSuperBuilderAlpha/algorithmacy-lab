**D3, dressed in the language of Part 1, as the author asked.** *Drafted by Claude for the author to
accept, change or discard. The last sentence is the author's own, from Part 1, line 140.*

> Disclosing an algorithm does not change a person's ability to navigate it. Disclosure hands the person
> a text—model weights, a saliency map, an activity log, a notice of change—and so asks for a reader.
> That makes it an intervention in literacy. Algorithmacy is exercised elsewhere, in the decisions a
> person makes inside a coordination that a third party keeps adjusting, and no document about the third
> party makes those decisions for them. A transparent triad remains a triad.

The first sentence states the ruling as the author gave it. What the evidence in hand supports is
narrower: Part 1 (line 138) reports that FeedVis and Instawareness raised awareness without producing
critical evaluation or agency, and facet H finds weaker and less consistent effects for aids that hand
the person something to read (table (i)). A first sentence the sources carry: "Disclosing an algorithm
has not been shown to change how well a person navigates it."

**What the rulings do to the argument (Claude's inferences, none sourced).**

- D3 settles the legibility problem better than Claude's recommendation did. Word separation disclosed
  nothing: the letters were the same before and after. It changed the form of the page, which is Zhang
  and Norman's representational effect (1994, p. 89). The paper can oppose disclosure and keep its
  precedent.
- Word separation is still an intervention on the literacy side. D10 answers this: the history guides
  the search for an algorithmacy-side convention and does not supply one.
- D7 and Part 1 line 66 point at the same place. If people come to understand a mediator by deciding
  and taking in the results, and the mediator returns results that are "partial, delayed, and mediated
  through platform metrics" (Part 1, line 66), then a convention that returns the result of a decision
  quickly and plainly is a candidate for the critical move of D6. Facet I tests whether the literature
  supports this.
- D4 needs the post's own split. Chain-of-thought transparency (P9) and two of the seven principles
  are disclosure outright: observe everything (P15) and incident disclosure (P20). Two more are testing
  and audit, which produce evidence for an auditor to read (P16, P18). Three disclose nothing: model
  diversity (P14), independent controls (P17) and containment (P19). The paper can say that everything
  the post offers a *person* is something to read, and that its other controls are held by the
  organisation. That keeps the author's contrast and matches the text.

Also open: the due date, length and rubric for the course paper, and whether the instructor must agree to
the change of topic.

### 2. The page before and after (18%)

- Unspaced writing and who read it. Knox 1968, p. 435: "Ancient books were normally read aloud, but there
  is nothing to show that silent reading of books was anything extraordinary…" `[ok]`; carry the whole
  sentence. Saenger 1997, p. 85: reading rested on a "prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship"
  `[ok]`.
- Romans had word dividers and gave them up. Heilmann 2020, p. 182 `[ok]`. Saenger, p. 11: the ancient
  world "did not possess the desire, characteristic of the modern age, to make reading easier and
  swifter" `[ok]`. The device was known and set aside.
- Who separated words and for whom. Saenger, p. 83: the Book of Mulling (before 692) and Adomnán's *Vita
  Columbae* (before 713) `[ok]`. Parkes 1992, p. 1: learners of Latin as a foreign language `[lib]`.
- The slow spread. Saenger, p. 100: "not adopted by Continental scribes until the mid-tenth century";
  pp. 176–177: standard by the mid-eleventh `[ok]`. Three and a half centuries from Ireland to the
  Continent's norm (before 692 to c. 1050).
- What Saenger claims and what he does not. Speed and reference reading (p. 13); scholasticism (p. 122);
  "not a general history of literacy" (p. x) `[ok]`.
- The dispute, told as a dispute. Vatri: "Saenger's model must be rejected" (2012, p. 639, via Heilmann
  2020, p. 181) `[ok]` for the sentence; Vatri's article `[lib]`. O'Donnell 2000 on the "necessary and
  sufficient" claim `[ok]`.
- A complex of conventions: word order, punctuation, capitals, later tables and running heads `[D6]`.
- *Author's sentence:* "Word separation did not train the populace to read *scriptio continua*; it changed
  the interface so that reading no longer required the training." (`draft.md:263`) `[D2]`.

### 3. What the scribes did, in human-factors terms (18%)

Organised by the course's three headings.

**Perception.**
- Removing spaces costs practised readers of English a third of their reading rate (Winskel et al. 2009,
  p. 344: "an average of 33% decrement") `[ok]`; about half in Rayner et al. 1998 `[abs]`.
- The cost belongs to the missing boundary: alternating bold recovers most of it (Perea and Acha 2009)
  `[abs]`.
- Fluent readers of unspaced Chinese read no faster with spaces: 5,221 ms against 5,239 ms, 16 readers
  (Bai et al. 2008, Table 1) `[ok]`. Thai: "marginally significant", F(1, 7) = 4.21, p < .08, n = 8
  (Kohsom and Gobet 1997) `[ok]`.

**Cognition.**
- The representational effect: "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can
  cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (Zhang and Norman 1994, p. 89) `[ok]`.
- Task transformation: "a memory and scale reading task is transformed into a judgment of spatial
  adjacency"; "Individual pilot memory has not been enhanced" (Hutchins 1995, pp. 283, 282) `[ok]`.
- The work moved to the scribe; it was not removed. Hutchins 1995, p. 280 `[ok]`. `[D2]`

**Human factors and design.**
- Who gains. Learners most, on mixed evidence: Shen et al. 2012 `[abs]`, Blythe et al. 2012 `[abs]`;
  against, Bassetti and Masterson 2012: "no facilitative effects" `[ok]`. `[D10]`
- Costs of an artefact absorbing a task: Risko and Gilbert 2016, Box 3 `[ok]`; Bainbridge's remedy of
  deliberate pacing (1983) `[ok]`.
- The contrast case, set up for section 6: "The human monitor has been given an impossible task"
  (Bainbridge 1983, p. 776) `[ok]`.

### 4. What the precedent does not show (10%)

- It did not spread literacy. Ten histories of literacy name schools, religion, administration, commerce
  and print; one indexes word separation, once (Clanchy, p. 130) (facet C; searched, not read).
- Timing. Cressy 1980: "a reasonable guess might place male illiteracy around 80%" in 1558, five
  centuries after spacing was standard `[ok]`, page `[lib]`.
- An unspaced script learned informally by adults: the Vai (Scribner and Cole 1981) `[ok]`, pages `[lib]`.
- One convention among several, in every parallel case: Guido's staff ("untenable" as invention, Haines
  2008, p. 331 `[ok]`); the 1299 Florentine rule ("a matter of speculation", Nothaft 2020, p. 15 `[ok]`);
  Hangul, marginal for four centuries (facet F; second-tier sources).
- A measured modern parallel from the numeracy line the author already uses: easier formats help
  low-numeracy people decide (Peters et al. 2007 `[abs]`; Hibbard and Peters 2003).
- Street's critique stands: literacy is "a social practice, not simply a technical and neutral skill"
  (Street 2003, p. 77) `[ok]`. `[D1]`

### 5. From the page to the mediator (14%)

- What algorithmacy asks of a person, in the author's current definition
  (`algorithmacy_ot/manuscript/PAPER.md:19-21`): interpret, specify intent, keep track.
- Where the precedent carries: work that a convention could carry is being done by each person, by folk
  theory (Eslami et al. 2016; repo cards).
- Where it breaks, stated in the paper's own voice:
  - Media hold still; mediators adapt. Hutchins 1995, p. 277; Scaife and Rogers 1996, p. 210; Vicente
    and Rasmussen 1992, V.B `[ok]`. Part 1, line 118.
  - The scribe shared the reader's interest; the interface's owner does not. Jensen and Meckling 1976,
    p. 308 `[ok]`; Part 1, line 60. `[D8]`
  - No trained elite exists to learn from. Part 1, lines 66, 138, 156.
  - Algorithmacy is a sensibility of persons; the remedy sits in artefacts. The paper says how the two
    relate.
- *Author's sentence:* "The design object is the engagement layer — where reading and rewriting the shared
  mediator is the fundamental mode rather than a product layer that hides the mediator behind a
  distribution network." (`phd1750_topic_and_rationale.md:52-54`) `[D7]`.

### 6. The insider post, read closely (14%)

- What it gets right, conceded in the body: the model cannot be read (P2, P4); chain-of-thought
  transparency is "not sufficient or dependable" (P9); controls belong outside the model (P11);
  disclosure "to those affected" (P20) `[ok]`.
- Its lineage: the reference monitor (Anderson 1972) and Saltzer and Schroeder 1975, whose list includes
  "psychological acceptability", a human-factors principle the post leaves unused `[ok]`.
- The difference, on three counts `[D4]`:
  1. *Authority.* It rests with "Orgs" (P5, P17). The brake belongs to "an authorized person" (P19), so
     it resembles structural refusal in form only.
  2. *The residual human task.* A person reads "human readable evidence" (P15) and decides when to halt
     (P19). Sustained monitoring is the task people do worst: Bainbridge 1983, p. 776 `[ok]`; Green 2022
     `[ok]`; alert-review studies `[abs]`. Limit: the evidence does not reach after-the-fact
     reconstruction by trained auditors.
  3. *Who is absent.* No user, customer or counterpart appears; "those affected" appear once, after a
     failure (P20).
- Containment as the floor under the paper's proposal, not its opposite.
- The two tests that separate the paper from the post, what task the person keeps and whom the move
  serves, are the paper's own `[inf]`. Say so.

### 7. Candidates for the convention, and the experiment nobody has run (12%)

- Sort candidates by what they do with the work: remove a task by fixing the mediator's rule; hand over
  a finished comparison; hand the person something to read or tune; hand the task to the person's agent;
  remove exposure.
- Rules that remove a strategic task: under a formally equivalent rule, "36% of games do not end in the
  dominant strategy outcome" against 7% when the rule's form makes the dominance obvious (Li 2017, §5.2,
  144 students) `[ok]`. In the field 23% still misreport (Rees-Jones and Skowronek 2018, n = 1,714)
  `[ok]`. Presentation decides who benefits, which is the spacing lesson.
- A finished comparison: 28% against 17% switched plans (Kling et al. 2012, n = 406) `[ok]`.
- Reading aids: mostly satisfaction, several nulls (facet H, table (i)) `[abs]`.
- The task relocates: Arteaga et al. on Chilean school choice `[ok]`.
- The missing experiment `[D9]`: two human parties and a mediator that reads both; one arm fixes the
  mediator's rule, one adds a reading aid, one gives a delegate; novices and experienced users sampled
  separately; outcomes are joint surplus and each party's regret.

### 8. What Decision Experience Design is, on this footing (4%)

- Its object: conventions that carry the work of coordination through an algorithmic third party.
- Its expertise: Part 1, line 150 ("UX designers did not become vision scientists…").
- Its constraint: conventions the mediator's owner cannot withdraw alone `[D8]`.
