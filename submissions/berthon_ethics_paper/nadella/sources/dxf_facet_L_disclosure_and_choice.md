# L — Disclosure and choice: the literatures on disclosure the project has not yet covered

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_07.md); WRONG and OVERSTATED rows corrected in place.*

**What this facet covers.** Section 4 of the author's outline ("the failure of disclosure"), and the ruling behind it (SOURCE.md, D3): "disclosure of the algorithm doesnt affect our ability to navigate it. disclosure is a literacy based intervention, not an algorithmacy based one." Five literatures: (Q1) the law-and-economics and behavioural critique of mandated disclosure, with the targeted-transparency work that says when disclosure works; (Q2) privacy notices and consent, the largest tested case of disclosure; (Q3) nudging and boosting, the nearest rival to "design, don't inform"; (Q4) meta-analyses of explainable-AI and human-AI decision studies; (Q5) any study of disclosure or transparency in a two-sided, mediated or strategic setting with a behavioural outcome.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What do these literatures say about whether disclosure changes what people do, by what mechanism it fails or succeeds, whether the nudge/boost distinction already draws the paper's line, and whether anyone has tested disclosure where two humans coordinate through an algorithm. Where they cut against the paper, I say so.

### Q1. The critique of mandated disclosure

**1. Ben-Shahar, O., & Schneider, C. E. (2011). The failure of mandated disclosure. *University of Pennsylvania Law Review, 159*, 647 ff. (final page and issue number not verified).** Key `benshahar2011failure`.
- *Verified:* Crossref resolves the 2010 SSRN working-paper record (10.2139/ssrn.1567284), which carries the same title and authors. The 2011 article has no DOI that Crossref returns; the Chicago Unbound record gives the volume and page (search 5).
- *Read status:* **read in full**, from a 103-page offprint hosted at ifs.org (a text layer I could not use; I read an OCR of all pages). Page numbers are the law review's own, taken from the running heads. The offprint is not the publisher's page, so check each quotation against the journal.
- *What it argues:* Mandated disclosure is a regulatory technique that "chronically fails." The evidence is that disclosers do not always disclose, disclosees do not read, understand or use, and decisions do not improve; the cause is false assumptions about people, about the decisions, and about the long chain of things that must go right.
- *Quotations.*
  - "Although mandated disclosure addresses a real problem and rests on a plausible assumption, it chronically fails to accomplish its purpose." (p. 651)
  - "We are not asking what information people need to make good decisions. We are asking whether a regulatory technique—mandated disclosure—works." (p. 651)
  - "disclosees often do not read disclosed information, do not understand it when they read it, and do not use it even if they understand it." (p. 665)
  - "Financial literacy education is an important component of the disclosure and informed consent paradigm, and much evidence now shows that this effort has largely failed." (p. 667; the footnote cites Willis 2008, pp. 208–09)
  - On simplification: "for decades experts have labored intelligently and earnestly to present complex information accessibly, and it is now clear that only modest progress is possible." (p. 713) And: "Unfortunately, complexity cannot be explained simply." (p. 713)
  - "Our task is not to propose an alternative." (p. 651) In the conclusion: "We might have had a fifth purpose: to prescribe a regulatory alternative." (p. 742)
  - **The concession.** "brief, simple, easy disclosures are at least preferable ... perhaps to the point of using symbols instead of sentences." Los Angeles grade cards "seem to have influenced consumers—and, in turn, led to cleanlier restaurants. This is information that anyone can understand, and the single datum is enough to affect customers' choice of restaurants." (p. 743)
- *Bearing.* **Supports** D3: the mechanism they give is that disclosure hands a text to a reader and the reader cannot or will not read it, and their treatment of literacy and financial-literacy education as the weak complement is the very pairing the paper draws. **Qualifies** it in two ways. First, they say disclosure "regularly fails", not that it has no effect; their target is a regulatory technique, and they decline to say what replaces it. Second, their positive examples are the Los Angeles grade cards (offered with a hedge: "There is little real evidence, but we find some reason to think", p. 743) and Medicare satisfaction ratings (p. 743), and on pp. 744 and 748 they add that grade cards also work by making disclosers "try to get their grades up", so that "disclosees need not rely on the disclosed data". *My inference, not theirs:* that a grade is a re-representation that removes the reading task, the author's type (i) move. Their own stated reason is that the datum is brief and simple ("information that anyone can understand", p. 743). A paper that quotes them should say they support "the reader cannot be made the remedy" and that their positive examples are brief, symbolic signals whose effect may run partly through the discloser.

**2. Loewenstein, G., Sunstein, C. R., & Golman, R. (2014). Disclosure: Psychology changes everything. *Annual Review of Economics, 6*, 391–419.** Key `loewenstein2014disclosure`. DOI 10.1146/annurev-economics-080213-041341 (Crossref confirms title, authors, volume, pages).
- *Read status:* **read in part** (author copy from CMU, 32 pp.): abstract, §3 opening, §3.8, §§4.1–4.8, §6, and the passages on privacy notices. I did not read §§3.2–3.7 line by line.
- *What it argues:* Disclosure rules work through two channels, the recipients' choices and the disclosers' reactions. Psychology weakens the first (limited and motivated attention, biased probabilities) and often helps the second.
- *Quotations.*
  - "In many cases, disclosure does not much affect the recipients of the information but does significantly affect the behavior of the providers, sometimes for the better and sometimes for the worse." (abstract, p. 391)
  - "A pervasive theme is that disclosure may have little effect on recipients but large effects on providers." (p. 398)
  - "In the classic account of how disclosure works (e.g., Fung et al. 2007), mandated disclosure leads to changes in the behavior of disclosees, which in turn cause disclosers to clean up their act." (p. 403; the telltale-heart effect, §3.8) The next sentences name the Los Angeles hygiene grades as "a case often cited as a paradigm for successful disclosure" and add: "But in many situations (to some extent including the case of restaurant hygiene ratings), an industry response can be found amid little evidence of a consumer response" (p. 403); footnote 8 says Jin and Leslie found the grade cards "also led inspectors to distort their ratings".
  - Kling et al. (held): "Plan switching was 28% in the intervention group, but only 17% in the comparison group ... this intervention combined a number of different features (comparative and personalized information), so we cannot isolate a single mechanism that explains its effectiveness" (p. 407).
  - Choi et al. 2010 and Beshears et al. 2010 on mutual funds: "These findings suggest that making disclosure simple and accessible may provide insufficient help to consumers who lack the tools to process that information." (p. 408)
  - On the privacy notices that "no one reads on Internet sites, the volume of information is overwhelming and not worth the investment one must make to read it." (p. 411)
  - On intermediaries: "Instead of attempting to provide information directly to consumers, disclosure requirements could make information available in standardized formats so that intermediaries can arise to process it, make sense of it, and (perhaps for a fee) provide it in a form that is usable to its end users." (p. 411, §4.6)
  - "policy makers need to be vigilant against the risk that mandatory information disclosure policies will be implemented as a substitute for other, often more effective, regulatory interventions" (p. 412, §4.8)
- *Bearing.* **Supports** D3 on the recipient side, and gives the paper a second effect it can use: disclosure acts on the discloser. Applied to Nadella (P15, P19), the discloser is the model's operator, which is the party who holds the log. **Qualifies** D3 by reporting that "simplified disclosure, standardized disclosure, vivid disclosure, and social comparison information can all be used to enhance the effectiveness of disclosure policies" (abstract, p. 391) and that an intermediary can do the reading for the person. The intermediary route is a form of counter-delegation (type iii), so the review gives the paper a bridge from "disclosure" to its own remedies, and also a warning: once an intermediary reads the disclosure on the person's behalf, the paper's triad has acquired a fourth party. "Doesn't affect" is stronger than their claim, as T §9.2 already noted.

**3. Fung, A., Graham, M., & Weil, D. (2007). *Full disclosure: The perils and promise of transparency*. Cambridge University Press.** Key `fung2007full`. DOI 10.1017/9780521699617 (Crossref confirms).
- *Read status:* **read in part** (full 304-page PDF from the Harvard Ash Center site; read ch. 1, ch. 4 in the sections on user and discloser embeddedness, and Tables 4.2–4.3). Page numbers are the book's printed pages, which fall 20 behind the PDF pages.
- *What it argues:* A "targeted transparency" policy has to put the information inside the user's existing decision routine and the discloser's routine ("double embeddedness"). Whether users act depends on perceived value, compatibility with the routine, and comprehension.
- *Quotations.*
  - "Transparency systems alter decisions only when they provide pertinent information that enables people to substantially improve their choices without imposing significant additional costs." (p. 55; the next sentence contrasts window-front grades in Los Angeles with Internet databases: "the added cost of obtaining new information ... is very low")
  - "For transparency systems to be effective, it is necessary but not sufficient that information become embedded in existing decision-making processes." (p. 55)
  - "Both systems convert data and expert interpretation into simple normative signals such as stars or letter grades." (p. 57; on the Los Angeles hygiene grades and rollover ratings; the text adds that "underlying details can be accessed by those who want to study them")
  - "Information is comprehensible when users have the capacity to relate it to the decisions they face." (p. 59). Workers "were able to grasp only about 60 percent of the information" in hazard data sheets (p. 59).
  - "Highly effective transparency policies, then, are doubly embedded." (p. 65)
- *Bearing.* **Qualifies** the paper's line. Fung et al. give the success condition as a design property of the disclosure (compatibility with the routine, a simple signal at the point of choice), not as reader skill. That is the paper's position about moving work from the reader to the artefact, reached from the transparency side. Their lead success, the Los Angeles grades, is the case Ben-Shahar and Schneider use. Fung et al. themselves say a rating system is "one way to simplify the format of complex information" (p. 57) and that effectiveness needs double embeddedness, so the discloser's response counts as much as the user's (p. 65). *My inference, not theirs:* that the best disclosures therefore work "by re-representation, not by reading". The sources support the first half (simple, low-cost signals); neither states the second, and a letter grade is still read.

**5. Fernandes, D., Lynch, J. G., Jr., & Netemeyer, R. G. (2014). Financial literacy, financial education, and downstream financial behaviors. *Management Science, 60*(8), 1861–1883.** Key `fernandes2014financial`. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1849 (Crossref confirms).
- *Read status:* **read in part**, and only the **June 2013 working-paper version** (103 pp., NEFE report; page numbers are its own). I read the abstract, the decay analysis and the policy discussion. I did not read the three empirical studies.
- *What it argues:* A meta-analysis of financial-literacy interventions finds a small effect that decays.
- *Quotations.*
  - "We find that interventions to improve financial literacy explain only 0.1% of the variance in financial behavior." (abstract, p. 2)
  - "at delays of 20 months or greater, there is no significant effect of even 24 hours of instruction" (p. 14)
  - "Others have advocated defaults, 'nudges', and 'choice architecture' ... as less costly and more effective alternatives to financial education ... But defaults work best when almost all consumers have similar needs. When consumers' needs are heterogeneous, one needs to know something to decide for oneself. Here, 'just-in-time' financial education may have promise, alone and embedded in decision support systems that help select tailored options." (p. 22)
- *Bearing.* The best quantified evidence that "teach the person" is a weak remedy (**supports** the paper), and the authors' own recommendation, education delivered at the moment of decision and embedded in decision support, is a case for a hybrid. They also name the limit of defaults: defaults fail when needs differ. *My inference, not theirs:* the paper's triad is such a case, because each party's intent differs. Treat this as a counter-case to "design, don't educate" for heterogeneous needs.

### Q2. Privacy notices and consent

**8. McDonald, A. M., & Cranor, L. F. (2008). The cost of reading privacy policies. *I/S: A Journal of Law and Policy for the Information Society, 4*(3), 543–568.** Key `mcdonald2008cost`. No DOI (Crossref has none). AUDIT_07 confirmed volume, issue and pages from the journal-paginated copy at the Stanford repository.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (the 22-page author's pre-press version; abstract, method and the cost estimate). The 244 hours is what a person would spend *if* they read every policy word for word at every new site (the authors' own framing: "if website users were to read the privacy policy for each site they visit just once a year"); it is not an observed behaviour.
- *Quotations.* "We calculated the average time to read privacy policies in two ways." "...the national opportunity cost for just the time to read policies is on the order of $781 billion." - *Bearing.* **Supports** the cost side of D3 with a modelled figure for the reader's task. It models time, not behaviour or welfare; it is a cost estimate, not an effect.

### Q3. Nudging, choice architecture and boosting

**14. Hertwig, R., & Grüne-Yanoff, T. (2017). Nudging and boosting: Steering or empowering good decisions. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 12*(6), 973–986.** Key `hertwig2017nudging`. DOI 10.1177/1745691617702496 (Crossref confirms).
- *Read status:* **read in full** (author-hosted PDF, 14 pp.). Page numbers follow the page markers in that copy and are probably right to the page; check them against the journal.
- *What it argues:* Behavioural policy has two families. Nudges "steer people in a particular direction while preserving their freedom of choice"; boosts "foster people's competence to make their own choices". The two differ on seven dimensions: immediate target, research programme, causal pathway, assumed cognitive architecture, reversibility, programmatic ambition, normative implications.
- *Quotations.*
  - "we define boosts as interventions that target competences rather than immediate behavior ... A boost may enlist human cognition (e.g., decision strategies ...), the environment (e.g., information representation or physical environment), or both." (p. 977)
  - "In sum, the SMT nudge does not aim to foster people's competences. Instead, it skillfully designs an external choice architecture ... the nudge approach steers behavior without taking the detour of honing new competences, whereas the boost approach invests in building on and developing people's competences." (p. 978)
  - Reversibility test: "If, ceteris paribus, the policy maker eliminates an efficacious ... behavioral intervention and behavior reverts to its preintervention state, then the policy is likely to be a nudge. If ... behavior persists when an intervention is eliminated, then the policy is more likely to be a boost." (p. 981, the rule begins at the foot of p. 980)
  - Qualification the authors attach to that test: "nudges that affect behavior repeatedly may produce behavioral routines through learning that 'survive' the removal of the nudge in the choice architecture. In such cases, our empirical criterion indicates that the nudge intervention has a boosting 'side effect'" and "The nudge has thus turned into a boost and had lasting effects." (p. 981)
  - On disclosure: "Sunstein (2016) introduced the notion of educative nudges, citing reminders, warnings, and information such as nutrition labels as examples. In our view, educative nudges and short-term boosts largely overlap. Both ... require—in contrast to classic nudges, such as defaults—a modicum of motivation and cognitive skill." And: "The mere provision of information is often not enough. Health statistics or nutritional information, for instance, bring no benefits if they are intransparent (e.g., reliant on conditional probabilities), overwhelming (like software license agreements), or misleading." (p. 977)
  - "competences are often best fostered by redesigning aspects of individuals' external environment or by teaching them how to redesign them." (p. 980)
  - On the 2008 book: "words such as 'competence,' 'knowledge,' 'skills,' and empowerment do not even feature as entries in the book's index." (p. 981)
- *Bearing on Q3.* **Does the distinction draw the paper's line? Partly, and the match is not clean.**
  1. *Where it matches.* The nudge side, "an external choice architecture" that works with the person's processes untouched, is the paper's "change the interface so that reading no longer requires the training". The reversibility test gives the paper a check. *My inference, not theirs:* spacing persists in the page whether or not the reader is trained, and (from the held reading-science results) the benefit goes if the page loses its spaces, which is the nudge signature. The authors' own qualification (p. 981) cuts the other way for H-cause: a nudge met repeatedly can build routines that survive its removal, which is a boosting side effect, so the test cannot settle H-cost against H-cause by itself.
  2. *Where it does not.* Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff report Sunstein's (2016) category of "educative nudges" (reminders, warnings and nutrition labels; Sunstein adds disclosure requirements, p. 981) and say these "largely overlap" with *short-term* boosts: both require "a modicum of motivation and cognitive skill" (p. 977). They then set long-term boosts apart, as aimed at situations "that will not be reached by mandated disclosure requirements, warnings, and labels" (p. 981). The overlap is close to the paper's own D3 claim (disclosure is literacy-based) reached from the behavioural-policy side, in the authors' own terms, so the paper can cite it. But their *boost* is a competence-building programme (risk literacy, financial rules of thumb, representation training) that the paper would also class as literacy. So the boost/nudge split puts "literacy" on the boost side and "interface" on the nudge side only if one forgets that boosts may "enlist ... the environment (e.g., information representation)" (p. 976). The natural-frequency format of entry 15 is a re-representation that is also a boost. In their taxonomy the scribes' space would be a representation that matches the reader's cognitive algorithms and, if it raised reading skill that persisted, a boost; if it only lowered the cost for as long as the page stayed spaced, a nudge. The distinction does not decide the question the author asks, whether the work moves from person to artefact. The relevant axis in this literature is *reversibility*, not *person versus environment*.
  3. *The triad.* Both families assume one chooser and a choice architect whose objective is the chooser's good, or the policymaker's. Neither says what to do when the architect is an adaptive third party with its own objective that reads two choosers. See entry 20.

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. What mechanisms do the mandated-disclosure critics give, and what evidence?

*Mechanisms of failure.* (1) The disclosee does not read: Ben-Shahar and Schneider put it as "do not read ... do not understand ... do not use" (p. 665). (2) Length and complexity: "complexity cannot be explained simply" (p. 713). (3) Attention, motivation and probability errors (Loewenstein et al., §3). (4) Disclosure serves as a substitute for harder regulation (L-S-G p. 412; Ben-Shahar and Schneider pp. 651, 684). (5) The financial-education complement fails: 0.1% of variance in Fernandes et al. (87 effect sizes; r² = .0011), and no significant effect at delays of 20 months or more. *Mechanisms of success.* (1) A brief, simple signal at the point of choice (grades, stars; Fung et al. pp. 55–57; Ben-Shahar and Schneider p. 743, where the restaurant example is hedged: "There is little real evidence"). L-S-G (p. 403 and n. 8) warn that for the restaurant grades the industry response is clearer than the consumer response. (2) Effect on the discloser, not the disclosee (L-S-G p. 403). (3) An intermediary that reads for the person (L-S-G p. 411). (4) Double embeddedness (Fung et al. p. 65). *Evidence.* The critique rests on case surveys and field and lab experiments of mixed design. Kling et al. is a bundle (held). *My inference, not the sources':* the successes the critics name are almost all brief symbolic signals (letter grades) or discloser effects. None of the sources calls them re-representations or says they work "not by reading".

What the paper can keep: the diagnosis that the remedy requires a reader. What it must change: the critics do not say disclosure never works and do not equate disclosure with literacy. Their positive cases resemble the paper's type (i) and (iii) moves (my reading, not theirs). Ben-Shahar and Schneider decline to name what replaces disclosure (pp. 651, 742). Fernandes et al. do name one: "just-in-time" education, alone or embedded in decision support, and machine-readable "Smart Disclosures" read by "infomediaries" (WP p. 22).

### Q2. Privacy notices and consent

Estimated: the time cost of reading (McDonald and Cranor, a modelled 244 hours a year if every policy were read in full). Measured: the dependence of consent on layout (Utz et al.: under 0.1% accept all purposes in the opt-in setting, on one German website; Nouwens et al.: 22–23 points from removing the reject-all button, 40 participants). Not read: Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch's 543-student result [unverified: see AUDIT_07] and Bakos et al.'s clickstream study (abstract: only one or two of every 1,000 shoppers open the license agreement). Together these say that consent tracks the interface even where the notice's topic is constant; the conditions do differ in the choices offered, and Utz et al.'s wording change had a small effect (CV = .08). That fits the paper's claim. It also shows an asymmetry the paper does not mention: the interface lever has been used mostly by the discloser to produce consent. The notice problem is itself a triad (user, site, third-party trackers), and no study here asks what the user can do to the notice, which is the engagement the author wants.

### Q3. Nudge, boost, and the paper's line

The nudge/boost distinction does not draw the paper's line. It draws a nearby line, and the paper should say which. The parts that agree: a nudge changes the choice environment and leaves the person's competence unchanged; the reversibility test points to spacing as nudge-like (my inference; the authors' own qualification, p. 981, is that repeated nudges can turn into boosts, which is the H-cause question); Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff say educative nudges (Sunstein's category, which includes disclosure) "largely overlap" with short-term boosts, both needing "a modicum of motivation and cognitive skill" (p. 977). The parts that do not: a boost can be a re-representation (natural frequencies), so "environment versus person" is not the axis; both literatures assume one chooser and a benevolent or at least non-adaptive architect. Measured effects add a warning: Mertens finds structure beats information (corrected category averages run from d = 0.28 to 0.54; contrast b = 0.19, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.31), and files re-representation ("translation", d = 0.28) under information; Maier shows that, after bias correction, there is evidence *against* an effect for information nudges and only "undecided" evidence for structure nudges, with heterogeneity that leaves room for some effective nudges; Mertens et al.'s reply concedes publication bias; DellaVigna and Linos find 1.4 percentage points at scale in nudge-unit trials, still significant, and attribute about 70 percent of the gap from academic papers to selective publication. "Design the decision environment" in this literature means the arrangement of options for one chooser (defaults, order, effort); the paper's version restructures what a coordinating pair sees of each other through a mediator (Mejia and Parker's field experiment on what the driver sees and when; the phrasing is facet H's). Nobody in the nudge literature I opened treats that as choice architecture, though digital-nudging work (Yeung; Mills and Sætra, unopened) gets near it. I found no use of "decision experience design".

### Q4. Do explanations improve decisions?

Not on average. Schemmer et al.: the explanation estimate is 0.07 SMD (95% CI −0.15 to 0.30; 11 studies), not distinguishable from zero, over the AI prediction alone. Vaccaro et al.: explanation and confidence were not significant moderators in a 106-experiment, 370-effect-size dataset (the authors say "300+ effect sizes" for the moderator result; it is a between-study contrast), and human–AI combinations did worse than the best single party in decision tasks (g = −0.27). The heterogeneity is large in both (Schemmer I² 57% for the AI-versus-XAI contrast; Vaccaro says much is unexplained), so "does not help on average" is correct and "never helps" is not. The held single studies are mixed rather than in agreement: Vasconcelos finds a highlighted-path explanation lowers overreliance only in the hard task; Bucinca finds that cognitive forcing functions (not explanations) beat simple explanations only on wrong-AI questions, and still did worse than no AI there; Poursabzi finds a clearer model left people worse at catching its mistakes in some experiments. This is the strongest quantitative support for D3 in the facet, with the limits that every study is one human and one machine, mostly online and with accuracy as the outcome.

### Q5. Two-sided, mediated or strategic disclosure

The two earlier rounds found none, and so do I for the specific design (an intermediary's rule disclosed to two humans, behaviour of both measured). What exists is adjacent and mostly cuts against "disclosure does not affect behaviour": a sentence about the algorithm changed how people used it by 1.9 times (Cen et al.); removal explanations lowered later removals (Jhaver et al., observational); a message stressing the platform's procedural justice lowered later rule violations against no-message controls in a randomised study (Tyler et al., abstract); introducing a decision-support system for one bargainer made the other demand a better deal, an effect the abstract attributes to perceived unfairness, which interpretability mitigated (Erlei et al.; the abstract does not say that transparency itself changed the demands). Equally, it can hurt: erroneous scores disclosed produced self-fulfilling behaviour (Bauer et al.), and information about the algorithm lowered the quality of strategy (Hagenbach et al.). The honest summary is that disclosure changes behaviour, and often in the wrong direction or on the wrong party. D3 as worded ("doesn't affect") is not supported; "does not reliably help the person coordinate" is.

### Claims in the paper's position that these sources contradict or qualify

1. **"Disclosure of the algorithm doesn't affect our ability to navigate it" (D3).** Contradicted as a claim about behaviour by Cen et al. 2024 (1.9x between the likes-told and dwell-told groups), Jhaver et al. 2019 (observational), Tyler et al. (randomised message, abstract), Erlei et al. (abstract; the behavioural effect on the counterpart follows availability of the system) and Loewenstein et al. (disclosure affects disclosers). Supported as a claim about *performance* by Schemmer, Vaccaro, and the held Poursabzi, Bucinca, Cabrera. The wording needs to say "behaviour" and "performance" apart.
2. **"Disclosure is a literacy-based intervention."** Supported by Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff (educative nudges, a category that includes disclosure on Sunstein's account, "largely overlap" with short-term boosts, p. 977; long-term boosts are set apart, p. 981) and by Ben-Shahar and Schneider's pairing of disclosure and literacy education. Qualified by Fung et al. and Ben-Shahar and Schneider, whose successful disclosures (grades) are brief symbolic signals (that they "work as re-representations" is my reading, not theirs, and both also credit the discloser's response); and by Loewenstein et al., for whom disclosure acts through the discloser.
3. **"Change the interface and the training is not needed."** Qualified by Fernandes et al. (needs differ, so defaults and fixed designs fail; just-in-time education is the authors' recommendation) and by Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff (a boost is the more durable remedy: behaviour is likely to "revert to the prenudging state" when a nudge is removed, p. 981, unless repeated exposure builds routines that survive it).
4. **"Design beats informing."** Mertens supports it (corrected: structure d = 0.54 against information 0.34), though re-representation counts there as information; Maier undermines the evidence for structure (undecided) while finding evidence against information nudges; DellaVigna and Linos show smaller effects at scale but do not compare structure with information.
5. **"Nadella's remedy is disclosure."** Not tested here. Loewenstein et al.'s point that disclosure reaches the discloser suggests the sharper reading: P15 and P19 mostly bind the organisation that holds the log.
6. **"Nobody has tested disclosure in a triad."** Still true for the exact design; Erlei et al. (abstract) is a two-human bargaining experiment with an algorithm available to one side and explanation and interpretability manipulated, and Casella et al. is a two-human experiment with an algorithmic mediator. The claim should be "I found no study that discloses an intermediary's rule to both parties".
