# W — Word spacing, oracy and literacy

*Facet W, round 5, 2026-10-15. Agent-drafted (Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**The abstract sentence this facet serves.** "Seventh-century Irish scribes ... broke up scriptio continua by adding spaces between words (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997). Unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line just to hear where each word ended, scaffolding literacy with oracy; word separation freed literacy by moving boundary detection from the voice to the page, exploiting a representational effect (Zhang & Norman, 1994) that enabled readers to internalize text."

**What this facet covers.** (1) Post-1997 scholarship on the Insular introduction of word separation and on the oralisation thesis, and what of it could be opened. (2) The evidence for and against the claim that unspaced text required reading aloud, from the two founding texts of the reading-aloud consensus (Balogh 1927, Hendrickson 1929) to the classicists' replies already carded in facet B. (3) Reading science on spacing and on the voice in reading: what spaces do for readers of spaced and unspaced scripts, whether boundary detection without spaces is voice work or eye work, and what the inner voice does in silent reading. (4) The oracy/literacy framing: where the word "oracy" comes from, what the literacy theorists claim, and the evidence that word boundaries are partly a literate concept. Facets B, C and D already hold the classicists' dispute, the historians of literacy and the spacing experiments; this facet reads around them and does not repeat their entries.

## Findings by question

### Q1. The best current scholarship on the Insular introduction of word separation and on the oralisation thesis

**What could be opened.** Almost none of the named post-1997 scholarship is open. Parkes's "grammar of legibility" essay (1987, in Maierù, *Grafia e interpunzione*; reprinted in *Scribes, Scripts and Readers*, 1991) and his "Rædan, areccan, smeagan: how the Anglo-Saxons read" (*Anglo-Saxon England* 26, 1997, 1–22) are behind publishers; the Cambridge extract of the latter gives its frame: the inherited *grammaticae officia*, in which *lectio* "was the process whereby a reader had to identify elements of the text — letters, syllables, words and sentences (discretio) — in order to read it aloud according to the accentuation required by the sense (pronuntiatio)" (abstract only). That sentence is the closest thing in the record to the abstract's "sound out the line", and it describes a schooled discipline, not a physiological need. Johnson 2000 (*AJP*), Johnson 2010, Vatri 2012, Gavrilov 1997 and Burnyeat 1997 remain as facet B left them: openings and footnotes only. Bronner, Busch, Fleischer & Poppe (2018), the one recent study of word division in early Irish and German manuscripts, is a De Gruyter chapter whose PDF request returned an empty page; its abstract restates the standard account ("conventionally been associated with the writing habits of Irish scribes and scholars, who learnt Latin as a foreign language and who applied to their writing practice their grammatical knowledge about word classes") and promises counts of division patterns in vernacular manuscripts. Moffat (2011) applies "the principles of Saenger and Parkes" to ogam; Busch & Fleischer (2015) find that in Old High German and Old Saxon before c. 975 "clitics were often written together with their hosts" and that scribal habit mattered more than date or dialect; Moran (2025) treats word separation as a practical problem in ninth-century Irish Greek-Latin bilinguals; O'Sullivan (2017) sets heavily glossed texts against Parkes's grammar of legibility as a different kind of reading (all abstract only).

**What was opened.** Two things. `doyle2025assessment` (read in full) quotes Thurneysen (1946, p. 24): in Old Irish manuscripts "words which are grouped round a single chief stress and have a close syntactic connexion with each other are written as one", and shows editors still disagreeing about where the words are. With `tunbridge1992study` (pp. 89–98) this fixes the Insular move as a learner's analysis of Latin, not a native habit. And the founding texts of the oralisation thesis, `balogh1927voces` and `hendrickson1929ancient`, which were second-hand in facet B, are now read. Both place the end of voiced reading at the Renaissance and the press (Hendrickson pp. 193–194; Balogh pp. 236–237), and neither credits any change in the page before print. Balogh's one passage on *scriptio continua* (pp. 227–228) explains its difficulty by whole-word recognition and "lange Übung", and treats silent reading as the suppression of an instinct, not as the fruit of a layout.

**Verdict for Q1.** The seventh-century Insular dating and the learner's motive stand (Tunbridge; Saenger's ch. 4 per facet A; Bronner's abstract). The oralisation thesis, in its founders' hands, dates the change to c. 1500 and gives it a different cause; its modern form (Saenger) is the one the abstract uses. No post-1997 palaeographer could be read in full this round; Parkes 1997 and Bronner 2018 are the two to get from a library.

### Q2. Did unspaced text require reading aloud? Evidence for and against

**For.** (a) The testimonia: Balogh's collection, Hendrickson's survey, and Busch's 2002 defence (`busch2002lautes`, pp. 8–40) establish that voiced reading was the ancient default and that silence is marked as special (*tacitus*, *sine murmure*). (b) A mechanism: Busch after Raible (p. 20) and Saenger (facet A) hold that voicing gives an unspaced text a second channel. (c) A field case: Scribner & Cole's Vai readers say syllables aloud "until they click into meaningful units" (1978 proxy, p. 456; card `scribner1981psychology`). (d) Cost data: readers of spaced scripts slow by 33–50% without spaces (`winskel2009eye` p. 344; `rayner1998unspaced` abstract) and by 69% in Perea et al. (2015, p. 10, 3,701 against 2,188 ms).

**Against.** (a) The classicists: Knox's unspaced letters read silently in classical Athens (pp. 432–434); Heilmann's "quasi-dogma" and his reading of the laboured-reading passages as being about learners (p. 181, n. 28); Hendrickson's own "only strange, not necessarily difficult" (p. 186) and his "many gradations" (p. 193). (b) The founders' mechanism: Balogh grounds the difficulty of *scriptio continua* in practice and whole-word recognition (pp. 227–228), not in a need to hear. (c) Living unspaced scripts: 46 Chinese readers segment silently by lexical knowledge with the same word-based eye-movement signature as German readers (`li2014reading`); Chinese word-spaced and unspaced text tie (`bai2008reading`, 5,239 against 5,221 ms); Thai adults target word centres by boundary-character frequency (`kasisopa2013eye`, abstract; `kasisopa2016child`, Table 7) and Thai children do the same from Grade 6 at 88–93% comprehension, silently (`kasisopa2016child`, pp. 12–13). (d) The voice is not the detector even when it is used: in reading aloud the eye leads the voice by 16.2 letters at fixation onset and 9.7 at offset, about 561 ms (`laubrock2015eyevoice`), so words are found before they are said. (e) Speech does not mark word ends: "continuous speech lacks directly accessible cues to word boundaries" and pauses "are rare and occur only inconsistently" (`matzinger2021influence`, Introduction); listeners segment by statistics, prosody and the lexicon, and the cue that helps most in the experiments is a 250 ms pause, the auditory analogue of a space. (f) Word boundaries are partly literate: 60 American adults disagreed with written convention about where words end in proportion to their reading level (`barton1980awareness`, Tables 1–3; "number of words" errors 52.5% basic against 17.5% high).

**Verdict for Q2.** The evidence supports "voiced reading was the norm and unspaced text was costly for the unpractised"; it does not support "unspaced text forced a reader to sound out the line just to hear where each word ended". Sounding out finds boundaries only for a reader who already has the words by ear, which is a claim about learners of Latin (Parkes 1997 abstract; Tunbridge; Heilmann n. 28), and for them the mechanism is matching a sounded string to a known form, not hearing a boundary in the signal.

### Q3. Reading science on spacing, and whether spacing shifts segmentation from phonological to visual processing

**Spacing.** Facet D holds the inventory. This round adds: `perea2015colours` (alternating colour recovers all but 4.7% of the cost of removing spaces; the word-frequency effect falls from 85 to 45 ms; landing sites still shift; a 3,000-word training leaves a 9 wpm gap; Thai readers unaffected by colour but slowed by spaces, pp. 10–13), which is the cleanest demonstration that the page needs a visible boundary, not space as such; `kasisopa2016child` (spaces scaffold Thai children's use of first-character cues, pp. 13–18); `li2014reading` (word-based control without spaces). `rayner1998unspaced`, Perea & Acha 2009, Epelboim 1994, Rayner & Pollatsek 1996, Sainio 2007, Veldre 2017 and Kasisopa 2013 stayed closed (abstract only).

**Phonological or visual.** No study was found that measures inner speech or vocalisation as a function of spacing; the question as posed in the brief has no direct test in the open literature I reached. What exists cuts the other way round. Leinenger's review (`leinenger2014phonological`) shows that skilled silent readers of spaced text generate phonological codes within 80–125 ms of a word, before fixation through preview, and later as an articulatory inner voice that rises with difficulty; deaf readers use phonological codes too. The voice therefore did not leave reading when spaces arrived; what spaces remove is a segmentation task that fluent readers of unspaced scripts do visually and lexically (Li 2014; Kasisopa 2013, 2016) and that readers of spaced scripts cannot do without a cue (Perea 2015). Oral reading remains the costlier mode on a spaced page: 31% longer text time and 1.62 against 1.24 fixations per word in adolescents (`krieber2017eye`, Table 1); fixation 253 against 209 ms in young adults (`laubrock2015eyevoice`, Table 1). The learner side is where a phonological-to-visual shift is documented in general, not for spacing: Leinenger's dual-route summary (beginning readers assemble phonology for every word) and Kasisopa's Thai children moving from spaced to unspaced text.

**Verdict for Q3.** "Moving boundary detection ... to the page" is supported as a representational claim with a measured cost; "from the voice" is not supported for fluent readers and untested for learners; "enabled readers to internalize text" is contradicted if it means text without inner speech, and unsupported if it means something else, since no source defines it.

### Q4. The oracy/literacy framing

**The word.** "Oracy" is Andrew Wilkinson's coinage of 1965 ("The concept of oracy", *Educational Review* 17(4), 11–15, abstract only; the Consensus extract shows the piece is a short educationalist's essay) for competence in speaking and listening, by analogy with literacy and numeracy; later usage (Stubbs 1981; Mercer et al. 2017; Alexander 2020; Cushing 2024, all abstract only) keeps that sense, and Cushing traces its 1960s "deficit" foundations. None of the sources read uses "oracy" for the voiced reading of a text; they say "reading aloud", "oral reading", "vocalised reading" (Heilmann), "lautes Lesen" (Balogh, Busch). The abstract's "scaffolding literacy with oracy" therefore uses the word in a new sense, and a reviewer from education will read it as speaking-and-listening skill. "Oralisation" (Saenger, per facet A) or "voiced reading" is what the sources mean.

**The theory.** Ong 1982, Havelock 1963, Goody & Watt 1963, Olson 1994 and Street 1984 are books or closed articles; nothing new was opened. Facet C already records that none of them credits spacing, and that Ong ties legibility and word spacing to print. What this round adds is the empirical side of Olson's "model" thesis (writing provides the categories we use to hear speech): Barton & Hamilton 1980 on adults (read in full); Kolinsky, Cary & Morais 1987 (illiterate adults poor at judging word length), Morais et al. 1986 (phoneme awareness depends on alphabetic literacy), Homer & Olson 1999 (children's concept of word predicted by awareness of the written word), Havron et al. 2016 (literacy improves lexical segmentation in a second language), Veldhuis & Kurvers 2012 (literacy affects off-line word segmentation in Dutch children), all abstract only. The direction is uniform: the word as a segment of speech is in part learned from writing. Street's "ideological model" (facet C; `street2003whats`) stands as the caution against reading any of this as a cognitive great divide; Barton & Hamilton themselves conclude for specific skills, not a changed mind (§5).

**Verdict for Q4.** Use "voiced reading" or "reading aloud" where the abstract says "oracy", or define the term; and note that the boundary the abstract has the voice supply is one the sources show writing taught.

**The most defensible rewrite the sources allow** (agent-drafted for the author to accept, revise or discard; not the author's words):

> Seventh-century Irish scribes, reading Latin as a foreign language, began to separate its words with space (Tunbridge, 1992; Saenger, 1997). For an unpractised reader, an unspaced line was parsed by voicing it and matching the sound to words already known by ear (Parkes, 1997; Busch, 2002); separation put the boundary on the page, where any salient mark will serve (Perea, Tejero & Winskel, 2015), and so removed a segmentation task from the reader without removing the inner voice from reading (Leinenger, 2014).
