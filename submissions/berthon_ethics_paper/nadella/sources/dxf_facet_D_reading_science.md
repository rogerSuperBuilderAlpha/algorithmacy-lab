# D — Reading science: what the space between words does for a reader

*Corrected on 2026-10-12 against `../audit/AUDIT_02.md`: 12 factual fixes.*

*Facet D of the foundation paper for Decision Experience Design. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-12. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Experimental reading research on word spacing: (1) what happens when spaces are removed from a spaced script (English and other alphabetic scripts); (2) what happens when spaces are added to an unspaced script (Chinese, Thai, Japanese, Tibetan, Javanese), for fluent readers, learners and children; (3) spacing and crowding in beginning and dyslexic readers; (4) the perceptual mechanism and the standard figures (perceptual span, saccade length, fixation duration), with sources; (5) the word superiority effect; (6) literacy and the visual word form area (VWFA), under the standing ruling; (7) any experiment that simulates *scriptio continua* in Latin or Greek; (8) a named human-factors construct for a display change that removes a segmentation task from the operator. A final block checks the figures asserted in the two Gemini essays.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Does experimental reading research support H-cost (spacing lowers the cost of reading, most for novices) and H-cause (spacing caused the spread of silent reading and literacy)? What effect sizes can the author quote, and which claims in the Gemini essays survive?

**Standing ruling that governs this facet.** The paper does not claim that a design change restructures cognition. This facet therefore reports what a spacing change does to measured performance (reading time, fixations, accuracy) and treats the neuroscience as background only.

## (b) Entries

Entries are grouped by the eight questions. Each gives the full reference, the bib key, how I verified it exists, read status, what it argues, quotations with locators, and its bearing on H-cost, H-cause and the four problems in `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §4.

### 1. Removing spaces from spaced scripts

**rayner1998unspaced** — Rayner, K., Fischer, M. H., & Pollatsek, A. (1998). Unspaced text interferes with both word identification and eye movement control. *Vision Research, 38*(8), 1129–1144. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989(97)00274-5
- *Verified:* Crossref title match; PubMed PMID 9666972.
- *Read status:* **abstract only** (publisher PDF bot-walled; row 8).
- *What it argues:* Two experiments with normal text, text with spaces removed (filled with x) and text with spaces kept but words flanked by x. Removing space information slows reading, enlarges the word-frequency effect on target words (word identification harmed) and moves initial landing positions (eye-movement control harmed).
- *Quotation (abstract):* "reading rate decreased by approx. 50% when space information was not available". Also: "The removal of space information increased the effect of word frequency on the fixation times for selected target words, indicating that word identification was interfered with by the lack of spaces."
- *Secondhand detail:* Bai et al. 2008 (Introduction) report that without spaces readers "fixated proportionally longer on low-frequency words than on high-frequency words" and "fixated much earlier in the word (as their average saccade lengths were much shorter when the spaces were removed)". Treat as Bai et al.'s account.
- *Bearing:* **Supports H-cost** for readers of spaced scripts: roughly a halving of reading rate when the artefact stops doing the segmenting. It says nothing about where the cost falls in a reader who never had spaces (see entries in section 2). **Complicates** any claim that the cost is only oculomotor.

**pollatsek1982eye** — Pollatsek, A., & Rayner, K. (1982). Eye movement control in reading: The role of word boundaries. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 8*(6), 817–833. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.8.6.817
- *Verified:* Crossref title match.
- *Read status:* **not accessible** (APA; no PubMed abstract; OpenAlex abstract empty). Cited only through Winskel et al. 2009 and Bai et al. 2008, which list it among the studies behind "30–50%" and "up to 50%".
- *Bearing:* none directly. Listed so a human with library access can open it (section d).

**morris1990parafoveal** — Morris, R. K., Rayner, K., & Pollatsek, A. (1990). Eye movement guidance in reading: The role of parafoveal letter and space information. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 16*(2), 268–281. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.16.2.268
- *Verified:* Crossref title match; PubMed PMID 2142198.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *What it argues:* Gaze-contingent manipulation of what the reader can see to the right of fixation (spaces only; spaces then letters; both at once), with the onset delayed 0–250 ms. "Although both letter and space information influenced saccade length and initial landing positions within words, only letter information had an effect on fixation duration." Two processes run in tandem: when to move (letters) and where (letters and spaces).
- *Bearing:* **Supports** a two-part mechanism: spaces serve targeting; word identification serves timing. Names the split that Rayner et al. 1998 and later work test.

**epelboim1994reading** — Epelboim, J., Booth, J. R., & Steinman, R. M. (1994). Reading unspaced text: Implications for theories of reading eye movements. *Vision Research, 34*(13), 1735–1766. https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(94)90130-9
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 7941379.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *What it argues:* High-precision eye-tracking while subjects read spaced and unspaced passages silently and aloud. "Modest increases in fixation durations and decreases in overall reading speed were observed when unspaced texts were read. However, subjects read unspaced texts with the same level of comprehension and percentage of regressions as they read spaced texts." Progressive saccades were shorter in unspaced text, in proportion to the text's higher density; the same oculomotor strategy held "for both silent reading and reading aloud". Conclusion: "Words, not spaces, may serve as the perceptual units that guide the line of sight through the text."
- *Bearing:* **Complicates** H-cost: the authors report a modest cost, not a collapse (the abstract says "subjects"; the 1997 abstract says "skilled readers"). This is the one study I found that tested unspaced text read aloud against read silently (entry 7). The abstract gives no percentage; I did not read the paper.

**raynerpollatsek1996reading** — Rayner, K., & Pollatsek, A. (1996). Reading unspaced text is not easy: Comments on the implications of Epelboim et al.'s (1994) study for models of eye movement control in reading. *Vision Research, 36*(3), 461–465. https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(95)00132-8
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 8746235.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.** The publisher link redirected to a cookie page.
- *Quotation (abstract):* Epelboim et al. argued that "unspaced text is relatively easy to read"; Rayner and Pollatsek "argue that (1) reading unspaced text is not easy for most readers and (2) there are more diagnostic ways to examine the role of spacing".
- *Bearing:* Records the dispute the plan asks for. The reply says "most readers", which points to variation by reader (see Veldre et al. 2017 below).

**epelboim1997fillers** — Epelboim, J., Booth, J. R., Ashkenazy, R., Taleghani, A., & Steinman, R. M. (1997). Fillers and spaces in text: The importance of word recognition during reading. *Vision Research, 37*(20), 2899–2914. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989(97)00095-3
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 9415369.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotations (abstract):* "Surrounding each word with randomly chosen letters, while preserving spaces, slowed reading by 44-75%--as much as, or more than, removing spaces from normal text. Removing spaces from text with Latin-letter fillers slowed reading by only 10-20% more." "fillers in text disrupt reading by affecting word recognition directly, without necessarily affecting the eye movement pattern."
- *Bearing:* **Complicates** the picture of spaces as a pure targeting aid. In this design the cost of junk characters, not the absence of spaces, does most of the damage. Skilled readers read aloud and silently.

**perea2009space** — Perea, M., & Acha, J. (2009). Space information is important for reading. *Vision Research, 49*(15), 1994–2000. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2009.05.009
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 19463847.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotation (abstract):* "Although there was a reading cost in the unspaced sentences relative to the normally written sentences, this cost was much smaller in alternatingbold unspaced sentences than in regular unspaced sentences."
- *Bearing:* **Supports** the segmentation reading of the cost. A different cue (alternating bold) recovers much of what the space did. The cost belongs to missing word boundaries, not to missing whitespace as such.

**sheridan2013unsegmented** — Sheridan, H., Rayner, K., & Reingold, E. M. (2013). Unsegmented text delays word identification: Evidence from a survival analysis of fixation durations. *Visual Cognition, 21*(1), 38–60. https://doi.org/10.1080/13506285.2013.767296
- *Verified:* Crossref; OpenAlex abstract.
- *Read status:* **abstract only.** (Crossref record: vol. 21, issue 1, pp. 38–60.)
- *Quotation (abstract):* "the earliest discernible influence of word frequency was 112 ms from the start of fixation in the normal text condition, and 152 ms in the unsegmented text condition." Random digits stood in for the spaces.
- *Bearing:* **Supports** the word-identification half of the mechanism with a time figure: a 40 ms delay (my subtraction) in the earliest frequency effect. This is the only paper I found that is about "alternating-shade" or unsegmented text from the Sheridan group; I found no Sheridan paper on alternating shades.

**mirault2019reading** — Mirault, J., Snell, J., & Grainger, J. (2019). Reading without spaces revisited: The role of word identification and sentence-level constraints. *Acta Psychologica, 195*, 22–29. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2019.03.001
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 30861421.
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/mirault2019reading.md)**; first read as abstract only (HAL blocked). The card confirms the abstract quotations. The "40–70%" range appears on p. 22, where the authors attribute it to Rayner & Pollatsek (1996) and Rayner et al. (1998), not to their own data.
- *Quotation (abstract):* "the relative ease with which skilled readers can read unspaced text is due to a combination of an increased use of bottom-up word identification in guiding the timing and targeting of eye movements, plus an increased interactivity between word identification and sentence-level processing." And: "the length of the currently fixated word determined the amplitude of forward saccades leaving that word during the reading of unspaced text."
- *Bearing:* **Complicates** the oculomotor story: unspaced text still lets skilled readers target by word length once the word is identified. **Supports** the point that skilled readers adapt (relevant to "reader skill").

**mirault2019precise** — Mirault, J., Snell, J., & Grainger, J. (2019). Reading without spaces: The role of precise letter order. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 81*(3), 846–860. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-018-01648-6
- *Verified:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/mirault2019precise.md)**; first listed as not read. The full text confirms the secondary summary that letter transpositions hurt unspaced reading more, and adds that the interaction was absent in first-fixation and gaze duration and was weaker when boundary letters were frequent.

**veldre2017spelling** — Veldre, A., Drieghe, D., & Andrews, S. (2017). Spelling ability selectively predicts the magnitude of disruption in unspaced text reading. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 43*(9), 1612–1628. https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000425
- *Verified:* Crossref; Consensus abstract. *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotation (abstract):* "Higher reading ability was associated with greater overall reading speed and fluency in all conditions. In contrast, spelling ability selectively modulated the effect of interword spacing with poorer spelling ability predicting greater difficulty across the majority of sentence- and word-level measures."
- *Bearing:* **Supports** "for whom": among skilled adult readers, the cost of removing spaces varies with the quality of lexical representations. Better spellers are "inoculated".

**drieghe2017parafoveal** — Drieghe, D., Fitzsimmons, G., & Liversedge, S. P. (2017). Parafoveal preview effects in reading unspaced text. *JEP: HPP, 43*(10), 1701–1716. https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000441
- *Verified:* Crossref. *Read status:* **abstract only.** Replicates "increased reading times during unspaced reading" and finds "rather limited evidence for more distributed processing".

**christofalos2024impact** — Christofalos, A. L., Arco, L., Laks, K., et al. (2024). The impact of interword spacing on inference processing during text reading: Evidence from eye movements. *Discourse Processes, 62*, 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2024.2413811
- *Verified:* Crossref (first three authors only). *Read status:* **abstract only.** Unspaced passages gave "longer reading times, longer fixation durations, reduced skipping, and shorter saccades", and the effect of inferential constraint seen in spaced passages was "absent for unspaced passages". Bearing: the cost reaches above the word level in the study; one study, abstract only.

**degno2019coregistration** — Degno, F., Loberg, O., Zang, C., Zhang, M., Donnelly, N., & Liversedge, S. P. (2019). A co-registration investigation of inter-word spacing and parafoveal preview: Eye movements and fixation-related potentials. *PLOS ONE, 14*(12), e0225819. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0225819
- *Verified:* Crossref; PMC6919607. *Read status:* **read in part (card: library/degno2019coregistration.md)** (abstract, introduction, participants, discussion paragraphs on the 300–500 ms window). The card adds that "unspaced" means unspaced in the parafoveal preview, so the study does not show how an entire unspaced page reads.
- *What it argues:* 39 Southampton students read English sentences with spaces replaced by random letters, with and without a valid preview of the next word, while eye movements and EEG were recorded.
- *Quotation (Results/Discussion):* in the 300–500 ms window, "none of the differences between unspaced and spaced preview conditions as observed in the FRP topographies reached significance in the permutation analysis."
- *Bearing:* Brain evidence about unspaced text exists only as scalp potentials, not fMRI. The window that corresponds to the N400 showed no reliable spacing effect here. **Complicates** any claim that unspaced text visibly changes meaning-level brain response in alphabetic readers (see section 6).

**ozkan2026wordinitial** — Özkan, Z., & Perea, M. (2026). Word-initial capitalization preserves lexical processing in unspaced text. *Scientific Reports.* https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-67473-2
- *Verified:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/ozkan2026wordinitial.md)**; first read as abstract only (via Consensus). Initial capitals leave the unspaced cost "additive" with word frequency, but "interword spaces provide additional visual and oculomotor cues that capitalization alone does not fully restore." Correction from the card: the design has no lowercase spaced baseline and no unspaced lowercase condition, so it cannot show what capitals restore relative to ordinary text. It shows only that the frequency effect is the same size with and without spaces once capitals are present. The authors say the design "does not test any additional cost or benefit that word-initial capitals themselves may impose relative to ordinary lowercase spaced text" (Discussion, PDF p. 11). The global reading-time cost also remained (about 7%, reviewer arithmetic from Table 2). The earlier "capitals restored lexical processing" reading is withdrawn; the Perea & Acha substitution point stands on its own.

### 2. Adding spaces to unspaced scripts

**bai2008reading** — Bai, X., Yan, G., Liversedge, S. P., Zang, C., & Rayner, K. (2008). Reading spaced and unspaced Chinese text: Evidence from eye movements. *JEP: HPP, 34*(5), 1277–1287. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.34.5.1277
- *Verified:* Crossref; PMC2662925 (NIH author manuscript, no page numbers; I cite section names).
- *Read status:* **read in full (card: library/bai2008reading.md)**; first read in part (abstract, Introduction, Experiment 1 method, global and local results, General Discussion, Tables 1–2). The card adds no contradiction.
- *What it argues:* Sixteen undergraduate native readers of Chinese (Tianjin Normal University), four spacing conditions: normal unspaced, word-spaced, nonword-spaced, single-character-spaced. Experiment 2 used highlighting for the same four conditions.
- *Quotations:* "sentences with unfamiliar word spaced format were as easy to read as visually familiar unspaced text" (abstract). "inserting spaces between words (or highlighting word boundaries) did not facilitate reading Chinese, at least beyond the level observed for normal unspaced text. On the other hand, and perhaps more importantly, demarcating word boundaries either through the use of spaces or highlighting did not interfere with reading" (General Discussion). "text presented with word spacing was as easy to read as normal unspaced text" (Exp. 1, total sentence reading times).
- *Numbers (Table 1, Exp. 1, means):* total sentence reading time 5,221 ms unspaced vs 5,239 ms word-spaced vs 5,867 ms nonword-spaced vs 5,686 ms single-character-spaced; reading speed about 239 characters/min unspaced and word-spaced; mean fixation duration 246 ms unspaced vs 227 ms word-spaced; mean saccade length 2.7 vs 3.6 character spaces; fixations per sentence 17.7 vs 19.3; regressive saccades 3.5 vs 4.0.
- *Bearing:* **Complicates H-cost for fluent readers of an unspaced script**: adding the aid changed fixations and saccade length but not total time. **Supports** the claim that the word is a unit for these readers, and that a *wrong* segmentation cost them about 12% (5,867 vs 5,221 ms; my arithmetic). Also gives the secondhand figure "reading speed typically decreases by up to 50%" for spaced scripts (Introduction). From the card: the null result is on N = 16 (Exp. 1) and N = 24 (Exp. 2) skilled adults only; the "words are the unit" claim rests on the character-spacing and nonword-spacing costs; the source says nothing about learners, so it cannot carry the learner half of P4. Bai found no gain over unspaced text (5,239 ms word-spaced against 5,221 ms unspaced), which agrees with Bassetti & Masterson on no gain.

**zang2013interword** — Zang, C., Liang, F., Bai, X., Yan, G., & Liversedge, S. P. (2013). Interword spacing and landing position effects during Chinese reading in children and adults. *JEP: HPP, 39*(3), 720–734. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030097
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 23067120. *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotation (abstract):* "interword spacing reduced children and adults' first pass reading times and refixation probabilities indicating spaces between words facilitated word identification. Word spacing effects occurred to a similar degree for both children and adults".
- *Bearing:* Spaces helped adults too, in contrast to Bai et al. 2008; children did not benefit more than adults here. **Complicates** "most for novices". The abstract gives no effect sizes and no ages; I did not read the paper.

**shen2012eye** — Shen, D., Liversedge, S. P., Tian, J., Zang, C., Cui, L., Bai, X., Yan, G., & Rayner, K. (2012). Eye movements of second language learners when reading spaced and unspaced Chinese text. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 18*(2), 192–202. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027485
- *Verified:* Crossref; PubMed PMID 22545927. *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotation (abstract):* "Eye movement measures showed least disruption to reading for word-spaced text and longer reading times for unspaced than character-spaced text, with nonword-spaced text yielding the most disruption. These effects were uninfluenced by native language". Four L2 groups (American, Korean, Japanese, Thai).
- *Bearing:* **Supports H-cost for novices**: for second-language learners word spacing was the least disruptive format. Note the native-language null: an L1 with spaces or without did not change it.

**blythe2012inserting** — Blythe, H. I., Liang, F., Zang, C., Wang, J., Yan, G., Bai, X., & Liversedge, S. P. (2012). Inserting spaces into Chinese text helps readers to learn new words: An eye movement study. *Journal of Memory and Language, 67*(2), 241–254. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2012.05.004
- *Verified:* Crossref; Consensus abstract. *Read status:* **abstract only.**
- *Quotation (abstract):* "We recorded adults' and 7- to 10-year-old children's eye movements as they read new 2-character words... In the learning session, participants in the spaced group read the new words more quickly than participants in the unspaced group. Further, children in the spaced group maintained this benefit in the test session (unspaced text)."
- *Bearing:* **Supports** the effect on the cost of *becoming* a reader: spaces helped children learn words and the gain persisted in unspaced text. This is the closest experiment to the novice-cost claim, but it is about vocabulary, not about learning to read. The author's note that this study is about "children learning new words" is correct.

**bai2013interword** — Bai, X., Liang, F., Blythe, H. I., Zang, C., Yan, G., & Liversedge, S. P. (2013). ### 3. Beginning and struggling readers: spacing and crowding

**pelli2008uncrowded** — Pelli, D. G., & Tillman, K. A. (2008). The uncrowded window of object recognition. *Nature Neuroscience, 11*(10), 1129–1135. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2187

- *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract, "The Bouma law", the reading and dyslexia sections, figure captions; not the object-recognition sections in full).
- *Quotations:* "the Bouma law: for an object that can be identified in isolation, our ability to identify it among similar objects depends solely on the ratio of the object spacing to the observer's critical spacing at that location. ## (c) Synthesis

### c-1. What the space does, how big it is, and where it acts

1. **Removing spaces from a spaced script costs roughly a third to a half of reading speed.** Rayner et al. 1998 report "approx. 50%" (abstract). Winskel et al. 2009 measured 33% (36 English monolinguals) and 45% (36 Thai–English bilinguals reading English). The 30–50% range quoted in the Gemini essay is Winskel et al.'s own summary of earlier work (Introduction). Mirault et al. (2019, p. 22) give "about 40% to 70%" slower than normally spaced text, and attribute that range to Rayner & Pollatsek (1996) and Rayner et al. (1998), not to their own data. Epelboim et al. 1994 found only "modest" costs (the abstract says "subjects", not "skilled readers"); Rayner and Pollatsek 1996 replied that unspaced text "is not easy for most readers". The figures differ with the filler used, the readers and the measure; a paper should quote a range with its source, not one number.
2. **The cost has two parts.** Word identification: the frequency effect is larger without spaces (Rayner et al. 1998) and arrives later (112 ms against 152 ms; Sheridan et al. 2013, abstract). Eye guidance: initial landing positions move toward the word's start and saccades shorten (Rayner et al. 1998; Winskel et al. 2009 for English). Morris et al. 1990 separate the roles: letters govern fixation duration; spaces and letters both govern saccade length and landing site. Epelboim et al. 1997 and Winskel et al. 2009 (Thai) argue that word recognition carries most of the load.
3. **Other cues can replace the space.** Alternating bold cut the cost "much" (Perea & Acha 2009, abstract). With initial capitals the word-frequency effect is the same size with and without spaces, and a global reading-time cost of about 7% remains; the design has no lowercase baseline, so it does not show what capitals restore relative to ordinary text (Özkan & Perea 2026, Discussion, PDF p. 11). Kanji does the work of spaces in mixed Japanese (Sainio et al. 2007, abstract). Korean particles do too (Baek et al. 2022, abstract). The space is one way of doing a job, segmentation, and the job can be done other ways.
4. **A mechanism for why a space helps at all.** Letters at word ends are much less crowded: "letters at the ends of words are much less crowded and have a larger uncrowded window" (Pelli & Tillman 2008; an aside in their paper, and the paper has an erratum). I infer, without a test of word spacing, that a space beside a word end is what leaves that side free. Pelli and Tillman's account of reading speed as span times fixation rate (4 per second) puts the space inside a general limit on the span of recognisable letters.

**What this means for the paper's wording.** The data support this statement: *for readers who lack the habit of a script, word spacing speeds identification; for habituated readers it adds little at the level of the whole sentence, and can slow them while the format is unfamiliar, though it can still speed word identification (Thai, Tibetan).* That fits the paper's claim that spacing "changed the interface so that reading no longer required the training" (the author's draft) only in part. The evidence says the aid matters most before the training has happened. It does not say the training was ever unobtainable: fluent Thai, Chinese and Japanese readers show little or no cost from the absence of spaces in their own script.

### c-3. Beginning and struggling readers, crowding and letter spacing

The dyslexia literature is a different question (letter spacing, not word spacing) and its results are unsettled. Zorzi et al. 2012 report that extra-large letter spacing substantially improved text reading in dyslexic children (abstract); "74 children", "factor of two" and "about 11%" [unverified: see AUDIT_02], with the widening including word spaces. Of the replications I reached, two find accuracy gains with no speed gain (Hakvoort et al. 2017; Dotan & Katzir 2018), two find faster reading or word identification (Perea et al. 2012; Stagg & Kiss 2021), and one reports a general gain without separating the two (Duranovic et al. 2018), one finds nothing (Łuniewska et al. 2022: no effect on accuracy, comprehension or speed) and one finds none for beginning readers (van den Boer & Hakvoort 2015). One study (Hakvoort et al. 2017) shows the effect is not specific to dyslexia, and others show it depends on word spacing and default spacing (Slattery et al. 2016; Galliussi et al. 2020). The safest reading is that wider spacing helps weaker readers' accuracy modestly, speed less reliably, and that word spacing is part of the effect. Pelli & Tillman 2008 find that crowding does not account for the whole dyslexic deficit.

### c-4. H-cost and H-cause

**H-cost** (spacing moved parsing work from the reader to the page, lowering the cost of reading and widening the group who could read without long training). *The reading science supports the cost half and gives partial, mixed support to the "most for novices" half.*
- For: a cost of one-third to one-half of speed when spaces are taken from a spaced script; faster word identification (about 40 ms earlier frequency effect); benefits for second-language learners and, in several studies, for the youngest Chinese readers and for low-proficiency Tibetan readers; a gain that shrinks as the format becomes familiar.
- Against or complicating: fluent readers of unspaced Chinese show no net gain from spaces, and fluent Thai readers lose sentence-level speed when spaces appear, though they gain at the word level (Winskel; Chantavarin & Leung 2025), and Tibetan readers gain on global measures (Wang et al. 2024); two studies of Chinese children report no benefit or a cost (Bassetti & Masterson 2012; Lin & Lin 2016 for children without difficulty); Epelboim's group reports only modest costs for skilled English readers; skilled readers adapt (Mirault et al. 2019; Veldre et al. 2017).
- Gaps in the claim: no study I found compares the *time to acquire* reading in spaced against unspaced instruction. Blythe et al. 2012 (new-word learning) and Chen et al. 2021 (format training) are the closest. The effect sizes are seconds per paragraph or tens of percent of reading speed per reader, not differences in who can read.

**H-cause** (word separation caused the spread of silent reading and literacy). *The reading science is silent.* Every study measures speed, fixations or accuracy in a lab in readers who already read. None measures who becomes literate, or whether reading is silent. Fluent unspaced reading in Thai, Chinese, Japanese and Tibetan That literate populations read Thai, Chinese and Japanese without spaces is known independently; these experiments show the cost to such readers of lacking spaces is small or absent. This facet therefore neither supports nor refutes H-cause. It does refute a strong version: that spacing was necessary for fast or private reading. Facet C holds the historical test.

**Problem 2 (spacing as a legibility remedy).** The reading science gives the author a defensible distinction: a space acts on the page, before the reader's effort (proximity grouping, a facet inference; Wagemans et al. 2012 do not discuss word spaces), whereas the aids that need the reader to do more are not in this literature. Nothing here tests the observability side, which belongs to facets E and G.

### c-5. Claims in the Gemini essays

Files: `ux_evolution_scriptio_continua.md` (essay 1) and `ecological_ergonomics_algorithmacy_uxd.md` (essay 2), under `dial_response_algorithmacy/sources/`.

| # | Claim (essay) | Verdict | Source and note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cattell discovered the word superiority effect in 1886, showing "humans process whole words faster than individual letters" (essay 1) | **Correct in part.** Cattell (1886) made the early observation, but the controlled effect is Reicher (1969) and Wheeler (1970); the early work was confounded by guessing. "Faster" is not the measure in the sources I read: the effect is accuracy for letters under brief display. | Jordan et al. 2024 (Introduction; R-WP section); Houpt et al. 2014 (Introduction). Cattell's paper itself not accessible. |
| 2 | The word superiority effect shows "lowercase text is read faster than all-caps text" (essay 1) | **Not found; contradicted in part.** The word superiority literature I read is about words against nonwords and letters, not case. A study of letter case found upper-case read faster than mixed case at small sizes, and the advantage vanished at larger sizes; the abstract calls lowercase's advantage something "thought" by scientists and typographers. | Jordan et al. 2024; Arditi & Cho 2007 (abstract only). I found no source for the lowercase claim. |
| 3 | The "Bouma shape" model, named for Herman Bouma, says readers recognise words by outline (essay 1) | **Not confirmed.** The Bouma source I could reach describes Bouma's 1970 observation as a crowding result: critical spacing about half the eccentricity. I did not find the word-outline model attributed to Bouma in an opened source. | Pelli & Tillman 2008 ("The Bouma law"). Bouma 1970 itself not accessible. |
| 4 | Perceptual span "3 to 4 character spaces to the left... 14 to 15... to the right" (essay 2) | **Confirmed (secondhand).** | Frey et al. 2026; Yu & Kwon 2023; Rayner 1986 abstract (14–15 right). |
| 5 | "ballistic saccades averaging 7 to 9 letter spaces" (essay 2) | **Confirmed (secondhand).** | Wertli et al. 2023. |
| 6 | "fixations lasting 200 to 250 milliseconds" (essay 2) | **Plausible, no source found for the range.** Measured means: 214 and 217 ms (adults, intact viewing); 246 ms (Chinese). | Yu & Kwon 2023; Bai et al. 2008 Table 1. |
| 7 | "regressive saccades—normally comprising 10% to 15% of eye movements" (essay 2) | **Not found.** | Bai et al. 2008 Table 1 gives roughly 20% for unspaced Chinese (my ratio of 3.5 to 17.7). |
| 8 | Removing spaces makes "reading velocity plummet by 30% to 50%" (essay 2) | **Confirmed as a range, with caveats.** Winskel et al. (2009) put it at "30–50%"; Rayner et al. 1998: "approx. 50%"; Winskel's own data 33% and 45%. Mirault et al. (2019, p. 22) quote 40–70%, which they attribute to Rayner & Pollatsek (1996) and Rayner et al. (1998), not to their own data. | See c-1. The range depends on the readers and the filler. |
| 9 | In unspaced text saccades are "erratic" and fixations "swell" (essay 2) | **Partly confirmed.** Landing positions move toward word starts and saccades shorten; the 1998 abstract does not use "erratic". Epelboim et al. 1994 found "modest increases in fixation durations". | Rayner et al. 1998 (abstract); Epelboim et al. 1994 (abstract). |
| 10 | VWFA recognises letters and words "within 150 to 200 milliseconds of fixation" (essay 2) | **Not found.** | — |
| 11 | "Without visual spacing, the ventral stream fails to isolate orthographic entities, kicking processing... into high-load, serial parieto-frontal attentional networks and mandatory auditory loops" (essay 2) | **Not found for word spacing.** Cohen et al. 2008 (abstract) show a shift toward serial dorsal parietal processing for degraded words, with widened letter spacing among the degradations. No fMRI study of unspaced alphabetic text found. The "mandatory auditory loops" are not in any source I read. | Cohen et al. 2008; Zhou et al. 2023 (Chinese). |
| 12 | "fMRI studies show that degraded text... triggering increased N400 electrophysiological responses" (essay 1) | **Contradicted.** N400 is an ERP component recorded with EEG. | Kutas & Federmeier 2011 (abstract and opening). |
| 13 | Reading acquisition makes visual words "invade" cortex "weakly specialized for tools" and "subtly displaces face recognition", pushing it right (essay 1) | **Contested.** Dehaene et al. 2010 report "a small competition with faces"; Dehaene et al. 2015 report face responses "shift towards the right hemisphere". Hervais-Adelman et al. 2019 find "without destructive competition"; Kubota et al. 2024 say longitudinal studies "do not provide support" and point to recycling of cortex that processed "limbs". | Abstracts for Dehaene; Hervais-Adelman and Kubota read in full (cards). |
| 14 | Ancient readers "had to subvocalize... to identify word breaks" and silent reading was rare "because the interface imposed a punishing working-memory bottleneck" (essay 2; essay 1 similar) | **Not tested by the experiments I found.** Unspaced English was read both aloud and silently with "the same oculomotor strategy" in one study (Epelboim et al. 1994, abstract); the evidence on Greek and Latin readers is historical (facet B). | Epelboim et al. 1994 (abstract only). |
| 15 | "Dehaene's... parallel recognition engine requires discrete visual boundaries" (essay 2) | **Not found.** Skilled readers of Thai, Chinese and Japanese read unspaced text fluently. | Bai et al. 2008; Winskel et al. 2009; Sainio et al. 2007. |

Claims the sources contradict, in one list: (i) N400 as an fMRI result; (ii) lowercase advantage as part of the word superiority effect; (iii) "faster" as the word superiority measure; (iv) the "30–50%" as one study's finding; (v) the face-displacement story as settled; (vi) the abstract of Kohsom & Gobet (1997) read as a robust Thai speed-up.

## (d) Gaps and unverified leads

**Sources I could not open, and where a human with library access would find them.**
- Pollatsek & Rayner (1982), *JEP:HPP* 8(6), 817–833, DOI 10.1037/0096-1523.8.6.817 (APA PsycNet). No abstract found.
- Morris, Rayner & Pollatsek (1990), full text (APA PsycNet).
- Rayner, Fischer & Pollatsek (1998), *Vision Research* 38, 1129–1144 (ScienceDirect; open archive but bot-walled). This is the key paper for the 50% figure and the two-part mechanism; read it for the word-length and landing-position effects, the number of subjects, and the reading-rate table.
- Epelboim et al. (1994, 1997) and Rayner & Pollatsek (1996), *Vision Research* (ScienceDirect). The size of the 1994 "modest" cost is not in the abstract.
- Perea & Acha (2009), *Vision Research* 49, 1994–2000 (ScienceDirect).
- Mirault, Snell & Grainger (2019), *Acta Psychologica* 195, 22–29: HAL `hal-02138873` (Anubis wall today). The page-22 quotations and the 40–70% figure are now checked against the full text (card): the range is Rayner & Pollatsek's (1996) and Rayner et al.'s (1998).
- Sheridan, Rayner & Reingold (2013), *Visual Cognition* (T&F); volume and page range unchecked.
- Shen et al. (2012), Zang et al. (2013), Blythe et al. (2012), Bai et al. (2013), Li et al. (2021), Lin & Lin (2016/17): effect sizes, ages and Ns beyond the abstracts. Blythe's adult and child group sizes are the first thing to read.
- Sainio et al. (2007), the reading-time numbers.
- Rayner (1998, *Psych Bull*) and Rayner (2009, *QJEP*): the primary statements of perceptual span, saccade length, fixation duration and regression rates. Until read, the 3–4 left figure, the 7–9 saccade length, the 200–250 ms range and the regression rate stay secondhand or unfound. Clifton et al. (2016), "Eye movements in reading and information processing: Keith Rayner's 40 year legacy", *J. Memory and Language*, may be a shorter route.
- Cattell (1886), *Mind* os-XI(41), 63–65 (OUP, paywalled). Check what Cattell measured and whether he said anything about case.
- Wheeler (1970), *Cognitive Psychology* 1, 59–85.
- Bouma (1970), *Nature* 226, 177–178; and any source for the word-outline ("Bouma shape") model.
- Vatri (2012), *Classical Quarterly* 62, 633–647. Facet B territory.
- Zorzi et al. (2012) full text (own reading, not extractor); Skottun & Skoyles and the reply in PNAS 109(38).
- Price & Devlin (2003); Dehaene et al. (2010, 2015); Dehaene & Cohen (2007): abstracts only. Cohen et al. (2008), Zhou et al. (2023): abstracts only.
- Mirault et al. (2019, *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* 81, 846–860), PMC6407912 (open; I did not read it).

**Gaps in the evidence.**
- No Lao, Khmer or Burmese study found.
- No experiment with Latin or Greek text and trained readers.
- No study of time to acquire reading under spaced against unspaced instruction.
- No study of practice effects on unspaced alphabetic reading beyond Chen et al. 2021 (Chinese, right-to-left) and the Thai-to-English transfer null.
- The VWFA literature contains nothing on word spacing; the single fMRI study of spaced against unspaced text is in Chinese and I read only its abstract.
- Primary sources for the standard figures were not opened (see the table in section 4).
- Where I give "et al." or three authors in an entry, I did not compare the full author list with Crossref.

**Bibliographic checks.** Volume, issue and pages for the journal articles were taken from the Crossref record on 2026-10-12. Exceptions: Bai et al. (2013), Cui (2023), Hervais-Adelman et al. (2019), Frey et al. (2026), Özkan & Perea (2026), Chantavarin et al. (2025) carry no page range in Crossref. Chantavarin et al. were listed by Crossref with two authors; I show "et al." because the full list was not checked.
