# Facet N4: standards, incident disclosure "to those affected", and AI skilling

*Facet N4, round 1, 2026-10-24. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

## What this facet covers

Three questions behind P6 and P20 of Nadella's post and the post's silence on capacity. (1) **Standards:** who writes AI standards (ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF and its generative-AI profile, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21's harmonised standards under AI Act Art 40, the Frontier Model Forum, Partnership on AI, MLCommons), who sits on the committees, and whom the standards bind. (2) **Incident disclosure:** what "disclosure to those affected" means in law: AI Act Art 73 in full, Arts 85-86, the 2026 Omnibus; GDPR Art 34 as contrast; California SB 53; NIS2 Art 23; and, added because it answers O02 directly, the revised Product Liability Directive. For each: does the affected person get notice only, or standing and remedy? (3) **Skilling:** Microsoft's skilling claims and what controlled evidence exists on literacy interventions for laypeople.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Do the standards P6 calls for bind anyone toward the people acted on; does any incident regime give the affected notice with standing; and is there evidence that skilling beside the product builds the capacity of the people acted on?

**Serves:** RESEARCH_PLAN M2 (P6, P20 standards and "industrywide" learnings), M9 (P20 disclosure to those affected), M11 (capacity silence); premises A4, A8; objection O02; ledger E16 (and D05, K12, N17 where noted).

**1. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act).** `eu2024aiact`; card `berthon_ethics_paper/eu2024aiact.md` (upgrade). Verified: OJ L 2024/1689 header in the CELLAR copy. *Read in part* (Arts 3(49), 4, 16, 17(1)(i), 20, 26(5), 40-42, 55(1)(c), all of 73, 85-87, 99; recitals 20, 121, 155, 170, 171). Art 73(1): providers "shall report any serious incident to the market surveillance authorities of the Member States where that incident occurred." Every recipient named in Art 73(1)-(11) is an authority or the Commission. Deployers inform "first the provider" (Art 26(5)). Art 40(1): conformity with OJ-cited harmonised standards gives a presumption of conformity "to the extent that those standards cover those requirements". ## (c) Synthesis

**Q1. Who writes AI standards and whom do they bind?** The drafting bodies are private and industry-weighted. EU law defines a standard as one "with which compliance is not compulsory" (`eu2012standardisation`, Art 2(1)); NIST's two documents call themselves voluntary (`tabassi2023artificial`, p. 2; `nist2024artificial`, p. 1). On composition the only count is an NGO's: 55 per cent corporate and consultancy, 9 per cent civil society among 143 self-reported JTC 21 members, with Microsoft heading at least three national delegations and having edited ISO/IEC 42001 (`corporateeurope2025bias`). The Commission disputes the shares with a questionnaire, but concedes it holds no affiliation data, keeps conflict-of-interest rules out because they "would prevent industry stakeholders who are themselves providers" from taking part, and calls industry's role "central" (`europeancommission2025reply`, pp. 13, 16). Laux et al. (§1) and Gornet and Maxwell (p. 16) give the scholarly version: "Those impacted by the use of AI have no role to play in standardisation".

The binding question splits. ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST RMF and the Frontier Model Forum's work bind no implementer of their own force. EU harmonised standards are different: once cited in the OJ they "form part of EU law owing to [their] legal effects", and departing from them "may prove difficult, or even impossible" (`cjeu2024publicresource`, paras 70, 75); Gornet and Maxwell call them "quasi obligatory" (p. 11). California binds a developer to its own framework, built from "national standards, international standards, and industry-consensus best practices", with Attorney General penalties (`california2025transparency`, 22757.12(a)(1), 22757.15). In both binding cases the party that binds is a public authority, and the person acted on meets the standard only as a presumption to rebut (para 76). As of the Commission's 2025 reply, and the Omnibus of July 2026, no AI Act harmonised standard had been cited in the OJ.

**Q2. What does "disclosure to those affected" mean in law?** In the AI-specific regimes it means disclosure to authorities. AI Act Art 73 reports to market surveillance authorities, the Omnibus reroutes some reports to the AI Office, and SB 53 reports to the Office of Emergency Services, with the reports shielded from public records and enforced "only by the Attorney General". The FMF's own taxonomy draws the same arrow: "industry → authority". NIST notes that "Formal channels do not currently exist". Neighbouring regimes go further. NIS2 tells service recipients about significant incidents "where appropriate" and what self-help steps they can take. The GDPR tells data subjects of high-risk breaches and backs the notice with a judicial remedy, representation and compensation. The revised Product Liability Directive gives the injured a compensation claim for AI software that causes death, injury or data loss, from December 2026. The affected person under the AI Act holds a complaint that is a surveillance input (Art 85) and an explanation (Art 86).

**Q3. AI-skilling claims and evidence.** Microsoft reports skilling as reach and credentials ("engaged ... trained ... credentialed") aimed at employability; I found no Microsoft outcome evaluation and no independent one. The controlled evidence on laypeople is thin and mixed. Chiang and Yin's tutorials helped only those already competent (p. 159); Puppart and Aru's brief text shifted error from over- to under-reliance (n = 36). Connell Pensky et al. report that 90-minute modules with practice built students' checking skills equitably, on an abstract reading. None of these studies trains the people a deployed system acts on, and none is run by a system's operator.

**Claims the sources contradict.**
1. RESEARCH_PLAN M2, "standards bodies bind no implementer": wrong for EU harmonised standards once cited in the OJ (`cjeu2024publicresource`, paras 70, 74-76; `gornet2024european`, p. 11). It holds for ISO/IEC 42001, NIST and the FMF.
2. Any claim that the law never pairs notice to the affected with a remedy: wrong. GDPR Arts 34, 79, 82 do (`eu2016gdpr`); the Product Liability Directive gives a remedy without notice (`eu2024productliability`). The defensible claim is specific to AI incident regimes.
3. The superseded `eu2024aiact` card's caution ("the whole of Art 73 was not read line by line") is resolved: the full article confirms no notice to affected persons.
4. Facet V1, contradiction 1 ("on incident disclosure, the post asks for more than the AI Act") stands, now on the full Art 73 and on SB 53. The paper should keep conceding it.
5. Ledger E14 lists "$4 billion and 20 million credentials as courses beside the product". I found no outcome evidence for these courses; the paper may say Microsoft reports outputs, not that the courses fail.
6. Latham's alert says non-compliance with reporting carries fines "up to €15 million or 3%". Art 73 is not itself in the Art 99(4) list; the route is indirect, through Art 16 and the Art 17(1)(i) quality-management procedures (`eu2024aiact`). Minor; flag if the paper cites a fine for Art 73.
