# N2 — Custody of the medium: the page and the press

*Facet N2, round 1, 2026-10-24. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Who held the conventions of the page and the press, by what instrument, and whether contemporaries objected or a professed standard broke. Four clusters: (1) Saenger 1997 pp. 10-13 and whom word separation served; (2) Carolingian custody of the page (the *Admonitio generalis*, Charlemagne's letters); (3) the English press from the 1586 and 1637 Star Chamber decrees to the 1695 lapse, with Milton re-read in a paginated original-spelling text; (4) the bars on vernacular Bible reading: the 1543 Act and its repeal, Trent's Rule IV and its later history, and the ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts. The search for contemporary objectors to the 1543 Act was the priority. It extends facets B (print), O (custody), A (the record) and J; it does not repeat their reads except where I re-opened a text to check or upgrade it.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Who held the page's conventions and the press, by what instrument, and did contemporaries object or a professed standard break?

**Premises and outline sections served.** A2, A3 (failures: custody by an interested party; the gate held by the few), A5 (whom the scribes' correction served), B1. Outline record moments 1 ("The page takes on the reader's work") and 2 ("The press held by its licensers").

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. Who held the page's conventions, and by what instrument?

In the Carolingian texts the ruler holds the text by letter and capitulary. Charlemagne writes to the religious readers subject to his rule ("religiosis lectoribus nostrae ditioni subiectis"), says he has corrected the whole Bible, corrupted by copyists' ignorance ("librariorum imperitia depravatos"), and fixes the homiliary by his own authority ("nostra ... auctoritate") (Boretius no. 30, pp. 80-81); the *Admonitio* orders the books corrected because men pray badly through uncorrected books ("per inemendatos libros male rogant", c. 72, p. 60); the letter to Baugulf is to be circulated "si gratiam nostram habere vis" (no. 29, p. 79). These texts name the beneficiaries: the lector, the congregation, and in the schools boys of servile condition as well as the free-born ("non solum servilis conditionis infantes, sed etiam ingenuorum filios", c. 72). None mentions word separation. Saenger's p. 11 sentence (ancient elitism kept the costly script) is located, but only in an unauthorised copy, and it describes antiquity's literate class, not a medieval custodian. On what the record shows, the first moment has two custodians the paper must keep apart: the Insular scribes who made the convention (facet A, Saenger pp. 83-91, Tunbridge) and the ruler and church who held correct text on the Continent (this facet, facet O).

### Q2. Who held the press, and by what instrument?

A sequence of instruments gave the press to the Stationers' Company and the bishops: the charter of 1557 (facet B), the Star Chamber decrees of 1566, 1586 and 1637, and the Licensing Act 1662. The 1586 decree froze new presses until the Archbishop and Bishop of London judged the number fit and made breach of the Company's own ordinances a Star Chamber offence (Deazley 1586, section 5). The 1637 decree made entry in the Company's Register a condition of print and capped the trade at "Twentie Master Printers", with vacancies filled by the bishops and High Commissioners (items II, XV). Deazley calls 1637 the Company's "high point" and quotes Loewenstein that nearly "everything in the new decree served the monopolies of the London stationers". The Company sought both decrees.

### Q3. Did contemporaries object?

For the press, yes, repeatedly and from inside the trade and Parliament: the unprivileged printers of 1577 and 1582; Wolfe (1582) and Bourne (1586), who challenged the patents' legality (through Deazley); the junior Stationers in 1643 and Milton in 1644 (facet B; Milton now paginated); and the Commons on 17 April 1695, whose seventeen reasons ended licensing.

For the 1543 Act, **yes, one direct objector exists**: *A Supplication of the Poore Commons* (1546) names "a lawe" that keeps the Scripture from anyone who cannot "spend x. pound by yere", calls it a famishing of souls, asks the King to judge "whither this lawe be indifferent or not", and asks him to "restore vnto vs the Scripture in oure English tonge" (EETS 1871, pp. 64-66). It misstates the Act's criterion (rank and sex, not income). Foxe objects to the same parliament's work as "the advancement of popery, under the colour of religion" but reports the reading bar without separate comment (p. 527). Brinklow objects to clerical and householder withholding around the Act, not to the Act. I found no objection by Cranmer, whose 1540 prologue nonetheless stated the standard the Act broke.

### Q4. Did a professed standard break?

For 1543, yes, in three texts that stated the standard: Cranmer's prologue to the Great Bible ("all manner of persons, men, women ... artificers, husbandmen", p. 121); the King's injunction, which the 1546 petition recalls, that a Bible be set in every church "so that euery man myght frely come to it, and read therin" (p. 67); and the 1543 Act's own preamble, which records that the Bible was set forth "to be reade by his loving Subjectes" before restricting it for the "lower sorte" (p. 896). For Trent, Rule IV's ground ("ob hominum temeritatem") is the standard itself; I found no professed universal standard it broke inside Catholic law.

### Q5. Refutation evidence, sought

- *Licensers served readers or authors.* The patentees argued in 1586 that without privilege "no bookes at all shoulde be printed" because the first printer bears the author's costs (Deazley 1586); Johns (2002, p. 119) says privilege and registration "were designed to protect good works as well as to hinder bad"; Milton accepted "every mans Copy" (p. 34); Parliament in 1642 required the author's "name and consent" (Arber 1868, p. 24). Against this, the Commons in 1695 said the Register let the Company "enter a Title to themselves, and their Friends, for what belongs to, and is the Labour and Right of, others" (reason 3). Readers appear only as buyers forced to pay "the extravagant Price" for "ill and incorrect Editions" (reason 8).
- *Rule IV as access.* The rule licenses reading for the approved; Clement VIII removed the bishops' power to license in 1596 (Reusch pp. 333-334).
- *The Index widely ignored.* Yes, for Rule IV north of the Alps: Serarius (1612) for Germany, Véron (1646) for France (Reusch pp. 335-336). It bound in Spain (total ban from 1551), Portugal, Italy and Belgium.
- *Eisenstein's democratising thesis.* Not read; record only.
- *1543 as protective.* The Act protects the accused (purgation by witnesses, a one-year limit, a month's notice of new doctrine; pp. 896-897); Foxe credits the first. Henry VIII's 1545 speech presents the restriction as order and reverence (Hall p. 866). Nothing protects the barred reader.
- *Villers-Cotterêts.* Its stated ground is the parties' understanding of judgments (arts. 110-111). It reads as a correction under royal custody as much as a failure.

### Claims the sources contradict

1. **Facet B, failure 2: "Who objected at the time. Not found."** Contradicted: *A Supplication of the Poore Commons* (1546), `furnivall1871four` pp. 64-66.
2. **Facet B, search log row 4 and gaps: "Arber vol. II (`transcriptofregi02statuoft`) ... no usable hit" for the 1586 decree.** That item is not Arber vol. II; it is the Eyre and Rivington 1640-1708 transcript, vol. 2 (1913). Arber vol. II is lending-only (`transcriptofregi0000stat`). The 1586 decree is at Arber 2: 807 (Copyright History record).
3. **Facet B and ledger H11, lapse date "1694 (Innis) or 1695 (Johns)".** The Commons refused renewal on 17 April 1695 (`commons1803journal`); Deazley gives "its expiration in May 1695". Innis's 1694 is wrong.
4. **Facet B: "Search-result summaries say the Act was repealed in 1547; unverified."** Confirmed by statute: 1 Edw. VI c. 12, p. 19, and the side-note in vol. 3, p. 893 (OCR quality: paraphrase only).
5. **Outline moment 2 and A2 framing of 1543 as a print-stage institution.** The bar lasted four years (1543-1547) and was repealed by the next reign, not by the barred. It is a real failure by A3's tests but a brief one; the paper should not treat it as a standing print-era regime.
6. **Facet B, Villers-Cotterêts as failure 4 (language bar).** The ordinance's own stated ground is the parties' understanding of judgments written in Latin (Isambert arts. 110-111). Its exclusionary reading depends on Brunot's interpretation (facet B, Martel).
7. **The 1546 petition's own account of the 1543 Act** (income of £10 a year) contradicts the Act's text (rank and sex). The paper must quote the petition's objection, not its description of the law.
