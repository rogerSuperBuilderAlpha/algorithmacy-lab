# Consolidation: the ideas of the eight versions, in one place

*Written by Claude on 2026-10-24, by hand, after reading all eight versions and their notes. None of this prose
is the author's. It does not choose a thesis: it sets out what the versions argue, theme by theme, so that the
author can decide what the new paper carries (`CONFLICTS.md`). Each paragraph ends with the ledger rows it
rests on; the sources, their cards and their flags are in those rows of `CONCEPT_LEDGER.md`. No citation here
lacks a ledger row.*

---

## 1. The construct

Every version names one competence, *algorithmacy*: the capacity to coordinate with other people through an
automated third party that reads all of them and commits outcomes that bind them. The definition barely moves.
The design-ethics draft calls it a communication competency; Part 2 breaks it into interpreting the
intermediary, specifying intent through it and keeping track of it as it changes; the author's drafts make it
"relational" and "binding on both sides"; and v4 widens "both" to "all" so that an agent acting for an
organization counts. What moves is the scope. The author's first draft calls the triad "irreducible"; v2 and v3
confine the term to true triads (a screening vendor between applicant and employer, a credit bureau between
borrower and lender, a ride-hail platform between driver and rider) and set aside a lender scoring its own
applicants as one party judging another. Irreducibility, by the lab's standing rule, is explored rather than
established. [K01, K02]

The word invites the objection that Kamperman and Graff level at "AI literacy," that it uses "literacy
uncritically as a metaphor," and Street's deeper one, that the autonomous model of literacy promises effects
"regardless of the social and economic conditions." The author answered it in his outline: the comparison runs
"across institutional positions within compulsory mediation, not across cognitive mechanisms." Part 2 answers it
differently, by modelling algorithmacy on numeracy, a measurable skill that predicts decisions once general
intelligence is controlled. Version 4 states the same refusal as "institutions, not minds." Only the design-ethics
draft pushes the contrast further, into a category error: design ethics treats algorithmacy as literacy extended,
when the two differ as much as literacy did from oracy, and its two-paradigm table sets static text against an
adaptive, adversarial, gatekeeping mediator. Two of that table's four rows did not survive the record. [K03, K05,
K04]

Those surviving rows lead to the one disanalogy that every version keeps: the medium answers back. A printed
statute, in the author's words, "is physically fixed; its letters do not rearrange themselves based on who is
reading," while an adaptive system updates on the person it reads, so that "the target shifts precisely as the
user adapts." Part 2 turns this into a design rule, backward-compatible updates. Version 4 turns it into the
reason the operator must scaffold: a person can learn a fixed text alone, but not a system that only its operator
can see into. [K09]

The sociology behind the triad comes from Simmel and Brandt. Simmel's third may be an impartial mediator or the
*tertius gaudens* who profits from the split, and a ride-hail platform is matcher, commission-taker and the
driver's counterparty at once. Brandt's sponsors of literacy "regulate, suppress, or withhold literacy—and gain
advantage by it." Her sponsor stands between a learner and literacy; the intermediary stands between two people
and an outcome. The earlier comparisons stop short of that position. Vee sets programming beside mass literacy and
sees power shifting to the skilled; Kamperman and Graff carry the literacy myth to AI literacy; neither treats a
gate, a law against teaching, a binding record, a monopoly or a third party between two people. The gap the
versions claim is there, in the positions. [K06, K07, K08]

The author named those positions in his first sentence of argument: the wrongs "were inflicted by the institutions
that controlled the test, the access, and the record." Claude's introduction had opened on the same three cases —
Knox's convention, Auld's prohibition, the treaty record in *Marshall* — and v2 and v3 add the means and the
promise to make five. The spine's force lies in a claim the history then documents: the examiner, the teacher, the
record-keeper and the owner of the press are separable offices. Version 4 replaces the spine with three wrongs (the
burden, the medium, the missing scaffold), a change `CONFLICTS.md` X01 sets before the author. [K10]

Two further premises recur in every version. The first is that comprehension is not leverage. Douglass wrote that
reading gave him "a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy," and the platform record agrees: knowledge
lets a worker maneuver inside terms she cannot alter. The author's drafts put this as "zero leverage" and "zero
power"; v3 softened it on Cameron's evidence of covert worker tactics. The second is that participation is
mandatory. Part 2 shows exit unavailable or ruinously priced — credit invisibility, a fifteen-second acceptance
window, a single screen across employers, patients never asked — and adds that a person can be bound and still feel
free. [K11, K12]

The design-ethics draft framed the same field from the side of what it fails to promote. AI-ethics guidelines
name non-maleficence far more often than beneficence, and none of five leading design-ethics accounts assigns the
promotive work to the relation between the two people a mediator binds. Its opening turned that into Vallor's own
admission that a "collective agreement on what technologies are for" has no address. Every version, from its own
direction, reaches the same verdict on the field's main remedy: informing the person is necessary and not
sufficient. That verdict is the bridge to the history, which shows what institutions did when informing was all
they offered. [K13, K15, K14]

## 2. The historical record

### 2.1 The five things institutions did with literacy

The author's drafts and v2–v3 document five practices, each the act of a named institution once standing came to
depend on reading. The first is the **gate**. Alabama's convention president told the delegates in 1901 that their
purpose was "to establish white supremacy in this State"; registrars chose the passage and judged the answer, so
that one white applicant passed by writing "FRDUM FOOF SPETGH" while Black applicants were set the Chickasaw School
Fund; and the states that tested for reading had run "separate and inferior schools." Knox himself recorded the
objection that registrar discretion "perpetuates the very form of abuse" and answered it in the name of "the purity
of the ballot." The same device travelled: Natal's 1897 act, Australia's fifty-word dictation test, Brazil's
Saraiva Law and the early constitutions of Latin America. The contemporaries who named it a wrong were presidents
vetoing immigration tests — Cleveland's "misleading test," Wilson's "penalty for lack of opportunity" — and the
voting tests ended by statute in 1965 and 1970. No one ended them by learning to read better. [H01, H02, H03]

The second practice is the **withheld competence**. South Carolina's act of 1740 and the Georgia, Virginia, Alabama
and North Carolina acts of 1829–34 forbade teaching, each more narrowly than its usual summary; Auld told his wife
that reading would "forever unfit him to be a slave"; and Margaret Douglass called the Virginia law "one of the
most inhuman and unjust laws that ever disgraced the statute book." Outside the United States the withholding
attached to reading matter and language: Castile's order against fiction for the Indies, the Russian bans on
Ukrainian, and Phule's appeal to the Queen's Proclamation against caste exclusion from schools. [H04, H05, H06]

The third is the **record over the voice**. That writing outranks speech is a legal choice, not a property of
writing, as Messick's shari'a courts show. Where the common law chose the document, it bound the party that could
not make one: the Supreme Court of Canada observed that the Indian parties to eighteenth-century treaties had no
practical "opportunity to create their own written record" and held that the lower courts "erred" in confining the
treaty to its text. Waitangi shows a third party at work, a missionary who made the Māori text, interpreted
"sentence by sentence," held land and had his bishop's instruction to support cession; whether he deceived is
disputed. Of the five practices this one has the thinnest contemporary objection. [H07, H08]

The fourth is the **monopoly**: the Stationers' charter confining printing to the Company and its patentees, the
Licensing Act of 1662 capping master printers at twenty, Milton against licensing, and the Company's own petition
of 1643 against eleven "Monopolizers of Printing." It carries the least weight of the five, since it rests on one
kingdom and every Protestant state regulated print. The fifth is the **oversold promise**, the only practice with
world-scale actors. Jomtien proposed halving adult illiteracy and the rate fell by about a quarter; Dakar's target
"was not achieved"; 739 million adults still could not read a simple statement in 2024. Beneath the missed targets
lies what Graff named the literacy myth, that literacy "was more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to
those of most individuals within it," and its general form, responsibilization, in which the person who was to
acquire a competence bears the blame for a condition it did not cure. [H11, H13]

### 2.2 What the record does not show

The record limits all five. It is not a survey: the gate is documented for the American South and Natal, the
withholding chiefly for the slave states, the record over the voice for colonial treaties, the monopoly for one
kingdom. Literacy also delivered, and people sought it at cost, from the Lutheran programme that made reading
"everybody's calling and right" (and enforced it) to "a whole race trying to go to school." The charge, as the
author put it, is against "what institutions did with literacy," not against learning to read. Nor did societies
require literacy for standing in general: unskilled work in Graff's Ontario "seldom, if ever" needed it, so the
claim holds only at the particular gates the record documents. The gravest wrong done in schooling's name is not a
sixth practice. Canada's residential schools and the American boarding schools harmed children through removal,
labour, abuse and the suppression of their languages; what transfers to the present is narrower, compulsory
participation and one enforced language of instruction. [H15, H16, H17, H14]

### 2.3 Third parties and their safeguards

The literacy transition had its own third parties, and the record documents the safeguards around them more firmly
than abuse by them. Castile's Pragmática of 1503 required notaries to read their notes "presentes las partes e los
testigos" and a witness to sign for a party who could not; Anglo-Iroquois councils brought in second interpreters
when either side doubted the first; English law gave *non est factum* to the signer misled about the paper; and the
notary's professed duty runs to both parties and to the weaker. The safeguards were imperfect, since Cuzco's
notaries "could manipulate the legal record," but each put someone other than the writer at the point where the
record was made. This finding moves the analogy: binding outcomes are not new, and what differs now is which of
those safeguards has a counterpart. [H10]

### 2.4 Version 4's cases: burden, medium, scaffold

Version 4 reads the passage from speech to writing for three wrongs and finds a case for each. The **burden** falls
on the person who must read. The Requerimiento of 1514 was read aloud in Spanish before an attack, referred its
hearers to writings "which you can see if you wish," assigned them the consequences, and closed on the notary's
testimony that it had been read; Las Casas and Oviedo condemned it, and its author judged it by its form. The common
law reached the same result by the duty to read: in *L'Estrange* the signer was bound whether or not the form was
read to her, and later courts held that a person who cannot read "has a duty to find someone to read the contract
to him." The examiner could also raise the burden, as the neck-verse shows, where the judge chose the passage a
felon read for his life; and under Edward I the Crown's lawyers demanded a written charter for a remembered right
until they conceded "time out of mind." [H18, H19, H20, H21]

The **medium** wrong is a text standing in for what was said. Treaty 6 promised a medicine chest at each agent's
house and wrote one "at the discretion of such Agent," after a reading "first read and explained." The modern jurat
is the same form: a certificate that an affidavit was read to the deponent, who "appeared to understand it." The
**scaffold** was rarely built by the institutions that bound people, and some forbade it; capacity came from below,
in the freedpeople's native schools, or from sponsors with their own ends, from Cuba's campaign primer to Hannah
More's "I allow of no writing for the poor." Plato had put both objections first: letters without instruction make
readers "seem to know many things," and the written word "always needs its father to help it." [H09, H22, H25, H27]

Against these wrongs v4 records a slower history of correction. Some institutions checked understanding, as Spain's
notarial rules and the English probate registrar must; some moved the risk to the drafter, as *Jones v. Meehan*
read a treaty "in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians"; Spain in 2021 let parties
with disabilities use easy-read text and pictograms; and a consent study found that re-reading to a stated standard
did what reading aloud alone did not. Version 4 orders these into four stages — the signature sufficient,
disclosure, the check or the shift of risk, the building of capacity — and finds most institutions never left the
first two. That ordering is the frame for its reading of the present. [H23, H24]

### 2.5 The other historical material

The design-ethics draft read the print revolution against Eisenstein's democratizing story: Johns's fixity as a
social achievement, Innis's monopolies of knowledge, Clanchy's trust moving from persons to documents, and
Havelock's binding constraint, the supply of readers. Part 2 used the scribes' adoption of word spacing as an
analogy and nothing more: the space lowered a cost, it did not invent silent reading, and it served a population
for whom Latin was foreign. Every version that touches the great-divide thesis disavows it, down to Goody's own
later "never a question of crossing a single frontier." Version 4 also meets the objection that chat returns us to
orality: Ong's secondary orality rests "permanently on the use of writing and print," and whatever the interface,
the governance is written. [H12, H26, H28, H29]

## 3. Normative frameworks

The versions judge the past by a narrow standard and describe its positions with a broad one. The standard is
internal critique, three benchmarks the author built on Walzer: a practice failed if contemporaries condemned it,
if the institution broke a standard it professed, or if the outcome fell short of the promise. The versions apply
it to both periods, and Moody-Adams shows that nothing forces the choice: what agents did not do cannot show what
they could not do, affected ignorance is "choosing not to know what one can and should know," and "a culture …
cannot be an 'agent' of anything." Her last point grounds the author's ruling that blame falls on named
legislatures, courts, churches, a company and conference sponsors, with "the global community" kept for the
campaigns that set and missed targets. [N01, N02, N03]

The description comes from republican theory, which the author's second draft introduced. Domination is the
standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily, whether or not anyone uses it; v2 and v3 use it to describe positions,
not to condemn them, and take from Lovett both views of what removes arbitrariness (rules that are common knowledge,
and control by those subject to them) and the warning that being "better able to cope" leaves domination intact.
Muldoon and Raekstad carry the concept to platform work, where accounts are deleted without warning or right of
contest. Young's social-connection model then names who else must act — the employers who adopt a vendor, the
lenders who buy a score, the legislators who could require the duties — without making the operator's duty depend
on it. [N04, N05, N06]

Two alternatives are set aside. Platforms cannot be information fiduciaries, because loyalty cannot run to both
flanks of a triad; the notary's duty was never loyalty but impartiality toward two parties and care for the weaker,
enforced by an office neither controlled. And a right to explanation, whether grounded in trust or now in EU law,
asks what an explanation delivers when nothing else changes. [N07, N08]

The design-ethics draft examined five design-ethics frameworks in turn, each on its own terms before saying where it
ran out. Winner's inherently political technologies leave the enrolled person only the adoption decision, already
spent. Vallor's virtues depend on occasions for practice that a fast mediator removes, and her concrete programme is
protective. Verbeek's ethics is promotive but built for one user and one artifact. Value Sensitive Design lists
welfare and borrows the method for it. Nissenbaum's contextual integrity judges flows, not the determinations that
bind a second person. Part 2 examined a sixth, choice architecture, whose defence needs an avoidable nudge and an
accountable architect; mandatory participation breaks the first and an optimizing system the second, and the
measured effects shrink toward zero once publication bias is corrected. [N09, N10, N11, N12, N13, N14]

Hirschman's exit and voice runs through three versions with a changing role. Part 2 uses it to say that where exit
is closed, voice carries everything and needs both a capacity to speak and some power behind it, while platform
voice is "microphones, not megaphones." Version 2 cast Hirschman as the classical defence of exit; v3 made him an
ally, since he himself names economists' bias toward exit. [N15]

Berthon's own work sits closer to the versions' position than the design-ethics draft allowed. His policy paper
calls digital addiction "both akratic … and engineered," sorts remedies into informing, guiding and restricting,
argues that "public policy solutions are required," and says "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change
behavior." On Claude's reading his remedy follows his reader, the regulator for policy-makers and the firm for
managers. Three sentences of the earlier draft misstated him and Williams, and v4 departs from him in one respect,
by separating education from disclosure. The responsibilization critique backs that departure and threatens it:
Giesler and Veresiu, Willis and Docherty show how teaching the person can move a firm's or a market's harms onto the
person harmed. Section 5 returns to that threat. [N16, N17]

## 4. Present-day evidence

The evidence for the positions comes chiefly from hiring and platform work. Where employers share a screening vendor,
4% of applicants to ten positions were recommended for rejection by all ten, so one reader's judgment follows the
applicant from door to door. Ride-hail drivers had about fifteen seconds to accept a trip without its destination or
fare, Uber claimed its data were "akin to an objective, third-party witness," and drivers who asked received
template replies. An opaque score that replaced a visible rating left workers unable to learn its criteria and
disciplined them through that opacity. And knowledge alone did not end their precarity: data requests corrected
individual errors and left practice unchanged. [E01, E02, E03, E04]

Law now supplies checks, which is why v2 and v3 narrowed the author's claim of "complete absence." The Platform Work
Directive requires a human decision and written reasons on account suspension; the Digital Services Act requires
supervised complaint handling and creates out-of-court bodies; the GDPR gives a right to human intervention; the P2B
Regulation requires reasons and notice; the EU credit directives require "adequate explanations"; and
unconscionability asks whether a party had "a reasonable opportunity to understand." What survives the list is
narrower: no check before the person is bound, no independence at the first instance, and no separation of the
counterparty from the arbiter. The fourth position, the means, stays an open row, since concentrated compute has not
been shown to be held by anything like the Stationers' grant. [E05, E06, E07]

Disclosure fails in measurement as well as in theory. One or two visitors in a thousand opened a licence agreement;
reading every privacy policy would take about 200 hours a year; 98% of one study's participants missed a clause
signing away their first-born. The design-ethics draft adds the bias that falls on people outside any test: word
embeddings with human-like associations, toxicity classifiers that flag African American English up to twice as
often, face classifiers that err up to 34.7% on darker-skinned women. [E08, E09]

Part 2 brought the evidence on skill. Deskilling is a property of design, not of technology: the labour-process
debate settled that much, Bainbridge showed that automation leaves the monitor "an impossible task," and the measured
losses (pilots, endoscopists, accountants) are of the skills a person remains accountable for. The generative-AI
studies point both ways, and the split is the finding: delegating the answer harms later unaided skill, hints and
questions do not. The conversational interface, Part 2 argues, nearly closes the gulf of execution and may widen the
gulf of evaluation, leaving users a verification burden they skip when checking costs more than accepting.
Displaying what a system will and will not do — a behavior description, a highlighted low-confidence number — raised
accuracy where explanation did not. [E10, E11, E12]

Version 4 read the present through one text. Nadella's insider-risk essay offers controls held by the organization
and texts for everyone else, calls chain-of-thought transparency "not sufficient or dependable," and mentions the
people its systems act on once, as recipients of a disclosure after failure. Microsoft's documentation tells users
to "always review and verify," while its own Responsible AI Standard asks whether people "can interpret the relevant
system responses reasonably well." The oversight research says why the verifying user is in trouble: Bainbridge's
monitor, Green's "uninterrogated assumption," Elish's moral crumple zone, and Sudjianto's point that "An experienced
model validator does not become experienced by reading validation reports." The law moved the other way in 2026,
when the Digital Omnibus replaced the AI Act's literacy duty with a duty to "support" literacy and kept the duties to
disclose. [E13, E14, E15, E16]

The learning sciences say how operational skill is acquired: early restriction transfers, computer-based scaffolds
help (g = 0.46), adaptive fading beats permanent prompts on weak evidence, and expertise reversal makes assistance
cheap insurance for novices. Version 4 adds the definition it holds to — support that is interactive, contingent and
withdrawn as the person becomes able — and the modest record of financial education, whose effects decay unless the
teaching sits at the point of decision. [E17, E18]

## 5. What is owed, and by whom

Every version places the duty on the operator, the party that designs, runs and profits from the intermediary,
because the harms come from its positions and not from the user's deficits. The author's first draft states it
plainly: "ethical obligations belong to the designers and operators of the intermediary, not to the individuals
trying to navigate it." Version 2 derives the duty from the three benchmarks, since operators profess non-maleficence
while keeping procedures their subjects cannot foresee or contest, and from republican theory's two requirements.
Version 4 adds that the operator writes the record, chooses the medium and alone can make the competence learnable.
[D01]

The duties themselves differ by version, and `CONFLICTS.md` X02 sets them side by side. Three come from the author's
drafts and run through v3. **Structural refusal** keeps open a route to the same standing that does not pass through
the intermediary's reading, at no penalty, so that no single reader holds the registrar's position. A **declared
operating envelope** binds the operator in advance to limits on what the system may do to the person, stated as
outcomes the person can act on — a pay floor, the information shown before acceptance, the grounds for suspension —
and is only as good as the constraint on changing it. **Counter-delegation** lets the person keep her own record and
bring her own reader, and sends disputes to a body the operator does not control; since a user's software agent is in
law its user's instrument, the technical half is no notary without the binding half. [D02, D03, D04]

Version 4's three duties answer its three wrongs. The operator should **check understanding and carry the risk**,
gathering evidence that overseers can oversee and subjects can interpret, and bearing the cost where understanding
cannot be secured. It should **change the medium**, putting safeguards at the point of use in a form cheaper than the
task. And it should **scaffold inside the system**, with support that fades as the person becomes able and comes
paired with standing. Part 2's five obligations sit between the two sets: display the envelope, give a cheap channel
for intent, make tests cheap, let support track competence, and keep updates backward-compatible. [D06, D07, D08, D09]

Two limits bound every set. Skill alone never discharges the duty, because automation bias "cannot be prevented by
training" and a scaffold without standing teaches a person to work a system that still binds her. And design has an
edge: duties are worth their enforcement, some practices warrant prohibition, and concentration in the means belongs
to competition policy. [D10, D05]

## 6. Meaning, the Context Engine and critical algorithmacy

The author's second draft carried three sections that v2 and v3 cut and that are in scope again by his ruling of
2026-10-24. The first is **meaning**. On Martela and Steger's account opaque mediation breaks coherence, co-opts
purpose and erodes significance; the design-ethics draft found their coherence predictability-shaped and so an ally
of bounded outputs; Part 2 mapped the three dimensions onto predictability, a channel for intent and standing, and
noted that the experiment behind "meaning as coherence" failed to replicate while mattering predicted meaning. Beside
meaning the author set the material politics of artifacts: the script a design writes (Akrich's term, Latour's
"prescription," Verbeek's mediation) and Vallor's virtues of moral attention, humility and wisdom, asked here of the
designer. [M01, M02, M03]

The second is the **AI Context Engine**: four layers of provenance, interpretability, mediation exposure and user
autonomy. The third is **critical-algorithmacy pedagogy**, which would train citizens to find proxy variables, audit
feedback loops, trace provenance and obfuscate when systems coerce. The review found both in tension with the paper's
own argument, since an interpretability layer delivers the data dump the paper condemns and the pedagogy is a
user-side remedy. The transparency-paradox passage beside them rests on another paper's equation, uncited. The
author's thirteen figures and his titles belong here too: the triad diagram, the collapse of safeguards, "The Scribe's
Ghost in the Machine," "Who checks the third party?" `CONFLICTS.md` X10 lists what each needs before it returns.
[M04, M05, M06, M07, M08]

## 7. Objections and replies

Versions 2 and 3 answer three objections, and v4 four more. *The users consented*: consent to an unavoidable gate is
thin, consent to terms does not reach the positions, and a person may consent and be dominated. *The law already
provides*: it does in part, which supports the duties, but its rules are confined, internal at first instance and
without a binding independent party. *The history is ornament*: it shows that the positions are separable, that the
remedies that worked were statutes and courts rather than better readers, and what safeguards societies built.
*A second track costs too much, and the human may be no fairer*: hence no penalty, and the duty denies one reader the
registrar's position without promising a better one. *Envelopes expose secrets and leave domination*: an envelope
discloses committed outputs, not method, and needs a constraint on redeclaring it. [O01, O02, O03, O04, O05]

Version 4's objections press on its own thesis. *The essay is for enterprises*: its staff are the readers, and its
systems act on people outside. *Disclosure respects autonomy*: disclosure is the second of four stages, not the last.
*Scaffolding is responsibilization*: it is not if the scaffold is built into the system, owed rather than offered,
withdrawn only as ability grows, and paired with the operator's check and risk. That last condition carries most of
v4's weight. [O06, O07, O08]

## 8. Limits

The historical cases are narrow — chiefly English-speaking jurisdictions and their empires, with Castile, Brazil,
Russia, India, Scandinavia and Yemen as checks — and uneven in strength, and many books are known through reviews. The
analogy compares positions; it cannot show that any operator means to exclude, and it predicts nothing. The duties are
derived for true triads, and the present-day evidence comes from hiring, ride-hail and platform labour (in v4, from one
essay and one firm's documents). The normative frame is thin by choice. Part 2 adds its own limits: its history bears
no explanatory weight, its learning-sciences transfer is untested on systems that move, and the verification burden
is measured only for programming. Before print, a library must still supply several sources that v3 and v4 hold at
second hand. [L01, L02, L03, L04, L05, L06]

## 9. Propositions and open decisions

Part 2 ends on five propositions, each stated so a named design could refute it: a conversational interface raises
assisted and lowers retained skill; support removed on demonstrated competence beats permanent or absent support; a
scaffold without standing raises skill without autonomy; meaning tracks the envelope, the channel and standing, not
the volume of disclosure; and exposure to failures reduces complacency. The versions also differ on genre and venue,
from a Substack essay to *Business Ethics Quarterly*. These, with the eleven other decisions in `CONFLICTS.md`, are
the author's: which thesis governs, which duties, which history, what the history is for, where the analogy breaks,
how strong the safeguards claim is, how disclosure is charged, what becomes of the scaffold and the parked sections,
which author text governs, and the venue. [M10, M09]
