# 07 — Special-issue editor

*No name for this one. The real editors of this issue are three named people and inventing a fourth
would be in poor taste. Read this as the chair of the issue, reading a submission against the call
she wrote.*

---

## 1. Who I am

I commissioned an issue on theorizing phygital hospitality because the phygital literature had
grown a vocabulary faster than it had grown a theory, and because hospitality scholarship kept
being handed technology papers with the word *welcome* stapled on the front. What I wanted was a
paper that treated the hybrid physical-digital realm as a place with a politics, not as a delivery
channel with better throughput. I came to this submission expecting the usual failure mode, which
is a framework imported wholesale from an adjacent field, dressed in hospitality nouns, with
Lashley cited once in the introduction and never again. That is not what arrived. This paper reads
*Hospitality & Society*'s own scholarship, argues with it, and takes a position the field has not
taken. It is short of the floor, its abstract promises two things the paper does not contain, and
its submission packet points at the wrong sections. Those are all fixable in a fortnight.

## 2. The read

### F1 — The abstract names two constructs the manuscript never introduces. **[FIX]**

The single most damaging thing in the packet. The abstract says:

> It introduces hospitality algorithmacy, a competence guests and employees can develop, and
> distinguishes it from coordinative sovereignty, a standing only organizations can grant.

Highlight four repeats it: 'Hospitality algorithmacy is a competence; coordinative sovereignty is a
standing.' Neither phrase occurs anywhere in `DRAFT.md`. I grepped the whole file. What §5 actually
delivers is the distinction unnamed:

> The distinction can be stated simply: competence is learned, while standing is conferred.

The argument is there and it is good. The names are not. A referee reads the abstract, then reads
for the construct it announces, and finds a sentence instead of a construct. That reads as a paper
that was cut down and whose abstract was not re-read, which is the worst first impression an
otherwise strong submission can make. It also strands the Statement of Contribution, which
carefully avoids the names ('a competence guests and employees can develop from a standing only
organizations can grant') and therefore promises something less than the abstract does. Two
documents, two different papers.

Fix it in the body, not the abstract. The names are worth having: *hospitality algorithmacy* is the
paper's one piece of new vocabulary and I would not give it up to save fifty words. **Cost: ~58
words in §5.** Sentences drafted in part 4.

### F2 — Anonymity: line 3 names the special issue. **[FIX]**

`DRAFT.md` line 3 reads:

> For the *Hospitality & Society* special issue on phygital hospitality.

That is the manuscript naming the issue it is being submitted to, in the third line. Lines 5–8 are
worse: a drafting note that names an author ('Pierre's rewrite of 2026-08-11'), names an internal
file, and describes the paper's own revision history. The formatting checklist in `FRONT_MATTER.md`
says 'Repo-relative markdown links and drafting notes removed', which would catch lines 5–8 if
someone reads it as covering them, but line 3 is not a link and not obviously a drafting note. Make
it explicit. **Cost: minus ~40 words, which the paper can afford to lose.**

I am not troubled by 'Hospitality & Society has already raised the question' in §1. Naming a
journal as a body of scholarship is ordinary practice and in this case it is the paper doing the
thing I most wanted, which is arguing inside the field rather than at it. Keep that sentence.

### F3 — Three names for one object. **[FIX]**

The five things are called *design features* in the §6 heading and again in §8, *design principles*
twice inside §6, and *design affordances* in both the abstract and the Statement of Contribution.
The CFP alignment note calls them affordances too. Pick one and propagate.

I would pick **design principles**. 'Affordance' carries a specific ecological and HCI sense that
the paper does not use and does not want, and §6's five items are plainly normative rather than
dispositional: 'decisions should be explained where they take effect'. That is a principle. A
referee from the HCI side of this issue will make exactly this objection and it costs nothing to
pre-empt. **Cost: zero words, six substitutions.**

### F4 — The abstract promises a definition the paper does not give. **[FIX]**

> the paper gives the neutral case a definition rather than a hedge

Two problems. First, §8 does not define the neutral case. It gives a *range*: 'the same mediation
can enchant, disenchant, or re-enchant, depending on the surrounding arrangements' (De Vos et al.
2024), and 'studies of automation in hospitality report positive, negative, and conditional effects
side by side'. A range of reported findings is not a definition of neutrality. The claim is
undischarged.

Second, and this is the editorial point: 'a definition rather than a hedge' is a sentence addressed
to someone who knows what the previous version said. In a double-anonymized abstract it reads as a
reply to a referee report, and the referee reading it has not written one yet. It is the only
sentence in the packet that breaks frame.

Two routes. Define neutral in §8, which I prefer, or cut the clause. The cut also solves a
mechanical problem: the abstract stands at **199 words against a 200-word ceiling**, so it cannot
absorb a single added word as it stands. **Cost: ~40 words in §8, minus 12 in the abstract.**

### F5 — The load-bearing test is imported without a warrant. **[CONSIDER]**

§4's whole apparatus rests on three conditions:

> Three conditions are particularly important: common understanding, predictability, and
> accountability (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009).

That is the paper's central machinery, and it arrives from coordination research inside a single
parenthesis, with no argument that hospitality is the kind of problem those conditions were
formulated for. I am sensitive to this because it is the failure mode I wrote the call to exclude.
The paper is not guilty of it in substance, since §2 and §3 do real hospitality theory before §4
arrives, but §4 as written *looks* guilty for about half a page, and a hostile referee reads the
look.

The materials to earn the transplant are already in §2. Hospitality is a coordination problem of a
distinctive kind, because the standing §2 describes (asking, declining, requesting an exception)
depends on the parties holding a shared account of what is happening between them. Say that, in
three sentences, and the transplant stops being a borrowing and becomes an argument. **Cost: ~55
words at the head of §4.**

### F6 — Fit: strong on relation, thin on space. **[CONSIDER]**

My call asks for work on 'hybrid physical-digital realms', 'spatial practices', and 'the politics of
space'. Counting the manuscript: *threshold* occurs once, in the Introna sentence; *lobby* never;
*spatial* never. §3's second paragraph is the only passage where the phygital is theorized as a
place rather than as a relay, and it is seven lines long carrying the entire weight of the paper's
claim to be about phygital hospitality at all rather than about algorithmic mediation in general.
It is a good seven lines. It should be twelve.

The concrete thing missing is the observation the paper keeps circling without landing: the
threshold has moved. It used to be a physical location where a person stood and where an arriving
stranger could be seen, and the dual movement has relocated it into an application the guest crosses
before she leaves home. That is a claim about the politics of space and it is the paper's, not
mine. **Cost: ~70 words in §3.**

### F7 — Keywords: correct in form, one wasted slot. **[CONSIDER]**

> phygital hospitality; algorithmic mediation; guest agency; employee discretion; well-being;
> hospitality theory

Six, all one or two words, compliant. But 'hospitality theory' does not discriminate this paper
from anything else I will publish in this issue; it names the journal, not the article. And
'well-being' is currently carried by one paragraph of §8, which is thin support for an indexed
term. If F4 lands and §8's well-being paragraph gets its definition, 'well-being' earns its place.
'Hospitality theory' does not either way. I would swap it for **contestability**, which is the
paper's actual mechanism, appears across §4, §6 and §8, and is a term the algorithmic-accountability
literature will search on. **Cost: zero.**

### F8 — The abstract sells a guest-centric paper; §5 is half about employees. **[CONSIDER]**

The first topic hook in my call is 'power structures and guest–staff relationships in phygitally
mediated hospitality'. §5's employee half, including the lending of competence and the intersection
with employee silence, is a third of what is original here. The abstract mentions discretion once,
in a list. Ten words would fix the balance and would make the fit to the call visible to a referee
who is checking it. Funded by F4's cut. **Cost: ~12 words, net zero on the abstract.**

### F9 — Citation density on this issue's editors. **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**

Six Batat entries, three Mosca, three De Vos, plus Brochado et al. (2026). Thirteen of
seventy-seven references. I know why, because I asked for some of it, and the paper is saved from
the obvious reading by §7, which criticizes Batat and Hammedi (2023) and Batat (2024) by name and
does so substantively. Engagement is not flattery. But do not add one more, and do not let the
revision answer any of my remaining points by citing another one of us.

### F10 — The response letter points at the wrong sections. **[FIX — front matter]**

`RESPONSE_TO_EDITOR.md` is the first thing I read, and its table is, as it says, 'the part editors
actually read'. Every pointer in it is off:

| The letter says | Where it actually is |
|---|---|
| Human-centricity: '§6, in the paragraph on organizational gains, and §8' | §8 only; §6 has no such paragraph |
| Well-being: '§4, final two paragraphs' | §8; §4's final paragraphs are about check-in |
| Industry gains and transition challenges: '§6, two paragraphs before the roadmap' | §8; §6 contains no roadmap |
| Roadmap: '§6, "A phygital hospitality transformation roadmap", with Table 2 setting out three phases' | §8, no such subheading, and Table 2 has **five** stages |

The section map was written against a pre-buildout draft and never re-run. The three-versus-five
discrepancy is the bad one, because it is a factual claim about a table I can see. The letter also
reports 'approximately 8,900 words including references'; `wordcount.py` on the current draft gives
**8,280**. Both numbers are inside the ceiling, so nothing is at risk except the impression that the
authors have checked their own arithmetic. **Cost: fifteen minutes and about six words.**

### On the question mark

The title earns it, but only just, and by implication. §2 sets the question up well ('a third party
had unsettled the question of who hosts'), and §9 answers it obliquely: 'somebody still owes the
guest a welcome.' The paper never states its answer as an answer. One sentence in §9 converts a
rhetorical question into a claim the reader can carry out of the paper, and titles with question
marks live or die on whether that sentence exists. See part 4.

### On what the abstract otherwise promises

I checked the abstract clause by clause against the body. Triadic coordination: §3, delivered. The
dual movement of digitalization and physicalization: §3 ¶2, delivered, thin (F6). Redistribution of
knowledge, discretion and authority: §3, delivered, and the concession that the evidence is uneven
is to the paper's credit. Substitution as a joint withholding of accountability, predictability and
common understanding: §4, delivered and well done. Five design items: §6, delivered under a
different name (F3). Roadmap: §8 and Table 2, delivered. Well-being across individual, collective
and societal levels: §8, individual and collective delivered, *societal* present only as 'what a
community may expect', which is collective again. Seamlessness is not inherently hospitable: §7,
delivered, and the best-argued section in the paper. Two failures out of nine clauses, both named
above. That is a good abstract with two bad sentences in it, not a misleading one.

## 3. Where it is strong

Do not cut any of the following when trimming to make room.

**§4's counterintuitive paragraph.** 'An opaque algorithm can still augment hospitality if its rules
are reasonably stable and an empowered employee can question or override its decisions. By
contrast, a perfectly transparent algorithm can still substitute for hospitality if nobody is
required to answer for its decisions.' This is the paper's best single move. It takes the field's
default remedy, transparency, and shows it is aimed at the wrong thing. Everything else in §4 exists
to make this paragraph possible.

**§2's Airbnb paragraph.** 'Hospitality scholarship, in other words, registered that a third party
had unsettled the question of who hosts before the service literature formalized the triad.' This
is the sentence that tells me the paper belongs in this journal rather than in a service-marketing
one. It gives the field priority over the field the paper is arguing with, and it is true.

**The Garcia et al. (2026) pricing case.** A genuine empirical anchor, used twice, where the
mechanism does exactly the work the paper needs: the rule in operation was not the rule the staff
believed they were working with, so 'nobody at the desk could have explained that decision
accurately, however willing they were'. Conceptual papers usually reach for hypotheticals here.

**§7's self-limiting paragraph.** 'The argument here is therefore a narrow one: the field is far
from uncritical, and the claim is only that one particular design value has so far escaped the
criticism applied to everything around it.' That sentence is why the seamlessness argument will
survive a phygital-service referee who would otherwise take the section personally. It cost the
paper a rhetorical high point and bought it a defensible claim. Correct trade.

**§3's evidence concession.** Back-of-house documentation is clear, front-desk evidence points the
other way, guest-facing evidence barely exists. A paper that concedes this in its own theory section
is a paper I can send to referees without bracing.

**§5's treatment of the Zhou scale.** 'Everything the scale measures is real.' Then the limit. That
is how to differentiate from a published instrument without picking a fight with its authors.

**The five diagnostic questions and Table 1.** Portable, teachable, and the thing practitioners will
actually take. Table 1 is the artefact most likely to be reproduced in someone else's slide deck,
which is how conceptual contributions travel.

## 4. Best spend

The arithmetic first, because the brief undersells the room. `wordcount.py` puts the body at
**5,786** against a ruled budget of **6,850**, and the all-in total at **8,280** against a 9,000
ceiling. So the real constraint is about **700 words all-in**, not 400, and every added word of body
costs one word of that. F2 hands back 40. My four spends below total 223. That lands the body near
6,010 and the total near 8,500, comfortably inside both.

If I could buy only 150, I would buy A and B in full and the first sentence of C.

**A. Name the constructs. §5, immediately after 'competence is learned, while standing is
conferred.' — 58 words.**

> Two names follow from the split. Hospitality algorithmacy is the competence: the capacity of
> guests and employees to interpret an intermediary, to specify a request in terms it can act upon,
> and to adapt when its categories move. Coordinative sovereignty is the standing: the authority to
> be answered, which no amount of algorithmacy confers and only an organization can grant.

This discharges F1, and it discharges it in the place a referee looks. It also gives §8's third
contribution a noun to point at, since that paragraph currently runs to two sentences and reads as
the thin one of the three.

**B. Answer the title. §9, opening the final paragraph. — 57 words.**

> The question in the title has an answer. The host is whoever can be asked and can say yes. A
> system that recognizes, routes, and admits has taken the host's functions; the guest is still
> hosted only by a party able to receive her claim. Where no such party remains, she has been
> processed rather than received.

The existing final paragraph then runs on unchanged and its last line, 'somebody still owes the
guest a welcome', becomes a restatement of a claim rather than the paper's only attempt at one. This
is the sentence that earns the question mark.

**C. Warrant the coordination transplant. §4, before 'Three conditions are particularly
important'. — 55 words.**

> The three conditions come from research on coordination rather than from hospitality, and the
> transplant needs a reason. Hospitality is a coordination problem of a particular kind, because the
> standing described above depends on the parties holding a shared account of what is happening
> between them. A guest who cannot form that account cannot ask to be treated as an exception.

**D. Define the neutral case. §8, in the well-being paragraph, before 'The two levels have rarely
been studied together'. — 40 words.**

> Neutral is not the absence of an effect. It names the case in which mediation leaves a guest's
> standing where it found it: nothing she could previously ask has become unaskable, and nothing
> previously unaskable has become possible.

This discharges F4 and it earns the 'well-being' keyword. It also lets the abstract's twelve-word
meta-clause be replaced with something about the employee half (F8) at no net cost.

**Where I would not spend.** Not on more evidence for the employee-silence link in §5. The paper
already says the interaction 'has not been tested' and calls it an empirical opening, which is the
honest and correct handling. Adding hedges there buys nothing and costs words that §3 needs.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revisions.** I would send this to two referees today if lines 3–8 were gone, and I expect it
back with revisions rather than a rejection. It fits the call on three of four criteria without
strain, it engages this journal's own scholarship as a live interlocutor rather than as
front-matter furniture, and its central distinction is one the field can use.

**The single most important fix: F1.** The abstract announces *hospitality algorithmacy* and
*coordinative sovereignty*; the manuscript contains neither phrase. Fifty-eight words in §5 closes
it, and the fifty-eight words are the best value in the whole revision because they also convert §8's
weakest contribution paragraph into a real one.

## 6. VERIFY

Editorial-risk items only; the reference-list mechanics belong to reviewer 08.

1. **The absence claim in §7.** 'the most recent integrative framework for phygital tourism, built
   from 57 studies and 84 industry cases, contains no discussion of friction at all (Mameli et al.
   2026).' This is the highest-risk sentence in the manuscript: an absence claim about a specific
   paper, refutable by one search of that paper's text, and it appears in the section whose whole
   argument depends on it. Confirm the two counts (57 studies, 84 industry cases) and confirm that
   *friction*, *seam*, and *effort* return nothing substantive.
2. **The two direct quotations in §7.** 'a continuum in terms of customer value from physical to
   digital settings and vice versa' (Batat and Hammedi 2023) and 'fluidifying' (Batat 2024). Both
   are quoted and both currently lack page numbers, which Intellect Harvard requires for direct
   quotation. Verify wording against the sources, not against notes.
3. **Germann Molz (2026).** The paper leans on it hard in §1 and quotes 'guests without hosts'.
   Confirm the issue and pages (16:1, pp. 63–82), and confirm that 'visible, disciplined, and
   ultimately dispensable' is the manuscript's paraphrase and is not sitting close enough to her
   wording to need quotation marks.
4. **Okhuysen and Bechky (2009).** Confirm the three conditions are theirs as named, and confirm
   the term they use for them, since spend C above turns the borrowing into an explicit argument
   and an explicit argument must attribute precisely.
5. **Garcia et al. (2026).** Confirm the mechanism as §4 describes it: that the system biased its
   own recommendations in anticipation of managerial adjustment. This is the paper's best concrete
   case and it is doing double duty in §3 and §4.
6. **Eligibility, not a manuscript matter but a submission one.** `cfp/README.md` flags that the CFP
   restricts full-article submissions to APA summit attendees and that this was never resolved.
   Eighteen days out, that is worth settling before any more effort is spent on the packet.
