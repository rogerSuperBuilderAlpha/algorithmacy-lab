# Review 01 — Ruth Ellery · critical hospitality theory

## 1. Who I am

I work on hospitality as an ethical and spatial relation rather than a service category, which in
practice means Lashley's domains, Derrida's aporia and the arguments that have been made against it,
Bulley on what happens after the crossing, Lynch on the mundane, Germann Molz on network hospitality.
I referee regularly for this journal and I have a standing suspicion of papers that arrive from the
technology literatures carrying the word "hospitality" as a warrant. My test is simple: strip the
word out and see whether the argument still needs it. This paper passes that test. Its central
distinction, between taking over a host's functions and inheriting a host's obligations, is a
hospitality claim and not a dressed-up technology claim, and the competence-versus-standing pair in
Section 5 is the sharpest restatement of the old host-guest asymmetry I have read in a phygital
paper. What frustrates me is that the paper argues in service-research vocabulary while the field's
own vocabulary sits unused in its reference list. Three of my findings are versions of that one
complaint, and all three are cheap to fix.

## 2. The read

### F1 [FIX] — The load-bearing sentence imports unconditional hospitality into a commercial setting

Section 3 rests the whole argument on this: "A welcome is owed simply because someone has arrived,
and it is the only one of these duties that defines the role of the party who owes it."

The second clause I accept. The first, as written, is refuted in the opening week of any critical
hospitality seminar. A hotel owes nothing to everyone who arrives. It owes a welcome to the person
with a reservation and a valid card, and Lashley's commercial domain is *defined* by that condition.
The sentence as it stands states unconditional hospitality and attributes it to a business, and the
referee pool for this special issue is precisely the pool that will notice.

What makes this worth 150 words rather than a deletion is that the paper does not need
unconditionality. It needs a distinction the tradition already supplies: hospitality is conditional
at the threshold and stops being conditional once the guest is across it. Admission is filtered;
reception is not supposed to be. That distinction gives the paper its sharpest possible statement of
its own thesis, which is that algorithmic scoring reopens the condition on the inside of the door
and keeps recomputing it for the length of the stay. Section 4's check-in passage is already halfway
there ("A system that weighs her history or her profile in deciding how she will be received is
doing something categorically different"), but it treats that as a fact about check-in rather than
as the general result it is.

**Cost: ~125 words.** This is also my best spend; see part 4.

### F2 [FIX] — Bulley is cited for the thing his article was written to argue against

Section 1: "A host receives the arriving stranger and assumes some responsibility for the conditions
of that person's welcome (Lashley 2000; Bulley 2015)."

I checked the published abstract. Bulley's argument is that "Derrida's focus on the threshold moment
of sovereign decision" is exactly what international ethics must move beyond, and that we should
instead think hospitality as "a practice that continues once the guest crosses the threshold of the
home," one that manages "the resistant guest" through tactics of containment. The manuscript cites
him for a threshold-moment account of reception. That is his target, not his position.

Two things follow. The citation is wrong and a referee who works on Bulley will say so. More
usefully, the paper is discarding its best ally. Look at where its evidence actually lives: room
allocation, upgrade refusal, dynamic pricing at the desk, the exception that a front-desk employee
can no longer make. Every one of those happens after the guest is inside. The paper has been
describing Bulley's continuing practice in Derrida's threshold vocabulary for nine sections. Fixing
the citation and letting Bulley carry the post-threshold claim costs almost nothing and makes
Section 3's "keeper of the threshold" line land harder, because the reader now understands the
threshold as a relation the guest lives inside rather than a door she passes through once.

**Cost: ~10 words to correct the attribution; the rest is absorbed by the F1 paragraph.**

### F3 [FIX] — Riordan gets one parenthetical, and he is the closest published competitor in this journal

Section 2 makes an absence claim: hospitality scholarship "registered that a third party had
unsettled the question of who hosts before the service literature formalized the triad. It has not
yet asked what that third party owes."

Riordan (2024) is cited once, in Section 1, as scene-setting. From the published abstract, that
article is framed by Lashley's domains, argues that platform-mediated exchange fits none of them and
requires a fourth *virtual domain* covering "exchanges at the threshold of material and virtual
contexts," and introduces the concept of *algorithmic hostility* to describe how platforms and
others exploit contingent workers. An article that coins "algorithmic hostility" in this journal and
gets one passing mention in a paper about algorithmic hospitality will read to a referee as unread.

The paper's defence is available and it is a good one: Riordan's hostility runs platform-to-worker
and describes conduct, whereas this paper asks about obligation and asks it on the guest's side.
Put it on the page. And take the gift while you are there. Riordan's virtual domain gives the
algorithmic intermediary a location in the field's own scheme, which is worth more to this
manuscript than another sentence about the triad.

**Cost: ~45 words net.**

### F4 [CONSIDER] — Lashley is cited three times and his domains never appear

The paper says "commercial hospitality" repeatedly as though it were self-evident. In this journal
it is a term of art with a source. Naming the domains once, at the point where F3's sentence already
names them, costs nothing extra and discharges the editor's engagement-with-the-field test in the
same breath. Fold it into F3.

**Cost: 0 if merged with F3.**

### F5 [CONSIDER] — Derrida decorates

He appears once, in Section 2, in a citation parenthesis, supporting the claim that ethical
traditions emphasize a "mutual relationship, even if the relationship is unequal or temporary."
Lashley carries that claim alone. And mutuality is an odd thing to want from *Of Hospitality*, whose
organizing structure is the aporia between the unconditional Law of hospitality and the conditional
laws that betray it; unconditional welcome in that text is emphatically not reciprocal. A single
decorative Derrida is a tell in my field, and this one sits in the weakest available position.

The remedy is promotion, not addition. Derrida is load-bearing at exactly one place in this paper,
and it is F1. Move him there and the charge evaporates.

**Cost: 0. Absorbed by the best spend.**

### F6 [CONSIDER] — The Manfreda and Harkison characterization may be too strong

Section 2: the study "carefully describes this conduct as reciprocation driven by gratitude rather
than by any obligation." The published abstract situates the model's contribution within "social
exchange and reciprocity." A reciprocity norm is standardly an obligation and not a sentiment, so
if the article grounds itself there, the manuscript's own cited source has partly asked the question
the manuscript says nobody asks. I could not read past the abstract. Either soften to "describes
this conduct as reciprocation rather than as a duty owed to a commercial host," or quote the
article's own wording.

**Cost: ~15 words, or a swap of equal length.**

### F7 [CONSIDER] — Lynch 2017 is doing work it may not support

Section 2: "A guest can ask questions, decline an offer, request an explanation, challenge a
decision, or ask to be treated as an exception to an ordinary rule (Lynch 2017; Beatty et al. 2016)."
Beatty et al. covers the exception. Lynch's mundane welcome, from its abstract, concerns everyday
encounters with strangers and elaborates "hospitality life politics," which is a claim about
welcomers in ordinary life rather than about guest prerogatives inside a commercial encounter. If no
passage supports the list, drop him here; he is better used elsewhere and Lynch et al. (2011)
already carries the relational claim in Section 1.

**Cost: 0.**

### F8 [FIX] — Anonymity, housekeeping

Line 3 names the journal and the special issue outright: "For the *Hospitality & Society* special
issue on phygital hospitality." Lines 5 to 8 are an internal production note. Both go.

I looked hard at Section 1's "*Hospitality & Society* has already raised the question of what
happens to hospitality when platforms make the human host less visible" and concluded it is fine.
It cites a journal's own conversation, which reveals nothing about authorship and reads as ordinary
scholarly siting. Do not over-correct and lose the fit signal.

**Cost: deletion only.**

## 3. Where it is strong

Do not cut any of the following when trimming.

The competence-and-standing pair in Section 5 is the best idea in the paper and "Skill cannot
manufacture a counterparty" is the single sentence I would keep over every other. It is a
hospitality claim in seven words and it will be quoted.

Section 4's refusal to let transparency be sufficient — "a perfectly transparent algorithm can still
substitute for hospitality if nobody is required to answer for its decisions" — arrives at a
critical-theory conclusion by a service-research route, which is the hardest thing this paper
attempts and the thing it does best.

The scripting passage in Section 7 is the finest writing here. "Algorithmic personalization
industrializes the scripting while removing the improvising person," followed by the guest who fails
to fit "because of her body, her documents, her language," is critical hospitality without the
apparatus, and the Nguyen-to-Costanza-Chock move earns it.

The remedy critique that closes Section 7 is the most original thing in the paper and I suspect the
author does not know it. Noticing that the reputational fix for discrimination "works through the
same instrument as the harm," and that it relocates disadvantage onto the guest who has no record,
is a genuine contribution to a literature that has been recommending more reviews for a decade.

Section 3's four sources of duty — care from need, fiduciary from trust, governance from risk,
welcome from arrival — is a real taxonomy and not a rhetorical figure, and it survives F1 intact.
The fourth item needs the qualification; the structure does not.

Finally, the paper's evenhandedness about its own evidence in Section 3 ("The evidence for this
shift is uneven") is the reason I trust the rest of it.

## 4. Best spend — 125 of the 400 words

Put them in Section 3, immediately after "A welcome is owed simply because someone has arrived, and
it is the only one of these duties that defines the role of the party who owes it." This single
insertion discharges F1, F2 and F5 together, and it converts the paper's most vulnerable sentence
into its most defensible one.

> Commercial hospitality qualifies this. A hotel does not owe a welcome to everyone who approaches
> the door; it owes one to the person holding a reservation, and the condition sits at the door
> (Lashley 2000). The qualification governs admission rather than reception. Once the guest is
> inside, the house is not supposed to recalculate the terms of her welcome against what she is
> worth, and the tradition that makes hospitality conditional at the threshold treats it as
> unconditional thereafter (Derrida and Dufourmantelle 2000; Bulley 2015). Algorithmic mediation
> reopens the condition. A system that scores a guest through her stay makes reception conditional
> in the way admission always was, and it does so at every point where an employee might once have
> set a rule aside.

That is 117 words plus citations. It reads at roughly the register's sentence mean, uses no
em-dashes, keeps the citations parenthetical, and hands Section 4 a cleaner handle for the check-in
example than it currently has.

If a further 45 words are going, spend them in Section 2, replacing the last clause of the
"Hospitality scholarship, in other words" paragraph:

> Riordan (2024) has already argued that platform-mediated exchange belongs to none of Lashley's
> three domains and needs a fourth, a virtual domain holding encounters at the boundary of material
> and digital contexts, and he names what platforms do to the workers caught there as algorithmic
> hostility. Hostility describes conduct. What the intermediary owes the guest remains open, and
> that is the question this paper takes up.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revisions.** This is a good paper and I would be glad to see it in the issue. It has
something to say about hospitality and not merely about technology, which is more than most
submissions to a phygital call manage.

The single most important fix is F1, discharged by the paragraph in part 4: qualify the arrival
claim so that welcome is conditional at admission and unconditional in reception, and let Bulley and
Derrida carry that qualification instead of standing decoratively where they now stand. Everything
else on my list is worth under an hour.

## 6. VERIFY

- **Germann Molz (2026)**, and this one matters most. Section 1 describes the article as showing
  "algorithmic governance renders the human host visible, disciplined, and ultimately dispensable,"
  and as asking "what happens to hospitality as the host is progressively erased." I could not
  reach the article. What I could reach describes "guests without hosts" as originally naming the
  *blurring* of hosting and guesting practices in network hospitality, which is a different claim
  from erasure, and a related talk by the same author carries the subtitle "Hopes for Inhospitable
  Hospitalities in the Digital Age," which sounds like a normative proposal rather than a diagnosis.
  If she poses the obligation question herself, Section 1's pivot and the first contribution in
  Section 8 both need rewording. Check the article directly.
- **Manfreda and Harkison (2025)**: confirm "gratitude rather than any obligation," and confirm the
  three behaviours (tidying rooms, returning glasses, praising employees in reviews) appear in the
  text. The abstract situates the contribution in social exchange and reciprocity theory, which
  cuts the other way. See F6.
- **Lynch (2017)**: find the passage supporting the list of guest prerogatives, or drop the
  citation there. See F7.
- **Derrida and Dufourmantelle (2000)**: if the mutuality citation stays in Section 2, give a page.
  I would rather it moved. See F5.
- **Introna (2010)** and the claim that philosophy of technology "has not yet considered the machine
  as a keeper of the threshold." I did not audit that literature. The claim is narrowed enough to
  survive, but it is an absence claim and one citation could refute it.
- **Verified by me, no action needed beyond the fixes above:** Bulley (2015), abstract confirms the
  argument runs beyond the threshold moment and toward hospitality as continuing practice;
  Riordan (2024), abstract confirms Lashley's domains, the proposed virtual domain, and the coinage
  of algorithmic hostility applied to platform workers.
