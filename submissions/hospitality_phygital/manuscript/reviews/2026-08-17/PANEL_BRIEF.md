## The bar

The author's instruction is that this is already a good paper, and the job is to make it **better,
crisper, and cleaner**. Take that seriously in both directions.

- **Do not manufacture problems.** If a section is sound, say so in a sentence and move on. A panel
  that returns twenty findings on a good paper is a panel that has stopped reading.
- **Do not propose restructuring.** The architecture discharges an abstract the editor has already
  blessed, sentence by sentence. Section-level surgery is out of scope.
- **Crisper means the argument, not the prose.** Where a claim is doing less work than the words
  spent on it, say so. Where two paragraphs make one point, say which to cut.
- **Cleaner means what a referee will catch.** A misdescribed source, an overclaim, an absence claim
  a single citation would refute, a construct that collides with a published one.

## Standing rules

- **Verify before you assert.** Every source in this paper has been read and carded; the project has
  twice caught defects in its own citations. If you claim a source says something, check it. If you
  cannot, mark **VERIFY** and say what you could not confirm. Do not invent citations, page numbers,
  or findings.
- **Anonymity is strict.** The manuscript may not name the special issue, its editors, or its own
  positioning. A previous pass removed four such sentences. If you find another, that is a finding.
- **The register is Pierre's.** Sentence mean 20.2, paragraph mean 123, no em-dashes, one authorial
  first person in §1 by design, parenthetical citation throughout, no bold in running text. Judge
  content against that voice rather than against it.

## Output

Write to your assigned path:

1. **Who I am** — one paragraph, your position and what you came to this paper believing.
2. **The read** — findings ranked by damage. For each: what the paper claims, what fails, why it
   fails for you, and the cost in words to fix. Tag **[FIX]**, **[CONSIDER]**, or
   **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**.
3. **Where it is strong** — name what must survive any revision. This is not padding; the author
   needs to know what not to cut when trimming.
4. **Best spend** — if you had 150 of the 400 spare words, where would you put them and what would
   you write? Draft the sentences.
5. **Verdict** — accept / minor / major, and the single most important fix.
6. **VERIFY** — anything needing a source check before submission.

Be specific and be brief. Quote the manuscript when you flag something.

## The panel

Names are invented. Each stands for a real position in a real literature.

### 01 — Ruth Ellery · critical hospitality theory
`01_critical_hospitality.md`

Reads and publishes in this journal. Lashley's domains, Derrida, Bulley on the threshold, Lynch on
mundane welcome, Germann Molz on network hospitality. She judges whether a paper about algorithms
has actually said something about *hospitality*, or has borrowed the word to dignify a technology
argument. The paper's core move — that a party can take a host's functions without inheriting a
host's obligations — is hers to accept or reject. Press whether "welcome is owed in virtue of
arrival" survives the conditional-hospitality tradition, and whether Derrida is doing work here or
decorating.

### 02 — Davit Sarkisyan · phygital service research
`02_phygital_service.md`

Works in the frameworks the paper argues past: phygital customer experience, extended reality,
transformative phygital service research. §7 says his field left seamlessness unexamined while
criticizing everything around it. He will check that claim against what those frameworks actually
say, and he will notice if the paper has read the agenda-setting pieces for their abstracts. Ask
whether the friction argument is a contribution or a restatement of the HCI seamful-design
literature in a hospitality register.

### 03 — Mei-Ling Chow · hospitality technology and service robots
`03_hospitality_technology.md`

Gursoy, Belanche, Liu, Lee and Lu, the whole encounter-triad literature. The paper's novelty claim
lives in her field: everyone else asks what the machine can do or whether guests perceive it as
hospitable, and this paper asks what it owes. Press that claim hard, because if any published work
has asked the obligation question the contribution collapses. Also judge whether §3's redistribution
account is even-handed — the paper concedes the evidence is uneven and cites Garcia on revenue
managers keeping discretion.

### 04 — Tomas Reinholt · algorithmic management and platform labour
`04_algorithmic_management.md`

Rahman, Möhlmann, Spektor, Zhou, the folk-theories line. §5's competence-versus-standing distinction
is the paper's cleanest idea and it is built on his literature. Check that the Zhou differentiation
is fair to the scale's authors. Press the employee half: the paper says staff lend guests their
competence and that the lending is fragile, which is a good observation with thin evidence behind
it. Ask what happens to the argument if the employee-silence link is weaker than §5 implies.

### 05 — Ana Beatriz Furtado · ethics of responsibility and machine agency
`05_responsibility_ethics.md`

Bovens on accountability, Santoni de Sio on responsibility gaps, Kropf on care robots. The paper's
load-bearing sentence is that welcome is the only duty that constitutes the role carrying it. She
should decide whether that is true, whether it is argued or asserted, and whether the four-duty
pattern in §3 is a real taxonomy or a rhetorical figure. Also press §4's accountability condition:
Bovens's forum requires a relationship the guest may not have.
