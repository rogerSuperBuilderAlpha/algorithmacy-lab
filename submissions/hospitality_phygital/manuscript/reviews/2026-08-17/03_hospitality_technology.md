# Reviewer 03 — hospitality technology and service robots

## 1. Who I am

I work on service robots and AI in hotels and restaurants: adoption, the encounter triad, robotic
hospitableness, what guests attribute to a machine when it fails them. I came to this paper
expecting the usual thing — a critical-theory vocabulary borrowed to re-describe findings my field
already has — and expecting to find that somebody had already asked the obligation question, because
my field publishes fast and the ethics-of-service-robots line has been busy for two years. I went
looking for the paper that would sink this one. I did not find it, and I want to be clear about
what I did find instead, because the paper's framing is more exposed than its argument is.

## 2. The read

### [FIX] The absence claim in §1 is refutable, and by one citation. ~70 words to fix.

The exposed sentence is in §1: 'These terms describe what technology is capable of doing, but they
do not identify what, if anything, technology owes the guest.' Inside hospitality and tourism that
holds — I searched hard and report the result in part 6. Outside it, machine ethics has asked a
version of the question and answered it deontologically. Stock-Homburg and Kegel's scoping review of
customer–robot service interaction runs a Kantian frame over the machine directly and speaks of the
robot's duties to the customer: keep promises, do not deceive, do not cheat, preserve the customer's
dignity (*International Journal of Social Robotics*, 2025). A referee who works in HRI will know that
review, and an unqualified 'nobody asks what it owes' will read as a literature the author has not
walked.

The repair is cheap and it makes the contribution sharper rather than smaller. Those Kantian duties
are duties any agent owes anyone it transacts with. They are not held in virtue of a role and they do
not constitute one, which is precisely the distinction §3 already draws when it separates care,
fiduciary duty, and governance duty from welcome. The paper should name the machine-ethics answer and
then say why it is the wrong kind of duty. Doing so costs about seventy words and it protects the
load-bearing claim in the strongest place a referee will push on it.

### [FIX] Sharma and Mattila is the nearest neighbour and it is read too thinly. ~35 words.

§3 says the paper 'catalogues the responsibilities of hospitality service robots, but frames them as
matters of governance: liability, safety, data protection, and consent'. That is not wrong, but it is
not the whole of it. Sharma and Mattila argue through stakeholder theory across moral, ethical, and
legal aspects together, and the part that should worry this author is the procedural move: they
propose that firms identify which duties currently held by a human employee could be delegated to a
robot. That is one step from this paper's own question, published in a journal the editor's referees
read, and it deserves a sentence that distinguishes the two rather than a subordinate clause that
files it under governance.

The distinction is available and it is good. Delegation is the right frame for any duty a job
description can specify, which is most of what a front-desk employee owes anybody. The host's duty is
not among them, because it is not held in virtue of the job. Say that and the nearest neighbour
becomes a foil instead of a threat.

### [FIX] Belanche et al. 2020 sits in the reference list and is cited nowhere — and it is the best
evidence the paper has. ~70 words.

I checked the reference list against the text for the works in my area. Belanche, Casaló, Flavián and
Schepers (2020) appears at line 533 and never appears in §1–§9. A copyeditor will catch it as an
orphan. I am flagging it for a different reason: it is the single most useful empirical finding
available to this argument and the paper is throwing it away.

Their two vignette studies used a hotel reception service and a restaurant waiter service. Customers
made stronger attributions of responsibility toward the human than toward the robot, the gap widened
when the service failed, and customers routed responsibility to the firm rather than to the frontline
robot. My field reads that as a service-recovery result. Read as evidence about position rather than
perception — which is exactly the reading §3 sets up — it says guests already decline to treat the
machine as a party that owed them anything. The paper claims the machine does not inherit the
obligation. Here are guests behaving as though they agree.

### [CONSIDER] Garcia et al. does opposite work in §3 and §4 without the paper reconciling it. ~35 words.

You asked me to judge whether §3's redistribution account is even-handed. It is, and I say so
properly in part 3. One seam shows. In §3 Garcia et al. is the concession: 'revenue systems there
anticipate that staff will deviate from their recommendations, and the deviations are durable and to
some extent designed for'. In §4 the same study becomes the clearest case of a rule nobody at the
desk could explain. Both readings are true and they are compatible — the system biases its output
because it expects deviation, so retained discretion is the mechanism that hides the operative rule —
but the paper never says they are compatible, and a referee reading straight through will see a
concession quietly withdrawn. One sentence in §4 joining them turns an apparent inconsistency into
the paper's sharpest illustration: discretion can be preserved and the rule still be unavailable.

### [CONSIDER] §4's inference from a modelling choice to guest psychology is thin. ~10 words.

'Willingness to accept identification technology at the hotel door is routinely modelled as a
trade-off between privacy and benefit, which is a calculation guests only make when something more
than convenience is at stake.' The premise is about how researchers model the decision; the
conclusion is about what guests sense. Xu et al. and Boo and Chua chose a privacy calculus framework;
that is a fact about the framework's popularity in our field, not evidence that guests experience the
door as consequential. 'Guests appear to sense' is doing more than the citations support. Soften to
the modelling claim and let the check-in example carry the point, which it does on its own.

## 3. Where it is strong

The perception-versus-position distinction in §3 is the paper's real contribution to my field and it
must survive any cut: 'Whether a party is perceived as hospitable and whether it occupies a position
that owes the guest something are different matters, and only the second is independent of what the
guest happens to believe.' My literature has spent a decade on the first clause. Nobody has written
the second. The characterizations of Liu et al. and Lee and Lu that set it up are accurate — I
checked both, and 'felt welcome, attentiveness, and reassurance' is the construct Liu et al. actually
build.

The four-sources passage is the best thirty seconds in the paper. Care from need, fiduciary duty from
trust, governance duty from risk, welcome from arrival, and only the last constitutes the role. That
is the sentence I would quote if I were arguing for this paper in a panel.

Keep the check-in example. A system that confirms who she says she is against a system that weighs
her history is a distinction any hotel technologist recognizes immediately and none of us has
theorized. Keep the handling of Schmidt et al. as well: refusing to let an information-desk
indifference finding generalize to consequential encounters is a discipline my field routinely fails
at, and the paper does it in three sentences without condescension.

## 4. Best spend

I would spend about 140 of the 400 on two insertions.

In §1, after 'they do not identify what, if anything, technology owes the guest':

> Machine ethics has asked a version of this question. Reviews of customer–robot interaction apply
> deontological duties to the machine directly: keep promises, do not deceive, preserve the
> customer's dignity (Stock-Homburg and Kegel 2025). Those are duties any agent owes anyone it deals
> with. A host's duty is not of that kind. It is owed to a particular person because she has arrived,
> and hospitality research has not asked whether an algorithmic system acquires it.

In §3, after the sentence ending 'only the second is independent of what the guest happens to
believe':

> Guests may already sense the difference. When a hotel reception or a restaurant service fails,
> customers attribute less responsibility to the robot than to a human employee and route the
> attribution to the firm instead (Belanche et al. 2020). Service research reads that as a problem
> for recovery. Read as evidence about position, it says something stronger: guests decline to treat
> the machine as a party that owed them anything in the first place.

The second insertion also retires the orphan reference, which is why I rank it first if only one goes
in.

If a third insertion fits, put it in §3 on Sharma and Mattila:

> Delegation is the right frame for any duty a job description can specify. The host's duty is not
> among them, because it is not held in virtue of the job.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revision.** The novelty claim survives. I could not find any published work in hospitality,
tourism, or service research that asks whether a system taking over the host's functions inherits the
host's obligations, and I looked in the places it would be if it existed.

The single most important fix is the first one: qualify the absence claim in §1 so that it names the
machine-ethics answer and rules it out on principle. As written, the claim is stated at a generality
that one citation from an adjacent field refutes, and the paper does not need that generality to make
its case. Narrowing it costs seventy words and buys the argument its strongest defence.

## 6. VERIFY

**What I checked and confirmed.**

- Liu et al. (2026), 'Conceptualizing hospitableness in human–robot hospitality interactions',
  *IJHM* 135: 104640. The construct is 'robotic hospitableness' across mediated, assisted and
  personified dimensions, producing felt welcome, attentiveness and reassurance. §3's description is
  accurate.
- Xing and Zhang (2026), *IJCHM* 38:4, pp. 1433–1452. Co-creation and five dimensions of AI-reshaped
  customer experience management. No obligation claim. §1's grouping is fair.
- Belanche et al. (2020), *Journal of Service Management* 31:2, pp. 267–289. Two vignette
  experiments, hotel reception and restaurant waiter scenarios; stronger responsibility attributions
  toward humans than robots, gap wider on failure; attribution routed to the firm rather than the
  frontline robot. Confirmed via the publisher listing. Not cited in the body.
- Sharma and Mattila (2025), *Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research*, DOI
  10.1177/10963480251393749. Stakeholder theory across moral, ethical and legal aspects of
  human–robot interaction.
- Binesh and Syah (2026), *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence*, AI ethics in hospitality and
  tourism. A PRISMA scoping review of 32 studies organized around risk classification and life-cycle
  safeguards. It does not put AI in the host's position or ask what it owes. It does not threaten
  the contribution, and the author may want it as one more piece of evidence that the field's ethics
  turn has gone to governance rather than to obligation. Optional, ~20 words.

**What I could not confirm and the author must check.**

- **Stock-Homburg and Kegel (2025), *International Journal of Social Robotics*.** Springer's article
  page returned a redirect I could not follow and the RePEc working-paper record 404'd. I have the
  title, journal, year, and the deontological framing of duties to the customer from multiple
  independent secondary sources, and a volume and page range — 17, pp. 1129–1159 — from a single
  search summary that I could not corroborate against the publisher. **Verify authors' initials,
  volume, and pages against Springer before this enters the reference list.** If the author cannot
  obtain the record, the §1 hedge can be written against the deontological machine-ethics tradition
  generally without the specific citation, at a small cost in force.
- **The delegation claim in Sharma and Mattila.** My source for 'firms identify which human employee
  duties could be delegated to a robot' is a trade summary of the article, not the article. SAGE
  returned 403. The author has this paper carded already; check whether the delegation framing is in
  the text before writing the foil sentence against it. If it is not there, the existing
  'governance' characterization stands as written and this finding drops out.
- **Garcia et al. (2026), the §3 and §4 double use.** I did not re-read the study. The
  reconciliation I propose in part 4 assumes the deviation-anticipating mechanism the paper already
  describes in §4. Confirm that §3's 'designed for' and §4's 'biased its own recommendations'
  describe the same finding and not two.
