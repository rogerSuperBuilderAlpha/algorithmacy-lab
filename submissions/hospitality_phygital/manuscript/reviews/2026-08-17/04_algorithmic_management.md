# Reviewer 04 — Tomas Reinholt · algorithmic management and platform labour

## 1. Who I am

I study algorithmic management and platform labour: how opaque evaluation steers conduct without
issuing an instruction, what workers learn about the systems that rate them, and what that learning
does and does not buy them. Most of what my field knows about hospitality it knows from
housekeeping, from delivery riders, and from Airbnb hosts, because those are the places where
researchers could get access. I came to this paper expecting the usual borrowing: platform-labour
findings dressed in hotel clothes, with the guest bolted on. That is not what this is. Section 5
makes a distinction my own literature needs and has not made, and Section 3 says out loud that the
evidence base is lopsided, which is more candour than most papers importing this material manage.
My findings are about places where the paper's citations are carrying more than they contain, and
almost all of them make the argument stronger, not weaker, once repaired.

## 2. The read

### F1. Section 2 cites a housekeeping study for a front-desk claim, and Section 3 contradicts it forty lines later [FIX]

The paper writes:

> A front-desk employee may explain that the system has assigned a particular room, rejected an
> upgrade, changed a price, or required a particular form of identification, while having no
> authority to alter that outcome (Spektor et al. 2023).

Spektor et al. (2023) is a participatory study with unionized guest room attendants and housekeeping
supervisors at hotels, run with UNITE HERE, about UpKeep, an algorithmic room-assignment system. Its
"room assignment" allocates rooms *to housekeepers to clean*. It does not allocate guests to rooms,
it contains no front-desk participants, and the front desk appears in it as a party that *sends*
assignments to room attendants, including late ones that participants complained about. Cited here,
the source is not merely off-target; on the authority question it points the other way.

The damage is compounded because Section 3 gets this exactly right:

> The evidence for this shift is uneven. The clearest documentation comes from back-of-house work,
> where housekeeping systems have been observed to fix task sequences and withhold information from
> the workers carrying them out (Spektor et al. 2023, 2025). Front-desk work appears different [...]
> The guest-facing half of the question has barely been studied.

A referee who reads Section 3 first will go back to Section 2 and find the paper doing the thing it
later disavows. That is the one finding here that costs credibility rather than precision. The fix
is to let Section 2's sentence stand as the paper's own conjecture, which is what it is, and say so:
strike the citation and add a clause of the form "a case the literature has documented behind the
scenes rather than at the desk." **Cost: about 15 words, and a citation deleted.**

Companion, same source family: Section 3's clause about staff "interpreting machine decisions,
explaining them to guests, or finding ways of working within categories established elsewhere
(Christin 2017; Pedersen and Pors 2022; Spektor et al. 2025)". Spektor et al. (2025) is an interview
study in the housekeeping department of a single unionized hotel, about peer and supervisor
communication. It does not reach guests. Christin and Pedersen and Pors carry that clause between
them. **Cost: zero, one citation removed.**

### F2. The Zhou differentiation misstates what the scale measures, and understates its own case [FIX]

Two problems in one paragraph, both cheap, and the repair makes the paper's cleanest idea sharper.

First, priority. The paper calls it "the first validated scale of algorithmic competency". Dogruel,
Masur and Joeckel (2022) published a validated 22-item algorithm literacy scale in *Communication
Methods and Measures*, tested on 1,041 German internet users, three years earlier. Zhou and
colleagues make the narrower claim, which is about on-demand platform workers specifically. This is
an absence claim a single citation refutes, and the argument does not need it. Write "a recently
validated scale of workers' algorithmic competency". **Cost: four words.**

Second, and more serious for fairness to the authors, the paper says "its remediating dimension
includes gathering evidence in order to appeal". That phrase comes from an interview exemplar in the
scale-development table — a rider saying that on receiving an unfair rating he can use evidence such
as recordings to appeal — which is item-generation material, not a validated item. The remediating
dimension that survived exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis has three items:

1. "I can address deficiencies in AM by integrating personal experience."
2. "I can supplement AM's shortcomings (i.e., imprecise navigation) through the help of WeChat
   groups or other tools."
3. "I can use platform APP functions (i.e., reporting exceptions and appealing) to resolve
   vulnerabilities in AM."

Zhou and colleagues would be within their rights to object that the paper has described their
construct from an appendix quotation. They would also, I think, be surprised to find that the
correction helps the paper. Two of the three items are self-help and peer help; only the third
touches the platform's own channel, and it frames appeal as an app function the worker can operate
to fix a defect in the system. Remediating, as validated, is largely what workers do when nobody is
obliged to answer them: they route around the institution rather than through it. That is a better
illustration of competence-without-standing than the appeal exemplar, and it is in the scale itself.
**Cost: about 40 words, net gain to the argument.**

A related fairness point. "Some approaches to algorithmic competence risk treating the ability to
appeal as if it were itself an individual competence" is immediately followed by the scale
"illustrat[ing] the point", which reads as an accusation against the authors. Their nomological net
is about worker performance and gig-work identification; they nowhere claim an appeal will be heard.
The risk lives in how the scale gets used. One clause — "not in the scale's own claims but in what
follows from taking it as the measure of a worker's position" — is more accurate and more generous.
**Cost: about 20 words. [CONSIDER]**

### F3. The employee half's citations do not measure competence [FIX]

You asked the right question in Section 5 and then cited around it. The claim:

> Indeed, employees who understand most clearly how little influence they have may become less
> willing to speak about algorithmic decisions with guests or managers (Cheng et al. 2025; Lin et
> al. 2026; Duggan et al. 2026).

None of the three measures understanding, competence, or knowledge of the system. Cheng and
colleagues measure AI awareness, which in that literature is employees' concern about their future
careers under AI adoption, and the mechanism they report is a perceived breach of the psychological
contract. Lin and colleagues measure exposure to four algorithmic functions, mediated by felt
responsibility for constructive change. Duggan and colleagues describe enforced silence
qualitatively in app-based food delivery. The paragraph then concludes that "greater technological
competence does not necessarily produce greater voice," which none of the three tested.

I believe the claim. I would probably design the study myself. But the paper is one whose central
move is precision about what an instrument can and cannot measure, and it should not, in the same
section, cite three studies for something they did not measure. State the inference as an inference.
The section already knows how to do this: the paragraph that follows is a model of it. **Cost: about
40 words, and see the best spend below.**

### F4. Lin et al. is cited for half of its result [FIX]

Lin and colleagues report differential effects: algorithmic direction, scheduling and monitoring
depress voice, while algorithmic *feedback* raises it, through the same mediator. The paper cites it
only on the silencing side. This matters twice over. It is a partial representation of the closest
quantitative neighbour, which a referee working in this area will catch. And it costs the paper its
own even-handedness: Section 8 insists "none of this should be read as a case against the
technology," and Section 4 builds a test precisely because sophistication does not settle the
question. The differential finding is evidence for the paper's own position that the arrangement,
not the technology, decides. **Cost: about 25 words, and it buys back consistency.**

### F5. Zientara et al. measures collective voice, not the voice at issue [CONSIDER]

> hospitality's own voice research suggests that employees in the sector often prefer quietly
> leaving to speaking up (Zientara et al. 2023)

That study models intention to join a union and intention to quit among UK hospitality workers, and
finds that stress and job dissatisfaction predict quitting but not unionizing. The exit is real; the
voice foregone is *collective and formal*, which is a different thing from telling a duty manager
that the system has got a guest wrong. The gloss is defensible but loose in a paragraph whose point
is that constructs must be kept apart. "Prefer quietly leaving to organizing" is more accurate and
shorter. **Cost: minus two words.**

### F6. The lending claim rests on a conference abstract when the paper already owns better [CONSIDER]

Section 5's best observation — that employees lend guests their competence by reformulating a
request into terms the system will accept — carries one citation, De Vos et al. (2023), a Global
Fashion Management Conference proceedings paper about online higher education, cited for the
construct of need identification. That is thin support for the paper's most original empirical
proposition, and a referee will say so.

The paper already cites, in Section 2, Beatty et al. (2016), a critical-incident study of frontline
employees assessing and complying with customer special requests that fall outside standard duties.
That is the front-of-house half of the lending claim, in a service journal, in the paper's own
reference list. Add it. **Cost: five words, no new reference.**

### F7. Section 8's second proposed study needs to say what adapting the scale requires [CONSIDER]

> The second would operationalize the three competences described in Section 5 and examine how they
> relate to the validated competency scale now available (Zhou et al. 2025).

The scale was validated on Chinese ride-hailing and food-delivery workers, 97.1 per cent male in the
exploratory sample, and one of its four dimensions — embracing, defined as willingness to trust the
efficiency and accuracy of algorithmic management — is a disposition rather than a capacity. A study
relating it to guests, or to hotel employees, is adapting it in two directions at once. Saying so
makes the agenda item look designed rather than gestured at. **Cost: about 25 words.**

### F8. Where the back-of-house evidence is asked to carry front-of-house claims, overall [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]

Beyond F1, the paper's handling is sound and I want that on the record. Section 3 concedes the
asymmetry explicitly, distinguishes back-of-house from front-desk work, cites Garcia et al. (2026)
for a front-desk system that anticipates and designs for deviation, and states that "the
guest-facing half of the question has barely been studied." That paragraph is the reason a
platform-labour reader can trust the rest. The structural limitation — that the paper's picture of
employee discretion under algorithmic direction comes overwhelmingly from housekeepers and gig
workers, and that hotels' guest-facing staff have simply not been studied this way — cannot be
fixed in a revision. It is already named. Carry it forward as the field's problem, not this paper's.

## 3. Where it is strong

- **"Competence is learned, while standing is conferred."** This is the sentence my literature has
  been circling for five years without writing. Algorithmic-literacy work keeps proposing training
  as the remedy for a problem that training cannot touch. Do not soften it, do not qualify it, do
  not move it.
- **Section 4's counterintuitive result.** "An opaque algorithm can still augment hospitality if its
  rules are reasonably stable and an empowered employee can question or override its decisions. By
  contrast, a perfectly transparent algorithm can still substitute for hospitality if nobody is
  required to answer for its decisions." Rahman showed opacity works as control; the field's reflex
  remedy has been transparency ever since. This paper gives a principled reason why that reflex is
  wrong and a test that discriminates. Keep the whole passage including the two worked examples.
- **Section 3's uneven-evidence paragraph.** Under trimming pressure this will look like a
  concession that can go. It cannot. It is what licenses everything the paper borrows from my field.
- **The distinction in Section 6 between the first three principles and the last two**, and
  especially: "A hospitality organization that responds to a problem of contestability by providing
  more guest education has therefore misdiagnosed its own condition." That is the practical payoff
  of the whole paper.
- **Garcia et al. (2026) as the pricing example.** A front-desk system that biases its own
  recommendations because it anticipates deviation is a better illustration of the paper's thesis
  than anything the platform-labour canon offers, because the staff there have discretion and it
  still does not help the guest.

## 4. Best spend

I would put roughly 150 words into Section 5, split between the Zhou paragraph and the silence
paragraph. Both are places where the paper's own evidence, stated accurately, is stronger than the
summary it currently gives. Drafted in the manuscript's voice, parenthetical citation, no em-dashes:

**Replacing the Zhou sentences (net gain about 40 words):**

> Developed with Chinese ride-hailing and food-delivery workers, it measures understanding,
> embracing, leveraging, and remediating algorithmic management. Two of the three remediating items
> ask workers whether they can supplement the system's shortcomings from their own experience or
> through peer groups, and only the third mentions appeal, as the capacity to use the platform's
> reporting and appealing functions to resolve its vulnerabilities (Zhou et al. 2025). Everything
> the scale measures is real, and much of it corresponds to the competences described above. What
> no item can measure is whether anyone is obliged to consider the appeal, since that obligation,
> where it exists, belongs to the institution and not to any capacity of the worker. The dimension
> is a record of what workers do when the obligation is missing: they route around the institution
> rather than through it.

**Replacing the silence sentence (net gain about 75 words):**

> Indeed, employees who understand most clearly how little influence they have may become less
> willing to speak about algorithmic decisions with guests or managers. The evidence for this is
> adjacent rather than direct. Awareness of artificial intelligence raises frontline silence through
> a perceived breach of the psychological contract (Cheng et al. 2025), app-based platform work
> produces a silence its researchers call enforced (Duggan et al. 2026), and algorithmic direction,
> scheduling, and monitoring depress voice while algorithmic feedback raises it (Lin et al. 2026).
> None of these studies measures competence. The step from understanding a system to declining to
> speak about it is therefore a proposition this framework offers rather than a finding it reports.

Together these spend about 115 of the 400 available words, remove three misdescriptions, and turn
the section's weakest evidential moment into one of its clearest research propositions.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revisions.** The argument holds, the central distinction is a real contribution to my
field as much as to hospitality, and nothing here requires restructuring or new theory.

The single most important fix is **F1**: re-cite or de-cite the front-desk sentence in Section 2, so
that the paper does not assert in Section 2 what it carefully qualifies in Section 3. Everything
else on this list is precision work, and most of it pays for itself.
