# Reviewer 2 — Who Hosts the Guest?

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The fixes are local and cost under 400 words in total, so I mean
this at the light end. But two of them are conceptual rather than cosmetic: the paper's central
decision rule contradicts the two illustrations it is introduced with, and the question the title
asks is answered by assertion. I expect to recommend acceptance on a second look.

---

## 1. Who I am

I referee conceptual papers in service and hospitality journals and I do not enjoy them. A paper with
no data has to earn its place by making a distinction the field cannot already make, and most such
papers instead rename one it can. I came to this one expecting to find the service triad with
Derrida bolted on. That is not what I found: §5's separation of competence from standing is a real
distinction, cleanly argued, and §2's observation that hospitality scholarship registered the
third-party problem before service research formalized the triad is a legitimate claim to priority in
this journal. What I found instead were four places where the paper's own sources do not say what it
says they say, and one place where it does not argue the thing it announces. Those are fixable. I am
recording them because a referee who knows these literatures will catch every one.

## 2. The read

### F1. The novelty claim rests on a source the paper misdescribes. [FIX] — ~45 words

§3: "A related line of work catalogues the responsibilities of hospitality service robots, but frames
them as matters of governance: liability, safety, data protection, and consent (Sharma and Mattila
2025)."

The published abstract does not support that reduction. Sharma and Mattila ask "whether — and with
what consequences — hospitality service robots should be afforded rights and responsibilities," and
they argue it "through the lens of stakeholder theory embedded in the context of moral, ethical, and
legal aspects related to human-robot interaction." That is a moral-status argument about what a
machine may be owed and may owe. It is the nearest published neighbour to this paper's question, and
the paper walks past it by calling it governance.

This is the single most damaging item in my list, because the whole contribution turns on this one
sentence. The repair is not to concede; it is to differentiate on the right axis. Sharma and Mattila
ask whether the machine should be afforded the standing of a responsible party. This paper asks what
the arrangement owes the guest whether or not the machine has any standing at all, which is why its
answer does not depend on how anthropomorphic the robot is. Say that and the contribution survives in
a stronger form than it currently has. **VERIFY the full text before rewriting** — I have read the
abstract, not the article.

### F2. §4's decision rule contradicts both of the illustrations that follow it. [FIX] — ~55 words

The rule: "Mediation becomes substitutive when all three conditions are withheld at once. It remains
augmentative when they are preserved somewhere within the relationship."

The illustration, eight lines later: "a perfectly transparent algorithm can still substitute for
hospitality if nobody is required to answer for its decisions or has the authority to revise them."

Under the stated rule that case is not substitutive. Common understanding is preserved by
transparency and only accountability is withheld, so one of three conditions fails, not all three.
The paper's most quoted sentence is ruled out by the paper's own test. The conjunction also leaves an
enormous undefined middle: two conditions withheld is neither substitutive (not all three) nor
clearly augmentative, and most real touchpoints live there.

The rest of the manuscript already knows the answer. §5 says skill cannot manufacture a counterparty,
principle four says preserving a human face matters less than preserving human authority, and §8's
third contribution is that standing is conferred. Accountability is doing the deciding everywhere
except in the sentence that states the rule. Make it the necessary condition and demote the other two
to gradients. Drafted in part 4.

### F3. The augment/substitute pair is not this paper's, and the collision is with a source it cites. [FIX] — ~50 words

§8: "the paper proposes a checkable distinction between mediation that augments hospitality and
mediation that substitutes for it."

Odekerken-Schröder et al., cited once in §1 and never again, is an empirical study of the service
triad investigating service robots' "augmentation or substitution role in driving utilitarian and
hedonic value and ultimately customer repatronage" (JOSM 33:2, pp. 246–292). Same pair, same word, in
the paper's own reference list, in the field the contribution is claimed against. Raisch and
Krakowski's automation-augmentation paradox is cited too, in §4's opening sentence.

The defence is available and it is short. Odekerken-Schröder et al. ask whether the robot augments or
substitutes the *employee* in producing value; this paper asks whether the mediation augments or
substitutes the *relationship*, and its criterion is what remains available to the guest rather than
what the guest spends. One sentence in §4 or §8 does it. Without it, a triad referee reads
contribution two as a rebrand.

Note also: 33:2 is the 2022 issue. The paper dates it 2021.

### F4. The pricing example does not survive its own five questions, and it is described backwards. [FIX] — ~40 words

Two problems in one citation.

First, the mechanism. §4: "the system biased its own recommendations because it anticipated that
revenue managers would adjust them." Garcia et al. report the opposite direction. Price adjustment
costs make managers update *too infrequently*; the algorithm exaggerates its recommendations to
induce movement, and the paper's closing move is to estimate the gains from removing the human and
going fully automated. "Anticipated that they would adjust them" states the friction the wrong way
round.

Second, and worse, run the table on it. Q3 asks who can override, and in Garcia et al. the revenue
manager overrides constantly. Q4, who must answer, is not addressed by the study at all, and neither
is Q5. So the paper's clearest condemned case scores well on the one diagnostic question the source
actually speaks to. §3 knows this and says so: "revenue systems there anticipate that staff will
deviate from their recommendations, and the deviations are durable and to some extent designed for."
The same study is evidence that discretion survives in §3 and evidence that it has been withdrawn in
§4.

Related and smaller: §3 files revenue management under "front-desk work." It is not front-desk work,
and the guest-facing claim the sentence is making does not follow from a study of pricing analysts at
midsized European hotels.

### F5. The augmentative example contains no decision, so the diagnostic has nothing to bite on. [CONSIDER] — ~35 words

Le Petit Chef projects an animated chef onto a table. Ask the first question of it, "what made this
decision?", and there is no referent, because the technology decides nothing about the guest. The
example shows a case where the diagnostic does not apply, not a case where it applies and returns
"augmentative." The sentence "the same test that condemns the first arrangement approves the second"
is therefore not earned by the pair.

An augmentative case needs an actual algorithmic determination about a guest that a present employee
can reverse. Room allocation with a front desk that can move her is the obvious one and the paper
already names it twice.

### F6. §2's absence claim is refutable by one of the best-known scales in service research. [FIX] — ~35 words

"Neither asks what, if anything, a guest owes a commercial host, and no instrument measures conduct
flowing in that direction."

Yi and Gong's customer value co-creation behaviour scale measures exactly that conduct: feedback,
advocacy, helping, and tolerance, under the heading of customer citizenship behaviour, with a
sizeable hospitality literature applying it to hotel guests. The clause as written is false and a
service-research referee will know it from memory.

The claim underneath it is fine and the repair makes it sharper, because those instruments classify
guest conduct as *extra-role* by construction. The field has measured what guests do voluntarily
precisely by defining it as what they are not required to do. Drafted in part 4.

### F7. §1's promise is deferred, not discharged. [FIX] — ~70 words

"This paper therefore asks whether a technological system that takes over the functions of hosting
also inherits the obligations of the host. The central argument is that it does not."

I looked for where that is argued. §3's four-duty passage is the closest, and it argues something
else: that welcome is the only duty constituting the role of the party owing it, and that "a welcome
is owed simply because someone has arrived." Read plainly, that premise cuts *towards* inheritance.
If the duty attaches to whoever receives the arrival, and the system now receives the arrival, the
system is the natural candidate to owe the welcome. The paper needs a reason why arrival-triggered
duties cannot land on the party that meets the arrival, and it does not give one.

What follows in §§6–9 is a different and much less contested claim: that organizations must arrange
for someone to answer. Nobody disputes that. §9's "somebody still owes the guest a welcome" restates
the design conclusion, not the inheritance verdict, and the title's question is left standing.

The argument the paper needs is short and it already owns the materials. Bovens's forum requires a
party that can be required to answer; a system cannot stand in a forum; a duty of welcome is
therefore not the kind of thing that transfers with the function. Drafted in part 4.

One more thing, which is why this finding is cheap. Belanche et al. (2020) sits in the reference list
and is cited nowhere in the body — the only such entry. It reports that after a service failure
customers attribute responsibility more strongly to a human employee than to a robot, and in the
robot condition direct it at the firm. That is guests performing the paper's allocation
unprompted. Either use it here or cut it.

### F8. Contribution one is the field's, not the paper's. [CONSIDER] — 0 words, rewrite in place

To answer the question I assume the editor put to this panel: the three contributions are three, not
one restated. They sit in §3, §4 and §5, and they use different machinery. I am satisfied on that
point.

But the first one is not a contribution. "Repositions AI from a neutral service technology to a
constitutive mediator of hospitality relationships" is the settled position of the literature the
paragraph itself cites. Scott and Orlikowski, Christin, Rahman and Möhlmann et al. did that
repositioning and the paper leans on them for it. Claiming it here reads as padding in a list that
does not need padding. The genuinely new claim is the one §1 makes and never argues: functions
transfer and the obligation does not. Put that in slot one and the discussion matches the
introduction for the first time.

### F9. Two of the five design features do not descend from the argument. [CONSIDER] — ~55 words

Features one, three and four map onto common understanding, predictability and accountability
respectively, and the derivation is visible. Features two and five do not. Adjusting parameters is a
form of control over one's own case that nothing in §4 requires, and the genuine alternative is
*exit*, which is a different currency from everything else in the paper, all of which is voice. The
paper half-notices this and describes the split by remedy type ("the first three can largely be
addressed through interface design"), which is a different cut and does not answer the derivation
question.

While I am here: the paper runs three lists of five (questions, features, roadmap stages) plus a
triple of conditions, and none of the four align. Q1 has no feature; features two and three have no
question. I do not propose renumbering anything. I propose one sentence saying which features answer
which conditions and conceding that two of them add something the conditions do not carry.

### F10. Minor. [CONSIDER]

"The three-party arrangement" is the field's service triad under a new name, and §3, the section that
most needs to engage the triad literature, does not cite Odekerken-Schröder et al. at all. Use the
field's term once so the reader knows you are in the same room. Be aware that "service triad" also
carries a prior operations meaning (buyer, supplier, customer) if you decide to adopt it.

## 3. Where it is strong

Do not cut any of these when you trim.

- **§5 in its entirety.** Competence is learned, standing is conferred, and the Zhou et al. handling
  is the most careful use of a source in the manuscript. It concedes that everything the scale
  measures is real and then names the one thing a scale of individual capacity cannot reach. That
  paragraph is the paper.
- **§2's platform-accommodation paragraph.** "Hospitality scholarship registered that a third party
  had unsettled the question of who hosts before the service literature formalized the triad." That
  is the paper's claim to belong in this journal rather than a service journal, and it is true.
- **§7's remedy paragraph.** The reputational-records point is the sharpest thing here after §5. The
  remedy runs through the same instrument as the harm, and it relocates disadvantage onto the guest
  with no record. Filippas et al. on inflation is exactly the right supporting citation.
- **§7's narrowing sentence.** "The argument here is therefore a narrow one." A hostile reader looks
  for overclaim and finds the author has already fenced it. Keep it.
- **The four-duty pattern in §3.** Care from need, fiduciary from trust, governance from risk,
  welcome from arrival. It is a real taxonomy and it is where F7's missing argument belongs.

## 4. Best spend

The brief says 400 words of headroom. My count of §1–§9 excluding tables and headings comes to about
5,640, below the 5,841 figure I was given, so the headroom may be larger than advertised and worth
recounting before allocating. Here is where my 150 go.

**(a) §3, immediately after "it is the only one of these duties that defines the role of the party
who owes it." Seventy words. This discharges F7.**

> The reason the obligation does not travel with the function follows from the duty itself. A welcome
> is owed by a party that can be required to answer, and being answerable is not a capability that
> can be specified, configured, or deployed. A system may hold the threshold, admit, and refuse, and
> still be the wrong kind of thing to owe a welcome. The obligation does not transfer. It stays with
> the organization that placed the system at the door.

**(b) §4, replacing "Mediation becomes substitutive when all three conditions are withheld at once.
It remains augmentative when they are preserved somewhere within the relationship." Fifty-five words
net of the twenty-three replaced. This discharges F2 and makes the transparent-algorithm illustration
follow from the rule instead of contradicting it.**

> Accountability decides the case. Mediation becomes substitutive whenever no identifiable party is
> required to answer, however well the guest understands the system and however stable its rules. It
> remains augmentative while someone in the relationship must answer and can act, even where
> understanding and predictability are thin. The other two conditions govern how hard the
> relationship is to use, not whether anyone is on the other side of it.

**(c) §2, replacing "and no instrument measures conduct flowing in that direction." Thirty words plus
one reference. This discharges F6.**

> The instruments that measure conduct in that direction settle the question by definition. The
> validated co-creation scale files feedback, advocacy, helping, and tolerance under citizenship
> behaviour, which is behaviour the customer is by construction not required to perform (Yi and Gong
> 2013).

The remaining 250 I would put into F1 (45), F3 (50), F4 (40), F5 (35) and F9 (55), in that order.
Every one of those is a sentence that stops a referee, and none of them require a new source.

## 5. Verdict

**Major revisions**, at the light end, and the second round should be short.

The single most important fix is F1: correct the characterization of Sharma and Mattila and
re-differentiate on the axis that actually separates the two papers. Everything else in this review
concerns whether the argument holds together. That one concerns whether the paper has a contribution
at all, and at present it is defended by a sentence that the source will not support.

If I could have a second, it is F7. A paper whose title is a question should answer it in the body,
and this one currently answers a different and easier question at greater length.

## 6. VERIFY

- **Sharma and Mattila (2025), full text.** I read the published abstract only. It describes moral,
  ethical, and legal arguments via stakeholder theory about whether robots should be afforded rights
  and responsibilities. The draft's "frames them as matters of governance: liability, safety, data
  protection, and consent" is not supported by the abstract. Confirm against the article before
  rewriting, and check the volume and page numbers, which the reference list currently omits.
- **Garcia et al.** Confirm the direction of the strategic bias. The published abstract has adjustment
  costs producing *infrequent* updating, with the algorithm exaggerating in response; the draft has
  the algorithm anticipating that managers "would adjust." Also confirm whether the decision makers
  are described as revenue managers rather than front-desk staff, and note that the study estimates
  the gains from full automation, which sits awkwardly with the use the draft makes of it. Year:
  online 2024, issue 72:1 in 2026. The draft's 2026 is defensible, but be consistent with how the
  other online-first items are dated.
- **Odekerken-Schröder et al.** JOSM 33:2, pp. 246–292 is the 2022 issue; the draft dates it 2021.
  Confirm the augmentation/substitution framing in the abstract, which is where I read it.
- **Yi and Gong (2013)**, *Journal of Business Research*, 66:9, pp. 1279–1284. Confirm the page range
  and the four citizenship components before adding.
- **Belanche et al. (2020).** In the reference list, cited nowhere in the body. The reported finding
  (responsibility attributed more strongly to humans than robots, and to the firm in the robot case)
  matches the abstract; confirm from the article before building a sentence on it.
- **Mameli et al. (2026) "contains no discussion of friction at all."** An absolute absence claim
  about a 57-study framework. I did not check it. One counterexample sentence in that paper costs
  this one a referee's confidence in every other absence claim it makes, and §2, §3, §5 and §8 each
  contain one.
