## Part 1. Rigour and argument, from the staff side

In the paper's framing, the concern for staff is real. In its instruments, it is thin. The framing protects workers twice:
- The host ¶2 refuses to make the frontline employee the host, against service research that ends up "conflating an employment role with an institutional obligation" (l. 37).
- Approach ¶3 puts the obligations on the firm, "not to the individual worker standing in the lobby" (l. 61).

Those two sentences are the most worker-protective commitments in the paper, and a labour referee will welcome them. The instruments do not keep faith with them:
- Table 2 records what each stage secures "for the guest" and nothing for the worker at the end of the route.
- The one duty of care the paper places on a person falls on the worker (l. 142).
- The rewrite moved the goodwill problem from the host to "staff" in three places.
- The closing advice to operators drops training and protection.

Every one of these repairs is short. Taken together, they decide whether a labour referee reads stage 3 as a protection or as a new job description. I take the brief's four questions in turn, then add one point the triad misses and my view of the referees.

### 1.1 Do the Helsinki, Bendoly and Uber passages say what their sources say?

The Uber passage does. The Helsinki passage adds a cause its source does not give, and the Bendoly passage claims more than an abstract can carry. Both changed between v8 and v9.

**Helsinki (The property ¶3, l. 73).** Two of the three claims hold:
- The manager says no receptionist "has to call someone to ask for permission whether he/she can give some discount" (FM, Schumacher and Komppula 2016: 122).
- Staff report that they are "reluctant to provide discounts higher than 10–20% of the room rates" (p. 122).

The third claim is the word "because": clerks "hesitated to exceed 10 to 20 percent of the room rate because" management never set limits. The source puts the two facts side by side, and concessively: "Even though there is no set amount that would be officially determined ... the interviewees consistently suggested that the frontline employees are reluctant" (p. 122). One receptionist gives another reason for her restraint: "I always think (in service recovery situations) right away what I should do according to the hotel's practice instead of what I would do myself" (F2, p. 122). The study reports two facts, and the authors infer the link between them. v8 kept the facts apart ("hesitated to go beyond 10–20 per cent ... and did not know where their authority ended"); v9 joined them with a cause.

The same study holds two findings the paper needs and does not use:
- Staff wanted written limits as proof that they were covered: "If we had written instructions we would have in black and white that we can compensate 20%" (F7, p. 123). No source the paper cites comes closer to its demand that the host "grant override authority in writing" (l. 144).
- Many staff were content with graded authority and wanted large cases to go up: "I don't need authority. I don't need a supervisor status. It is not my thing" (F1, p. 124). That bears on stage 3 (1.3).

At During the stay ¶3 the paper says "Staff at a Helsinki hotel depended primarily on guest feedback" (l. 117). The source agrees and gives the reason: "It is not a normal procedure that frontline employees would ask the customers how their stay has been at the hotel" (p. 121). The paper drops that clause, and from my side the clause matters. Guests were the main detector because the hotel did not task its staff with asking. How failures are found depends on staffing, and automation thins staffing.

**Bendoly (l. 73).** The library holds only the abstract (the card flags it as an abstract-only debt and a swap candidate). The rewrite changed three things:
- *Scope.* v8 said agents may accept "a rate below what a revenue-management system prescribes", which is the abstract's "latitude to accept rate bids below the pricing prescriptions". v9 says they "hold formal latitude to override prices suggested by revenue-management software". That is wider (any override, in either direction) and firmer ("formal").
- *Verb.* v8 wrote "Bendoly notes"; v9 writes "Bendoly (2013) shows". The abstract states the dependence on agents' judgment, and on "how their actions will be viewed by higher levels of management", as the study's premise. What the laboratory experiment tests is how two feedback metrics, one "revenue-focused" and one "framed around pricing-curve adherence", change agents' decisions. An abstract cannot show what the paper says Bendoly shows.
- *Contradiction.* The next sentence calls the same latitude "informal": "Algorithmic tools can strip away even that informal leeway". It cannot be formal one sentence and informal the next.

**"Strip away" (l. 73).** No hotel source in the paper supports this sentence. The paper's own hotel cases point the other way:
- Garcia et al.'s managers keep "the final decision right" (card, working paper p. 5).
- Bendoly's agents keep their latitude.
- Xu and Greiffenhagen's workers "manually checked them in" (p. 5035, n. 4).

The sentence therefore rests on Uber alone. The authors held hotel evidence and cut it "for want of words" (card). Spektor et al. (2023) ran workshops, interviews and prototyping sessions with 75 participants, unionized guest room attendants (p. 626). Their p. 628 does three jobs this paper needs:
- *Leeway removed:* "Most of the configurations used by our participants do not allow GRA's to order their own assignment order".
- *Blame for a systemic flaw:* a room attendant short of linens is told "again you haven't completed your rooms again" (Maria, p. 628).
- *Voice suppressed:* "due to the power differentials between GRAs and managers, most are unable to express complaints related to additional workload. This type of behavior could be understood as insubordination, and subject the GRA to discipline or even termination" (p. 628).

One citation and a reference entry would give the staff side a hotel source.

**Uber (l. 73).** The quotation is exact (Möhlmann et al. 2021: 2009), and the context fits: UberPOOL passengers asking for things that the "system's routing app and driving instructions" did not allow. The supporting sentence says workers are left "responsible for guest satisfaction without the controls needed to deliver it". The next page carries "responsible" better: drivers' ratings "varied greatly, sometimes due to circumstances beyond their control" (p. 2010). Extending the locator to 2009–2010 costs nothing. One caution: Uber drivers never held the latitude that Bendoly's agents hold. The analogy shows control without leeway, not leeway taken away.

**Jianu et al. (Conclusion ¶2, l. 179).** The Delphi panel forecasts that algorithmic management "will intensify work demands" (p. 1), and p. 7 speaks of "an expansion and intensification of line managers' jobs". Page 6 carries the nearby forecasts that the systems demand "higher efficiency levels" and may mean "more pressure for employees". v8 wrote "experts expect"; v9 writes "experts warn that algorithmic management also intensifies frontline hotel work". The rewrite states a forecast as present fact. Restore the forecast and extend the locator to 1, 6–7. (Naming Jianu et al. as the subject: also R3.)

**Sterz et al. and Li and Sun (At the touchpoint ¶4, l. 144).** Sterz et al. support the sentence: an overseer with only "'pseudo-options'" cannot "really make a relevant difference" (p. 2498). Li and Sun support something else. By the Crossref record, the article reports two survey experiments on public trust in algorithms and bureaucrats, in which accountability counts for more than accessibility (*Governance* 38:4, e70067). That supports the next sentence, "no substitute for an accountable counterparty", not the claim about a worker's power. Move the citation one sentence later. (The Li and Sun card gives the authors as "Hui" and "Zhiyuan". Crossref gives Huanhuan Li and Zongfeng Sun, as the v8 reference list does, so the manuscript needs no change; see VERIFY.)

### 1.2 Does "Training builds competence, whereas organizational rules confer standing" make sense for staff?

The first half is right and the noun is wrong (At the touchpoint ¶4, l. 144). I can read the sentence three ways, and two of the readings fail.

1. **Standing as the worker's power.** The paper already has a word for this. Authority is "the power to alter a decision or the rule behind it" (The host ¶4, l. 41), whereas standing is the guest's position (The host ¶3, l. 39). Using "standing" for staff gives the word a third sense (also R1, R2, R3).
2. **Standing as the guest's standing, conferred by the host's rules.** This reading fits the paper's definition. A guest's complaint counts when it is "addressed to a matching host who is bound to receive and answer it" (l. 41), so the rule that makes a clerk an authorized receiver would give the guest standing at that desk. But a standing that the host's own rules confer cannot also ground a duty on the host, and the abstract, introduction and conclusion all make standing that ground (ll. 5, 19, 177). On this reading, the one sentence about staff brings back the circularity in the panel's first finding.
3. **Standing as the worker's own standing toward the firm.** This is the reading a labour scholar would want. The worker at the end of the route needs a recognized position from which her report about a failing system counts, and is answered by the firm or vendor that can change the system. The sources show what happens without it:
   - Möhlmann's drivers had no such position and "blamed the algorithm" in front of passengers instead (p. 2010).
   - The Helsinki staff "have never received feedback from the managers" on the compensation they gave (p. 124).
   - Spektor's room attendants risked "discipline or even termination" for complaining (p. 628).

   Hales and Klidas name the missing element "voice", that is, "greater involvement in organizational decision-making", as against "choice" over how one's own work is done (1998, abstract only; card). The paper's audit question 6 and stage 4 build an upward route, but only for the guest's complaint, never for the worker's own report. A fourth sense of "standing" would cost more than it buys. The authors can give the worker that route in their own words (1.4).

The neat split between training and rules also overstates its sources:
- Sterz et al. list training as a facilitator that "could, in principle, target all of the conditions of effective human oversight", causal power included (p. 2501). Without competence, they add, an overseer is unlikely to be effective "if they even can be effective at all" (p. 2500).
- The law names the items side by side. The AI Act requires overseers with "the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support" (art. 26(2), card). The platform-work Directive the paper already cites uses the same formula (art. 10(2), card).

The authors' point is that training cannot do the work of authority, and that is also the law's point. Make it with the law's noun: **[TR]** "Training builds competence, whereas organizational rules confer authority." One word changes. Best spend (§4) adds what that authority needs in order to hold.

### 1.3 Does stage 3 of Table 2 ("Empower") protect workers or load them?

As written, stage 3 loads them. It grants "written authority, training, and protection" (Table 2, l. 170; question 4, l. 151), and these are the right three items. They match the statutory formula above, and the Helsinki staff asked for the first in their own words. But the paper leaves each item open at exactly the point where algorithmic management bites. The hotel empowerment literature has a name for that outcome. Hales and Klidas found that, in five-star hotels, empowerment "amounted to increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice over how work was done or more voice in organizational decisions" (1998, abstract only; card).

**Protection from what?** At the touchpoint ¶4 names one threat: workers should not absorb "blame for systemic flaws they cannot fix" (l. 144). Neither Table 2 nor question 4 says what the protection covers. The paper's own Bendoly sentence names the threat that matters most: agents' willingness to depart from the system turns on how management will view it. Sterz et al. say the same about oversight. A person who expects to "file a report after overwriting system outputs", and fears having "no good explanation for overriding", may not override at all (p. 2502). Written authority becomes a trap if overrides are logged and then scored against the worker on an adherence metric. The Directive the paper already cites at l. 121 states the protection exactly: those who oversee automated decisions "shall enjoy protection from dismissal or its equivalent, disciplinary measures and other adverse treatment where they exercise their functions" (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, art. 10(2), card). Stage 3 needs that clause, in the Directive's words or the authors' own.

**Authority over what system?** A written permission does not make an override possible. Among Sterz et al.'s "pseudo-options" are "environments in which the human's actions are by default not considered" (p. 2498). Spektor et al. found that a worker's leeway is a configuration the property chooses:
- self-sequencing "existed within UpKeep already, but simply weren't turned on in most properties" (2023: 634);
- room attendants "working together with management" phased out the one-room-at-a-time setting (2023: 629).

(The 2025 paper says instead that UpKeep "does not support self-sequencing as a formal algorithmic feature" (§6.1). Either way, the property and its labour contract set the leeway; see VERIFY.) Question 4 should ask whether the system will accept the override, not only whether a document permits it. That also strengthens the paper's allocation rule. The party that controls the space usually configures the vendor's system, so it holds the authority that the route needs.

**Knowledge.** The vendor section names three capacities: knowledge, discretion and authority (The vendor ¶1, l. 97). Stage 3 hands the worker authority and training but leaves knowledge with the vendor. Without the system's reasons, a clerk asked to override a rate or a refused identity check is in the position Jianu et al.'s experts describe. Line managers must coach frontline associates through "algorithmic decisions that are outside of their sphere of influence, and the workings of which manifest opaquely" (p. 7). Sterz et al. make "epistemic access" one of their four conditions of effective oversight (p. 2496). The asylum hotels the paper cites show what is at stake: staff "were made aware of" a resident's deteriorating mental health the night before an attack "but they lacked the knowledge needed to act" (Petie et al. 2025, MS p. 11, card). Petie et al. also report that a government evaluation found his queries "were dealt with appropriately" (MS p. 12, card), so the case should be used with care. Stage 3 should give the person at the end of the route access to the system's reasons.

**One person, or a graded route?** Stage 3 empowers "the person at the route's end". The Helsinki hotel ran a graded route instead. Reception handled small failures, and the hotel manager took "significantly large failures" and large compensation (p. 121), an arrangement many staff preferred (p. 124). If every frontline worker must be able to override every output, the stage loads people who did not ask for that power. A right to refer a case up without penalty protects them, and it costs a few words in the table.

**Who staffs it?** In one sentence, Conclusion ¶2 praises systems that "allow small teams to serve far more guests" and concedes that algorithmic management intensifies frontline work (l. 179). Meanwhile Table 1 requires "An on-site staff member empowered to intervene" for hazards (l. 129), and stage 3 requires someone at the end of every route. The paper never says that the further duty has a labour cost, or who bears it. The Directive does: platforms must "ensure sufficient human resources" for oversight (art. 10(2)), and the AI Act adds "the necessary support" (art. 26(2)). One clause in At the touchpoint ¶4 would close the gap. (Table 1 and self-check-in rentals: also R1.)

**A legal hook the paper misses [CONSIDER].** Annex III, point 4(b) of the AI Act lists as high-risk the systems used "to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits or characteristics or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons" in work relationships (saved OJ text). A hotel that assigns or scores its staff by algorithm may therefore owe art. 26(2) oversight on the staff side, from 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems (card, amended art. 113). That obligation depends on art. 6(3)'s exceptions, which I did not check (VERIFY). The paper notes the Act's exclusion of identity verification (l. 142). It does not note that the Act's firmest hold in a hotel may be on the systems that manage the person at the end of the guest's route.

My verdict on stage 3: it protects on paper and loads in practice. About 30 words in the table, question 4 and the conclusion, plus a 63-word anchor on art. 10(2) at l. 144, would turn it round (§4).

### 1.4 Is the paper's concern for staff real or ornamental?

The concern is real in the framing (ll. 37, 61). In five places where the instruments speak, it is ornamental.

1. **The goodwill problem moved onto staff.** In v8, question 2 and During the stay asked whether the guest could use the route "without depending on the host's goodwill". v9 reads:
   - "without relying on staff goodwill" (question 2, l. 149);
   - "rather than depending on staff goodwill" (During the stay ¶3, l. 117);
   - "depend on staff goodwill", new in Table 2's stage 2 (l. 169).

   The rewrite moved the obstacle from the firm to its employees, against the paper's own premise at l. 61. Xu and Greiffenhagen show why the firm is the right address. Guests could skip the kiosk "only ... when there was a technical problem (after all, the hotel was advertised as a 'robotic hotel')" (p. 5034). The "do it yourself" redirection (p. 5022) was the hotel's operating model, carried out by its workers. Restore "the host's goodwill" in all three places. This costs no words.
2. **The one duty of care on a person falls on the worker.** At the touchpoint ¶3 says "Whoever holds that override power must also exercise it with reasonable care" (l. 142). In practice, whoever holds the power is a clerk or a guard. The case cited holds the hotel: "a hotel that [evicts] a guest has a duty to exercise reasonable care" (*Groh* ¶ 4, card). The training claim also runs against the employer, on the principle that "a person or business conducting an activity through employees is subject to liability" for negligence in employing them (¶ 23, card). Make the duty the host's (Part 3, row 11). Four words.
3. **The *Groh* verdict stops at the guards.** The audit verdict rests on the allegation that the guards "lacked training to handle intoxicated patrons safely" (At the touchpoint ¶6, l. 156). As a verdict on the hotel, that holds. But the opinion records that a guard "summoned another Westin security guard as well as the guest services manager-supervisor" (¶ 6, card), and it does not say what that person did. The plea reached a manager. A labour referee will ask why the analysis ends at the guards' training. Elish has a name for that move: a "moral crumple zone", in which responsibility is "misattributed to a human actor who had limited control" and the arrangement "protects the integrity of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human operator" (2019: 41). One clause recording the manager-supervisor (¶ 6) would fix it. (*Groh* locators and the guest/friend distinction: also R1.)
4. **Table 2 speaks only for the guest.** Its third column is "What it secures for the guest" (l. 167). Stage 3 is the only stage whose first beneficiary is a worker, and the table records nothing for her. Let the "What the host does" cell carry the protection (§4).
5. **The take-home drops the protection.** The closing advice tells operators to "grant frontline workers written authority to override automated errors" (Conclusion ¶6, l. 187). Training and protection, two of stage 3's three items, are missing from the paragraph that practitioners will quote. The research agenda (Conclusion ¶5, l. 185) proposes four studies, and none of them studies the workers at the end of the route. Staff appear only as the independent variable, whether guests "reach an authorized on-site employee versus when they cannot". Each place needs about six words.

The paper's one sentence on labour cost (Jianu, l. 179) sits in a concessive clause, cut off from the duty whose costs it describes. The concern becomes real once Table 2, question 4 and the conclusion carry what At the touchpoint ¶4 already says. One more step would turn it into an argument [CONSIDER]: complaint tracking (question 6, stage 4) should log failures by touchpoint, not by employee. Jianu et al.'s experts warn that "there are more metrics that individual employees can compare between each other (and that management can compare without them knowing)" (p. 7, quoting a panellist). A learning system built for the guest can turn into a monitoring system for the worker unless the paper says otherwise.

### 1.5 What the knowledge–discretion–authority triad misses: steering

The vendor section reads the Garcia case as a clean split. The vendor holds authority over the rule, and "managers kept discretion over individual room rates" (The vendor ¶1, l. 97). The source supports more than that, and the extra is what matters to algorithmic management. In Garcia et al.'s model, managers' "adjustment costs ... induce a conflict of interest with the algorithmic advisor", and in equilibrium the recommendations are "strategically biased" (working paper, p. 4; card).

Read without any claim about the vendor's intent, the result is that the advisor's output is shaped by how the humans use their discretion. (The referees rightly object to "Knowing that managers rarely intervened, the vendor designed", l. 11; also R1, R2, R3.) Möhlmann et al. report the same mode at Uber, citing press accounts of nudges "to 'nudge them into driving longer'" through "alerts, pop-ups, and text messages" (p. 2008). The triad of knowledge, discretion and authority has no place for control that works through a recommendation rather than a command. A guest who reaches the manager reaches someone whose discretion was exercised under a system tuned to move her. One clause at l. 97 records it (Part 3, row 7). The intent claim at l. 11 can then go without losing the point. (VERIFY the clause against the version of record.)

### 1.6 Why hospitality, from the labour side [CONSIDER]

R2's fourth point asks what is specific to hospitality (also R2, R1). The staff side offers part of an answer the paper does not use. In a bank or an online shop, the end of the route is a call centre. In a hotel, the person at the end of the route shares the building with the aggrieved guest, often at night and often alone on shift, and bears the guest's displeasure in person. Two sources record the pattern outside hospitality and inside it. Möhlmann's driver says a passenger "starts fighting with me" over a route the system set (p. 2010). Schumacher and Komppula give a whole section to "Recovering from aggrieved customers" (§4.3.1, pp. 122–123). This is my argument, not a finding of either source. If the authors want it, one sentence in At the touchpoint ¶4 would make protection a hospitality-specific need rather than a generic one.

### 1.7 The referees and Grok, in my field

- **"Organizational rules confer standing."** I agree with R1 (4.3), R2 (point 3) and R3 (4.8) that this gives standing a third sense, and I add the circularity reading (1.2).
- **R3 on authority.** R3 calls its first-round point "answered in substance" (R3 3.6). I agree only in part. The paper now treats authority as bounded, but it treats the bound as missing paperwork that writing can cure. R3's first-round point was that authority "is always organisationally constrained". Under algorithmic management, configuration and metrics carry that constraint too (1.3). Until question 4 asks whether the system accepts the override and whether the override is penalized, an editor rereading R3's report will see that point answered only on paper.
- **R1 on Xu's "do it yourself".** R1 reads it as failing question 2 "on its goodwill limb" (R1 4.4(a)). R1 has the right limb and the wrong address. The redirection expressed the hotel's "robotic hotel" model (p. 5034), so the goodwill at issue is the host's, which is how v8 phrased question 2 (1.4, item 1).
- **Xu's "authorized to override" (l. 156).** I agree that this is the authors' inference (also R1, R3). Grok's suggested rewrite of At the touchpoint ¶2 ("push the person with override authority behind the machine") would harden that inference into the paragraph's opening claim. Xu and Greiffenhagen say only that workers "manually checked them in" (p. 5035, n. 4). Do not adopt the rewrite.
- **Grok on the triad.** Grok calls the knowledge–discretion–authority triad "precise". It is precise at l. 97, and then the paper drops it: stage 3 and question 4 give the worker authority without knowledge (1.3).
- **"Live repair" (l. 121).** R2 and R3 note that this rests partly on a platform-worker statute. I add that the article cited does not describe live repair. Article 11(3) requires rectification "without delay and in any case within two weeks of the adoption of the decision" (card), which is far longer than most stays. It should not count towards "routinely prioritize live repair".

## Part 2. The slop audit

In the staff passages, what reads as machine-made is the shape of the paragraphs, not the vocabulary.

- **Epigram after the point is made.** At the touchpoint ¶4 (l. 144) makes its point once ("accomplishes little if that worker can offer sympathy but lacks the power"). It makes it again as an aphorism ("Giving a guest a friendly face to vent to is no substitute"). It then closes a run on a chiasmus ("Training builds competence, whereas organizational rules confer standing"). The second sentence can go, unless the authors use it to carry Li and Sun (Part 3, row 12). The third should become a hook into the protection that the following sentences need (§4). Conclusion ¶2 (l. 179) states one idea three times: "genuinely human-centered requires keeping an answerable counterparty within reach", then "human-centricity depends on", then "Supplying that reachable counterparty is the core purpose of the further duty". One of the three is enough.
- **Stacked topics.** At the touchpoint ¶4 runs to 225 words over six topics:
  - Helsinki;
  - the human in the loop;
  - what the host must grant;
  - training against rules;
  - complaint tracking, with the UN Guiding Principles;
  - routing, with Lord Denning.

  Split it at "The host must also track complaints". The second half becomes the routing paragraph, and both halves land inside the 125–200 band.
- **Intensifiers.** "Genuine" or "genuinely" appears four times (ll. 31, 144, 170, 179). Each can go without loss.
- **A chain of connectives.** The property ¶3 (l. 73) opens four sentences running on contrast and comparison markers ("however", "Yet,", "Similarly,", "However,"). Relation markers suit this register, but four in a row read as assembled. The comma after "Yet" is out of place.
- **An abstract noun as agent.** The property ¶4 reads "Yet operational reality inside the building often diverges from legal responsibility" (l. 75). An abstraction does the acting, and three small sources cannot carry "often". Part 3, row 6 gives a rewrite.

Four passages do not read as machine-made, and they should stay:
- the sequence in The property ¶3, which moves in four steps from Lord Denning to a staffed hotel, a pricing desk and a platform;
- Lord Denning's handrail (l. 144), an exact legal analogue for reporting up the chain;
- "Guests walked past staff to use the lobby kiosks" (l. 140);
- "a phone number that the owner actually answers" (l. 162).

Two notes on Grok's style list. The authors' house rules allow em-dashes that weld a gloss onto a term, so dashes are a matter of the journal's dash style, not of removal. And of Grok's three passive-voice targets, only "is owed for as long as" (l. 121) occurs in v9. "Duties are anchored to the property" and "a further duty is owed" do not.

## 1. Who I am

I study algorithmic management in service and platform work, that is, how rating systems, task allocators and pricing tools move discretion among workers, managers and the firms that build the software. My reference points are Möhlmann et al. on Uber, Rahman's "invisible cage", and the hotel studies by Spektor and colleagues on room attendants. I came to this paper expecting what I usually find in customer-side papers about the "human in the loop": a worker who appears as the guest's remedy, with no voice, no budget and no defence. The paper is better than that. Its framing refuses to make the frontline employee the host and places the duty on the firm. Its instruments have not caught up with its framing.

## 2. The read

Ranked by the damage each would do with a second-round referee or the editor.

**1. The staff-side sources drift from what they say [FIX].**
- *Claim:* Helsinki clerks held back "because" limits were undefined. Bendoly "shows" that agents hold "formal latitude to override prices". Algorithmic tools "strip away even that informal leeway". Experts "warn" that algorithmic management "intensifies" frontline work (ll. 73, 179).
- *What fails:* the Helsinki source gives two facts and no cause (p. 122). Bendoly is an abstract that states as premise what the paper says it shows, about a narrower latitude, and the paper calls that latitude formal and then informal. "Strip away" has no hotel source. Jianu et al. make a forecast. Three of the four changes are v8 hedges or wordings that v9 dropped.
- *Cost:* about 10 words net (Part 3, rows 1–5, 23).

**2. Stage 3 loads the worker it means to protect [FIX].**
- *Claim:* the host grants "written authority, training, and protection" (l. 170; question 4, l. 151).
- *What fails:* the paper never says what the protection covers. Its own Bendoly sentence, and Sterz et al. (p. 2502), show that overrides which count against the worker will not be made. Written authority does not make an override possible in the system (Sterz et al. p. 2498; Spektor et al. 2023: 629, 634). The worker gets no access to the system's reasons, which the paper's own triad sends to the vendor (l. 97). Every frontline worker is made the end of the route, where Helsinki ran a graded one (pp. 121, 124). Directive 2024/2831 art. 10(2), already in the reference list, states the missing protection exactly.
- *Cost:* 30 words for the table, question 4 and the conclusion (rows 18, 22, 25), and 63 more to anchor the protection on art. 10(2) at l. 144 (§4).

**3. The rewrite moved the goodwill problem from the host to staff [FIX].**
- *Claim:* the guest must use the route without "staff goodwill" (ll. 117, 149, 169).
- *What fails:* v8 said "the host's goodwill". v9 relocates the obstacle onto employees, against l. 61. The Xu case shows a hotel model, not a staff disposition (p. 5034).
- *Cost:* 1 word (rows 9, 17, 21).

**4. The one personal duty of care falls on the worker [FIX].**
- *Claim:* "Whoever holds that override power must also exercise it with reasonable care" (l. 142).
- *What fails:* it places a duty on the clerk or guard, against l. 61. *Groh* holds the hotel, and the training claim runs against the employer (¶¶ 4, 23).
- *Cost:* 4 words (row 11).

**5. "Organizational rules confer standing" [FIX] (also R1, R2, R3).**
- *Claim:* l. 144.
- *What fails:* on one reading, the noun should be "authority". On the other, a standing conferred by the host's rules cannot ground the host's duty, which brings back the circularity in finding 1 (1.2).
- *Cost:* 0 words.

**6. The worker at the end of the route is given authority without knowledge [CONSIDER].**
- *Claim:* stage 3 and question 4 list authority, training and protection.
- *What fails:* the paper's own triad (l. 97) leaves knowledge with the vendor. Jianu et al. (p. 7), Sterz et al. (p. 2496) and Petie et al. (MS p. 11) all show staff who cannot act without the system's reasons.

The manager the guest reaches decides under a system tuned to move her. Recording this also lets the authors drop the intent claim at l. 11 (also R1, R2, R3) without losing the point.

**8. The paper has no hotel evidence on the algorithmic management of staff [CONSIDER].**

## 3. Where it is strong

These must survive any revision:
- **The decoupling of employee and host.** The host ¶2 (l. 37) and Approach ¶3 (l. 61: "not to the individual worker standing in the lobby") carry it. Repeat it where the instruments speak: at l. 142, in Table 2 and in the conclusion.
- **The knowledge–discretion–authority triad (l. 97).** Labour scholars can use it as it stands. It needs only to carry through to stage 3.
- **Question 4 asks whether override power is "real".** That is the right question. It needs two more words of content.
- **The blame-shield clause (l. 144)** and Lord Denning's handrail, which give routing a legal ancestor.
- **The Möhlmann quotation.** It is exact and well chosen.
- **The Helsinki hotel as a staffed baseline**, with "a phone number that the owner actually answers" (l. 162) as its small-host equivalent.

## 4. Best spend

With 100 of the spare words, I would make stage 3 protect the worker and carry the protection through to the audit, the table and the take-home. Four edits come to 96 words.

**At the touchpoint ¶4, l. 144.** Replace "Training builds competence, whereas organizational rules confer standing." (9 words) with (72 words; +63):

> **[TR]** Training builds competence, whereas organizational rules confer authority, and that authority holds only if a worker who uses it in good faith is not penalized for doing so. EU law already asks this of digital labour platforms: those who oversee automated decisions must have 'the competence, training and authority necessary', including 'for overriding automated decisions', and protection from 'disciplinary measures and other adverse treatment' when they do (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, art. 10(2)).

**Table 2, stage 3, "What the host does", l. 170.** Replace the cell (12 words) with (29 words; +17):

> **[TR]** Grants the person at the route's end written authority, training, access to the system's reasons, a manager to refer to, and protection from penalty for overriding in good faith

**Question 4, l. 151** (+6):

> **[TR]** Is that override power real: possible in the system, backed by written authority and staff training, and protected from penalty?

**At the touchpoint ¶3, l. 142** (+4), which keeps the duty with the firm, as l. 61 does:

> **[TR]** The host must also see that whoever holds that override power uses it with reasonable care.

If 7 more words are available, close the conclusion's operator list (l. 187) with "**[TR]** ... to override automated errors, and protect them when they use it". The zero-cost companion is restoring "the host's goodwill" at ll. 117, 149 and 169.

## 5. Verdict

**From the staff side, minor revision.** Every fix in my field takes under an hour, and the whole set, optional items included, costs about 150 of the roughly 500 spare words. The ground of the further duty lies outside my field, and I would not overrule R2's major recommendation on it.

**The single most important fix:** specify what stage 3 protects the worker from. That means protection from penalty for overriding in good faith, anchored on Directive 2024/2831 art. 10(2), carried into question 4, Table 2 and the conclusion. With it goes the zero-cost restoration of "the host's goodwill" in place of "staff goodwill".

## 6. VERIFY

1. **Bendoly (2013).** Abstract only. Check against the full text: whether "how their actions will be viewed by higher levels of management" is a premise or a finding; what the two feedback metrics did to agents' deviation; and whether the participants were hotel staff or students.
2. **Petie et al. (2025).** The card's locators are pages of the accepted manuscript (MS pp. 9, 11, 12), not the journal's (15:1, pp. 23–44).
