# 06 · Reviewer 2 (anonymous, unimpressed): DRAFT_v9, "Who hosts the guest?"

## Recommendation: major revision

Major revision, not rejection. The paper has one contribution worth publishing, and it is buried under four instruments the paper does not own. The contribution is a duty the host owes the guest during the stay: a route by which a failure in the guest's case reaches someone who can correct it while correction can still help, owed by the firm that controls the space the guest occupies. That claim held up against every neighbouring literature I checked it against. The seven questions, the five stages and the case against seamlessness do not survive as contributions in their present form. The questions and stages largely restate the effectiveness criteria the UN Guiding Principles set for grievance mechanisms. Contestability research already asks for a human reviewer with the authority to change the decision. The paper defines contestability so that it loses (The host ¶4, l. 41), says nothing of seamful design, and calls its own duty 'accountability' in the abstract and keywords although its own definitions place accountability after the stay. In round one Reviewer 2 asked whether this is 'organizational delegation or algorithmic accountability rather than a specific hospitality theory'. That question is answered only on paper. The repair costs words, not research, and cuts can pay for most of it.

## Part 1. Rigour and argument

### What the paper has already answered, and I will not press

- The paper is conceptual, and its cases 'illustrate analytical distinctions rather than test them' (Approach ¶2, l. 59).
- It proposes four studies to test itself (Conclusion ¶5, l. 185).
- It concedes that the further duty 'remains a normative guide … rather than settled law' (At the touchpoint ¶7, l. 158).
- It separates its window from runtime oversight (Baum and Laux, l. 121) and from Hindriks's duty of control (l. 119).
- It credits Ariyogi et al. and Suh et al. with recommending the route itself (l. 119).
- It treats frontline authority as bounded by the organisation (the Helsinki clerks at l. 73; question 4).

'No data' is not my objection either. A conceptual paper may stand on other people's cases, provided it reports them accurately. The misreports are already listed in the brief (items 5 and 6) and I do not repeat them.

### 1. What is new against the neighbours

**(a) Contestability.** The host ¶4 (l. 41) separates standing from four neighbouring concepts and gives contestability one uncited clause: 'contestability refers to a technical mechanism for challenging an automated output'. v8 hedged this: 'Contestability, at least as design work mostly uses it, is a feature of a system' (v8 l. 49). v9 drops the hedge and narrows the term twice, from a feature of a system to a *technical mechanism* and from a decision to an *automated output*. The field does not use the term that way.

Alfrink et al. define contestability by design as systems 'responsive to human intervention throughout the system lifecycle' (2023: abstract). They hold that human controllers 'should have the authority and capability to actually change previous decisions', citing Brkan. Citing Sarra, they hold that controllers 'must make room within their organizations for receiving, evaluating and responding to disputes'. They also require that a decision subject know 'that there is a means of contesting' (§§6.1.3–6.1.4; card, and wording confirmed in the saved text). Lyons et al. list 'who is held accountable for decisions' among the policy questions that contestability must settle first (2021: §5.1, arXiv p. 12; saved text).

Set those beside the audit. Question 2 (is the guest informed of a route?), questions 3–4 (can the person at the end really depart from the output?) and stage 5 (an organisational forum) already appear in contestability research. Grok calls the standing–contestability distinction 'one of the paper's sharper conceptual contributions'. On this evidence I disagree (also Grok, contra).

What contestability research does *not* have is the paper's real contribution, and the paper should say so:

- Alfrink et al. ground contestability in autonomy ('protects human self-determination', §2), not in a role.
- They run it across a system's lifecycle, not across a guest's stay.
- When Lyons et al. report who should be accountable, the submissions they summarize point to 'the entities that can influence a decision' (§5.1.3, arXiv p. 12). In the paper's cases that is the vendor. The paper puts the route on the firm that controls the space, *because* the guest can reach it. The allocation is different, and defending it is the paper's job.

Alfrink et al. even mention, for public services, 'the duty of care government organizations have towards citizens' (§7; saved text). So deriving a route from a duty of care has at least a passing precedent. Deriving it from the host role, with the allocation and the window that the role brings, does not.

**(b) The UN Guiding Principles.** The paper cites the Principles three times, each for a single point:

- accessibility (Conclusion ¶4, l. 183; pp. 31–32);
- learning (At the touchpoint ¶4, l. 144; pp. 32, 34);
- an independent forum (Conclusion ¶5, l. 185; p. 35).

It then says that policy frameworks 'point in a similar direction without grounding these safeguards in the role of host' (l. 183). The overlap is closer than 'a similar direction'. Principle 29 asks for operational-level grievance mechanisms so that grievances are 'addressed early and remediated directly' (p. 31), and Principle 31 sets eight effectiveness criteria (pp. 33–34; card). Here is the mapping:

| Paper | UN Guiding Principles (card quotations) |
|---|---|
| Q2; stage 2 'Tell' | 31(b) Accessible: 'being known to all stakeholder groups' and 'adequate assistance' for those facing barriers (p. 33) |
| Q5 (timing) | 31(c) Predictable: 'an indicative time frame for each stage' (p. 33) |
| Q6; stage 4 'Learn and route' | 31(g) 'A source of continuous learning' (p. 34); 'analysing trends and patterns in complaints' (P29 commentary, p. 32) |
| Q7; stage 5 'Answer' | an enterprise 'cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome' (p. 35) |
| Q3–4; stage 3 'Empower' | only P19: responsibility 'assigned to the appropriate level and function' (p. 20) |

On the card's quotations, the Principles lack two things. None of the eight criteria asks that the person who receives a grievance have power over the particular decision. And for an impact that an enterprise is only *linked* to through a business partner, the commentary says the responsibility 'does not require that the enterprise itself provide for remediation' (pp. 24–25). Questions 3–4 add the first. The paper's allocation goes beyond the second: it puts the route on the controller of the space whichever firm's component failed. Those two additions, with the stay as the window, are what the paper contributes. The instruments are a transposition of the Principles. A referee who knows the Principles will say so if the authors do not.

**(c) Service recovery.** During the stay ¶4 (l. 119) separates the further duty from service recovery, 'which treats complaint handling as a strategy for protecting customer satisfaction and loyalty'. That reading of Gelbrich and Roschk is thin. Their meta-analysis of 87 studies (N = 28,826) runs complaint handling through three justice perceptions (distributive, interactional and procedural) before it reaches satisfaction and loyalty (2011: 28, 36; card). The paper itself sets procedural justice aside as how fairly a person 'perceives a process' (l. 41).

The paper's operational output also looks like recovery practice. Stage 3, 'Empower', is the recommendation of the paper's own Helsinki source, whose title begins 'A case study on service recovery' and ends 'the role of empowerment' (Schumacher and Komppula 2016). Stage 4, 'Learn', is complaint management.

The paper does part from recovery in the next sentence of l. 119: a host with no channel 'wrongs its guests even if nothing goes wrong during their visit'. Service recovery starts from a failure, and accountability from a completed decision. A duty breached before either is the one thing neither literature can express, and it is the paper's hinge against both. The paper states it in one sentence and argues it in none (also R2, R3). R2 adds that it sits badly beside the dismissal of Hindriks for resting on risk. The paper's first reason, that the duty 'binds even when retaining the guest is unprofitable', separates a duty from a strategy, as any normative account would. Only the second separates this duty from recovery as such. Grok says the paper 'correctly distinguishes' the duty from service recovery. The distinction is correct, but the paper leads with the weaker reason and leaves the stronger one unargued.

**(d) Seamful design.** At the touchpoint ¶8 (l. 160) offers interruptible hospitality as 'the alternative to frictionless service' and sets it against phygital design frameworks alone. Interaction designers have argued against seamlessness for twenty years. Chalmers and Galani define seamful design as 'carefully revealing differences and limitations of systems, in ways that support social interaction' (2004; card, partly confirmed in the saved eprint). Ehsan et al. carry it to AI: 'strategically revealing (and concealing) seams to support user agency' (2024; card). Neither v8 nor v9 cites either, though the library holds both cards. A referee from HCI will read l. 160 as seamful design in a hospitality register.

The difference is real, and one sentence states it. A seam shows the guest where the system ends; interruptibility requires someone answerable to stand there. The words for that, 'with an accountable counterparty ready to catch the interruption', are already in l. 160. Naming the seam lets a reader see what they add.

**What survives.** Four things survive the four comparisons:

1. the ground, the host role;
2. the allocation, to the controller of the space whatever component failed;
3. the window, the stay;
4. the wrong without failure.

That is a contribution. The paper should claim it in those terms and stop presenting the instruments as its own.

### 2. Are the seven questions checkable in practice, or only in principle?

They are checkable in principle. In practice, three of the seven have a usable pass condition as written, the unit of the audit is unstated, and the paper's two worked applications show the gap.

*Pass conditions.*

- Q1 ('Who detects …') and Q7 ('who must answer …', l. 154) ask *who*, and grade nothing.
- Q5 turns on 'while the stay still allows a meaningful remedy' (l. 152) and gives no time standard. Table 1 sets 'that day' for one row and 'immediately' for another, but the list never points to it.
- Q6 (l. 153) asks two questions, learning across cases and routing between firms. A host can pass one and fail the other.
- Q2, Q3 and Q4 can be answered yes or no, though Q4's 'institutional protection' is undefined.

*Unit.* Table 2 applies the questions 'to each touchpoint' (l. 168). Every question, however, asks about 'a failure in the guest's case', and Table 1 shows that one touchpoint carries several decisions with different routes. The unit of the audit is therefore a touchpoint and a decision taken together. Saying so would turn Table 1 into the audit's rows.

*Evidence.* At the touchpoint ¶5 offers the test 'for researchers studying phygital services and for hosts auditing their own properties' (l. 146). Neither audience can answer Q3 and Q4 as things stand:

- A researcher sees outcomes, not authority. Xu and Greiffenhagen filmed check-ins. They report that a worker 'manually checked them in' and say nothing of the worker's authority (note 4, p. 5035; saved text). So 'authorized to override the kiosk' (l. 156) is inferred (also R1, R2, R3).
- A host auditing itself grades its own route. The paper's own Guiding Principles quotation (l. 185) doubts that a business can 'unilaterally determine' the outcome of complaints against it. I extend that doubt, by analogy, to a self-audit.

The paper should say what evidence settles each question. Q3–Q4 need a written instruction. Q2 and Q5 need a test report lodged at the touchpoint and timed. The third study proposed in Conclusion ¶5 (l. 185) is already that method.

*Outcome or design.* The first worked application finds that the kiosk arrangement 'passes the third question while failing the second' (At the touchpoint ¶6, l. 156). Yet by the saved text, all thirteen guests the kiosk could not process 'looked for a service worker who manually checked them in' (note 4, p. 5035). The test's headline question, 'can a failure in a guest's case reach someone able to correct it in time?' (l. 146), gets yes thirteen times in thirteen while a sub-question fails. The verdict is defensible, since a guest should not have to hunt for help. But the paper must say that the test grades the route, not the outcome; otherwise its first worked example contradicts its own headline.

*Reach.* Groh, the second application, involves a security guard and no system output (also R1). The test is never run on the opening pricing case, which has no guest and no report (also R1, R2, R3 on Garcia). Nor is it run on the brand, the platform, or Bosma's co-host who took instructions from the app (l. 91). A test that cannot be run on the paper's opening case tells the reader that the opening case is the wrong one.

Grok calls the test 'clear and operationalizable'. It is clear. It is not yet operational (also Grok, contra).

### 3. Do the five stages follow from the argument, or were they attached?

Three stages follow; two were attached; and the element that makes the duty distinctive has no stage.

- **The three that follow.** Stages 2–4 track Q2, Q3–4 and Q6 (also R1, R3).
- **The two attached.** Stage 1, 'Examine', is the test applying itself. Stage 5, 'Answer', cannot follow from the further duty, because the paper ends that duty with the stay. 'Once the stay ends, the guest's claims shift toward financial compensation and formal accountability' (During the stay ¶5, l. 121), and 'the further duty governs live repair during the stay' (Conclusion ¶5, l. 185). Stage 5 comes from Bovens and the Guiding Principles.
- **The missing one.** Timeliness (Q5) has no stage (also R3). It is the element that makes the duty 'further' and confines it to the stay.

Two changes since v8 make this worse:

- v8 said Table 2 set out 'five stages a host can take in turn' (v8 l. 171) and captioned it '(the order is ours)' (v8 l. 173). v9 drops the hedge from the caption (l. 164) and upgrades the text to 'five sequential stages for hosts' (At the touchpoint ¶9, l. 162). The stages are not sequential. Learning runs across stays, telling and empowering are standing conditions, and answering follows the stay.
- In round one Reviewer 3 asked for 'better and longer explanation' of Table 2 and of 'its connection to the previous discussion'. v9 gives one sentence (l. 162) and the definition of a forum. That point is answered on paper.

The stages also exist in three versions:

- the abstract's 'five steps for host accountability' (l. 5);
- Table 2's five stages;
- Conclusion ¶6's four actions (l. 187).

The fourth of those actions, 'accept institutional responsibility for the rules their software enforces', is not a stage, and the list omits 'Learn and route' (also R1 on the omission). The action also sits awkwardly beside The vendor ¶3, where the vendor 'governs the automated rule' (l. 101). The conclusion asks the property to answer for a rule the paper says it cannot rewrite. That is the split between authority and answerability the paper diagnoses at ll. 41 and 65, unless a contract with the vendor closes it (cf. R3 4.4; brief item 4).

### 4. Is 'What this article adds' one contribution or several?

Conclusion ¶4 names one contribution in two parts: 'What this article adds is a derivation of that escalation route' (l. 183), with the route assigned to the controller of the space. During the stay ¶4 says the same (l. 119). It is the right contribution. But the paper claims others elsewhere:

- The abstract offers three deliverables: it 'maps how duties split', 'offers seven questions' and 'outlines five steps' (l. 5). None of them is the contribution named at l. 183.
- The introduction and conclusion offer three claims (ll. 19, 177). The first two are, on the paper's own account, a reading of what law already records (The host's duty in law ¶4, l. 53).
- Interruptible hospitality challenges 'a central ideal in phygital hospitality scholarship' (l. 160), and the conclusion never mentions it (also R1).
- Standing is set apart from four neighbouring concepts (l. 41).

The journal's Statement of Contribution asks for one answer in 100–150 words (`JOURNAL_SPEC.md`). At present the abstract, which a referee reads first, names the instruments, while the conclusion names the derivation. The ground inside the l. 183 sentence ('directly from the host's underlying duty') also conflicts with l. 177 ('standing grounds'). That conflict is already found (brief item 1; also R1, R2, R3). Contribution stated late: also R3 3.12.

One dropped hedge belongs here. v8 scoped the novelty: 'In the literature we searched, we found no work that derives' (v8 l. 193). v9 drops the scope and states the addition flat. Approach ¶1 describes the search without its terms (l. 57; also R3). Restoring v8's clause costs a few words and costs the claim nothing.

### 5. Is this just algorithmic accountability for hotels?

v9 holds the materials for a 'no', then argues against itself in three places.

**Vocabulary.** By the paper's own definitions, accountability is 'the duty to explain and justify actions to someone entitled to ask' (l. 41), and it arrives after the stay (l. 121). The further duty is live correction, which in l. 41's terms is responsibility and authority. Yet the abstract sells 'five steps for host accountability' (l. 5), the keywords end on 'accountability' (l. 7), and Conclusion ¶4 ends 'accountability runs through control of that space' (l. 183). The paper describes its distinctive claim in the vocabulary of the literature it must be told apart from.

**The nearest rival is cited for something it does not say.** Cobbe et al. (2023) describe the algorithmic supply chain:

- there is 'no one actor in overall control' (p. 1192);
- providers 'disclaim legal accountability' (p. 1195);
- accountability should be allocated by the actual distribution of responsibility, which 'requires processes and criteria' (p. 1193);
- the authors 'do not offer concrete technical or organisational proposals' (p. 1187).

The card checks all four against the version of record. The paper cites Cobbe et al. once, beside Slattery, for the statement that 'Commercial lodging today routinely runs through several firms at once' (Introduction ¶4, l. 17). Cobbe et al. say nothing about lodging; their p. 1187 supports only the vendors. Used properly, they give the paper its cleanest novelty statement: they ask for an allocation criterion and offer none, and the paper supplies one (control of the space) for one relation (host and guest) and one window (the stay). See Best spend.

**The duties.** The two duties the paper calls 'core' are ones that any occupier and any trader owe. The Consumer Rights Act implies care and skill into 'Every contract to supply a service' (s. 49(1); card). The authorities on reliance and control concern a nightclub (Everett, Naylor), a leisure centre (Maguire), a demolition site (Ferguson), a foster placement (Armes), a bank's examining doctor (Barclays) and a care home (YL) (cards; also R2, who makes the occupier point).

The duty that is a host's alone in the paper's legal record is the innkeeper's duty to receive travellers. Blackstone's 'implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way' (l. 47), Constantine and s. 1(1) of the Hotel Proprietors Act (l. 69) all carry it. The thesis leaves that duty out of its 'two core duties' (ll. 5, 19, 177), though The property ¶4 lists it third (l. 75) (also R2, R3 on the count). The paper's last sentence then reaches for it: 'someone still owes the guest a welcome' (l. 187; also R1, R3).

The answer to R2's round-one question lies there, and the paper holds every piece of it:

- A host holds itself out to strangers.
- It is bound to receive them.
- It lodges them for a period in space it controls, where leaving is costly (During the stay ¶1 and ¶3, ll. 113, 117).

Banks and online shops hold themselves out but do not lodge anyone. Hospitals and care homes do lodge people, and one sentence should say how far the duty reaches them (R2 asks for 'a sentence each on a bank and a hospital'). Instead, Introduction ¶5 says the framework 'applies to any phygital service where several firms stand behind' a single touchpoint (l. 19). That concedes R2's point (also R1, R2, R3).

v8 also carried two boundary conditions: 'we leave aside guests who cannot report a failure and hosts who let rooms' in their own homes (v8 l. 27). v9 drops both along with the scope sentence. The brief records only that this left Kropf uncited. The first exclusion matters to the test, which assumes a guest who can report (Q1–Q2). The second matters to Bosma's rentals (l. 91).

A small legal point of scope belongs here. 'A guest who receives substandard service can demand repeat performance' (The property ¶1, l. 69) holds only for a consumer. The Act defines a consumer as 'an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside that individual's trade, business, craft or profession' (s. 2(3); card). On that definition a business traveller is not one. The same limit narrows 'routinely prioritize live repair' (l. 121; brief item 7).

## Part 2. The slop audit

**What reads as machine-made or padded:**

- **The landing-line drumbeat.** About a dozen prose paragraphs end on a maxim or a 'rather than' verdict:
  - Introduction ¶4 (l. 17);
  - Phygital hospitality ¶2 (l. 27);
  - The host ¶1 and ¶3 (ll. 35, 39);
  - The brand ¶2 (l. 81);
  - The vendor ¶1 (l. 97);
  - During the stay ¶6 (l. 134);
  - At the touchpoint ¶6 and ¶8 (ll. 156, 160);
  - Conclusion ¶2, ¶3, ¶4 and ¶6 (ll. 179–187).

  R3 names three of them. Each is tolerable alone. Together they flatten the one that matters, the second clause of the chiasmus at l. 160 ('an interface the guest cannot interrupt'). I would keep three: l. 81 ('not the sign above the entrance'), l. 160 and l. 187. The rest should become forward hooks or go.
- **The contrast frame.** I count 15 instances of 'rather than' in the body. The doctrinal ones are fine. The rhetorical ones are the antithesis engine: 'rather than merely speeding up existing routines' (l. 27), 'a question of duty rather than etiquette' (l. 39), 'rather than keeping every interaction face-to-face' (l. 179).
- **Intensifiers.**
  - 'Genuine' or 'genuinely' appears at ll. 144, 170 and 179; at l. 31 it is Lee and Lu's term and stands.
  - 'Clear' grades the paper's own argument at ll. 53 and 59, and 'pinpoints exactly' does at l. 156 (also R3).
  - 'Core' appears eight times ('core duties', 'core obligations', 'core purpose', 'core parts'). It does the work of a definition the paper never gives: which duties are core, and why receiving is not.
- **Two quartets in one paragraph.** The host ¶4 announces 'four related concepts' and then 'four neighboring terms' (l. 41). The paragraph reads as templated, and at 208 words it runs past the register's 200.
- **The abstract's last sentence.** A participial opener followed by a triplet of deliverables is the shape of a machine-written abstract. Here the deliverables are also the wrong ones (Part 1, §4).
- **A repeated hook formula.** 'To see where obligations remain intact … we examine' (l. 65) and 'To see why a phygital host owes … we must examine' (l. 109) use the same formula. Forward hooks are good practice; vary them. They do not stand in for the missing roadmap.

**What does not read as machine-made:**

- The doctrinal paragraphs of the party sections: named courts and judges, concrete numbers ('10 to 20 percent', l. 73), and cases doing things in subject position.
- The definitions of knowledge, discretion and authority at l. 97: plain, each glossed once and then used.
- 'Posting a phone number that the owner actually answers' (l. 162): concrete and human. Keep it, subject to brief item 8 on stage 5.
- At the touchpoint ¶2 (l. 140) says automation kept the worker in the building, 'yet it pushed that person behind the machine'. This is the paper's best sentence, and the Xu card supports it.
- There are no emphasis markers ('Crucially') and no filler transitions. Grok is right on that.

## 1. Who I am

I review for service-research and hospitality-management journals and, less often, for HCI and AI-governance venues. I have read a good many papers that put 'algorithmic accountability' in front of an industry name. I came to this one expecting service recovery in moral dress: a theory that, once its vocabulary is translated, asks hotels to keep a complaints desk with authority. I also believed that a conceptual paper in a field this empirical must earn its novelty against the strongest version of its neighbours, not the weakest. The paper partly changed my mind. The duty it derives is not service recovery, and its rule for allocating that duty is its own. It has not yet said so in terms that a referee from either neighbouring field would accept.

## 3. Where it is strong

- **The window.** During the stay ¶1 and ¶5 (ll. 113, 121) and the shape of Table 1 tie the duty to the guest's subjection to the host's space, and end it with the stay. This is the paper's real answer to both of R2's round-one points. It must survive any revision, and it should move forward in the argument.
- **The allocation rule.** Assigning the route to the firm that controls the space, whichever firm's component failed, goes beyond both the Guiding Principles' rule for linked impacts and contestability's 'entities that can influence a decision'. It is the paper's own.
- **The three capacities.** Knowledge, discretion and authority (The vendor ¶1, l. 97) is precise and usable, and it does the analytic work that the l. 41 quartet only announces.
- **The party sections.** They are concrete and doctrinal, and the brand section's cases in particular do real work. The occupier law is general, but the walk itself is the best evidence the paper offers that the split happens in practice.
- **Honest limits.** Approach ¶2 (l. 59) and At the touchpoint ¶7 (l. 158) concede what the law does not yet require. A hostile reader respects that, and so do I.
- **Line 140.** The sentence ending 'yet it pushed that person behind the machine' carries the paper's thesis in one observed fact.

## 4. Best spend

I would spend 100 words answering R2's question in the paper's own terms, in place of Conclusion ¶3 (l. 181). That paragraph repeats the party verdicts (also R3) and runs to 140 words, so the swap saves about 40. The paragraph sits beside the contribution sentence at l. 183. Drafted by me, not the authors' wording:

> Our question differs from the one algorithmic accountability asks. Cobbe et al. ask who must answer for what an automated system has done when several firms each control part of it, and they call for criteria to allocate that answerability without proposing any (2023: 1187, 1193). We ask who must be reachable while the guest is still inside, and we give one criterion: control of the space the guest occupies. A bank or an online shop holds itself out to customers but does not lodge them. A host does both, and its guest, who cannot easily leave, is owed correction before accountability.

## 5. Verdict

**Major revision.** On this I side with R2 against R1 and R3. They call it minor because the repairs are textual, and they are. But a revision that leaves the contribution where a contestability referee or a business-and-human-rights referee can take it apart is not minor.

**The single most important fix:** state one contribution, and set it against contestability, the Guiding Principles' grievance mechanisms and algorithmic accountability by naming what each already has. That contribution is the further duty, drawn from one ground, owed during the stay by the firm that controls the space. The seven questions and five stages then become what they are: the application, not the contribution.
