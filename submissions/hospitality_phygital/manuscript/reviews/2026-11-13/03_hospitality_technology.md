## Step 0. Register note

v9 reads as the authors' own, and for most of its length it meets the journal's register for conceptual articles. 'We advance three claims' (Introduction ¶5, l. 19) and 'We argue' (During the stay ¶2, l. 115) carry the thesis. In most sentences that lean on a scholar, the scholar is the subject: Liu et al., Sharma and Mattila, Xu and Greiffenhagen and Bosma all act in their own sentences.

My note concerns the case narration, where register and accuracy fail together. When v9 retells a study it slips into the voice of a story:

- a vendor 'knowing' and 'designing' (Introduction ¶1, l. 11);
- guests 'actively tracking' a worker down (At the touchpoint ¶2, l. 140);
- 'the thirteen guests whom the kiosk rejected' (¶3, l. 142).

Readers in my field check a study's verbs first. The sources' verbs are plainer: the algorithm *recommended*, guests *looked for* a worker, the kiosk *could not process* them, smart locks *allow* entry. Restoring those verbs fixes most of what follows. A second tell is the intensifier adverb, which in this draft sits almost exactly on the sentences that outrun their sources (Part 2).

## Part 1. Rigour and argument, from my lens

### 1.1 Does the novelty claim still hold?

My literature is where the novelty claim lives, and in its narrow form it holds. I found no work among the paper's neighbours, cited or held in the library, that does any of three things:

- derives what a guest is owed from the role of host;
- binds it to one of several firms by holding out, reliance and control;
- sets a window, the stay, within which a route to correction is owed.

The nearest neighbours, as their texts and cards record, stop short of all three:

- **Sharma and Mattila** leave the question of who is to blame to 'business governance and risk management' (2026: 915).
- **Liu et al.** ask 'whether guests feel hosted' (2026: 1), and they deny the robot 'moral disposition ... or ethical agency' (2026: 9).
- **Suh et al.** prescribe 'a visible route to human assistance' (§6.2) and give it no ground.
- **Ariyogi et al.** assign 'recovery ownership at organizational level' (2026: 79). They rest it on how guests evaluate the brand and on what makes recovery work, never on a role, and never ask which of several firms owns it (card).
- **Binesh and Syah** ground 'real human override at the service encounter' in guest autonomy (2026: 10).

The contribution as l. 119 and l. 183 state it survives all of them: why the guest is owed the route, and which party must provide it.

In three other places the paper states the claim more widely than that, and none of the wider versions holds.

**(a) What the guest is owed is not new.** 'None of these frameworks derives what the guest is owed—or which firm owes it' (The host ¶5, l. 43). Sharma and Mattila, cited in the sentence before, already say what is owed, in the paper's own terms. From the customer's side, they write, 'responsibilities relate to safety, security, and delivering the promise of service quality and reliability' (2026: 911). Those are the paper's two core duties. Sharma and Mattila do not derive them from the host role, so l. 43 survives only on its last words. A referee from my field will open the cited article at p. 911 and conclude that the authors have not read their own citation. Said openly, the same sentence gives claim one a convergent source (F2).

**(b) The route and its stakes threshold are already in print.** In two places the wording reads as if they were the paper's own:

- *Suh et al.'s P3.* A concierge's benefit weakens 'as service-failure severity, emotional sensitivity or the need for discretionary human judgment increases, unless immediate human escalation is available' (§6.2, PDF p. 14; card). This is the l. 142 claim that serious stakes require a natural person, stated as a proposition. The panel finds l. 142 unargued (summary item 8), and Suh et al. are where the authors can borrow the argument or at least credit it.
- *Ariyogi et al. on the booking.* Their sentence reads: 'A concierge who receives an escalated complaint but cannot amend a booking may provide emotional reassurance without completing recovery' (2026: 86). It is l. 144's worker who 'can offer sympathy but lacks the power to change a booking'. v9 credits that point to Sterz et al. and Li and Sun.
- *Suh et al.'s Agency principle.* It asks for 'the ability to decline phygital features without service penalties' (§2.3, PDF p. 5), which is Q2's 'unfair penalty'.

**(c) The robot question has fallen out of the paper.** v8 set aside 'whether a machine can itself owe a guest anything' and cited Kropf et al. for it. v9 dropped both the sentence and the citation, which leaves Kropf et al. listed but uncited (summary item 14). In my field that question is the main one: Liu et al., Lee and Lu, Sharma and Mattila on robot rights, and Gao et al. all ask it in some form. The paper needs one sentence saying it does not answer it and does not need to. Kropf et al. give the reason in robot-ethics terms. That the programmers, the manufacturers or other humans are accountable for a care robot's misconduct 'seems correct, but certainly also unsatisfactory for the people concerned' (2026: 9). Those humans have an address that the affected person cannot reach, which is the paper's problem exactly.

**Two further gaps.** The journal's own empirical robot study, Gao et al. (2026), 'Hostessing by robots? Hotel customers' experiences of gendered robotic services' (*Hospitality & Society* 16:2, pp. 177–199, by Crossref), is not cited. It studies hotel guests in China, and the paper's anchor case is two automated hotels in China. The library holds its abstract only. Three neighbours are also known only by abstract: Parhi (2026), Koo and Kwon (2026) and Lee and Lu (2024). Until Parhi is read, 'yet they rarely explain which firm owes what' (abstract, l. 5) is the safe wording, and it must not harden. By the gap search's report, which is unverified, Parhi spreads AI accountability across franchisor, franchisee and vendor (card).

**Verdict on novelty.** The claim holds for the ground, the assignment and the window. It does not hold for the content of the duties, nor for the route, nor for the stakes threshold, all of which v9's wording at l. 43, l. 142 and l. 144 lets a reader take as new. The cost is about 60 words of credit (F2).

### 1.2 The case base

I checked the six cases the brief names, plus four the paper leans on from my field. None is invented. Nearly every one is pushed a notch past its source, and always in the same direction, towards a guest who cannot reach anyone:

- *Garcia et al.:* authority 'shifts' (l. 15), managers log in 'each day', the hotels are 'European'.
- *Xu and Greiffenhagen:* errors cured 'most' of the time, guests 'rejected', a worker 'tracked down'.
- *Bosma:* smart locks replace 'any' handover.
- *Le Petit Chef:* staff who 'can adjust the experience on the spot'.

A referee who checks two cases and finds both pushed will discount the rest. The cases are F1, F3, F4, F6, F7 and F9 below. *Groh* is sound in substance, and on two of its points I disagree with the panel (F9).

The two cases that open the paper also share a structural weakness. The opening contrast sets an algorithm the guest never meets beside a projection the guest cannot touch: Batat records that diners 'do not interact with the projected character' (2021: 7). Neither case is a touchpoint at which a guest can complain. The paper defines a touchpoint as 'Any moment where the guest interacts with that arrangement' (Phygital hospitality ¶2, l. 27). By that definition the pricing screen listed at l. 138 is not one. The further duty also cannot reach a rate set before arrival (l. 113; also R2). So the case that introduces 'a central tension' (l. 15) is one the paper's audit cannot examine, and the audit never returns to it. R2 and R3 propose opening on Xu and Greiffenhagen, and I agree, for this definitional reason as well as theirs (F3).

### 1.3 Table 1

Table 1 is the right device and answers R2's fifth first-round point, but its rows are not the paper's cases. It cites one study (Xu and Greiffenhagen) and two statutes, and three cells read 'Authors' analysis' (also R1, R2, R3). Each of the paper's other cases maps onto a row that is missing:

- Garcia et al. is a rate set before arrival, the row where in-stay correction matters little and that R2 asked for.
- Schmidt et al. is a low-stakes robot exchange, which is the paper's own answer on boundaries but sits in prose after the table (l. 134).
- Bosma's smart locks give a digital-key failure in a rental with no one on site. The paper names that failure twice (ll. 17, 138), and it is the case that tests row 3's on-site requirement (also R1).

Row 2 also mixes two failures that behave differently in the source. The details are in F5.

### 1.4 Is the argument phygital?

Davit Sarkisyan will press this question, so I give my lens only. The argument is most phygital where a digital output becomes a physical fact: a door that opens, a body that the kiosk accepts. Xu and Greiffenhagen's kiosk tells guests to close their eyes, shake their heads and open their mouths. It 'will simply repeat the instruction again and again, so it is the response-giver who has to modify their behaviour' (2024: 5030). That is the most phygital moment in the paper's evidence. Yet l. 162, 'When a guest's body ... fall outside a software model's assumptions', rests on no case. Cite p. 5030 there (Part 3).

## Part 2. The slop audit

v9 does not read as machine-made in the ways v8 did. The 'not X; it is Y' pairs have thinned, scholars act in their own sentences, and the case paragraphs carry numbers and named people. The Xu paragraph (At the touchpoint ¶2, l. 140) reads like a fieldworker's summary. What remains falls into five groups.

**1. Intensifiers on paraphrase that outruns its source.** Five of these six sit on a sentence with a source problem:

- 'only by actively tracking one down' (l. 140): the source says guests 'looked for' a worker who sat by the door (F1).
- 'a guest can easily reach a staff member who lacks discretion' (l. 97): Garcia's managers had the discretion (also R1, R2, R3).
- 'pinpoints exactly' (l. 156; also R3): the Xu verdict is not shown by the source (F1).
- 'routinely prioritize live repair' (l. 121): this rests on three instruments (also R2, R3).
- 'leaving others firmly with the host' (l. 51): l. 71 has liability narrowing (summary item 3).
- 'Lord Pearson stated plainly' (l. 49) is outside my lens.

The practical rule for this draft is to delete the adverb and then re-check the sentence against its source.

**2. Story verbs on things that are not agents.** In l. 11 the vendor is 'Knowing' and 'designed' (also R1, R2, R3). In l. 142 'the kiosk rejected' guests. These are a novelist's verbs laid on an equilibrium estimate and a database lookup.

**3. 'Genuine' as inflation.** The word appears three times outside Lee and Lu's own term (l. 31): 'genuine authority' (l. 144), 'genuinely able' (Table 2, l. 170) and 'genuinely human-centered' (l. 179; also Grok). Cut each one; nothing is lost.

**4. Aphoristic closers in the last two sections.** Three stand out: 'Friction is valuable only because …' (l. 134; also R1, R3), 'A reporting route can exist on paper and still fail at a different link in every case' (l. 156; R1 notes the universal) and 'an interface the guest cannot interrupt' (l. 160). Keep the third, which carries the paper's design claim. Turn the other two into hooks or cut them.

**5. Contrast pairs.** There are 15 uses of 'rather than' and 16 of 'Yet/yet'. They do not read as an engine. Most mark a contrast a reader would otherwise miss, and I would not count them.

Paragraph length, sentence variety and the verdict closers that end each party subsection all read as written by people.

## 1. Who I am

I work on service robots and self-service technology in hotels: acceptance, perceived hospitableness, failure and recovery when a robot errs, and the robot–customer–employee triad. I review for the main hospitality management journals and read this one. I came to the paper believing that my field has asked three questions about machines that host. Can it do the task? Do guests accept it? Does it feel hospitable? After a failure my field asks a fourth: whom do guests blame? I expected a conceptual paper to restate the fourth question in the language of duty. I also doubted that a paper whose evidence is two hotel studies and some case law could tell my field anything about robots.

It does ask a different question. It asks what the host owes and which firm owes it, and it asks well. But it under-credits the three papers nearest to it in my field, and it describes its own cases more strongly than their authors do.

## 2. The read: findings ranked by damage

### F1. The anchor case, Xu and Greiffenhagen, is misdescribed at four points [FIX]

*Partly also R1, R2, R3, whose finding concerns the thirteen; the rest is new.*

**The claims.** Xu and Greiffenhagen carry the further duty, the audit and the conclusion's one illustration (ll. 117, 140, 142, 156, 177). v9 states four things about the case:

- 'most facial-recognition errors cleared up when guests stepped back and tried again' (At the touchpoint ¶3, l. 142);
- 'When retrying fails, however, repair requires the authority to override' (same);
- guests 'reached a staff member only by actively tracking one down' (¶2, l. 140);
- the arrangement 'passes the third question while failing the second' (¶6, l. 156).

**What fails.**

1. *Every recognition error cleared, not 'most'.* All 610 guests who reached the face scan were recognized in the end. Figure 11 (p. 5027) runs to 100 per cent of n = 610, and note 4 (p. 5035) explains that the 13 excluded cases never reached recognition: the process 'either did not start or stopped working very early on'. 'Most' understates the fact. 'When retrying fails' has no instance in the data, so the sentence builds the override case on a failure the study never observed.
2. *The cited page shows a human at work.* p. 5025 is cited for the claim that the route 'doesn't always have to end with a human'. It is the page where 'the service staff would suggest that customers take coverings off their faces and adjust their stances (e.g. "take off the glasses and step back a bit")'. The words 'stepped back' in v9 are the staff's advice, so the evidence for a process-only route includes a person.
3. *'Actively tracking one down' overstates the effort.* Guests 'looked for a service worker who manually checked them in' (p. 5035), and that worker sat 'next to the door' (p. 5022). v8 said 'seeking the worker out'. v9 hardens the verb, and Grok's rewrite hardens it again ('had to track a worker down').
4. *The Q2 verdict sits on the wrong evidence.* All 13 guests whom the kiosk could not process reached the worker and were checked in (p. 5035). On the source's evidence, the touchpoint worked for every guest whose case needed a person. I agree with R1 that the 'informed' limb of Q2 is not shown. The case does fail Q2, but on other evidence. A guest who approached the worker first was 'redirected towards the self-registration kiosk and asked to "do it yourself"' (p. 5022). Refusing the machine 'was only possible when there was a technical problem (after all, the hotel was advertised as a "robotic hotel")' (p. 5034). That is a guest who cannot interrupt the automated flow, which is the paper's interruptibility claim with evidence behind it. v9 never uses p. 5034. Grok's view that this application 'correctly show[s]' a route failing is therefore not right as written.

One further nuance. For the eight guests whose booking the kiosk could not find, there was no output to 'override'. The worker supplied what the system lacked. That is exception handling, a workaround rather than a reversal, and Q3's 'depart from the system's output' fits it loosely.

**Cost.** About 15 words net, using the Part 3 rewrites at ll. 140, 142 and 156. The paper also gains its best evidence for interruptibility.

### F2. The novelty claim is wider in three sentences than the field allows [FIX, plus CARRY FORWARD for the abstract-only neighbours]

### F3. The opening case sits outside the paper's own touchpoint and window, and its details drift [CONSIDER restructure; FIX the details]

*Also R1, R2 and R3 on authority and intent; also R2 on the window. The definitional point, the dropped hedge and the disagreement are new.*

**The claims.** The pricing case introduces 'a central tension' (Introduction ¶3, l. 15) and returns at The vendor ¶1 (l. 97) and At the touchpoint ¶1 (l. 138).

**What fails.**

1. *Definition.* A touchpoint is 'Any moment where the guest interacts with that arrangement' (l. 27). The guest never meets the pricing screen, as l. 15 itself says, yet l. 138 lists it as a touchpoint.
