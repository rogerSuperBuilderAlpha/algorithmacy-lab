## Part 1. Rigour and argument, from the phygital lens

### 1.1 What the frameworks say, and what the paper says they say

I checked every sentence in which the paper describes phygital research against the saved texts. Four of them hold and four do not.

**What holds.**
- The definition (Phygital hospitality ¶1, l. 25) is verbatim at Batat (2024: 1229), up to "media content elements". The source continues ", platforms, technologies, and extended realities, among others", so the cut is fair.
- "A coherent continuum in the delivery process of consumer value" (l. 25) is verbatim at p. 1229.
- "Not as a functional tool but as an 'experiential agent'" (Phygital hospitality ¶2, l. 27) is verbatim at Batat (2026: 17).
- Germann Molz's "fluid and interchangeable" and "guests without hosts" (Phygital hospitality ¶3, l. 29) are verbatim at 2018: 232, with the sentence opening on p. 231. This settles R2's VERIFY item 3: the phrase is in the 2018 article, where she credits her 2014a, as well as in the 2026 title.

**What does not hold.**

*(a) "Fluidity" in quotation marks* (Phygital hospitality ¶4, l. 31: 'phygital frameworks describe this movement as "fluidity"'). Neither cited source uses the noun. Batat (2024) writes "fluidifying" (pp. 1220, 1230, 1235). Batat and Hammedi (2023: 17) write "designing fluid and memorable phygital experiences". v8 had no quotation marks here, and the v8 verification record notes that neither source uses the noun (VERIFY_V8_A, O-046.1). The rewrite added them, so this is a new error. If the authors want the noun, Batat's PHIVE article has it ("creating fluidity between physical and digital realms", 2026: 12; "experiential fluidity across multiple touchpoints", 13). A second, smaller issue: what Batat makes fluid is the *customer's journey* between settings. The paper's "this movement" is the conversion of physical events into digital records and back. These overlap but are not the same.

*(b) "Two distinct ways of combining physical and digital hospitality (Batat 2024: 1230–1231)"* (Introduction ¶3, l. 15). Those pages name two processes in the customer's experience: "shifting from physical to digital ('digitalization') and from digital to physical ('physicalization')". The paper's contrast is a different one: whether the person who can adjust the experience stays within the guest's reach. That contrast is the authors', and a good one. Batat's pages do not draw it. Worse, the pricing case does not meet the definition the paper quotes ten lines later. Batat's phygital "adopts a consumer standpoint as a starting point" (p. 1229), and Garcia et al.'s guests never meet the pricing model; the study "tracked managerial behavior rather than guest complaints" (The vendor ¶1, l. 97). v8 said both cases "carry the guest between the physical and the digital", which the v8 verifier marked as the authors' reading (O-017). v9's wording drops even that marker. (The opening case is criticized on other grounds by R1, R2 and R3. Mine is that it is not phygital in the sense the paper quotes.)

*(c) "Friction-free speed as the primary virtue"* (At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160). None of the three cited works says this.
- **Batat (2024).** Speed appears once, as one functional need under one of six pillars: "customers' needs to avoid long waiting lines" (p. 1234). The framework's driving force is a continuum across intrinsic and extrinsic value, and it warns that it "should not consider a positive phygital experience to be the aim" (p. 1238).
- **Batat and Hammedi (2023).** They want smoothness ("Flawless interactivity and connectivity are thus necessary", p. 14). They also say "companies should never replace services delivered through humans by virtual agents, even if they are highly humanized" (p. 18). And they worry that "technology disintermediation has contributed to the escalation of the sense of objectification and separation" (p. 24). That is the paper's own problem in their words.
- **Mameli et al. (2026).** Their framework is experiential: "seamless integration of sensory, spatial, and technological elements" (p. 25). What they *observe* is that industry cases in "retail, hospitality (accommodation) and aviation" "lean towards prioritising functionality over hedonic attributes" (p. 18). That supports a claim about accommodation practice, not about design frameworks.
- **The paper's own second case.** Le Petit Chef's makers wanted "to make the waiting moments in restaurants more pleasant" (Batat 2021: 4), and one diner said "this experience made me eat slowly" (p. 8). Batat's own phygital exemplar fills a wait. It does not strip one away.

v8 said, accurately, "Smoothness has become a central goal" (v8 l. 169; VERIFY_V8_B O-243, SUPPORTED, noting that the list of queues and repetition was a co-author's gloss). v9 raised this to "speed" as "the primary virtue". That is a claim v8 carried at a supported strength and v9 overstates. (Extends R1 §4.2. I disagree with Grok, which calls the seamlessness critique "precise".)

*(d) PHIVE's ranking* (l. 160: "Batat herself ranks systems built purely for … below AI aligned with deeper human values", citing 2026: 13, 16). The matrix crosses two axes, integration depth and human-value alignment (Batat 2026: 7–8). Systems that are aligned with human values but poorly integrated sit in the Air quadrant, "FRACTURE / Broken Value", "Human-centric but insufficiently integrated" (Table 4, p. 16). Batat calls that quadrant "value destruction" (p. 8). So she ranks aligned-but-discontinuous AI *below* the seamless hospitality systems of the Water quadrant. Only Fire, high on both axes, ranks above Water. Fire is "elevated through the seamless convergence of technological precision, emotional resonance, and experiential meaning" (p. 13), and in it "AI reduces friction" (p. 15). In PHIVE, discontinuity is the harm and seamlessness belongs to the benchmark. v8 kept her words "good or fair" (p. 13), which showed that she values seamless systems positively. v9 drops them and adds "purely", which is not hers. (Also R1 §4.2. The Air-quadrant point is mine.)

### 1.2 Is "interruptible hospitality" a contribution, or seamful design in a hospitality register?

As a design property, it is seamful design with an escalation path. Interaction designers and phygital service researchers already ask for that. As the design face of the further duty, it is new. The paper says neither.

**The design neighbours, none of which the paper cites at l. 160:**
- **Seamful design.** Chalmers and Galani argue for "carefully revealing differences and limitations of systems, in ways that support social interaction" (2004, DIS '04, pp. 243–252; saved eprint). The library card for Ehsan et al. (2024) records that it was "Not admitted for want of words" and that "if a referee raises seamful design this is the citation to add first."
- **Intervenability.** Herrmann (2025: 148, preprint pagination) extends intervenability from "automated physical processes" to "workflows of AI-based decision-making for certain types of specific cases". That is the paper's "failure in the guest's case".
- **Contestability by design.** Alfrink et al. (2023) ask "who can contest, who is accountable" and require that human controllers have "the authority and capability to actually change previous decisions" (§§6.2.1, 6.1.4; card). That is close to audit questions 3–4.
- **Phygital service research itself.** Shabnam et al.'s TPSR says "algorithmic governance must remain contestable. Digitally enforced rules should incorporate escalation pathways and human oversight to prevent rigid exclusion" (2026: 14). On the same page it calls for "Training staff" and "feedback loops", which are close to Table 2's stages 3 and 4. Suh et al., whom the paper cites only for "a visible route", already make escalation depend on stakes. Their P3 says the concierge's benefit "will weaken as service-failure severity, emotional sensitivity or the need for discretionary human judgment increases, unless immediate human escalation is available" (2026: 14, §6.2). Suh et al. also write that "AI-enabled efficiency may reduce friction while eroding the warmth and discretionary care central to hospitality" (p. 5, §2.3).

**The term collides with two settled meanings.**
- In HCI, "interruptibility" is the human's availability to be interrupted by a system. Fogarty et al. (2005), "Predicting human interruptibility with sensors", *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, DOI 10.1145/1057237.1057243 (database record). The direction is the reverse of the paper's.
- In AI safety, "Safely interruptible agents" (Orseau and Armstrong 2016; saved text) are agents that an *operator* can stop with "the big red button".

An HCI-literate referee will meet the keyword "interruptibility" (Keywords, l. 7) through the first meaning. One sentence that places the paper's use against both settles this.

**What is new.** I word-searched the saved texts of TPSR, Suh et al. and Mameli et al. for "vendor", "outsourc", "supplier", "third-party" and "partner firm" and found no hits. The cards for PHIVE and HFPV record the same silence: one firm's leaders, and no allocation among firms. No phygital text I could read says to whom the route is owed, which of several firms owes it, or for how long. The further duty answers all three, and the conclusion's "What this article adds" sentence (Conclusion ¶4, l. 183) says so. Interruptibility becomes a contribution once the paper says it is seamful design with an addressee and a time limit.

**A logical gap in the name.** The further duty, as derived, is a route to *correction during the stay*. The paper widens it on purpose beyond the pre-effect window: Baum and Laux's runtime oversight acts "only before that decision takes effect", whereas "hospitality requires a broader window" (During the stay ¶5, l. 121). "Interruptible" names the narrower, pre-effect act: the definition opens "lets the guest pause the automated flow" (l. 160). The paper's own best case shows correction by bypass, not interruption. The kiosk had already failed, and a clerk checked the guests in by hand (l. 142). An automated flow can be uninterruptible and still correctable during the stay. So the abstract's "automated processes must therefore remain interruptible" (Abstract, l. 5) does not follow as worded. Either define interruptibility to cover stepping out of the flow after it has taken effect, or name the design consequence differently. Interruptibility also appears in no audit question, no table and not in the conclusion (also R1 §4.2).

### 1.3 Is the argument phygital at all?

Yes, in its allocation and its time window. No, in its instruments. The paper hides the first half.

Three load-bearing steps depend on the *physical* half of the phygital crossing:
- the duty of care is assigned to whoever controls the guest's space (ll. 75, 109, 183);
- the guest must be able to use the route "as a matter of right" because the guest "remains subject to the host's control of the premises" and exit is costly (During the stay ¶3, l. 117);
- the further duty lasts "for the duration of the stay, matching the period of the guest's subjection" (During the stay ¶5, l. 121).

The digital half supplies the problem: authority moves to a vendor's rule or a kiosk's output (The vendor ¶1, l. 97). In short, digital mediation moves the power to correct away from the guest, and physical control of the space fixes the duty in place. A purely digital service, such as a bank's chatbot, has the first feature without the second. So the allocation rule would not run unchanged there. This is the paper's answer to Reviewer 2's fourth first-round point, and the paper never states it. Instead the introduction claims the opposite breadth: "the framework applies to any phygital service" (Introduction ¶5, l. 19) (also R2 point 4, R3 §3.5).

The instruments are generic. None of the seven audit questions (ll. 148–154) mentions a physical effect, a space or a stay. Only question 5's "while the stay still allows a meaningful remedy" ties the audit to lodging, and Table 2 is the same. Both would run unchanged for a bank. The paper's prescription also stops exactly where its service turns purely digital. The vendor, which "governs the automated rule", is "most insulated from the guest" (The vendor ¶3, l. 101), and no route duty reaches it (also R3 §4.4). This dependence on the physical is an asset if stated and a gap if not. One phrase in question 3 would make the audit phygital (Part 3).

A smaller definitional slip points the same way. "Any moment where the guest interacts with that arrangement is a touchpoint" (Phygital hospitality ¶2, l. 27). Yet At the touchpoint ¶1 lists "a pricing screen at a manager's desk" as a touchpoint that "bridges digital computation and the physical room" (l. 138). The guest never touches that screen. Relatedly, Phygital hospitality ¶1 lists "pricing algorithms designed to make a stay faster and more tailored" (l. 25), with no source. Revenue management is not designed to make a stay faster. Batat's own framework also moves past "touchpoint": "The idea of connectors goes beyond the touchpoints logic often used in channel marketing and customer relationship management (CRM)" (2024: 1232). The authors need not adopt "connector", but a phygital referee will notice the choice.

### 1.4 Do the Batat citations earn their place?

Most do. Two do not, and two are misread. The misreadings, more than the count, are what make the citations look like courtesy.

The paper cites Batat 13 times across seven lines (13, 15, 25, 27, 31, 35, 160), from five works. Each one:
- **Batat 2021** (Introduction ¶2, l. 13) does real work as evidence. The last sentence, though, has dropped v8's "on our reading": "human staff maintains discretion, and the basic rules of table service remain intact". The study does not address discretion or rules (card; VERIFY_V8_A O-010). The study is also retrospective: online interviews with 20 diners, whom Batat says "we did not follow and observe … in the restaurants" (p. 11, card).
- **Batat 2024** earns its place for the definition (l. 25), but not for the contrast (l. 15), "fluidity" (l. 31) or "speed" (l. 160).
- **Batat and Hammedi 2023** appear twice, both times as bracket citations for positions they do not hold (ll. 31, 160). Their p. 24 passage on "separation and disconnectedness of service providers from their customers" would make them an ally.
- **Batat 2026 (PHIVE)** earns its place at l. 27 and is misread at l. 160.
- **Batat 2019** (The host ¶1, l. 35) is a book the authors have not read. The card is at abstract and chapter-list depth, and VERIFY_V8_A O-054a.2 is PARTLY. v9 also moves v8's "experience design" to "service design". The sentence does no work. It nods and then turns: "Yet, hospitality theory reminds us …". The card itself names the better point: "Empathy capital is a firm's asset and care is an obligation". Batat's PH-CX gives the citable version: "what Batat (2019a) calls employees' empathy capital, which should be an integral part of a company's human resource strategy" (2024: 1233).

**Where the citations sit.** Phygital research frames the argument at its two ends (ll. 13–31, l. 160) and is absent from l. 37 to l. 158, except for Suh at ll. 43 and 119. That bracketing reads as courtesy. The cure is to let one phygital idea work inside the argument. PH-CX already holds that the negative effects of the media, digital and physical connectors "can be fixed by positive human interactions. However, if the human connectors generate negative effects, this cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors" (Batat 2024: 1234). That is the paper's further duty stated as a design finding, without an addressee. Citing it at The property ¶3 (l. 73), where the Helsinki clerks lack authority, or at At the touchpoint ¶4 (l. 144) would turn a foil into a foundation.

**"Batat herself"** (l. 160). This is the one phrase in the body that reads as addressed to a known person. In a double-anonymized special issue that Batat leads (CfP, "Special Issue Editors"), "Batat (2026: 13, 16)" is safer.

**The other editors.** The paper cites neither of the issue's other two editors, Mosca and De Vos, and it need not. If the authors want a phygital source for widening "phygital" from the consumer's standpoint to the multi-firm arrangement (l. 27), Mosca et al. (2026) criticize PH-CX's "narrow customer focus" (p. 431, card) and ask for "a seamless continuum in which physical exhibits, digital content, and human mediation are intertwined" (p. 440). Cite it only if the sentence uses it.

**Phygital service research (PSR).** The call frames the issue through "phygital service research (PSR) (Batat, 2024b)", "placing all stakeholders, including guests, hosts, employees, and communities, at the center" (call for papers, saved text). The paper never engages PSR. The library holds Batat's PSR article only at abstract depth (card `batat2026psr`), and VERIFY_V8_A records a later *JSM* PSR editorial (online 27 Oct 2026) that nobody has read. I would read both before deciding. Adding a citation unread is worse than leaving it out.

## Part 2. The slop audit

These read as machine-made or padded:
- **The "speed" sentence** (At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160): "stripping away queues, repetition, and hesitation so guests glide through a stay". A decorative triplet plus an image, presented as the frameworks' content. The v8 verifier traced its ancestor to a co-author's gloss (O-243).
- **"Negotiation requires a moment in which negotiation can take place"** (l. 160) is a tautology (also R1).
- **"Interruptibility is the practical design consequence of the host's further duty"** (l. 160) closes a paragraph by asserting what the paragraph has not shown.
- **"pricing algorithms designed to make a stay faster and more tailored"** (Phygital hospitality ¶1, l. 25). A three-item list built for rhythm, whose third item is wrong.
- **"calling it a relay exposes an operational question that fluidity obscures"** (Phygital hospitality ¶4, l. 31). The paper grades its own move (also R3).
- **"Friction is valuable only because it creates a pause"** (During the stay ¶6, l. 134). An aphoristic closer (also R3) that has lost v8's "on this reading". "Only" is also refutable. Padigar et al. class authentication, payment security and privacy choices as "constructive friction" (2025, *Psychology & Marketing* 42(1), pp. 26, 28, 32; card), and Le Petit Chef's pause serves sociability (also R1).
- **"Making automated hospitality genuinely human-centered requires"** (Conclusion ¶2, l. 179). "Genuinely" (also Grok). "Human-centricity" is the call's own vocabulary, defined here only by what it is not.

These do not read as machine-made, and should stay:
- At the touchpoint ¶2 (l. 140): "do it yourself", and the sentence that ends "it pushed that person behind the machine". Concrete, and that sentence hands the next one its subject.
- At the touchpoint ¶8 (l. 160): the sentence ending "easily becomes an interface the guest cannot interrupt". This is the paper's best phygital sentence.
- Introduction ¶2 (l. 13), the Le Petit Chef paragraph up to its last sentence: specific, sourced and plain.

## 1. Who I am

I work on phygital customer experience: the PH-CX framework, extended reality in service settings, and the newer transformative strand (TPSR). I review for service and marketing journals and read hospitality from that side. I came to this paper expecting what outside critiques of "seamlessness" usually offer, which is a strawman of our frameworks followed by a call for "human touch" that seamful design and escalation-path research settled years ago. I half found it. The paper's description of our frameworks is wrong in four places. The further duty, though, is something none of our frameworks supplies: an addressee, a responsible firm and a time window for the route we all say should exist.

## 2. The read: findings ranked by damage

**1. The seamlessness paragraph misdescribes the frameworks it argues with.** [FIX] — At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160: "Design frameworks frequently treat friction-free speed as the primary virtue".
- **Claimed.** Batat and Hammedi (2023), Batat (2024) and Mameli et al. (2026) treat speed as primary, and Batat (2026) ranks seamless systems below value-aligned AI.
- **What fails.** All three frameworks treat continuity, not speed, as the goal. Batat and Hammedi refuse to replace human service (p. 18) and worry about "separation and disconnectedness" (p. 24). Mameli et al. report efficiency as a tendency of accommodation *applications* (p. 18). PHIVE puts value-aligned but poorly integrated AI in "value destruction" (pp. 8, 16) and keeps "the seamless convergence" and "AI reduces friction" in its benchmark (pp. 13, 15).
- **Why it matters.** v8's claim was supported; v9's is not. The lead special-issue editor wrote three of the five works the paragraph cites and will check them first. (Also R1 §4.2; the Air-quadrant point and the Mameli and Le Petit Chef evidence are new. Contra Grok.)
- **Cost.** Rewrite three sentences, net about +60 words (Best spend).

**2. Interruptibility is unpositioned and, as named, does not follow from the duty.** [CONSIDER] — At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160 and Abstract, l. 5.
- **Claimed.** Interruptibility is "the practical design consequence" of the further duty, and processes "must therefore remain interruptible".
- **What fails.** As a design property it is seamful design (Chalmers and Galani 2004), intervenability (Herrmann 2025) and TPSR's "escalation pathways and human oversight" (Shabnam et al. 2026: 14). None is cited at l. 160. The term also carries settled, different meanings in HCI (Fogarty et al. 2005) and AI safety (Orseau and Armstrong 2016). And the duty is a correction route that the paper deliberately extends past the pre-effect window (l. 121), whereas "interruptible" names a pause before effect. The paper's own kiosk case is correction by bypass.
- **Cost.** Define the term (about +20 words) and position it (about +35 words). Interruptibility in an audit question costs nothing if folded into question 3 (also R1).

**3. The nearest phygital-service neighbours are missing where the paper claims its gap.** [FIX] — The host ¶5, l. 43; During the stay ¶4, l. 119; At the touchpoint ¶3, l. 142; The host ¶4, l. 41.
- **Claimed.** Scholars prescribe safeguards without saying which firm owes them, and contestability is "a technical mechanism".
- **What fails.** The gap claim survives: I found no allocation among firms in TPSR, Suh et al. or Mameli et al. But TPSR already prescribes escalation, human oversight, staff training and feedback loops (2026: 14). Suh et al. already make human escalation depend on severity (P3, p. 14) and already warn that friction reduction erodes "discretionary care" (p. 5).
