All three referees found two of these rows: the ground of the further duty (finding 1) and the strength of the delegation claim (finding 3). The binding row is finding 2. I add three points the panel has not made.

**(a) The abstract makes three claims the body does not.**

- *'guests often cannot reach anyone with the authority to fix the problem'* (Abstract, l. 5). This is a claim about how often something happens, and the body never measures it. The body's own evidence comes from 'two small hotel studies' (During the stay ¶3, l. 117). In the strongest of them, the guest 'reached a staff member only by actively tracking one down' (At the touchpoint ¶2, l. 140): the guest did reach someone. The first study the paper proposes would 'measure how often guests report failures during a stay, whom they turn to' (Conclusion ¶5, l. 185). The abstract thus states as a finding what the conclusion says has yet to be measured.
- *'someone who can correct a failure on the spot'* (l. 5). The body asks for less. It asks for a route owed 'for as long as correction can still help' (l. 121), for someone 'able to fix the issue that day' (Table 1, l. 130), and for reports forwarded 'to whichever partner governs the failing component' (l. 144). For a small host, it asks for a phone number 'the owner actually answers' (l. 162). None of these is on the spot. A referee who reads the abstract and then Table 1 will find the duty smaller than promised.
- *'outlines five steps for host accountability'* (l. 5). The body separates correction during the stay from accountability after it: once the stay ends, 'the guest's claims shift toward … formal accountability' (l. 121). Table 2 runs 'from touchpoint audit to post-stay accountability' (l. 164), and only stage 5 is accountability. The abstract's label merges the two things the paper takes care to distinguish.

**(b) The front matter now contradicts the abstract.** The v8 Statement says 'what stays with the host depends on the duty' and 'the guest's claim justifies a further duty'. Highlights 3 and 4 repeat both. Both statements remain true of the v9 body (l. 71, l. 115). Neither is true of the v9 abstract, which says 'it keeps them' and grounds the duty in standing. Under double-anonymized review the Statement goes to the referees with the manuscript. R2, whose first-round point 3 was the circularity of standing, will then find 'claim' in the Statement and 'standing' in the abstract. That is his circle, reopened in the paper's own front matter.

**(c) v9 dropped the sentence that closed R2's circle.** v8 said in so many words that standing 'does not ground the duty but names what the guest has' (v8, The host ¶4, l. 49). v9 keeps the definition at l. 41 but drops that disclaimer. It then makes standing the ground in the abstract, introduction and conclusion. The brief asks reviewers to watch for a hedge that v8 carried and v9 dropped, and this one carried the most weight.

**Fix.** Choose one ground and one delegation claim, and state them identically in five places: abstract, introduction, conclusion, Statement and Highlights. The body already states the claim-based ground (l. 115, l. 119) and the duty-by-duty delegation (l. 71), so the shortest repair brings the front of the paper to the body. R2's proposal does the rest: let standing name 'the position of holding the claim-right'. Part 3 gives an abstract revised along these lines, at 199 words.

### 1.2 Fit to the call

**On two of the call's lines, power and the politics of space, the paper fits the issue better than most submissions will. On the call's socio-cultural line it is thin. And its central design concept argues against the call's own words without saying so.**

I checked the paper against the call's text (`sources/cfp2025apa/plain.txt`), not the digest.

- **Strong fit.** The call's third criterion asks how phygital ecosystems reconfigure 'the politics of space'. In this paper, control of the guest's space is what binds the duty of care, and that is the politics of space made precise. The call's topic 'Power structures and guest-staff relationships in phygitally mediated hospitality' is answered by:
  - the split of knowledge, discretion and authority (The vendor ¶1, l. 97);
  - the Helsinki clerks (The property ¶3, l. 73);
  - written authority for staff (At the touchpoint ¶4, l. 144).

  The call asks for 'human-centric strategies', and Conclusion ¶2 (l. 179) answers in those terms.
- **A fit the paper does not claim.** The call's fourth criterion asks how guest 'feedback shape[s] phygital hospitality design'. Audit question 6 (l. 153) and stage 4 ('Learn and route', l. 171) answer exactly that, since they turn guest reports into design correction across stays. One clause in At the touchpoint ¶4 (l. 144), where the paper cites the UN's 'source of continuous learning', would claim the fit.
- **Thin.** The call asks for 'a clear focus on phygital hospitality as a social and cultural phenomenon' and for guest 'agency, satisfaction, and well-being'. 'Well-being' does not appear in v9. 'Agency' appears once, and there it means apparent agency in franchise law (The brand ¶1, l. 79). Not every paper must answer every criterion. This one is a normative theory paper, and the roadmap I propose in 'Best spend' lets it say so.

**The collision over seamlessness.** The call says that 'hospitality settings must support seamless transitions between physical and digital touchpoints, ensuring continuity, trust, and emotional engagement' (call, Aim and Scope ¶3). The paper 'challenges a central ideal in phygital hospitality scholarship' (At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160). The call also 'invites critical engagement with the phygital turn' (¶4), so the challenge is welcome provided it targets what the call actually claims. As written, it targets a caricature (point 2 of The read).

It conceded that continuity and trust are goals 'which the phygital literature rightly seeks' (v8, At the touchpoint, l. 169). v9 cut the concession: 'Preserving trust across a digital crossing requires ensuring that whoever could answer…' (l. 160). That claim is stronger, fairer and harder to dismiss.
