# Reviewer 2, second round: "Who hosts the guest?" (revised manuscript)

*Locations* give the section or subsection heading as printed, then the paragraph counted within it (tables and lists not counted), then the line in DRAFT_v9.md: "The host ¶4, l. 41". No manuscript quotation exceeds 15 words. I checked sources only against the quotations and locators of five library cards: Garcia et al. 2026, Herstein 2023, Kiesewetter 2015, Jeske 2021 and Brandt 2017.

## 1. Summary

The paper now claims (Introduction ¶5, l. 19) that the host role defines two core duties, safety and careful performance; that holding out, reliance and control of the space bind them to a firm, with control keeping the duty of care with the host despite outsourcing and allowing shared duties; and that during the stay a "further duty" requires a timely route to someone able to correct a failure, whose design consequence is interruptibility. A five-party map assigns the duties, "During the stay" derives and bounds the further duty (Table 1), and an audit and five stages (Table 2) operationalise it. The authors concede that the law does not require the further duty outside a few regimes.

## 2. Recommendation

**Major revision.** The revision supplies most of the scaffolding I asked for. But the paper grounds its own contribution, the further duty, in three different ways in different sections, and one of them brings back the circularity I raised in round one. It also states what activates the duty, and what can be delegated, differently each time.

## 3. My first-round points

**Point 1. Source, holder, persistence, non-transferability: partly answered.** The source is now explicit (The host's duty in law ¶2, l. 49), and the five-party section answers who holds the duty. That section is the most improved part of the paper. On non-transferability the body now honestly concedes most of the ground: "liability narrows" when contractors are hired, and "no English judgment we examined imposes one on a hotel toward an adult guest" (The property ¶2, l. 71). The abstract still says the firm "keeps them even when contractors or algorithms carry out the work" (l. 5), extending to both duties what the body claims, qualified, for the duty of care alone. Nor does the paper say *what* persists: under s. 2(4)(b) of the 1957 Act, as reported, competent selection discharges the occupier (l. 71), which is not a residual duty to see the work done (concern 3). Figure 1 is cited (l. 138) but absent from what I received.

**Point 2. A theory of role obligation: partly answered.** I asked four things.

- *What activates the obligation:* the paper states this inconsistently (concern 2).
- *Who initially bears it:* answered. Holding out "establishes who assumed the role and when" (l. 49).
- *What can and cannot be delegated:* **not answered**. The paper names the task, "making it necessary to distinguish which duties travel with the work and which stay behind" (l. 51). It then says the answer "turns on the specific duty at issue" (l. 71), and no principle follows. Can the host delegate the duty to receive? To protect? The further duty itself, say to a vendor's call centre?
- *How responsibility is shared:* answered, through *Wheat*, where each occupier's duty depends on "his degree of control" (l. 73).

The theory itself remains a premise: "an institutional role defines the content of a duty, the moment it takes effect" (Approach ¶3, l. 61). The paper reads that content off legal doctrine while disclaiming law as "the moral foundation of hospitality" (l. 53), so the moral source is never stated. Nor does it engage the role-obligation literature. Hardimon, as Jeske quotes him, gives a role obligation content "fixed by the function of the role" and force that "flows from the role" (§4, para. 16; card). The paper keeps the first clause and swaps the second for three external conditions; it should say so and defend it. My worry about organisational delegation also stands: apart from *Al-Najar*, the authorities on reliance and control concern a nightclub (*Everett*, *Naylor*), a leisure centre and a care home, and the pub in *Wheat* let rooms as a sideline. They establish an occupier's duties, not a host's.

**Point 3. Circularity; having standing versus exercising it: partly answered. The circle has moved, not gone.** The theory section breaks the definitional loop. The obligation now rests on role and conditions; "standing names the position the guest occupies when that obligation is recognized" (The host ¶4, l. 41), and accountability now belongs after the stay (During the stay ¶5, l. 121). But a standing that sits downstream of the obligation cannot ground anything. Three places nonetheless make standing the ground of the further duty:

- the abstract: "the guest's standing also requires a timely route" (l. 5);
- the introduction: "the guest's standing during a stay grounds a further duty" (l. 19);
- the conclusion (Conclusion ¶1, l. 177).

Standing is a complaint addressed to "a matching host who is bound to receive and answer it" (l. 41); the further duty is to keep a route by which that complaint reaches someone who can answer it. Together: because the host must answer, it must keep a means of answering. That is my round-one circle in a new place. The section that derives the duty (During the stay ¶¶2–4, ll. 115–119) never mentions standing.

Having versus exercising is drawn only implicitly: the kiosk case "passes the third question while failing the second" (l. 156), and l. 134 speaks of a guest who "can exercise standing". And "organizational rules confer standing" (l. 144) uses standing for a worker's authority, a third sense. Define the term once and hold to it.

**Point 4. Why hospitality: not answered.** Banking, healthcare and e-commerce never appear. The introduction concedes the opposite: the framework "applies to any phygital service where several firms stand behind a single customer touchpoint" (l. 19). The material for an answer is there: the guest's bodily presence in controlled premises for a bounded period, with costly exit (ll. 113, 121). But the paper never claims these features as distinctive, and inpatient and residential care share them. *YL* (l. 107) is itself a care-home case. If the further duty follows from a general means–end principle, any outsourcing firm owes it. Jeske's test, whether an obligation is "derivative from an agent-neutral reason or a natural duty" (§6.4; card), needs an answer. A narrower claim would do: the mechanism is general, and hospitality is where it bites hardest, for stated reasons, with a sentence each on a bank and a hospital.

**Point 5. Boundary conditions: partly answered.** Table 1 is responsive, but:

- no row shows a decision where correction matters less; only the lobby-robot sentence (l. 134) addresses that half of my question;
- the start of the stay is unclear: refusal at arrival gets "None from the further duty", yet a check-in failure, also at the door, gets a route; when does "the guest's subjection" (l. 121) begin?
- on formats there is only "Focusing primarily on lodging" (l. 19); does the duty reach the authors' own Le Petit Chef diner, or a rental where a co-host takes instructions from the app (l. 91)?
- the opening pricing case falls outside the further duty, because the rate is set before arrival (l. 113).

## 4. New concerns, ranked

**1. Three grounds for the further duty, which yield different duties.**

- *(a) Standing:* the abstract, introduction and conclusion.
- *(b) The guest's claim-right plus means–end transmission:* "this underlying claim generates a further duty during the stay" (l. 115); the conclusion calls it a derivation "directly from the host's underlying duty" (l. 183).
- *(c) Subjection and exit costs:* "Because the guest remains subject to the host's control of the premises" (l. 117).

Ground (b) yields at most a duty to keep *some* timely means of discovery: the paper concedes "no single technical design is required" (l. 115) and that guest reports are the main source of discovery, "if not the only one" (l. 117). It cannot yield the guest-usable route that audit question 2 and Table 2, stage 2 require; that route rests on the one sentence in (c). The transmission step also outruns its source. Kiesewetter's principle concerns necessary means and the deliberative, all-things-considered ought, which he separates from moral obligation (pp. 922, 923, 932; card). The manuscript recasts it as "an obligation to achieve an end extends to the means" (l. 115) and founds a duty owed to a person on it. Herstein reports that a claim-right can favour new duties as facts change (§8.4, para. 2; card), but in a part of the entry on features "unique or particularly salient to legal rights", and he reports the reply that "dynamic rights" are "only inchoate rights" (Kramer, as reported), whereas the further duty is avowedly non-legal (l. 59). Last, a host without a channel "wrongs its guests even if nothing goes wrong" (l. 119) is a risk claim, yet the same paragraph sets Hindriks aside because his duty "rests partly on general risk to others". Pick one ground and state it identically everywhere; if standing stays, let it name the position of holding the claim-right.

**2. The paper states the activating conditions inconsistently.**

- The abstract makes them conjunctive: "invites travelers in, earns their trust, and controls the physical space" (l. 5).
- The legal section says "None of these three elements suffices on its own" (l. 49). But the elements it then lists are undertaking, reliance and vulnerability. Its sufficient condition is two of them: "When control over physical space combines with guest reliance" (l. 49).
- The brand test is disjunctive: "governs daily operations or holds itself out" (l. 83).
- For a platform, holding out alone suffices (l. 93).
- Blackstone's trigger is anyone who "hangs out a sign" (l. 47), yet the brand section says liability follows "not the sign above the entrance" (l. 81).
- The state's duty "remains hers" without physical control (l. 107). The next paragraph claims "one rule holds consistently" (l. 109).

A table setting each condition against each party and each duty would fix this. The Jeske locator (§§3–4) also misses the reliance clause, which the card places at §7, paras 2–3. And the card records that the entry never treats vulnerability as a ground, so the vulnerability clause is the authors' own reading.

**3. The paper states the delegation account differently in the abstract, introduction and body.**

- Abstract: "keeps them", meaning both duties.
- Introduction: "keeps the duty of care with the host" (l. 19), meaning one duty, shareable.
- Body: liability "narrows" (l. 71).
- Vendor section: "each firm answers only for its own conduct" (l. 99). The package-travel organiser, liable whoever performs (l. 51), contradicts this.

Brandt, cited only for a negative point (l. 109), supplies the needed distinction. Transfer leaves the first holder no "trace". Delegation leaves an obligation "to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled" (accepted ms p. 3; card). Brandt also rejects the view that careful selection discharges that obligation (n. 34; card). That conflicts with s. 2(4)(b) as reported; say which account governs. The paper should also say which "core duties" statute assigns to "the proprietor" alone (l. 51) (VERIFY). And it names two core duties (ll. 5, 19, 177) but lists "three duties" (l. 75), with the duty to receive in Table 1, row 1. Is receiving core or not?

**4. The paper does not settle who owes the further duty.** The conclusion assigns it to "whichever organization controls the physical space" (l. 183). But a platform that presents itself as the provider "incurs a host's obligations" (l. 93), and if the further duty derives from those obligations, such a platform owes it too.

**5. Person or process; host's forum or an independent one.** The route "doesn't always have to end with a human" (l. 142). Yet "whoever could answer the guest before automation can still be reached after it" (l. 160). And "a natural person needs to sit at the end of the line" (l. 142), a claim made without argument. Table 2 makes stage 5 something the host does. The conclusion demands an independent forum, because a business "cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine" the outcome (l. 185). Line 162 says a small innkeeper meets all five stages by "posting a phone number that the owner actually answers". Each pair of statements, on the route and on the forum, conflicts.

**6. The opening case overreaches.** The introduction says "pricing authority shifts to a software model" (l. 15), two paragraphs after "Hotel managers kept the final say over rates" (l. 11). The card confirms that managers "retain the final decision right" (working paper p. 5). The vendor section says a guest reaches "a staff member who lacks discretion to override" (l. 97). But in Garcia et al. that staff member had the discretion, and the study tracked managers, not guests. Opening on Xu and Greiffenhagen, the case the argument turns on, would serve better.

**7. Overclaims about existing law and philosophy.** Lines 59, 109 and 158 give three different lists of regimes that already require a route. "Routinely prioritize live repair" (l. 121) rests on three instruments, one of which (Directive 2024/2831) protects platform workers. "Neither ... moral philosophy obliges a delegator" (l. 109) rests on one article about gamete providers, and the next section derives such an obligation from moral philosophy.
