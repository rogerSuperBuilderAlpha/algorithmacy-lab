# Reviewer 3, second round: revised manuscript on host duties in phygital hospitality

Locations give the section or subsection name, the paragraph number within it, and the line number in DRAFT_v9.md, for example (The host ¶4, l. 41). No manuscript quotation exceeds 15 words. 'Card' marks a claim I checked against a library source card.

## 1. Summary

The authors ask what a commercial host owes a guest, why, and who owes it when hosting is split across firms (Introduction ¶5, l. 19). They answer in three claims:

1. The role of host carries two core duties, safety and careful performance.
2. Holding out, reliance and spatial control bind those duties to a firm.
3. During a stay, the guest's standing grounds a further duty to keep a timely route to someone who can correct a failure.

Legal doctrine supports the first two claims, and a tour of five parties allocates the duties among them. Two studies of automated hotels support the third claim, which yields a seven-question audit and a five-stage table. This version is much easier to read than the first, and I can now judge the argument.

## 2. Recommendation

Minor revision. The question is clear and the argument followable, but the core claims are stated at different strengths in different places (concerns 4.1–4.4), and I would like to see the corrected version.

## 3. My first-round points

**3.1 Style and rhetoric.** *Largely answered.* None of the phrases I named survives. Some sentences remain very long: the one at l. 107 runs to 67 words, and those at l. 121, 142 and 183 to about 60. A few aphoristic closers remain: 'Friction is valuable only because it creates a pause' (l. 134), 'an interface the guest cannot interrupt' (l. 160) and 'someone still owes the guest a welcome' (l. 187). The opening is now a published study (but see 4.5).

**3.2 The general 'we'.** *Answered.* The first person now marks the authors' own moves. Three residues remain: 'forces us back' (l. 31), 'reminds us' (l. 35) and 'gives us a clear test' (l. 53).

**3.3 Bitner et al. (2000).** *Answered.* The source no longer appears in the text or the references.

**3.4 Technology treated reductively.** *Largely answered.* Phygital hospitality ¶2 (l. 27) now examines a 'broader socio-technical setup'. After the introduction, however, technology appears only as something that fails, and the framework never says what it asks of technology that adds delight, such as Le Petit Chef.

**3.5 Hosting equated with reception.** *Partly answered.* The five-party tour and Approach ¶3 (l. 61) widen the frame, but the worked examples still turn on check-in. The claim that the framework 'applies to any phygital service' (l. 19) is asserted, not shown. It also conflicts with the further duty's grounds in the stay and in costly exit (During the stay ¶1, ¶5). Does the duty hold in a restaurant, the paper's own second case? The authors should argue that it does or narrow the claim.

**3.6 Authority treated as absolute.** *Answered in substance.* The Helsinki clerks (l. 73), the split into knowledge, discretion and authority (l. 97), and the requirement of written authority, training and protection (l. 144; Q4) all treat authority as organisationally bounded. The terms still drift (4.8).

**3.7 What guests owe.** *Answered by deletion.* One sentence on what the guest owes in return would help (cf. 'the guest who complies', l. 35).

**3.8 Welcome as a single duty.** *Partly answered.* The role now yields 'two core duties' (l. 19, 177). Yet The property ¶4 lists 'three duties', adding admission (l. 75). Table 1's first row rests on the duty to receive, and the last sentence returns to 'a welcome' (l. 187). The authors should decide whether admission is a core duty, and either define 'welcome' or drop it.

**3.9 Parts 6 and 7 meandered.** *Partly answered.* The parties section now reads well, and each subsection closes on a verdict. 'At the touchpoint' still runs to nine paragraphs, a list and a table. Its critique of seamlessness (¶8, l. 160) is a new argument placed at the end; it belongs with 'fluidity' (l. 31). The introduction still has no roadmap of the sections.

**3.10 'Roadmap of rising commitment' and 'forum'.** *Partly answered.* The phrase has gone, and Bovens now defines the forum (l. 162). The paper still does not say what that forum is for a hotel guest: a court, an ombudsman, an ADR scheme or a platform's resolution centre. Table 2 has the host respond 'before a forum', whereas l. 185 requires an 'independent forum'.

**3.11 Table 2.** *Partly answered.* A single sentence introduces the table (l. 162). Its mapping to the questions is left implicit: stages 2–5 match Q2, Q3–4, Q6 and Q7. Q1 has no stage, and neither does Q5 on timing, the paper's distinctive point. Stage 4 is not 'sequential'. The claim that a small innkeeper meets all five stages by 'posting a phone number' (l. 162) covers stages 2–3 at most.

**3.12 Contributions.** *Partly answered.* The contribution is now stated, 'why the guest is owed that route and which party must provide it' (l. 119; also l. 183). It appears only late, never in the introduction, and its 'which party' half is weak (4.4).

**3.13 Research question.** *Answered.* The question at l. 19 and the answer at l. 177 match.

**3.14 Well-being, 'two levels' and the Airbnb gap passage.** *Answered by deletion.* Edelman et al. survive as one clause (l. 87) whose role in the argument is unclear.

**3.15 Limitations and agenda.** *Largely answered.* Approach ¶2 (l. 59) states the limits, and Conclusion ¶5 (l. 185) proposes four designed studies. Still missing is the jurisdictional limit on a general normative claim drawn mainly from English law.

**3.16 AI assistance.** The version I received carries no statement on it; I leave this to the editor.

## 4. New concerns, ranked

**4.1 The three binding conditions are stated inconsistently.** The text says 'None of these three elements suffices on its own' (l. 49). Yet the brand verdict makes control *or* holding out sufficient (l. 83), and the platform verdict makes holding out alone sufficient (l. 93). Elsewhere control alone decides (l. 109), and 'accountability runs through control of that space' (l. 183). The sentence on insufficiency names undertaking, reliance and vulnerability, not the three conditions it follows (l. 49). Approach ¶3 says the role fixes 'the party bound by it' (l. 61), whereas l. 49 places that work 'outside the role itself'. The authors should say in one place which condition binds which duty, and whether the conditions work jointly or severally.

**4.2 The further duty has four grounds, and its key step is weaker than stated.** The paper grounds the further duty in four things:

- standing (l. 5, 19, 177);
- the guest's claim-right, through Herstein's account of Raz (l. 115);
- instrumental necessity, through Kiesewetter (l. 115);
- subjection and costly exit (l. 117).

The paper defines standing as the guest's position 'when that obligation is recognized' (l. 41), so standing cannot also ground a new obligation without circularity. The instrumental step needs guest reporting to be a necessary means, yet l. 117 calls it the main way 'if not the only one'. On the card, Herstein's entry says a claim-right 'seemingly can count in favor of different and changing duties' (§8.4, para. 2). It also reports a Hohfeldian reply that such rights are 'only inchoate', and it files the point under features 'particularly salient to legal rights'. The manuscript drops the hedge (l. 115), though it calls the further duty 'a normative guide' (l. 158). The authors should choose one ground and keep the hedge. The exit-cost premise also needs evidence, because an au pair's exit (l. 113) is not a hotel guest's. Finally, 'wrongs its guests even if nothing goes wrong' (l. 119) is the paper's strongest claim and the one it argues least.

**4.3 The delegation claim varies in strength.** The abstract says the firm keeps both duties 'even when contractors or algorithms carry out the work' (l. 5). The introduction and conclusion say spatial control keeps only the duty of care (l. 19, 177). The property ¶2 says 'liability narrows' under the competent-contractor rule (l. 71). These can be reconciled: the occupier's duty stays but is discharged by careful selection and checking. The paper should say so, and the reconciliation would also give it a basis for vendor oversight. Separately, l. 51 uses the Hotel Proprietors Act to carry the 'core duties' across delegation. Yet the *Al-Najar* card records the judge's view that under the old common law 'it seems that the InnKeeper was not liable for any criminal assault' (para 183). The guest-safety duty comes from modern negligence law (para 187), not from the statute.

**4.4 The allocation rule leaves the decisive party without a duty.** The vendor, the paper says, is best placed to fix an error yet 'most insulated from the guest' (l. 101). The paper assigns the route to the spatial controller, which forwards complaints (l. 144, 183). It derives no duty on a vendor or platform to receive and act on them, and vendors answer only for physical and property harm (l. 99). The chain therefore breaks at the link the paper diagnoses. The authors should either argue that the host must secure the vendor's cooperation by contract (l. 187 points that way) or state the gap as a limitation.

**4.5 The opening case does not show the problem.** Garcia et al. study no guests; the authors concede the researchers 'tracked managerial behavior rather than guest complaints' (l. 97). The paper also describes the case two ways. Introduction ¶3 says pricing authority 'shifts to a software model' (l. 15), but ¶1 says managers 'kept the final say' (l. 11). The card supports ¶1, since managers 'retain the final decision right' (working paper, p. 5). 'Knowing that managers rarely intervened, the vendor designed' attributes intent. The card gives the finding as recommendations 'strategically biased' 'in equilibrium' (p. 4), which is a model result. Xu and Greiffenhagen is the paper's strongest case, and I would open there.

**4.6 The legal floor is stated three ways.** The listed exceptions differ at l. 59, l. 109 and l. 158. Approach ¶2 says doctrine offers remedies 'after the fact rather than an on-site route to repair' (l. 59). Yet l. 69 and l. 121 cite the Consumer Rights Act's right to repeat performance, and l. 121 says statutes 'routinely prioritize live repair'. If British law already lets a guest demand repeat performance during a stay, the claim that the further duty is 'not yet required by law' must be narrowed. 'Routinely' rests on three instruments, one of them about platform workers.

**4.7 Table 1's boundary is inconsistent.** In row 1, correction during the stay can help ('Yes'), yet the route required is 'None'. Row 2, a check-in failure, gets the full route. The text says the route is owed while correction 'can still help' (l. 121). The authors should say when the stay begins. Three of the five rows cite 'Authors' analysis', whereas l. 57 says the cases are not 'hypothetical scenarios'.

**4.8 Key terms drift.**

- *Authority* is the power over 'a decision or the rule behind it' at l. 41. At l. 97 it is the power over the rule only, with case-level leeway called discretion. At l. 144 and Q3–4, 'override authority' means that leeway.
- *Standing* belongs to the guest (l. 39), yet 'organizational rules confer standing' on staff (l. 144).
- The abstract promises five 'steps', and Table 2 has five 'stages'.

**4.9 Register.** The introduction has no roadmap. Several sentences grade the paper's own argument: 'exposes an operational question that fluidity obscures' (l. 31), 'gives us a clear test' (l. 53), 'carry clear evidentiary and legal bounds' (l. 59) and 'pinpoints exactly where a touchpoint breaks down' (l. 156). The claim that the problem was 'never derived from the host role itself' (l. 57) should become 'we found no study that …', with the search terms. Conclusion ¶3 (l. 181) repeats the party verdicts.

## 6. VERIFY

On the cards I confirmed Garcia et al. (35 days, weekly updates, 0.72, 'strategically biased', final decision right), Xu and Greiffenhagen (pp. 5021–5035), *Al-Najar* (paras 183, 187, 240), *Wheat* (pp. 9–11, 16) and Herstein (§8.4). I could not confirm the following:

- Garcia et al.: the nine family-run hotels, eight at resorts; a vendor 'based in Europe'; daily log-ins; and the condition 'When a manager manually changed a rate' on the 0.72 figure, which the card's quotation (p. 7) lacks. The card's quotations come from the working paper, not the version of record.
- Batat (2021: 4, 7, 8): 'great atmosphere with smiling staff' and staff who maintain 'discretion'.
- Kiesewetter (2015: 922): whether the principle concerns obligations or reasons.
- Consumer Rights Act s. 65: as I read it, the section applies to any trader, not only where control and reliance combine (l. 49).
- Product Liability Directive art. 6(1): the damage categories (l. 99).
- AI Act: art. 14(4), the date of 2 December 2027 via Regulation 2026/1744, and the Annex III 1(a) exclusion.
- DSA arts 6(3) and 12(1); *SCHUFA* paras 52–53; *YL* para 29 (is it a majority speech?).
- *R (DMA)* para 100 and *RR v Enfield* paras 80–81; *Rasnick* at 569; *X v Kuoni* para 50.
- *Groh*: one guard (l. 142) or several (l. 156).
- *Crinkley*: apparent agency is attributed to control, which is ordinarily the test for actual agency.
- *Mobil Oil v Bransford*: whether it concerns lodging at all.
- Schmidt et al. (2025), on low stakes; Golubovskaya et al. (2017: 1296), on twenty-two interviewees and six.
- Pitt-Rivers (2012): a reprint; give the original date.
