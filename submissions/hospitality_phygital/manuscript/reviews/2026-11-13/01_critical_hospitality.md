## Part 1. Rigour and argument, from my lens

I came to judge whether this paper says something about hospitality or borrows the word to dignify a technology argument. My answer is that it says something. Its question, 'who is the host?' (Phygital hospitality ¶4, l. 31), asked of a multi-firm, algorithmic arrangement, belongs to this journal. But the section that should anchor the paper in hospitality theory misreads four of its sources. It also cites the field's critique of host power as if it were a warrant for host duty, and it leaves out the half of Pitt-Rivers that bears hardest on its central concept. I take the four questions put to me in turn.

### 1.1 Does 'The host' read the field fairly?

Partly. The paper reads O'Gorman and Morrison and O'Gorman fairly. The concession that Roman sources bound 'private hosts rather than commercial innkeepers' (l. 35) is the honest reading the O'Gorman card asks for. The Golubovskaya figure is right: 'only the minority (6 of 22 participants)' could define hospitality (2017: 1296; card). Bulley's 'assembled host' (AM p. 13) and Petie et al.'s several hosts (MS p. 4) are right in substance. Four readings fail: Lashley, Telfer, Slattery and Odekerken-Schröder. A fifth, Lynch et al., narrows its source. F2 gives the detail.

### 1.2 Is 'Read together, these accounts show …' earned?

Not as worded (F3). The accounts behind it come from six settings:

- ancient guest-friendship;
- a monastic rule;
- a report of what was 'traditionally' the host's responsibility;
- refugee camps;
- a Mediterranean honour code;
- a theme in an editorial.

Only Hemmington writes about commercial lodging, and his point is that the industry overlooks the guest's safety (AV p. 15; card). The sentence also folds two literatures into one. O'Gorman and Telfer describe obligation. Lynch et al., Bulley and Pitt-Rivers describe the host's power as a problem: 'social control' paired with 'resistance' (Lynch et al. 2011, PDF p. 5 of the deposited draft; card), and management of guests 'once inside' (Bulley, AM p. 10). 'Admitting a guest entails assuming responsibility … while exercising authority' reads the critique as a description of duty. The paper's own later argument needs the critical reading instead: the guest needs a route because the host controls the space and leaving is costly (During the stay ¶3, l. 117).

### 1.3 Would this journal recognize 'standing' as defined here?

Not yet, for three reasons.

1. **In this journal's literature, 'standing' means rank.** Pitt-Rivers uses the word for a newcomer's place 'within the hierarchy of prestige' (2012: 503) and for a stranger's 'social standing in his community of origin' (2012: 504). Lashley uses it for the 'social status and standing' that giving earns (2015: 3). A reader of this journal will hear 'the recognized status' (l. 39) in that sense. In that sense it is conferred by the host, which is the opposite of what the paper intends.
2. **The paper distinguishes standing only from service and design concepts.** The host ¶4 (l. 41) separates standing from consumer rights, procedural justice, contestability and customer participation. It names none of the neighbours a hospitality reader brings:
   - hospitableness, 'the willingness to be hospitable for its own sake' (Lashley 2015: 1);
   - the guest's honour, which the classical law grants in place of rights (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 514);
   - Kant's line between a stranger's right of visit and a guest's right, which needs 'a special friendly compact' (card `kant1795perpetual`, pp. 137–138).
3. **The definition is Darwall's second-personal standing in all but name, and it is uncited.** Compare 'an assumed individual authority or standing that the obligee has with respect to the obligor' (card `darwall2013bipolar`, p. 27). A philosopher will see the debt; a hospitality reader will see an unsourced coinage.

The agentless 'recognized' then opens a dilemma that F1 sets out.

### 1.4 Is the paper's guest still a hospitality guest, or a consumer with a complaint?

On the present text, closer to the consumer. The standing the paper gives the guest, to 'make a request, raise an objection, ask for an exception' (l. 39), is what the classical law of hospitality withholds. The paper quotes Pitt-Rivers at l. 35 for the host who ordains. On the next pages of the same essay:

- the guest 'has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host' (2012: 514);
- a guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps 'the host's right to ordain' (515);
- 'To complain openly would infringe the host's prerogative' (514);
- the dissatisfied guest 'has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out' (513).

Two other sources the paper cites point the same way. Hemmington says that 'it would be quite inappropriate for guests to dictate their requirements to their host' (AV p. 14). He also reports Darke and Gurney's finding that domestic rules 'such as not complaining' are 'at variance with the commercial manager-customer relationship' (AV p. 9; card). Golubovskaya et al. report, after King, that commercial hospitality 'empowers the guest, whose only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably' (2017: 1286; card).

The field therefore places the guest who complains in commerce. The paper can keep a hospitality guest, but only by saying two things:

- **What commerce adds:** a claim the guest may press.
- **What hospitality keeps:** a stranger on another's ground, dependent on the host's house (F7), and a sanctioned way to stop the service without leaving (F5 and Best spend).

Without these two things, the honest answer to R2's first-round question ('why hospitality') is that the paper's guest is a customer and the further duty is service recovery with a moral ground. The paper itself sets service recovery aside at l. 119.

## 2. The read: findings ranked by damage

### F1. Standing rests on the half of hospitality theory the paper leaves out [CONSIDER, about 100 words; minimum FIX about 40 words] (partly also R1, on who recognizes; also R2, R3, on circularity)

**What the paper claims.** Standing is 'the recognized status from which a person can make a request' (The host ¶3, l. 39). It 'precedes' four service concepts (The host ¶4, l. 41), and it exists 'when that obligation is recognized' (l. 41). The guest must be able to use the route 'as a matter of right rather than depending on staff goodwill' (During the stay ¶3, l. 117).

**What fails, and why.** Four problems:

- **The paper cites the half of Pitt-Rivers it likes.** He supplies the ordaining host (l. 35). He also says the guest has 'no right other than to respect', that claims and demands usurp the host's role, and that open complaint infringes the host's prerogative (2012: 513–515; 1.4). Hemmington and Golubovskaya et al., both cited, place the complaining guest in the commercial relation (1.4).
- **'Recognized' by whom?** The paper does not say, and either answer costs it something:
  - *If the host recognizes standing,* the guest's standing depends on the host's will, which is how Pitt-Rivers describes hospitality. That contradicts 'as a matter of right' (l. 117).
  - *If the law recognizes it,* standing becomes an entitlement held against a firm. That is how l. 41 defines consumer rights, the first concept standing is said to precede.
- **'Standing' means rank to this journal's readers** (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 503–504; Lashley 2015: 3).
- **The concept is Darwall's, uncited** (1.3).

**Fix.**

- **Minimum, about 40 words [FIX].** Name the recognizer. Add the hospitality neighbours (Part 3, item 12). Cite Darwall once for the concept.
- **Full fix, about 100 words [CONSIDER].** Say that the classical law withholds the guest's claim, and that commercial lodging supplies it while hospitality keeps a sanctioned interruption. Best spend drafts this.

The full fix also answers R1's first-round request for host–guest negotiation (F5) and gives R2's 'why hospitality' a positive answer.

### F5. Interruptibility and negotiation lack hospitality roots that the paper already holds [CONSIDER, about 60–100 words] (R1's first-round point answered only on paper: also R1; Batat: also R1)

**What the paper claims.** 'Negotiation requires a moment in which negotiation can take place' (At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160), and the paper proposes 'interruptible hospitality' in place of frictionless service (l. 160).

**What fails.** In round one, Reviewer 1 asked for 'articles that reference host-guest negotiation inherent in the hospitality literature'. v9 uses 'negotiation' three times (ll. 160, 162) and cites nothing for it (also R1). The sources are already in the paper's own bibliography or library:

- **Pitt-Rivers on reciprocity.** Host and guest's 'mutual obligations are in essence unspecific … each must accede to the desires of the other. To this extent the relationship is reciprocal' (2012: 513).
- **Pitt-Rivers's turned plate.** A guest dissatisfied with his place at table could turn his plate over, 'thereby making it impossible to serve him'. He thus 'retired from his role until the error was corrected, or at least until his protest had been registered, without showing any discourtesy to his host' (2012: 513–514; saved text). That is a sanctioned interruption: the guest halts the service to register a failure without usurping the host or leaving.
- **Bulley's wager.** 'Such a negotiation between host and guest would perhaps allow the flourishing of other forms of life' (AM p. 18).
- **Moysidou and Stanley.** Guests negotiate 'from indirect and passive approaches (when the person feels they have limited power in the relationship) to more direct and assertive approaches' (AM p. 6; card).
- **Lynch et al.** Hospitality space should 'enable contestation and conflict to occur' (card). The wording comes from a pre-publication copy, so check it against the version of record before quoting.

With these sources, interruptible hospitality becomes a hospitality concept with a genealogy rather than seamful design in a hospitality register, which is panelist 02's question. The turned plate also gives the paper what Batat cannot (R1): a hospitality value of interruption that owes nothing to friction for its own sake.

**Fix.** Best spend drafts the core in 98 words. A further six words at l. 160 could tie it back: '… interruptible hospitality, the turned plate made general: an arrangement that …'.

> The classical law of hospitality withholds this standing. A guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's right to ordain, and a dissatisfied guest 'has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out' (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 515, 513). ## 5. Verdict

**Minor revision**, from my lens. The paper has said something about hospitality. The question is the field's, and the answer by party is new. But its hospitality section misreads its sources in ways a referee from this journal will see at once, starting with Lashley in Lashley's own journal.

The single most important fix is F1: say what licenses the guest's claim. Name who recognizes standing. Concede that the classical law of hospitality withholds the guest's right to press a claim. Then show what commercial lodging adds and what hospitality keeps: the stranger on another's ground, and the turned plate. F3's five-word change ('We read these accounts together as saying') is the first step, and the F2 corrections cost nothing.

I agree with R2 that the further duty's ground is the panel's largest problem. From my field, the subjection ground (c) is the one hospitality theory can support.
