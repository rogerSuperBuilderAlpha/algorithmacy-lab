## Step 0. A register note

The paper's legal register is right for *Hospitality & Society*. ## Part 1. Rigour and argument

### 1.1 The ground of the further duty

The paper states the ground of its further duty in ten places, and they reduce to two grounds plus one supporting premise.

- **Standing**: the abstract (l. 5, 'the guest's standing also requires a timely route'), the introduction (Introduction ¶5, l. 19, 'the guest's standing during a stay grounds a further duty') and the conclusion (Conclusion ¶1, l. 177).
- **The claim, or its correlative duty**: 'this underlying claim generates a further duty' (During the stay ¶2, l. 115), 'the guest's underlying claim justifies this duty' (¶4, l. 119), and 'directly from the host's underlying duty' (Conclusion ¶4, l. 183). These are one ground stated from two sides. On the standard account a claim and a directed duty are the same relation: 'A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ' (Wenar and Cruft 2025: §2.1.2).
- **Subjection and costly exit** (During the stay ¶1, l. 113; ¶3, l. 117; ¶5, l. 121). This premise grounds two features of the duty, namely that the guest must be able to use the route without goodwill and that the duty lasts as long as the stay. It does not ground the duty itself, and the paper should present it that way.

Standing is the outlier. The paper defines it as 'the position the guest occupies when that obligation is recognized' (The host ¶4, l. 41), so it cannot also be the duty's ground (also R1, R2, R3). V8 had already fixed this. It wrote 'the guest's claim justifies a further duty' in the introduction and conclusion and said in terms that 'standing does not ground the duty but names what the guest has when the duty is met' (v8 ll. 25, 49, 185). V9 changes 'claim' to 'standing' in three places and drops the disclaimer. The repair is to restore v8's words (finding 1).

### 1.2 Is the claim-plus-means step valid as stated?

As stated, it is not. The step at During the stay ¶2 (l. 115) changes the kind of normativity twice. It starts from a **legal** claim ('legal duties owed to an identifiable person generate corresponding claims'). It passes through Herstein's report of **legal** right-dynamism, which his entry files under features 'unique or particularly salient to legal rights' (card herstein2023legal) and describes as how 'legal rights are often the instruments of legal change' (§8.4, para. 2). It then applies Kiesewetter's transmission principle, which governs the **deliberative**, all-things-considered 'ought', not moral obligation (Kiesewetter 2015: 923, 932). The step ends in a **directed moral** duty that the paper concedes is not law (ll. 59, 158).

Three further gaps follow:

1. **Necessity.** Transmission reaches only necessary means (Kiesewetter 2015: 922 and n. 2). The paper's own evidence calls guest reports the main route to discovery 'if not the only one' (During the stay ¶3, l. 117). So transmission yields at most a duty to keep *some* means of discovery, which the paper itself concedes at l. 115 ('no single technical design is required'). A guest-usable route follows only for failures that only the guest can see, and the first sentence of l. 117 says exactly that (also R2, R3).
2. **Directedness.** 'Because the guest's underlying claim justifies this duty, it is owed directly to the guest' (¶4, l. 119) is a non sequitur. What justifies a duty does not settle whom it is owed to. The same encyclopaedia entry reports Feinberg's 'public duties not owed to those individuals that they are designed to benefit' (Herstein 2023: §4.3, para. 1), and it reports the Hohfeldian reply that dynamic duties imply only 'inchoate rights' (Kramer, as reported, §8.4, para. 2). This step carries the paper's whole contrast with Hindriks, and the paper never argues it. None of the other reports raises it.
3. **Wronging without failure.** 'A host that maintains no channel … wrongs its guests even if nothing goes wrong' (¶4, l. 119). If nothing fails, the route was not necessary to performance. Kiesewetter gives measures that secure conformity 'second-order reasons', not a new 'ought' (p. 937, though he does not rule one out). The claim needs a duty to be in a position to perform, which is Hindriks's form of duty: 'control gaps can be latent: the risks they pose to others need not materialize' (Hindriks 2025: 14). Yet the same paragraph sets Hindriks aside because his duty 'rests partly on general risk to others' (also R2).

**A valid version, built from what the paper already cites.**

1. The host owes the guest performance and care, and the guest holds the correlative claim (Wenar and Cruft 2025: §2.1.2). The guests in *Al-Najar* and *Everett* sued in their own names (paras 7, 187; paras 1–2).
2. For a failure that only the guest can perceive, a route from the guest is a necessary means of correcting it while correction can still help (l. 117, first sentence; Table 1).
3. 'If an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it' (Hindriks 2025: 4, after Goodin). On Kiesewetter's account, an 'ought' transmits to necessary means (2015: 922).
4. *The authors' premise, which they should state as their own:* a duty to take the necessary means of performing a duty owed to the guest is itself owed to the guest.

The conclusion follows. For failures only the guest can see, the host owes the guest a route the guest can use, for as long as correction can help. This version uses standing only to name the guest's position in step 1. It needs no dynamic legal rights, so it no longer clashes with 'not yet required by law'. It also shows exactly where the paper parts from Hindriks: at step 4, directedness, not at risk.

## Part 2. The slop audit

The paper reads as human-edited prose. What reads as machine-made, from my lens, is **unearned certainty about sources**:

- 'Lord Pearson stated plainly' (The host's duty in law ¶2, l. 49);
- 'leaving others firmly with the host' (¶3, l. 51);
- 'routinely prioritize live repair' (During the stay ¶5, l. 121);
- 'a basic precaution any host can anticipate' (At the touchpoint ¶3, l. 142);
- 'genuine authority' (¶4, l. 144);
- 'pinpoints exactly' (¶6, l. 156) (also R3);
- 'genuinely human-centered' (Conclusion ¶2, l. 179) (also Grok).

Each intensifier sits on a statement the source makes in a qualified form. Together with the nine dropped hedges (Step 0), they give the paper a confidence its authorities do not share. A legal referee reads that as a tell faster than any em-dash.

The party subsections all end on a 'therefore' verdict (ll. 75, 83, 93, 101). The shape is uniform, but the verdicts help the reader, so keep them. The trouble is that two of them (l. 83, l. 93) carry the legal overstatements in findings 6 and 7. Fix their content, not their shape.

**Not slop.** The case-led paragraphs (The property ¶2, l. 71; The state ¶2, l. 107) put judges in subject position and carry pinpoints. The vendor's definitions of knowledge, discretion and authority (l. 97) weld each gloss to its term. The dash at l. 49 ('Holding out—presenting a property…') does the job the authors' style rules give dashes. Grok's call to remove every em-dash should be declined.

## 1. Who I am

I work on directed duties and the law of delegation. My question is when a duty-holder that performs through others stays answerable, and to whom. I read English, US and EU private law alongside rights theory: Hohfeld as Wenar and Cruft report him, Raz as Herstein reports him, Kiesewetter on transmission, Hindriks on the duty of control, Brandt on delegation and Bovens on accountability. I came to this paper expecting what I usually find when service research borrows law: a sound instinct (the host stays answerable) carried by authorities that hold something narrower. I also came believing the paper's further duty is defensible. A duty to perform does carry a duty to keep the means of learning that performance has failed, but only for failures the duty-holder cannot see by other means.

**1. The ground of the further duty regressed from v8. [FIX] (also R1, R2, R3)**

- *Claim.* 'The guest's standing during a stay grounds a further duty' (Introduction ¶5, l. 19; also l. 5 and Conclusion ¶1, l. 177).
- *What fails.* The paper defines standing as 'the position the guest occupies when that obligation is recognized' (The host ¶4, l. 41), so standing cannot ground the obligation. That was R2's first-round point 3. V8 had answered it by writing 'the guest's claim' at these three places and by saying 'standing does not ground the duty' (v8 l. 49). V9 reverses both changes. The section that derives the duty (During the stay ¶¶2–4) never mentions standing. A referee who reads the abstract and the conclusion will see the circle R2 named.
- *Fix.* Restore 'claim' at ll. 5, 19 and 177, and restore the disclaimer at l. 41. To meet R2's request to tell having standing from exercising it, the paper can borrow a home for standing from a source it already cites: on Darwall's account a claim-right 'includes a second-personal authority to resist, complain, remonstrate' (Darwall 2006: 18, quoted in Wenar and Cruft 2025: §2.2.2). Standing is the guest's authority to press a claim. It is not what grounds the claim.

**2. The derivation is valid only in a narrower form than the paper states. [FIX] for wording, [CONSIDER] for the premise (also R2, R3 in part; contra Grok)**

**3. The delegation account gives 'keeps' to the wrong duty and misses the paper's own best law. [CONSIDER] for the paragraph; [FIX] for MacLenan and Kong (also R1, R2, R3 on the inconsistency; the legal analysis is new)**

- *Claim.* 'spatial control keeps the duty of care with the host' (Introduction ¶5, l. 19), and the firm 'keeps them even when contractors or algorithms carry out the work' (Abstract, l. 5). Against this, The property ¶2 says 'liability narrows' (l. 71).
- *What fails.* In English law the safety duty is discharged by reasonable entrustment. The occupier who chooses and checks with care is 'not to be treated without more as answerable' (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, s. 2(4)(b); *Ferguson* 1987: 7; *Naylor* 2004: para 45; *Maguire* 2006: para 30). What stays with the occupier is a duty to choose and check with care and, on Lord Keith's reading, to act once it 'knows or has reason to suspect' unsafe work (*Ferguson*: 7). That second sentence is the paper's best legal bridge to the further duty, and the paper leaves it out.

  The duty that stays whoever performs is the duty to perform an undertaken service. Rix LJ contrasted the premises rule with 'the position in contract generally', where a party is bound 'whether he performs it by himself, by his servant or agent, or by an independent contractor' (*Maguire*: para 24). The package organiser answers for the services 'irrespective of whether' it performs them (Directive 2015/2302, art. 13(1)). The tour operator in *Kuoni* answered for a hotel employee 'because the services it undertook to provide were not provided with care and skill' (para 50). Brandt's library case makes the same point in moral terms: an agreed responsibility cannot be shifted by 'the unilateral alteration of a promise' (ms p. 10).

  So the abstract's 'keeps them' is right for undertaken services, by contract, for consumers. For safety it is right only in the sense that the duty stays while its content changes.
- *Three local errors.*
  - The absence claim at l. 71 is false as written (table, item 9: *MacLenan*).
  - The Hotel Proprietors Act cannot carry the point at l. 51 (item 3; also R3).
  - The vendor paragraph's 'each firm answers only for its own conduct' (The vendor ¶2, l. 99) inverts Kong, who makes the deployer answer for what its delegate does (p. 9).
- *Fix.* Section 4 below drafts the paragraph. Add the *MacLenan* sentence (Part 3) and the Kong correction.
- *Cost.* +97 words for the paragraph (Section 4), +40 for *MacLenan*, +8 for Kong.

**4. Two absence claims that the paper's own sources refute. [FIX] (new; R1, R2 and R3 noted that the lists differ)**

- *Claim.* Outside package travel, 'neither the law of delegation nor moral philosophy obliges a delegator' to keep a channel for discovering failures in a guest's stay (The state ¶3, l. 109). Approach ¶2 (l. 59) and At the touchpoint ¶7 (l. 158) name package travel as a regime that already requires the route.
- *What fails.* The paper's own state paragraph, two sentences earlier, cites *R (DMA)*. That court declared a delegating minister in breach for 'failing properly to monitor' contractor provision (para 333), because monitoring had to let her 'act immediately if there is a sign' (para 236). The Court of Appeal reads the case as requiring 'the necessary information … to discharge' a duty performed through contractors (*RR v Enfield* 2025: para 81). This is public law, statutory and set in an Article 3 context. But it is the law of delegation, it concerns the individual case, and the paper cites it. Moral philosophy also offers a counter: the paper's own Hindriks argues that an agent 'ought to put itself in the position that it can do it' (2025: 4). The package-travel exception, meanwhile, is weaker than the paper makes it: the 2015 text requires only a named contact point, and 'easily contactable' applies from 29 March 2029 (table, item 36).

**5. The derivation and the allocation pick different holders. [CONSIDER] (extends R1, R2, R3)**

- *Claim.* The further duty falls to 'whichever organization controls the physical space' (Conclusion ¶4, l. 183).

For safety that is the occupier, the party 'in control of the premises' (*Everett*: para 31). For an undertaken service it is the party that contracted to perform it (Consumer Rights Act 2015, ss. 2(2), 49(1); Directive 2015/2302, art. 13(1)). Table 1 already splits this way. Row 3, the hazard, belongs to the occupier. Rows 4 and 5, in-room service and the disputed charge, belong to the contracting party, which may be a brand or an operator that controls no room. - *Fix.* Add one sentence at l. 183: the occupier owes the route for safety, the contracting party for undertaken service, and where they differ the occupier routes reports to the contracting party.

## 3. Where it is strong

- **Law as an institutional record.** Reading law as 'an institutional record of what the role of host has been held to require' (The host's duty in law ¶4, l. 53) is the right method. Saying outright that the further duty is not yet law (ll. 59, 158) earns trust, and every revision must keep it.
- **The property section.** It sets the competent-contractor rule against the paper's own thesis ('liability narrows', l. 71). Few papers in this field would print the authority that cuts against them. It needs only finding 3's reframe to become the paper's best paragraph.
- **The time bound.** The rule that the route is owed 'for as long as correction can still help' (During the stay ¶5, l. 121) matches a real legal pattern: repeat performance comes before money in the Consumer Rights Act (ss. 55, 56(3)), and the package directive requires remedy during the trip (art. 13(3)). The time bound is the paper's most original idea, and the law supports it once the 'only when impossible' overstatement goes.
- **Routing.** Using *Wheat*'s handrail as the legal model for routing reports between holders (l. 144) is apt and accurate.
- **The contrast with service recovery.** The duty 'binds even when retaining the guest is unprofitable' (l. 119). In one clause, that separates a duty from a strategy.
- **The sources.** Of 36 legal statements, 12 are right as written. Every error can be fixed from a card the authors already hold, and no new research is needed.

> **Suggested (drafted by reviewer 05):** What stays with the host depends on the duty. A promise of service stays with the party that made it: in contract, Rix LJ observed, a party remains bound 'whether he performs it by himself, by his servant or agent, or by an independent contractor' (Maguire v Sefton 2006: para 24). The duty of care stays too, but its content changes. An occupier that entrusts work to a contractor must take the steps it 'reasonably ought' to check the contractor and the work (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, s. 2(4)(b)), and, Lord Keith said, may have to act once it 'knows or has reason to suspect' unsafe work (Ferguson v Welsh 1987: 7). The further duty asks for the means by which that knowledge arrives.
