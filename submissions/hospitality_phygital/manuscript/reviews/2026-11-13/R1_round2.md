# Reviewer 1, second round: "Who hosts the guest?" (revised manuscript)

*Locations.* Each location gives the section or subsection, the paragraph number counted within it, and the line number in DRAFT_v9.md, for example "The host ¶4, l. 41". Tables, audit questions and the stray line 89 are cited by line only. No manuscript quotation exceeds fifteen words.

## 1. Summary

This revision is close to a new paper. It advances three claims (Introduction ¶5, l. 19). First, the role of host carries two duties: to keep the guest safe and to perform undertaken services with reasonable care. Second, three conditions bind those duties to a particular firm: holding out, the guest's reliance and control of the space. Control keeps the duty of care with the host when contractors or algorithms do the work. Third, during a stay the guest's standing grounds a "further duty": a timely route by which a failure in the guest's case reaches someone empowered to correct it.

The authors build the first two claims from English, US and EU law, read as an institutional record of the host role (l. 53), and test them against five parties. They derive the third from rights theory and two hotel studies, map it in Table 1, and turn it into a seven-question audit (ll. 148–154) and a five-stage sequence (Table 2). Interruptibility survives as the further duty's "practical design consequence" (At the touchpoint ¶8, l. 160).

## 2. Recommendation

Minor revision. The paper is clearer and better grounded than in round one and meets most of my requests. It does, however, contradict itself on its central rule about control, misdescribe parts of its evidence and blunt the counter-rhetoric I valued. All three can be fixed without restructuring.

## 3. My first-round points

**3.1 Wilson-Nash et al. (2026): partly answered.** The article is cited once, in a subordinate clause: walking away "may carry a personal cost and loss" (At the touchpoint ¶9, l. 162). The card confirms the wording verbatim at p. 979, but here the citation does little work. The captive-guest argument sits elsewhere. During the stay ¶1 (l. 113) claims guests "rarely have an easy exit", citing an au pair from a homestay study, and ¶3 (l. 117) requires a reporting route "as a matter of right". Digital technology captivity, in which "the consumer cannot walk away" (p. 982), supports both claims. The "co-existence" trigger, an analogue route "retained but downgraded" (p. 978), states the paper's own point about an escalation option that exists only on paper (l. 162). I would name the concept and use it at ll. 113 and 117. Audit question 2 (l. 149) puts the same point into practice.

**3.2 Host–guest negotiation: partly answered.** The revision adds welcome work on power (Pitt-Rivers, l. 35; Lynch et al.; Bulley; Moysidou and Stanley, l. 113). It does not add literature on negotiation that goes beyond the functional and creates value for guests, which is what I asked for. "Negotiation" appears only at ll. 160 and 162, uncited. The revision even moves the other way. Warmth "rests on a deeper structure of obligation" (l. 35), and a human contact becomes "a friendly face to vent to" (l. 144). Friction is "valuable only because it creates a pause in which a guest can exercise standing" (During the stay ¶6, l. 134). That sentence contradicts the opening, where Le Petit Chef "sparks conversation among diners and between diners and servers" (Introduction ¶2, l. 13): a pause that serves sociability. The authors should either engage this literature in The host ¶1 or explain why they set it aside. Lugosi (2008) or Lynch (2017) might be a start (VERIFY: I name both from memory).

**3.3 "The split": answered.** The orphaned term is gone, and "this split" (l. 97) now has an antecedent close by. One residue remains: "split" now names three divisions. It names hosting across firms (ll. 19, 91), rule-setting from answerability (l. 65) and authority from discretion (l. 97). The knowledge–discretion–authority triad I credited in round one also arrives late, after a different quartet at l. 41 (see 4.3).

**3.4 The test and the abstract: answered in the body, partly in the abstract.** At the touchpoint ¶2 (l. 140) introduces the test. ¶5 (l. 146) states its question and its audiences and says it "follows the path a guest's complaint must travel". ¶6 (l. 156) applies it. This is the context I asked for. The abstract says only that the paper "offers seven questions for auditing a guest touchpoint" (l. 5). It should also say what the audit asks: whether a failure can reach someone able to correct it while the stay lasts. Two further points. First, the paper now has two tests: a control test for assigning duties ("a clear test", l. 53) and the diagnostic test (l. 146). They need different names. Second, in round one I read the test as a guide to whether a touchpoint should replace or augment a host. The revised audit examines touchpoints that already exist. The authors should say whether it also works at the design stage.

**3.5 "Framework": partly answered.** In the authors' own sense, "framework" appears at l. 19 ("applies to any phygital service") and at l. 185 ("Four studies would test this framework"). Neither passage says whether it means the three claims, the audit or Table 2. The other terms drift as well. "Seven questions" (abstract) becomes a "diagnostic test" and an "audit" (l. 146), and "five steps" (abstract) becomes "five sequential stages" (l. 162). Table 2 organises "these obligations" (l. 162), which have no clear antecedent. Its relation to the audit is never stated, although Stage 1 applies the audit and questions 6–7 restate Stages 4–5. The framework should be named once, its contents listed, and one term used for each part.

**3.6 Proofreading of "it" and "this": largely answered.** The prose is much cleaner. Unclear references remain in "This analysis also challenges" (l. 160) and in "these obligations" (l. 162). In "justifies this duty" (l. 119) the antecedent is a right. In "one that Hemmington notes" (l. 35) it is unclear whether the referent is the obligation or Telfer's view, and "in every case" (l. 156) reads as universal.

## 4. New concerns, ranked

**4.1 The control rule contradicts the paper's own material.** The state ¶3 (l. 109) says that across all five parties "one rule holds consistently": physical control anchors the duty of care. The paper's own sections say otherwise:

- A brand answers when it "holds itself out as the service provider" (l. 83). Orlando turns on advertising (l. 79).
- A platform takes on duties when it "presents itself" as the provider (l. 93).
- The package organiser is liable "regardless of who executes them" (l. 51).
- The state's duty "remains hers" by statute (l. 107).

The same evidence undercuts "each firm answers only for its own conduct" (l. 99) and "accountability runs through control of that space" (l. 183). Three further tensions follow:

- The abstract says the firm "keeps them even when contractors or algorithms carry out the work" (l. 5). Yet l. 71 shows that an occupier which hires a competent contractor is not liable for that contractor's faulty work. Keeping a duty differs from answering for another's fault.
- The route duty falls to "whichever organization controls the physical space" (ll. 144, 183). But l. 73 holds that "two or more may be 'occupiers'". Who provides the route when control is shared?
- Line 49 says "None of these three elements suffices on its own". It then argues the point for a different three: undertaking, reliance and vulnerability.

The remedy is to state the rule within its true scope. Control anchors the duty of care for physical safety, holding out and statute can add parties, and shared control needs a tie-break.

**4.2 The paper keeps what I valued, but blunts it.** The counter-rhetoric to seamless design now occupies one late paragraph (l. 160), and neither the abstract nor the introduction mentions seamlessness. "Interruptibility" appears in the abstract, the keywords and that paragraph, and nowhere else: not in the audit, the tables or the conclusion. A keyword concept should do work in the paper's instruments, and an audit question such as "Can the guest pause the automated flow and be answered?" would give it that work.

The paragraph also softens its target. It says design frameworks treat "friction-free speed as the primary virtue" (l. 160). Yet the paper's own Batat quotation promises "a coherent continuum" (l. 25), a value of continuity, not speed. It then enlists Batat as an ally (2026: 13, 16). The card confirms that Batat places seamless hospitality systems in her Water quadrant, "a good or fair improvement" (pp. 13, 16). Her benchmark, however, keeps "the seamless convergence" of precision, emotion and meaning (p. 13), and there "AI reduces friction" (p. 15). Batat asks whether seamlessness suffices; she does not question friction reduction as a goal. The paper's case for friction therefore runs against her benchmark. Saying so would restore the edge I credited in round one and sharpen the paper's distinctive point: a journey can be seamless while the guest has no one to answer to.

**4.3 The further duty has three grounds.** The paper grounds it in the guest's standing (ll. 19, 177), in the guest's "underlying claim" (ll. 115, 119) and in "the host's underlying duty" (l. 183). Standing itself is the guest's position "when that obligation is recognized" (l. 41). Standing therefore depends on the duty it is said to ground, which is the circularity Reviewer 2 raised, and the text does not say who does the recognising. Three terms also slip:

- "Standing" also describes staff: "organizational rules confer standing" (l. 144).
- "Authority" at l. 97 includes answering "for its design", which absorbs the accountability that l. 41 keeps separate.
- Line 109 says "moral philosophy" imposes no duty to keep a channel, on one account (Brandt). Line 115 then derives exactly that duty from Kiesewetter.

One paragraph stating the chain (role, duty, claim-right, standing, further duty) would fix most of this.

**4.4 The paper misdescribes parts of its evidence.**

(a) Line 142 cites "the thirteen guests whom the kiosk rejected" to show what happens when retrying fails. The card's quotation (p. 5035, n. 4) gives the breakdown:

- three cases where the kiosk was off;
- one unreadable ID card;
- eight unfound reservations;
- one frozen process.

None of these was a recognition failure or a rejection, and in three there was no output to override. The study says only that a worker "manually checked them in", so "passes the third question" (l. 156) is the authors' inference. Guests also noticed a worker "sitting next to the door" (p. 5022). The "do it yourself" redirection therefore fails question 2 on its goodwill limb, not its "informed" limb.

(b) The card records that in Groh the people who said "We are drunk. We can't drive" were Groh's friends, ordered out as non-guests (¶¶ 2, 6–7). The phrase "intoxicated guests" (l. 142) blurs a distinction a paper about standing needs. Groh involves no system output, so question 3 does not fit it. The card places the training claim at ¶¶ 11, 23 and 45, not at the paragraphs cited (l. 156).

(c) Line 11 attaches the 0.72 pass-through to "When a manager manually changed a rate". The card's quotation gives an unconditional average. Line 15 says "pricing authority shifts to a software model". Line 11 says managers "kept the final say over rates", and the source supports line 11.

**4.5 Table 2, scope and Table 1.**

- Line 162 says meeting the five stages "can be as straightforward as posting a phone number". Stage 5, however, needs a forum "authorized to question and judge" (l. 172), and l. 185 requires that forum to be independent. The authors should say which stages a small host can meet alone.
- The framework "applies to any phygital service" (l. 19), and the paper opens with a restaurant. Yet the Groh card records that the court confined its holding to hotels and excluded "taverns, restaurants" (n. 7).
- Table 1 requires an on-site staff member for safety hazards (l. 129). Self-check-in rentals (l. 91) fail this by design. Is that intended?
- Rows 1 and 2 of Table 1 overlap. A kiosk that cannot find a booking is in effect a refusal at arrival, yet the two rows give opposite answers on whether the further duty applies.
