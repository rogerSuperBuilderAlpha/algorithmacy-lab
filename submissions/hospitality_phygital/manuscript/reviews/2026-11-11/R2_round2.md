# Referee report, second round (Reviewer 2)

**"Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality"**

I count paragraphs within each section or subsection, leaving out lists and tables. HO = 'The host's power and the host's obligation'; DS = 'Delegation and standing'; Test = 'Three conditions and six questions'; Reachable = 'When a person must be reachable'.

## 1. Summary

The revision argues that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation. It is a bundle of duties (to receive, sustain, protect and attend) that innkeeper law attaches to whoever holds itself out to travellers, whatever its motive. So the work of hosting can pass to staff, vendors and systems while the duty stays with the holder. The party that holds itself out answers to the guest alongside the party in control, and parties that only arrange a service answer to the host. Standing is split into standing held and standing exercised. The thesis is that algorithmic mediation rarely removes the obligation. What it removes is the guest's path to anyone with authority to answer for it. A six-question test and Table 1 say where that path must stay open.

## 2. Recommendation

**Major revisions.** The paper now has the theory I asked for. But the step that carries the thesis, from a duty that persists to a guest entitled to reach someone with authority, is asserted rather than argued. The division of responsibility among firms also takes more from its legal sources than they give.

## 3. Round-one points

**R2.1 (origin, holder, non-transferability): largely addressed.** HO ¶4–5 ("the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation") gives the source of the obligation and HO ¶8 gives its trigger. Woodland supplies its persistence (DS ¶4: "The work can be delegated, and the duty cannot"). Non-transferability among several firms rests on a single sentence (DS ¶3; see New issue 2). **Figure 1** has gone, and the letter says nothing about it. The authors should say why. A figure of the holds-out / controls / arranges allocation would now earn its place.

**R2.2 (a theory of role obligation): largely addressed.** What activates the obligation, who first bears it and what can be delegated are where the letter says they are. My worry was that this would read as a familiar delegation point. DS ¶5's contrast with Kong answers it best: the duty is owed "from the moment of reception, whether or not anything goes wrong". That contrast belongs in the introduction. DS ¶2's four-term distinction is the clearest passage in the paper. The letter's "point for point" overstates the sharing question, though. Whether holders answer jointly or in sequence, and whom the guest addresses first, gets one sentence.

**R2.3 (circularity; held versus exercised): addressed in form, at a cost.** The loop is gone. DS ¶6 takes standing from Thompson's formulas, which "fix who owes what to whom without mentioning answering". Two costs follow:

- Standing held is now just the other side of the duty. That makes "the guest still holds standing" true by definition, not a finding.
- Darwall (2013: 35) does less than "as Darwall himself concedes" implies. The passage goes on to make the authority to claim part of the concept of a claim right.

My round-one circle came from using "accountability" in two senses: the moral authority to demand, and the institutional forum. A better repair would keep Darwall's authority inside standing held and reserve "accountability" for Bovens' forum. That would also build the bridge missing under New issue 1.

**R2.4 (what is specific to hospitality): addressed, and the concession is handled well.** HO ¶7 concedes plainly that the mechanism "is general". The claim about what is distinctive persuades only in part:

- Carriers share the surviving duty to receive.
- Woodland's hospital also takes in the stranger's person "overnight" in a house not their own.
- On the paper's own history, restaurants fall outside the common callings.

The distinctive feature the paper underuses is the host's command of a space the guest occupies in person (HO ¶2: Pitt-Rivers, Shryock). If the paper grounded distinctiveness there, the same principle would also justify Table 1. The Slattery sentence that closes HO ¶7 belongs in HO ¶11.

**R2.5 (boundary conditions; formats): partly addressed.** The Reachable ¶1 criterion, taken from Baum and Laux, is principled, and Table 1 mostly follows it. Three placements do not:

- A price that "bars access" at night amounts to a refusal, yet its row prescribes only a later appeal.
- The criterion covers effects "during the stay", but Reachable ¶3 applies it to cancellation, "which would cover a booking". Cancellations usually come before the stay.
- The complaints row's "at a time that suits the guest" follows from no criterion.

On formats, Reachable ¶4 restates features of decisions ("explains most where the guest is on site") rather than naming formats. Unstaffed hotels appear only in the research agenda. The Civil Rights Act edge is an anti-discrimination exemption, not evidence that the obligation "is at its weakest". It also misses the paper's hard case, the professional self-check-in rental, which is rarely the owner's own residence.

The letter gives accurate locations for my points; I checked each one.

## 4. New or remaining issues

**1. The step from a persisting duty to a reachable person with authority is asserted, not argued.** DS ¶5 moves to "the holder is the party the guest must be able to reach". DS ¶9 then concludes that the guest is owed "a hearing by someone with authority, not the exception itself". Neither follows from the bundle of duties in HO ¶6, which contains no duty to hear. The nearest candidate, attending, is the duty "the law did not carry", and Pitt-Rivers leaves the guest no recourse but walking out (DS ¶7). Sandoval-Strausz, whom HO ¶9 relies on, writes that travellers' protections "were not 'rights'" (2005: 66). That raises the question whether the duty was owed to the guest or to the public. The paper should do one of two things: argue that the role obligation is owed to the guest and includes a duty to give a hearing, or present reachability openly as its own proposal.

**2. 'Holding out' does two jobs, and the allocation reads too much into its sources.** In HO ¶8, holding out is an offer to travellers. In DS ¶3, it is Crinkley's apparent agency, which differs in three ways:

- It makes the brand liable for the operator's negligence, not for a duty of its own. Woodland separates that kind of liability from the personal duty the paper borrows.
- It depends on the guest's reliance on the brand, which weakens when guests do not choose it.
- Disclaimers affect it.

Three further problems:

- The 1956 Act binds the proprietor "and to no other person" (HO ¶8). HO ¶11 contradicts this with "or the brand under which it trades".
- Woodland's arranger category (para 25(3)) fits a booking platform better than a vendor whose system does the work. On the paper's own mapping, Airbnb would then answer to no guest, which is the evasion Germann Molz (2018) criticises.
- The proposal's second criterion, "the party that controls the operation", relies on control of the environment. Woodland para 24, quoted in DS ¶5, sets that ground aside.

To fix this:

- Replace "no court has held this" with what Crinkley held (liability through the operator, in one jurisdiction) and what no court has held.
- Present Conclusion ¶2's "held alongside whoever controls the operation" as a proposal.
- Say how an adult guest meets Woodland's criteria of vulnerability and custody (paras 23–24).

**3. 'Answering' has two senses.** Introduction ¶3 keeps "the answering for it" with the holder. Test ¶1 says a guest "has still been hosted by no one if no party is obliged to answer". On the paper's theory, question 5 always has the same answer, the holder, so it cannot tell touchpoints apart. Yet Test ¶3 says the other questions "depend" on it. The fix is to distinguish answerability (the holder's standing status) from accountability (an arrangement that can be missing). Question 5 should then ask whether the guest can reach, in time, someone with the holder's authority.

**4. Activation misses decisions made before arrival and some formats.** The obligation starts when "a traveller presents themselves" (HO ¶8). Most algorithmic pricing and booking-stage screening happen earlier, yet the test assesses the pricing case (Test ¶4). The restaurant case "passes", but nothing says how innkeeper law reaches restaurants or platform rentals. The paper should either argue that a digital request counts as presenting oneself and that the role extends beyond the inn, or limit the test's claims.

**5. The order of the roadmap conflicts with the theory.** Nothing can replace missing accountability (Test ¶1). Yet the roadmap orders its stages by "rising cost" ('Designing for interruptible hospitality' ¶4), so the decisive stage comes fourth. Table 2 also labels stage 5 "Accountability, through exit", though exit is Hirschman's alternative to voice. Stage 4 should be a precondition for Table 1's top rows, and stage 5 should be relabelled.

**6. Positioning.** HO ¶4 says the debate "on both sides, turns on the host's motive", then quotes Blain and Lashley's "externally imposed" obligation. The paper's contribution is the holder and the trigger, and the text should say so. It should also engage the philosophy of role obligations and role-responsibility (Hardimon; Hart) and read Telfer directly.

## 5. Minor points

- HO ¶9's "or that it places today's hotels in any special legal category" contradicts the 1956 Act cited in HO ¶8.
- DS ¶2 makes the retained duty one of seeing that the obligation is performed; DS ¶5 calls it "first-order". Say which one the holder keeps.
- Test ¶1 borrows its names "from the study of coordination". But Okhuysen and Bechky's accountability runs among coordinating parties, not to customers.
- Test ¶4's "Together the cases show" overclaims. No case measures satisfaction.
- Conclusion ¶3 leaves out North Carolina law (Crinkley).
- Conclusion ¶2's "no hospitality use as high-risk" needs qualifying. Annex III defines biometric uses by what they do, not by sector.
- Weak supporting sources: Mosca and La Rosa (2019) and Mosca (2026) in 'Designing for interruptible hospitality' ¶1; De Vos et al. (2024) in Conclusion ¶5.
- References: Baum and Laux (2026) and Darwall (2013) lack editors or place. Kaniadakis and Farmaki (2022) and Kim et al. (2025) carry volume numbers from later years.
- The body is about 8,470 words, so the fixes must fit in roughly 500 words. DS ¶8 and Test ¶5 can be shortened.

## 6. Confidential comments to the editor

The authors have revised in good faith. They rebuilt the paper on the theory I asked for and conceded R2.4 candidly. My remaining concerns are conceptual and confined to the second section, the test and Table 1, and they need no new data. If New issues 1–3 are resolved I expect to support publication. If the special-issue timetable is tight, I would review only those parts.
