# Second-round report: Reviewer 1

**"Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality" (revised)**

The ¶ numbers count paragraphs within each section or subsection. Lists and tables are not counted.

## 1. Summary

The paper argues that the commercial host's obligation belongs to a role rather than to a motive. It uses the law of innkeepers to give that role a content, a moment of onset (holding out to a traveller who presents themselves) and a holder, which can be a firm. The work of hosting can be delegated but the duty cannot, so the guest keeps standing under algorithmic mediation. What fails is the exercise of that standing, which needs a reachable party with authority. From this the authors build a six-question test of touchpoints, a typology of decisions (Table 1) and a five-stage roadmap (Table 2).

## 2. Recommendation

**Minor revisions.** The rebuild is real and the argument is far easier to follow, but three problems remain. "Authority" carries two senses, the test does not ask the question its argument turns on, and some case sentences claim more than their sources support. Each can be fixed within the word limit; the body is about 8,470 words with tables.

## 3. My round-one points

**R1.1 Wilson-Nash et al. (2026): addressed.** Designing for interruptible hospitality ¶2 uses it to ground the real alternative ("digital captivity is 'coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable'"). The quotations (pp. 976–981) are accurate, and the letter describes the change correctly. The source could do more work (issue 5).

**R1.2 Host–guest negotiation: partly addressed.** Delegation and standing ¶7 ("Exercising standing has conditions, and guests do not supply them") and ¶9 add apt sources (Xiang et al. 2022; Moysidou and Stanley 2023). But I asked about negotiation that creates value beyond the functional, and ¶9 reduces it to complaint and escalation. Its Lugosi sentence ("management's allowance gave patrons only the opportunity to make such things") describes co-created value, not a request reaching authority. It therefore supports neither the topic sentence nor the letter. The topic sentence ("Where requests do change outcomes in hospitality, they reach someone with authority") is a general claim resting on two studies. The letter speaks of "a bar in which guests' requests changed outcomes", which Lugosi does not show. Either add a few sentences on co-created moments as part of what a reachable host protects, or limit the claim to negotiation as the pressing of claims.

**R1.3 "The split": addressed.** The authority–answerability split ¶1 now opens on the definition ("By the authority–answerability split we mean an arrangement in which the party"), as the letter says. But the term vanishes after ¶3: the test, the tables and the conclusion never use it. "Answerability" is not among the four terms defined in Delegation and standing ¶2. "Discretion splits" (Split ¶4) also reuses the word in a different sense.

**R1.4 Purpose of the test; abstract: addressed in the body, partly in the abstract.** Three conditions and six questions ¶1 gives the preamble I asked for: purpose, users and limits ("it measures neither satisfaction nor harm"). The abstract only names the test ("It offers a test of phygital touchpoints, scoped by the type of decision"). The letter says the abstract states the test; it does not say what the test asks.

**R1.5 "Framework": partly addressed.** Tables are now named where they are used, and Conclusion ¶2 lists the paper's parts. The letter says every mention now names them, but the bare word "framework" survives three times:
- When a person must be reachable ¶4 ("The framework explains most where the guest is on site")
- Conclusion ¶4 ("The framework points to three studies")
- Conclusion ¶5

**R1.6 "It" and "this": largely addressed.** Pronoun reference is much cleaner, and the letter's claim is fair. Two strays remain:
- The abstract's "Each places the party that decides at a distance from the guest": does "each" mean the systems or the firms?
- Conclusion ¶2's "Its work can be delegated and its duty cannot": the role has work, but the obligation does not.

## 4. New or remaining issues

**1. Authority has two senses.** Delegation and standing ¶2 defines authority as "the power, assigned by an organization, to change a decision or the rule behind it". Split ¶4 then separates the two: "Discretion acts on the single case and authority on the rule". Table 1's first row requires "A reachable person with authority before refusal takes effect". That is power over the single case, which ¶4 calls discretion. The thesis concerns the path to "anyone with authority", so the distinction matters. Name the two levels, use them consistently, and say in Table 1 which level each row needs.

**2. The test does not ask about reachability.** The split turns on a party that "is not a party the guest can reach" (Split ¶1), and Table 1 adds timing. Yet the six questions ask who decides, who overrides and who answers, never whether the guest can reach that party in time. Add that question or rewrite question 5. Two further problems:
- Three conditions ¶1 allows conditions to be "present in degrees", while the cases report pass or fail. State the decision rule.
- Only two of the four cases in ¶4 get a verdict. A small table applying the questions to all four would help.

**3. The cases claim more than their sources support.** Three conditions ¶4 reads as findings.
- Garcia et al. (2026) study revenue managers who keep the final pricing decision, and they say nothing about desk staff or guests. "No one at the desk could explain the price" is therefore the authors' inference. Framed as authority that exists but cannot be reached from the desk, the case would illustrate the split better.
- Xu et al. (2021), as I know it, studies security, privacy and trust in facial-recognition check-in. Show where it describes a system that "weighs the guest's history", or mark the example as your own.
- "Together the cases show that a touchpoint can satisfy guests and harm no one" is unsupported, since no case reports guest satisfaction.

**4. Table 2 does not order the five principles.** Designing ¶4 says "the five principles line up as a roadmap of rising cost", but the stages are not the principles. The three interface principles are absorbed into stages 2 and 3, and audit and specify are new. The table also mislabels what two stages secure:
- An internal audit does not secure guests' common understanding.
- "Accountability, through exit" conflicts with the Bovens sense of accountability that the paper adopts.

Ordering by cost also puts accountability fourth, although "the other two cannot make up for a missing accountability" (Three conditions ¶1). Say whether the order is one of cost, dependency or priority, and state that stage 4 is not optional.

**5. Wilson-Nash and Table 1 point in different directions.** Table 1 offers "A staffed fallback" for identity verification. Wilson-Nash et al. (2026: 978), however, count an analogue route that is kept but downgraded as a trigger of captivity. Say what makes a fallback real (comparable price, time and dignity), which question 6 already approaches. The article also lists self-service hospitality check-ins among digital-only routes of access (2026: 974), which would anchor the check-in case.

**6. Does the obligation reach restaurants and bars?** The legal evidence concerns lodging, "while the shopkeeper remained free to refuse" (The host's power and the host's obligation ¶5). Yet restaurants are one of the paper's three formats, and Conclusion ¶2 calls the obligation "imposed by law on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers". State the basis for extending the account beyond lodging, or limit its scope. A related tension needs reconciling: the same section's ¶8 quotes the 1956 Act confining duties "to the proprietor of such an hotel", while ¶11 lets a brand hold the role.

**7. Signposting and interruptibility.** Introduction ¶4 says "The third section examines touchpoints in hotels, short-term rentals and restaurants". It does not say that this section, about 40 per cent of the body, holds the test, both tables and the roadmap. Preview its four subsections. Interruptibility, which I singled out in round one, is defined only in Designing ¶3 and is absent from the abstract and the conclusion. It is the touchpoint form of "standing exercised"; either say so, or drop it from the keywords.

**8. Positioning in the phygital literature.** The paper never defines "phygital". Introduction ¶2 says the phygital literature describes systems "without saying what the party that deploys them owes the guest". Yet Designing ¶2 cites Shabnam et al. (2026) for "routes of escalation and human oversight", and Batat (2024: 1238) warns against making a positive experience the aim. Present the contribution as extending a turn this literature has begun. The relay in Split ¶1 ("The movement can as accurately be called a relay") is the paper's freshest idea for this special issue and belongs in the conclusion.

## 5. Minor points

- **Pinpoints.** Morgan (2015) is cited at pp. 7–8 and 7 (Delegation and standing ¶2, ¶4), outside the article's pp. 109–139. Kaniadakis and Farmaki "2022: 14" (Split ¶2) does not match the reference (vol. 26, pp. 5763–5783). Crinkley is pinpointed by "paras", but cases reported in F.2d are cited by page.
- **Questions.** Two rhetorical questions remain in the body (The host's power and the host's obligation ¶9; Delegation and standing ¶4), plus one in a heading. The letter says one remains.
- **Wording.** In the opening paragraph of Assembled hosts at the touchpoint, "To keep with the theme" should read "In keeping with".
- **Conclusion ¶2.** The paper argues only the "sufficient" half of "neither necessary nor sufficient". Check "the Act lists no hospitality use as high-risk" against the biometric categories in Annex III.
- **Conclusion ¶5.** The claim about community well-being rests on De Vos et al. (2024), a study of an online MBA programme.
- **References.** Baum and Laux and Darwall each have a stray ", :". Brochado et al., Shabnam et al., Hatherley and Lin lack a volume or issue. Capitalization is inconsistent ("The New service metaphor", "jim crow").

## 6. Confidential comments to the editor

This is a genuine rebuild, and the response letter is accurate on most points. Where it overstates (the abstract's statement of the test, the surviving "framework", the rhetorical questions), the gaps are small. I recommend minor revisions but would want issues 1–4 resolved before acceptance, since they touch the paper's central term and its main practical contribution. Reviewer 3 is better placed than I am to judge the register, which now reads to me as suited to this journal.
