## Part 1. Rigour and argument, section by section

### Introduction

The introduction states an inference that the body never earns. Intro ¶3 (L22) says the duty stays with the holder "Because the law attaches the duty to the role, the work of hosting can pass". The paper's own authority on delegation rejects that step. Lord Sumption writes that "It does not, however, follow from the mere existence of a positive duty that it is personal to the defendant so as to make it non-delegable" (Woodland para 12). Finding 1 takes this up.

Intro ¶2 (L20) says that algorithmic-accountability research "keeps answerability with the party that delegates but ties it to harm". That is true of Kong (2026: 2, 5) but not of Nissenbaum. She would route answerability to "designers of the system, the human experts who served as sources, or the organization that chooses to put the system to use" (Nissenbaum card, preprint p. 11), and she does not single out the deployer. Finding 3 takes this up.

The scope sentence on machines in Intro ¶4 is sound and well sourced (Kropf et al. 2026: 7).

### The host's power and the host's obligation

The turn from motive to role in HO ¶4 is the paper's best idea, and HO ¶5 assembles its legal evidence carefully. Burdick (1911: 523), Wyman (1904: 159), Maese (2015: 501) and Bogen (1996: 52, 75) all match their cards. Six problems remain.

**(a) Bogen is widened by one word.** HO ¶5 (L36) reads "'English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment' that imposed them". The antecedent of "them" is the innkeeper's duties as a whole. Bogen's sentence concerns one duty: English law "imposed the duty to serve the public" (p. 75). [FIX, 0 words]

**(b) The paper reports only half of Bogen.** HO ¶9 (L44) calls the traveller's exposure "The most persuasive account we have found". It does not mention Bogen's own answer, though it cites him for dismissing holding out. Bogen holds that "concern for criminal activity in the inn, rather than any monopoly or innkeeper promise" produced the duty (p. 53), and that "The duty to serve the public was incidental to the innkeeper's liability for losses of his lodgers" (p. 91). A legal historian will ask why the paper's main legal history is cited for what it rejects and not for what it proposes. Bogen's thesis also bears on the argument. If the duty to receive grew out of a liability for goods, it is weak evidence for a duty of welcome. (Finding 6)

**(c) The sources describe undirected duties, and DS ¶6 needs directed ones.** HO ¶6 and ¶10 take the content of the host's duties from Pitt-Rivers and Sandoval-Strausz. Both deny that the guest held a matching right.

- Pitt-Rivers writes that "a diffuse obligation exists towards the beggar, he is not endowed with any corresponding right" (p. 509), and that the guest "has no right other than to respect" (p. 514).
- Sandoval-Strausz writes that "travelers enjoyed privileges and innkeepers bore obligations, but nobody possessed automatic entitlements" (p. 68), and that these "were not 'rights' in any proper sense of the term" (p. 66; also R2).

DS ¶6 then defines standing held as the guest's position "as the one to whom the host's directed duties are owed". In Thompson's vocabulary the sources describe monadic duties and the definition needs bipolar ones (Thompson 2004: 335, 338). The paper never argues the duties across that line. (Finding 2)

**(d) The comparators for distinctiveness are anti-discrimination rules.** HO ¶7 (L40) concedes that the mechanism "is not this mechanism, which is general". Two of its three comparators, however, are not duties to receive anyone. Equality Act s. 29(1) forbids a provider to "discriminate against a person requiring the service by not providing the person with the service". Payment Accounts Regulations reg. 18(1) likewise forbids discrimination in access to an account. Bogen adds that "The main significance of the right of access today is to protect against discrimination" (p. 91). So the general mechanism is anti-discrimination, and on Bogen's account the innkeeper's right of access now works mainly in the same way. What is distinctive about hospitality therefore has to be stated as breadth and duration. The innkeeper had to take any traveller, whatever the ground of refusal, subject only to room and fitness. And the duty ran through the stay, whereas EMTALA's duty ends at screening and stabilisation (§ 1395dd(a), (b)(1)). (Finding 10; partly also R2)

**(e) The trigger is two triggers.**

- The 1956 Act's s. 1(3) is a definition of "hotel" (card note). HO ¶8's paraphrase (L42) drops "without special contract", a phrase that supports the claim that the duty comes from status and not from contract.
- Maese separates two moments. Presenting is one: "If the guest thus 'presents himself' and is accepted, the relationship 'is instantly established'" (p. 501). The duty to receive applies when the guest presents; the duties of the stay begin when the guest is accepted.
- HO ¶8 merges the two moments in "The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers". Algorithmic screening before arrival falls on the first moment only (also R2, point 4). (Finding 8)

**(f) The Act already creates a special legal category.** HO ¶9 says the law does not place "today's hotels in any special legal category". Yet s. 1(1) deems a hotel an inn, and "any other establishment shall not". (Also R2)

**(g) A brand is not the statutory holder, and franchising splits the trigger.** HO ¶11 (L48) adds "or the brand under which it trades" to the list of possible holders, although s. 1(1) confines the duties to "the proprietor of such an hotel" (also R1, R2). More basically, the trigger is conjunctive. On Bogen's reading of a 1623 report, a man became liable for refusing shelter "simply by putting up a sign and lodging travelers" (p. 88). Franchising divides those two acts: the brand puts up the sign and the operator lodges the travellers. (Finding 3)

**(h) The conditional guest has a cost the paper does not count.** HO ¶10 is right that the guest's side is "stated and conditional". But if a guest who fails those conditions loses the relation (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 517; s. 1(3), "in a fit state to be received"), then an algorithm that decides whether they are met decides whether the guest still holds standing at all. That cuts against DS ¶6. (Finding 5; partly also R3)

- The four terms do not hold up later in the paper. (Finding 7)

DS ¶3 (L56) describes Crinkley accurately: the brand's liability was vicarious and the reliance "marginal" (card paras 49–52). The allocation that follows takes more from its sources than they give. (Finding 3)

DS ¶4 (L58) carries the slogan "The work can be delegated, and the duty cannot." (Finding 1)

DS ¶5 (L60) has three problems.

- It quotes Woodland para 24 accurately, but that passage concedes a control ground.
- It reports Kong's conclusion and not his denial that roles ground anything.
- It narrows Kropf et al.'s list of accountable humans. (Findings 3 and 6)

DS ¶6 (L62) removes the circularity. It does so at the cost of a clash with the legal meaning of 'standing' and two misdescribed sources. (Finding 5)

DS ¶7–¶9 (L64–68) are where the hearing is supposed to be earned. The two cases in DS ¶9 are descriptive, and the paragraph's "therefore" is not earned (also R2, R3). (Finding 2)

DS ¶8 (L66) on competence is the paragraph the argument needs least. It is the first place to find words.

### Assembled hosts at the touchpoint

**AH ¶1, placed guests.** I disagree in part with R1, R2 and R3 that the role account cannot reach placed guests. The structure the paper borrows from Woodland fits Petie et al.'s placed asylum seekers better than it fits a paying hotel guest. Woodland's category needs:

- a relationship that "places the claimant in the actual custody, charge or care of the defendant" (para 23(2)); and
- a claimant who "has no control over how the defendant chooses to perform those obligations" (para 23(3)).

The hotel resident placed by an agency matches both conditions more closely than a business traveller does. The cost is that the holder would then be the party that placed the resident, not the hotel. This is a [CONSIDER] for the scope paragraph. Whether English law imposes such a duty on the placing body is **VERIFY**.

**Split ¶4 (L82), "Discretion acts on the single case and authority on the rule".** This sentence gives authority a second sense (also R1, R3). Woodland adds a third: authority over the person in one's charge ("That authority confers on them a significant degree of control", para 25(1)). The paper is free to stipulate a sense, but it should say that the legal source it leans on uses another.

**Test ¶1 (L86).** The test's central condition is named after Okhuysen and Bechky. For them, accountability "addresses the question of who is responsible for specific elements of the task" (p. 483). That is the paper's *responsibility* (DS ¶2), not its *accountability*. The paragraph then derives the condition "from the duty to answer through someone with authority", but the bundle of duties in HO ¶6 contains no duty to answer (also R2, R3). (Findings 2 and 7)

**Reachable ¶4 (L123).** The paragraph misplaces the GDPR safeguard. (Finding 9)

**Design ¶4 and Table 2 (L133, L142).** They call the fourth stage accountability in Bovens' sense and then ask for it "in real time". (Finding 4)

### Conclusion

- **Conclusion ¶2 (L149)** restates the allocation as established ("held alongside whoever controls the operation"; also R2). It also says the obligation is "imposed by law on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers". The law in fact imposes it on the proprietor of a hotel, and on no one else (s. 1(1)). The sentence on the AI Act needs one correction. (Finding 11)
- **Conclusion ¶3 (L151)** calls the legal sources "English". The 1956 Act extends to England, Wales and Scotland (card, "extent E+W+S"). The Equality Act section cited is described in the paper itself as applying in Great Britain, and the Payment Accounts Regulations are UK law (also R3).
- **Limit 3** states the Woodland caveat well. The caveat the paper actually needs is wider, because it concerns which duties are non-delegable at all. (Finding 1)
- **The third study** in Conclusion ¶4 (L153) is exactly right. Mapping how management, franchise and platform agreements divide duties to guests is how to test the allocation proposal.

## The read: findings ranked by damage

### 1. The central slogan is either trivial or an exception the paper does not qualify for (extends R2) [CONSIDER] for the route, [FIX] for the wording

**What the paper claims.** "The work can be delegated, and the duty cannot" (DS ¶4, L58; also in the Abstract, Intro ¶3 and Conclusion ¶2). Intro ¶3 says this follows "Because the law attaches the duty to the role".

**What fails.** On the paper's own legal sources, the sentence is either true of every duty or true only of an exceptional class.

- *True of every duty.* Morgan: "No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be" (AM pdf p. 7). On that reading the slogan holds for any duty whatever.
- *The question that matters is what the duty requires.* The ordinary duty is to take care oneself, and a host meets it by choosing a competent contractor. The exceptional duty "extends beyond being careful, to procuring the careful performance of work delegated to others". It arises only where "the ordinary principle is displaced" (Woodland para 5).
- *A positive duty does not qualify just by existing* (Woodland para 12, quoted above).
- *The paper does not show that the host's duty qualifies.* It points to Woodland's custody and vulnerability criteria, which R2 showed fit an adult hotel guest poorly, and then disclaims the category (DS ¶4; Conclusion ¶3, limit 3).

**Why it matters.** Suppose the host's duty is the ordinary one. Then a hotel that chose a competent pricing or check-in vendor has done its duty, and the guest's claim runs, if anywhere, against the vendor. That is the reverse of the thesis.

**A related contradiction in DS ¶5.** DS ¶5 (L60) separates the paper from Kong on the ground that "it is not a responsibility for harm that a delegated system might cause". But the doctrine the paper borrows works through harm. Morgan restates the non-delegable duty as one where the defendant "must answer for any harm negligently caused" (AM pdf p. 8). The contrast with Kong survives for two duties only:

- the duty to receive, which a refusal breaches and which the refused traveller could sue on without any injury ("an Action will lie against him", Lane v. Cotton 1701: 484, Holt CJ dissenting);
- attending, which no law carries.

**A better route, already in the sources.**

- *Contract.* Morgan writes that "A contractual 'duty of care' is usually interpreted as one to see that care is taken (by whoever actually provides the relevant service)" (AM pdf p. 7). Woodland para 7 says a contracting party "will normally be taken to contract that the work will be done carefully by whomever he may get to do it". The paper already quotes para 25(5) on fee-paying schools. A paying guest is a contracting party.
- *The innkeeper's own history.* The innkeeper answered for "a default in him or his servants" (Calye fol. 33a, already in DS ¶4) and "for the acts or negligence of his servants, but also those of other guests or any unidentified party" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 65). Calye confines that strict liability to goods "and not his person" (fol. 33b). Whether Sandoval-Strausz's p. 65 also concerns goods is **VERIFY**.

**Fix.**

- Replace the slogan's three restatements with one qualified sentence (rewrites 1 and 2).
- Rest non-delegability on the contract and on the innkeeper's answering for his servants, and use Woodland only for its ground (para 24).
- Restate the contrast with Kong for the duty to receive.
- Cost: about +60 words, less about 25 saved from the restatements.

### 2. From duty to a hearing (also R2, R3), and how it can be made good [CONSIDER]

I agree with R2 and R3 that the step is asserted. Two additions.

**The obvious repair is closed.** One might derive the hearing from the duty to attend. But Pitt-Rivers' attending ("show concern for their needs and wishes", p. 516) is, on his own account, a diffuse obligation with no corresponding right (pp. 509, 514). In Thompson's terms it is monadic, not bipolar. Thompson's example is Rawls's promisor, against whom "All alike are positioned to charge" (Thompson 2004: 339). So attending cannot ground the guest's standing.

**The move can still be made, in a narrower form, from the paper's own sources.**

1. *Directedness from the traveller's own action.* Several sources record that the refused traveller could sue:
   - "If an Inn-keeper refuse to entertain a Guest, where his House is not full, an Action will lie against him" (Lane v. Cotton 1701: 484, Holt CJ dissenting);
   - a man who put up a sign and lodged travellers became "liable to an action on the case for refusing someone shelter" (Bogen 1996: 88, paraphrasing a 1623 report);
   - innkeepers were bound by "the private law of damages on suit" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 64).

   In private law only the person who holds the right may sue to enforce it (Liau, Introduction, eprint p. 5; **VERIFY**, book unread). The traveller's own action therefore marks the legal duties as directed to the traveller. That answers Sandoval-Strausz's "not 'rights'" for the legal duties, though not for attending.
2. *The hearing as the guest's power.* Hohfeld's pairing of power and liability (1913: 30) gives the hearing its shape. The guest holds a power, and using it puts the holder under a duty to reconsider. EU law already gives that shape to automated decisions. Article 22(3) secures "the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision". The review must come from someone with "the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision" (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). That is "a hearing by someone with authority" in positive law.
3. *The authors' own step, stated as theirs.* A holder that arranges its operation so that no one can receive the guest's claim leaves the guest's power with nothing to act on. Darwall's diary apology, which is no apology because no one receives it (2013: 31), shows why using a claim needs someone to receive it. The Darwall card warns, rightly, that the requirement of a reachable addressee is the paper's own claim, not Darwall's. The paper should say so.
4. *Timing that follows from what the duty is.* Damages paid later do not undo a refusal at night. For the duty to receive, then, the hearing has to come before the refusal takes effect. That grounds Table 1's top row in the host's duty itself, which Baum and Laux's criterion alone cannot do.

**What the paper must concede.** The hearing follows from the legal duties (to receive and to protect) and from the contract, not from attending. It is owed where those duties reach (Finding 8).

**Cost.** About 150 words, offset by cutting DS ¶8 (about 75). See "Best spend".

### 3. The allocation among firms rests on misread or contrary sources (extends R2) [FIX] for (b)–(d), [CONSIDER] for the reframing

**What the paper claims.** "a party which only arranges a service … such as a technology vendor, answers to the host"; and "no court has held this" (DS ¶3, L56). Conclusion ¶2 restates it as argued.

**(a) Woodland's arranger is a possible duty-holder, not the delegate.** Para 25(3) excuses a defendant whose "own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance". Para 24's example is the Ministry of Defence, which made "no delegation of any function which the Ministry had assumed personal responsibility to carry out". A vendor whose system prices, verifies and allocates is doing the work. In Woodland's frame it stands where the swimming teacher stood, as the contractor; it is not an arranger. Read correctly, 25(3) fits a brand or platform that only arranges bookings, and it excuses that party (also R2).

**(b) The paper's own sources on algorithmic accountability reach vendors.**

- Nissenbaum lists "designers of the system" first (preprint p. 11) and holds that "each may be held equally and fully answerable" (p. 14).
- Kropf et al. name "the programmers, the manufacturers (or responsible persons), or other human beings" (p. 9). DS ¶5 narrows this to "the humans who deploy it".
- Cobbe et al. want accountability allocated "based on a proper understanding of their technological and political economic dynamics" (p. 1193). They criticise a scheme in which "the actor who developed and controls that technology … would face no obligations" (p. 1193).

As written, the vendor tier rebuilds the arrangement Cobbe et al. criticise.

**(c) Legislation already qualifies "no court has held this".** Cobbe et al. report that "AI service customers are likely data controllers (the dominant party, ultimately responsible for compliance and accountability), while providers may be data processors". They add that "the factual situation outweighs contractual or other arrangements" (p. 1193). That is a legal allocation close to the paper's, with a limit attached. Citing it turns an absence claim into a precedent.

**(d) Crinkley's own footnote 3 records Hayman v. Ramada Inn.** There "a divided panel" rejected "a comparable apparent agency claim against a motel franchisor". "A single decision under North Carolina law" should say that the court was federal and that the state court nearest in time had gone the other way (rewrite 4).

**(e) The trigger is split, not shared.** Holding out and lodging are conjunctive (Bogen 1996: 88; 1956 Act s. 1(3)), and franchising divides them between brand and operator. That is why the paper needs "alongside". But "alongside" is then a decision about how to treat a split trigger, not something the trigger yields. Say so.

**Reframing.** State the allocation as a rule about the guest's first address: whom the guest may approach and must be heard by. Do not state it as a rule about where all answerability lies. That keeps the thesis, which is about the path to authority. It also leaves untouched any duty a vendor owes the guest in tort or in data protection.

**Cost.** About 40 words for (b)–(d) and about 30 for the reframing.

### 4. "Accountability in Bovens' sense" cannot be had "in real time" (extends R3; R1, R2 on stage 5) [FIX]

**What the paper claims.**

- DS ¶2 (L54) defines accountability "in Bovens' terms".
- Design ¶4 (L133) says "The fourth stage constitutes accountability in Bovens' sense".
- Table 2 asks for that stage "in real time where Table 1 requires it" (L142).

**What fails.**

- *Bovens' accountability is retrospective.* He writes that "accountability, as defined here, is in nature retrospective. Actors are to account to a forum after the fact", and that responsiveness and participation "do not constitute accountability" (p. 453). By the paper's chosen source, the recourse in Table 1's top two rows is not accountability at all.
- *The roles shift between DS ¶2 and Design ¶4.* DS ¶2 makes the guest the forum, since the holder answers to the guest. Design ¶4 makes the forum "the place where a guest's claim can be lodged" and the actor the manager or brand (also R3).
- *The guest is the weak kind of forum.* In Bovens the forum questions and judges, and it can "block or amend decisions made by the actor" (p. 452). A guest acting as forum is the case he doubts: client forums often lack "the possibility of judgement and sanctioning" (p. 457).
- *DS ¶2 leaves out consequences,* the element of Bovens' Box 1 (p. 452) that would carry the "authority to change the decision" the paper wants.

**Fix.** Give the recourse that must come before a decision takes effect its own name. The paper already has one: interruptibility (Design ¶3, L131). Keep 'accountability' for the account given afterwards (rewrites 3 and 11). Add consequences to DS ¶2. Say once that applying a framework built for public bodies to a firm and its guests is the authors' extension (the card notes Bovens' scope is public authority).

**Cost.** About 40 words. This also helps R1, who asked that interruptibility do more work.

### 5. 'Standing' clashes with its legal meaning, and standing held is not safe from algorithms (extends R2; partly R3) [FIX]

**(a) The legal meaning of 'standing' is the paper's 'standing exercised'.**

- Liau: standing is "conceived as a power to hold another accountable before a court" (eprint p. 1), and "Only the primary right-holder has the standing (a power) to sue" (p. 5).
- Darwall uses 'standing' in the same legal sense when he says the "legal authority or standing to bring cases" lies "uniquely up to a(n alleged) victim" (2013: 31).

The paper's "standing held" is what a lawyer calls the right. Its "standing exercised" is what a lawyer calls standing. A referee trained in law will read "the guest still holds standing" (L62) as a claim about the power to sue, and will think it false for many guests. One sentence fixes this (rewrite 9). Liau has been read only in its Introduction (**VERIFY**).

**(b) Two sources are misdescribed in DS ¶6.**

- Darwall's p. 36 glosses second-personal authority in general, not "the authority of an obligee" (the card notes it also covers representative authority). The statement specific to obligees is on pp. 31–32 (rewrite 8).
- "Thompson's forms of directed duty" includes "X has a right against Y". That is a form of right, and in it X holds the right, so the two quoted forms swap roles. Thompson calls them "forms of bipolar normativity" (p. 335) (rewrite 9).
- R2 has already flagged the "Even so" that follows Darwall's p. 35 (also R2).

**(c) Delegation can end standing held, not just its exercise.** DS ¶6 says "what can fail is its exercise". But HO ¶10 makes the guest's standing conditional. A fraud score that refuses a booking, or a sensor alert that triggers removal, decides whether the guest has met the conditions, and so whether standing held exists at all. Those are Table 1's top rows. Add one sentence saying so.

**Cost.** About 50 words.

### 6. The ground of the role obligation is plural and undefended against Kong (extends R2, point 6) [CONSIDER]; Hardimon [CARRY FORWARD]

**What fails.** The paper offers four grounds and does not choose among them:

- positive law (HO ¶5);
- holding out (HO ¶8, ¶11);
- the traveller's exposure (HO ¶9);
- Woodland's "control over the claimant for the purpose of performing a function", with para 11's assumption of responsibility (DS ¶5).

In a franchised hotel run on vendors' systems, these grounds pick different holders:

| Ground | Holder it picks |
|---|---|
| Holding out | The brand |
| Control over the guest | The operator |
| Assumption of responsibility | Whoever undertook the duty, usually the party to the contract |
| The traveller's exposure | Whoever has the traveller in its house |

R2.1 asked "who actually holds it". The answer depends on the ground.

**Kong is the rival who matters here.** He argues that "A role matters derivatively rather than intrinsically", and that "Roles are not an alternative to control-based responsibility; they are how it is distributed across a division of labour" (Kong 2026: 4). DS ¶5 reports Kong's conclusion and his control ground, and then adopts Woodland's ground of control over the claimant. That is also a control ground. The paper is closer to Kong than it admits. Its claim that the role itself grounds the duty (HO ¶4–¶5) goes undefended against the one source in its reference list that denies it. Kong also separates being answerable from being held answerable (p. 4), and he asks governance "to hold that addressee in view" (p. 13). That is the paper's diagnosis from the other end, and DS ¶5 should credit it.

**Positioning.**

- Pitt-Rivers already treats host and guest as roles: "this reciprocity does not obscure the distinction between the roles" (p. 513). That qualifies HO ¶4's claim that "The debate, on both sides, turns on the host's motive" (also R2).
- Blain and Lashley report that Telfer lists "allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable" among the motives of hospitableness (2014: 2, citing Telfer p. 42; secondary, **VERIFY**). So the field already knows duty, but as a motive. The paper's contribution is duty without motive, and HO ¶4 is sharper if it says so.

**Fix.** Choose a ground. I recommend Woodland's assumption of responsibility (para 11), read through the traveller's exposure (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). That reading also gives Woodland's vulnerability criterion a form that fits hospitality. Then treat holding out as the trigger only, as HO ¶9 already half says. Cost: about 60 words.

The standard philosophical reference on role obligations, Hardimon (1994), is carded at metadata depth only and must be read before it is cited. Kropf et al. give a definition the paper can cite now: "An actor (A) is responsible for an action (X) if and only if X belongs to the duties (D) that A has to fulfill due to an assumed role (R)" (p. 3). It comes by way of Vincent and Hart (p. 2).

### 7. The four terms of DS ¶2 do not survive into the rest of the paper (extends R1, R2, R3) [FIX]

- 'Responsibility' is never used again in its defined sense. Its later uses belong to Kong, Germann Molz and Cobbe et al.
- The test's central condition is called 'accountability' after Okhuysen and Bechky, whose accountability is responsibility for parts of a task (p. 483). That is the paper's 'responsibility'.
- 'Answerability', which names the split, is never defined (also R1).
- 'Authority' has two senses (also R1, R3).

**Fix.** Adopt Kong's definition of answerability: being "the correct address for the question 'why did this happen, and what have you to say for it?'" (p. 3). Either add it as a fifth term or let it take responsibility's place. The thesis then reads cleanly. Answerability stays with the holder. Accountability and interruptibility are arrangements that can be missing. That also settles R2's third new issue, which says 'answering' has two senses. Cost: about 30 words.

### 8. The paper's own law fixes its scope (also R1, R2, R3) [CONSIDER]

Four points from the paper's own sources settle how far the role account reaches.

- **Restaurants.** The 1956 Act, on which the paper relies for its trigger and its holder, says "any other establishment shall not, be deemed to be an inn" (s. 1(1)). By the Act's own definition, a restaurant without beds is not a hotel.
- **Short-term rentals.** Burdick, quoting Schouler, describes the boarding-house keeper who "reserves the choice of comers and the terms of accommodation, contracting specially with each customer" (1911: 524 n. 45). A short-term-rental host who accepts or declines requests (Bialski's "power to choose", Intro ¶2) stands in the boarding-house keeper's place, not the innkeeper's.
- **"Without special contract."** This phrase in s. 1(3), which the paraphrase in HO ¶8 drops, points the same way. Whether it excludes booking-only or app-only hotels from the Act is **VERIFY**.
- **Pre-arrival decisions.** Maese's two moments (presenting, then being accepted, p. 501) place screening before arrival under the duty to receive, if under anything (R2, point 4).

These limits are sharper than the Civil Rights Act exemption that Reachable ¶4 uses (also R2).

**Fix.** State what the law reaches: lodging that holds itself out to any traveller. Argue any extension to restaurants and to hosts who select their guests as an analogy, or limit the test to what the law reaches. Placed guests are discussed under AH ¶1 in Part 1. Cost: about 50 words.

### 9. The GDPR floor is higher than Reachable ¶4 says [FIX]

**What the paper claims.** "many hotel decisions may fall within the exception for decisions necessary to a contract", and "The law therefore secures a later review, not a person at the threshold" (L123).

**What fails.**

- *The exception carries the safeguard.* Article 22(3) attaches the right to human intervention to the contract exception itself ("in cases (a) and (c)"). Falling within the exception does not remove the right; it is the condition under which the right applies.
- *Outside the exception, the decision is prohibited.* The Working Party reads Article 22(1) as "a general prohibition … whether or not the data subject takes an action" (p. 19).
- *Necessity is a real test.* "If other effective and less intrusive means to achieve the same goal exist, then it would not be 'necessary'" (p. 23).

So a significant decision that fails the necessity test may not be left to the system alone. That means a person at the threshold.

**Cost.** About 25 words (rewrite 10).

Note Bogen's p. 91 remark that the right of access today works mainly against discrimination. Cost: about 30 words. Compressing the comparator sentences recovers most of it.

## Where it is strong

These must survive any revision.

- **The turn from motive to role (HO ¶4–¶5).** It is the paper's real contribution to this journal. The legal evidence behind it is quoted accurately.
- **The honesty of the legal borrowing.** "We borrow its structure without claiming…" (DS ¶4) and "no court has held this" (DS ¶3) are the right instincts. The fixes above extend that honesty; they do not reverse it.
- **The Woodland para 24 quotation** in DS ¶5 (L60: "not on 'control of the environment' but on 'control over the claimant"). It is the correct answer to anyone who would ground the host's duty in owning the building, and it marks a real difference from approaches built on owning or occupying premises.
- **The distinction between holding standing and exercising it.** The idea is right, and it is the one a lawyer would recognise once the terms are aligned (Finding 5).
- **The criterion of the reachable party with authority**, tied to the Working Party's "authority and capability to change the decision" (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). This is the paper's practical core, and positive law already supports it.
- **The conditional guest (HO ¶10).** It keeps the paper from sentimentalising the guest, and it should be used in Table 1.
- **The accurate account of Crinkley.** The brand's liability was vicarious and the reliance marginal. Few conceptual papers would have read the opinion this closely.

## Best spend: 150 of the roughly 600 spare words

*Suggested text, drafted by reviewer 05. It is not the authors' wording; replace it in your own voice.* Place it at the end of DS ¶9, replacing that paragraph's last sentence, and pay for it by cutting DS ¶8 (about 75 words).

> The innkeeper's duty to receive was owed to the traveller and not only to the public, since a refused traveller could bring an action of his own (Lane v. Cotton 1701: 484, Holt CJ dissenting; Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 64). The guest held a claim and the power to press it. European law has given that power a modern form for automated decisions: a person may obtain human intervention, put a point of view and contest the decision (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 22(3)), from someone with 'the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision' (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). The next step is ours, not the law's. A holder that arranges its operation so that no one can receive the guest's claim leaves that power with nothing to act on. Where a refusal would leave a guest without shelter for the night, the hearing must come before the refusal takes effect.

This passage does four things:

- it shows that the legal duty was owed to the traveller (Finding 2, step 1);
- it gives the hearing a legal template (step 2);
- it marks the step that requires a reachable addressee as the authors' own (step 3);
- it grounds Table 1's top row in the duty to receive (step 4).

It also stops relying on Lugosi and on two descriptive cases for a normative conclusion.

---

## Verdict

**Major revision.** The paper's architecture is sound and worth keeping. But its three passages in my field each take more from their sources than the sources give:

- the slogan (Finding 1);
- the hearing (Finding 2);
- the allocation among firms (Finding 3).

A referee trained in law or moral philosophy will find each of these from the cards alone.

**The single most important fix.** Say which of the host's duties stays with the holder when the work is handed on, and why. The reasons should be the contract and the innkeeper's answering for his servants, not Woodland's custody category. Then derive the hearing from the guest's power to press those legal duties, and mark the step requiring a reachable addressee as the authors' own.
