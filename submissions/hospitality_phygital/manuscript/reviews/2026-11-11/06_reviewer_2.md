# Panel review 06: Reviewer 2 (anonymous, unimpressed)

**Recommendation: major revisions.** The first half of this paper makes a real move: it takes the host's obligation out of the host's motive and puts it in a role. The second half, the test, the two tables and the roadmap, is contestability-by-design applied to hotels. It does not say so, it mischaracterizes the literature it borrows from, and the role account never reaches the instrument it is supposed to ground. A second-round referee who works on algorithmic accountability will see this in an afternoon.

## Part 1. Rigour and argument

### What the paper has already answered, which I will not re-raise

The paper concedes that it is conceptual and its cases illustrative (AH ¶1, L72; Conclusion ¶3, L151), that the mechanism is general (HO ¶7, L40), and that Woodland concerns schools and hospitals (DS ¶4, L58). It sets aside whether a machine can be a host (Introduction ¶4, L24). Those answers are adequate. My objections start where they stop.

### On the converging findings of R1–R3

I agree with all nine. Five are understated, and I add to them below: Bovens makes finding 1 worse (Design); the paper never runs the test of finding 3 (Test); holding out excludes a third group beyond finding 4 (HO); the roadmap of finding 5 predates the theory (Design); and holding out (finding 6) does no work anywhere in the application. To finding 7 I add the letter's answer to the journal's R2.2 (Letter). Finding 8 is outside my lane; to finding 9 I add a different tell (Part 2).

### Introduction

The thesis is phrased as a frequency claim the paper has no data to support. The abstract (L12) says what mediation removes 'is rarely the host's obligation and more often the guest's path'; Introduction ¶3 (L22) says 'algorithmic mediation rarely removes the host's obligation'. 'Rarely' and 'more often' are counts. A conceptual paper can say what mediation *can* remove and what it leaves in place; it cannot say how often. The fix costs no words.

Introduction ¶2 (L20) sets up the novelty claim on a characterization of algorithmic accountability that the paper's own reference list refutes. It says that 'Algorithmic-accountability research keeps answerability with the party that delegates but ties it to harm' (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5). Three of the paper's own sources contradict the second half:

- **Bovens (2007)**, whom the paper adopts for accountability, defines it as a relation in which an actor explains and justifies 'his or her conduct' to a forum (p. 450). Conduct, not harm.
- **The Article 29 Working Party (2018)**, cited at Reachable ¶4, reads Article 22(1) as 'a general prohibition' that 'applies whether or not the data subject takes an action' (p. 19). It asks for a reviewer 'who has the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision' (p. 27). No harm trigger there either.
- **Lyons et al. (2021)**, cited at Design ¶2, set out contestability's 'policy preliminaries': 'what can be contested, who is allowed to contest, who is held accountable for decisions, and the type of review' (pdf p. 12, §5.1). That is a decision-centred programme, not a harm-centred one.

The first half fails too. On the card's full-text quotations, Nissenbaum does not keep answerability with the delegating party. She names three candidates, 'designers of the system, the human experts who served as sources, or the organization that chooses to put the system to use' (pdf p. 11), and warns that 'unless alternate lines of accountability are worked out, accountability … will be lost' (pdf p. 11). That is the opposite of the default persistence the paper claims, and DS ¶5 (L60) repeats the misreading ('Writers on algorithmic accountability already hold that deploying an autonomous system does not lessen'). Kong alone supports the sentence, and Kong indexes answerability to 'foreseeable types' of outcome (p. 5). One author does not make a field.

### The host's power and the host's obligation

This is the strongest section, and the move from motive to role (HO ¶4–5, L34–36) is the paper's one original contribution to this journal's debate. Three problems sit in it.

**Holding out does no allocating in the paper's own statute.** HO ¶8 (L42) says 'The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers'. Section 1(3) of the Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 is a definition of 'hotel': an establishment 'held out by the proprietor' as offering food, drink and accommodation 'to any traveller presenting himself' (s. 1(3)). Section 1(1) then fixes the holder as 'the proprietor of such an hotel' and says the duties 'shall not attach to any other person'. Holding out picks out which establishments count; proprietorship picks out who holds. The paper uses the first to do the second's job. (Also R2, on the Act against the brand.)

**Holding out excludes a third group the referees missed.** The statutory trigger is an offer to 'any traveller presenting himself'. A short-term-rental host who screens and accepts guests makes no such offer, which is the position of the shopkeeper who 'remained free to refuse' (HO ¶5, L36). The paper's own Bialski quotation describes exactly that host: profiles gave hosts 'the power to choose one member over the other' (Introduction ¶2, L20). So the trigger leaves out not only restaurants and placed guests (converging finding 4) but the selective rental host, one of the paper's three formats. The paper should say whether a host who chooses guests holds itself out, and if so on what test.

**'Public calling' contradicts HO ¶9.** HO ¶9 (L44) declines to say the law places 'today's hotels in any special legal category'. Conclusion ¶2 (L149) then frames the role account as asking what 'the holder of a public calling owes the person it has received'. The phrase appears nowhere else in the body, and Bogen, whom the paper relies on, writes that 'the idea of a public calling, was a product of duties imposed rather than a cause of them' (1996: 52). Cut it.

### Delegation and standing

**The distinction from Kong does not survive Kong's p. 4.** DS ¶5 (L60) agrees with Kong's conclusion and claims a different ground: a duty owed 'from the moment of reception', resting on Woodland's 'control over the claimant' rather than 'control of the environment' (para 24). Kong anticipates the move. He allows answerability for one who 'occupied a role, undertook a task, or created a risk' (p. 3), then argues that a role 'bundles competence, control, and epistemic access, and assuming it is itself an exercise of control' (p. 4). The paper's trigger, holding out, is a voluntary act of assuming a role, so on Kong's account it is control under another name. Woodland's ground is control too, of the claimant, and Kong's is control over the act of delegating, not of the environment, so the Woodland contrast misses him. The paper needs one sentence that answers p. 4: either the role is not reducible to control (say why), or the paper's novelty is the content and timing of the duty, not its ground.

**The content difference then disappears at the instrument.** The real difference from Kong is content: a first-order duty owed 'whether or not anything goes wrong' (L60). But the test is dispute-triggered. Question 5 asks 'Who must answer if it is disputed?' (L94), and the conditions concern whether a guest can press a claim. Nothing in the six questions or either table asks whether the first-order duties (receive, sustain, protect, attend) were performed. The instrument tests the same thing Kong, the Working Party and the contestability literature test: whether a contested decision reaches someone who can change it. So the one thing that distinguishes the theory never enters the tool.

**Standing held does no work after the paragraph that defines it.** 'Standing held' and 'standing exercised' each appear once in the body, both in DS ¶6 (L62). Standing held is defined as the guest's position as the one to whom the duties are owed, so it exists exactly when the obligation does (also R2: 'true by definition'). The distinction itself is Darwall's, whom the paper quotes making it: a right holder 'need not actually claim her right' (2013: 35). Kong draws the same line on the holder's side, between being answerable and being held answerable (p. 4).

**Authority is stipulated to avoid a literature, including hospitality's own.** DS ¶2 (L54) defines authority in 'a sense we stipulate because the word carries technical meanings elsewhere'. The response to the journal's R2.2, which feared the paper would look like 'a more familiar problem of organizational delegation', should engage that literature, not stipulate past it. The nearest part of it is in hospitality. Hales and Klidas (1998) found that hotel 'empowerment' was framed as 'choice', discretion over how work is done, rather than 'voice' in decisions, and that there was 'little "empowerment" in practice'. Hewagama et al. (2019) found that service recovery in thirty Sri Lankan hotels is 'carefully stage-managed and "staircased"', with empowerment 'deliberately graduated according to rank'. Both are held in the library at abstract depth only. Choice against voice is the paper's discretion against authority, and the staircase is its graded path. Split ¶3 (L80), 'Authority in commercial hospitality has long been graded and organizational', currently rests on one manager's remark in Golubovskaya et al., the commercial home and Crinkley.

### Assembled hosts at the touchpoint

**The paper's empirical premise has no algorithmic instance.** Where a guest's path to authority actually fails, the cause is not algorithmic: Petie et al.'s asylum hotels fail through outsourcing among agencies (AH ¶1, L72), and Germann Molz (2018) documents a platform's legal posture. The algorithmic cases show authority present or say nothing about reach. In Garcia et al. managers 'retain the final decision right' (card, WP p. 5; also R1, R3), and Bosma's rental is hedged: 'no one with authority may be reachable' (Test ¶4, L99). Bosma's evidence is stronger than the paper uses: Airbnb requires self-check-in for listings to qualify for business travellers (2022: 605; also R3), which makes the platform the party with authority over the rule. No cited study shows a guest who could not reach authority because a system decided. A conceptual paper can live with that if Conclusion ¶4 says so in those words; study 2 is the right study to fix it.

**Split ¶4 (L82) makes three sources carry claims they do not hold.**

- Rahman (2021) and Calo and Rosenblat (2017) are cited for a system that holds 'a guest's history, preferences, spending and loyalty status'. Rahman studies freelancers' ratings on a labour platform. His knowledge asymmetry is about workers, whom the algorithm purports to 'know' in ways 'inaccessible to them' (p. 977). The Calo and Rosenblat card holds the abstract only, on Uber. Neither speaks to guests or loyalty data.
- Scott and Orlikowski (2012) are cited for authority over the rule drifting 'towards whoever designs, configures or controls it'. The card's quotation says something else: in travel, 'user-generated content subverts the previous hierarchical form of accountability in which complaints would be predominantly internal and dealt with through vertical lines of authority'.

Read correctly, that last source is a counter-case the paper never meets: the public review. Online reviews are the most visible new route a guest has to judgement and consequences in phygital hospitality, and Germann Molz (2018: 237) shows how much they weigh: hosts 'have to have a stellar reputation'. Reviews are arguably nearer to a Bovens forum than a front desk is. The paper can answer this. Bovens notes that clients as forums often lack 'judgement and sanctioning' (p. 457), and a review reaches the public, not anyone who can change the single decision in time. But it has to say so, or a referee will say the path was redistributed, not removed.

**The six questions are checkable in principle for half of them and not at all for the rest.** Take them in turn:

- **Q1** ('What rule or actor determined the outcome?') often has no answer on the paper's own source. Cobbe et al. find 'typically no one actor in overall control' of a supply chain (2023: 1192), and that 'no actor will have sufficient knowledge of or control over both production and deployment' (p. 1194). Yet Table 2 prices stage 1, an audit with these questions, at 'Attention'.
- **Q2** is two questions. 'Could the guest have anticipated it' is a counterfactual that needs guest data. 'Is the guest told when it changes' is observable from documents.
- **Q3, Q4 and Q6** can be answered from contracts, organization charts and price lists. They are the checkable core.
- **Q5** is answered by the theory, not the touchpoint: the holder must answer (also R2). No question asks whether the guest can reach that party in time (also R1).
- **Unit and decision rule.** The test examines 'a touchpoint' (Test ¶1, L86), but Table 1 classifies decisions, and one check-in touchpoint carries several (identity, room, upsell price). Nothing says who answers the questions, on what evidence, or what counts as passing (also R1, R2, R3 on the decision rule).

**The paper never runs its own test.** No case in Test ¶4 (L99) is taken through the six questions in order, and question 6, the cost of refusing, is put to none of them. The one case that 'passes' is outside the paper's scope. The Introduction defines an algorithmic system as 'software that takes or shapes a decision about a guest' (L18). Le Petit Chef projects an animated chef onto the table and decides nothing about anyone. And the verdict rests on rules that 'stay steady' and staff who 'keep their discretion' (L99). The card's quotations from the study show staff present and smiling (pp. 7–9) and nothing about rules or discretion; the card's own note says the study does not address them (VERIFY). So the paper's only passing case passes a touchpoint the test cannot examine, on facts the source does not appear to report. Swap it for a case with a decision in it. R3's suggestion, Xiang et al.'s guest without a test report re-set in an app-only hotel, would serve as the failing twin of a passing staffed case.

**Only one of the three conditions comes from a listed duty.** Test ¶1 (L86) says the conditions take their content from the host's duties. Common understanding comes from the duty to attend, which is on the list. Predictability comes 'from terms held steady enough to be negotiated', which is none of receive, sustain, protect or attend. Accountability comes 'from the duty to answer', which R2 and R3 already show is not on the list. One of three is derived; two are asserted.

### Designing for interruptible hospitality

**The roadmap was attached, and the round-one reports show it.** The journal's Reviewer 1 summarized the first submission as offering 'a meso-level 5-stage roadmap', and Reviewer 3 queried its 'roadmap of rising commitment'. The theory of role obligation entered only after the journal's Reviewer 2 asked for it (R2.2). The roadmap therefore predates the argument it is now said to follow from, and the text shows the join:

- Holding out and the duty to receive appear nowhere in Table 2.
- Stages 1–3 (audit, specify, design) would fit any information-technology governance programme.
- 'The five principles line up as a roadmap' (Design ¶4, L133) is false: five principles do not map onto five stages (also R1, R3).

Alfrink et al. (2023), which the library holds in full and v6 does not cite, already gives the content of stages 2–4. Its §6.2.1 asks 'what can be contested, who can contest, who is accountable, and what type of review is necessary', and its §6.1.4 says controllers 'must make room within their organizations for receiving, evaluating and responding to disputes'. Cite it for the content and own only the order.

**Bovens' accountability cannot be had 'in real time'.** Table 2's stage 4 requires a forum that answers 'in real time where Table 1 requires it' (L142). Bovens, the paper's source for accountability, says that accountability 'is in nature retrospective', that actors account 'after the fact', and that responsiveness and participation 'do not constitute accountability' (2007: 453). What Table 1's top rows require, a person who can change a decision before it takes effect, is synchronous intervention in Baum and Laux's sense (Reachable ¶1, L105), not accountability in Bovens'. The paper uses one word for both, and this is where the duty-to-hearing gap (converging finding 1) opens widest. Name the two: intervention before the decision takes effect, accountability after it. Then Table 1 says which a decision needs, and the 'accountability fourth' problem (converging finding 5) dissolves, because intervention is a design requirement and accountability an institutional one.

**Stage 5 could follow from the role account, and does not.** The stage keeps 'a real alternative for guests who decline the automated route' and is grounded in Wilson-Nash et al. and Hirschman. The paper's own law offers a better ground. The 1956 traveller need only appear 'able and willing to pay a reasonable sum' and be 'in a fit state to be received' (s. 1(3)). A hotel that admits only through an app adds a condition the innkeeper's duty to receive did not contain. That argument would make the role account do work in the roadmap for the first time. Whether it holds in law I cannot confirm (VERIFY), so present it as the paper's reading.

### Conclusion

**The 'three things' are one argument and one instrument.** Conclusion ¶2 (L149) says 'The paper offers three things': the obligation under delegation, standing held against exercised, and 'a test, a typology of decisions and five stages'. The second follows by definition from the first, and its distinction is Darwall's (above). The third is three products counted as one (also R3). The thesis sentence that states the first two appears in the abstract, Introduction ¶3, and Conclusion ¶2 and ¶5 (also R3). Say one contribution, the role obligation that survives delegation with the guest's standing, and one application, the test with its two tables. That saves about 30 words and removes the count a referee will audit.

**The closing echoes Kong without citing him.** The last sentence asks where 'the obligation of hosting still has an address, and whether the guest can reach it' (L155). Kong's conclusion is that 'the question "who must answer for this?" always has an addressee', and that governance must 'hold that addressee in view' (2026: 13); he also calls a diminished answerability 'a smaller quantity with an address' (p. 10). The paper's last image is Kong's. Either cite him there or change the image.

**The positioning sentence repeats the straw figure.** 'algorithmic accountability asks who answers when a delegated system causes harm' (Conclusion ¶2, L149). See Introduction above. Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021), held in full in the library, describe 'active responsibility' as 'forward-looking' (§2.4 ¶1). They also hold that a public organization's officers, 'not IT experts and private companies', must keep the capacity to explain its decisions 'no matter how much technology is involved' (§4.3 ¶1). That is the paper's holder move in the public sector, and it is not harm-indexed.

### The response letter

The letter answers the journal's R2.2 on paper. R2.2 feared the argument would look like 'a more familiar problem of organizational delegation or algorithmic accountability'. The letter replies that DS ¶5 'distinguishes our account from the closest argument in the literature', meaning Kong. Under R2.4 it then concedes that the mechanism is general. The test and Table 2 contain nothing a bank could not use unchanged. Only Table 1's rows and the ordering of admission and safety carry hospitality content. If the referee who wrote R2.2 reads R2.4's concession beside a test with no hospitality in it, the original worry stands confirmed. The fix is the positioning paragraph in Best spend, plus one sentence in the letter saying what the paper borrows and what it adds. Kong is also not obviously the closest argument. Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) and Alfrink et al. (2023) are at least as close, and v6 cites neither.

**Anonymity.** I found nothing in the body that identifies the authors. Whether 'To keep with the theme of this special issue' (AH ¶1, L72) positions the paper is the editor's call.

## Part 2. Slop audit

The prose is clean at sentence level, and HO reads as scholarship written for this journal. 'Assembled hosts at the touchpoint' reads differently: as a consultancy framework, addressed as much to the three referees as to readers. Five habits produce that effect. R3 flagged the aphorisms; the other four are new.

**1. Revision-by-reply.** Several sentences are answers to referees transplanted into the body. They address the reviewer, not the reader, and a second-round referee will recognize their own comment coming back:

- 'a sense we stipulate because the word carries technical meanings elsewhere' (DS ¶2, L54);
- 'the evidence is a single note' (DS ¶7, L64);
- 'using cases that illustrate the distinctions rather than test them' (AH ¶1, L72), which Conclusion ¶3 (L151) says again;
- 'so that a theory built on the hotel desk alone would not hold' (Split ¶1, L76), answering R3.8;
- 'Researchers can use it to analyse an arrangement, and managers to audit one' (Test ¶1, L86), answering R1.4;
- 'The placements are our own reading of the sources cited' (Reachable ¶3, L121);
- 'The framework explains most where the guest is on site' (Reachable ¶4, L123), answering R2.5.

Each is defensible alone. Together they make the paper sound as though it is defending itself. Keep the substance where a reader needs it (the scope of Table 1, the limits of the evidence) and state it once, in the limits paragraph. Cutting the clauses saves about 60 words.

**2. Coin-and-drop.** The paper coins about a dozen terms and uses several only where it coins them:

- 'standing held' and 'standing exercised', once each (L62);
- 'relay', twice, in one paragraph (L76);
- 'the authority–answerability split', which stops after Split ¶3 (also R1);
- 'interruptibility', defined in Design ¶3 and absent from the conclusion (also R1).

Meanwhile 'answerability' is never defined (also R1). A construct a paper does not use again reads as ornament. Either carry each term into the test and conclusion or drop it.

**3. The checklist walk in HO.** Paragraph openers march through the role's properties:

- 'The obligation also has a content' (HO ¶6, L38);
- 'The role has a beginning as well as a content' (HO ¶8, L42);
- 'The role has one further property: a firm can hold it' (HO ¶11, L48);
- 'Exercising standing has conditions' (DS ¶7, L64).

The section reads as a form being filled in. One or two of these openers could hook backward instead.

**4. Symmetry for its own sake.** These sentences are built for cadence:

- the chiasmus 'physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back' (Split ¶1, L76);
- the parallel triad 'Knowledge drifts … Discretion splits … Authority over the rule drifts' (Split ¶4, L82), whose third member is the miscited one;
- paragraph-ending aphorisms such as 'What guests need is not a human presence as such' (Design ¶2, L129) and 'A servant who erred could be found and named' (DS ¶4, L58) (also R3).

**5. Self-grading.** The register target forbids sentences that grade the paper's own argument. Three remain:

- 'The argument catches an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one' (Conclusion ¶2, L149);
- 'The role account brings into view the parties' (Introduction ¶4, L24);
- 'the framework is meant to help organizations keep those gains' (Conclusion ¶5, L155).

The first also overclaims (converging finding 3).

## Part 3. Line-level rewrites

All rewrites are suggestions, written plainer than the original. The authors' read-aloud decides.

1. **Abstract, L12.** *Original:* 'We argue that what algorithmic mediation removes is rarely the host's obligation and more often the guest's path to anyone with authority to answer for it.' *Suggestion:* 'We argue that algorithmic mediation leaves the host's obligation in place but can cut the guest's path to anyone with authority to answer for it.'

2. **Introduction ¶2, L20.** *Original:* 'Algorithmic-accountability research keeps answerability with the party that delegates but ties it to harm (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5)'. *Suggestion:* 'Kong (2026: 5) keeps answerability with the party that delegates, for the harms its delegate might foreseeably cause, and contestability research asks who must review a contested decision (Lyons et al. 2021)'. The sentence's close ('either in an injury or in a person's motive') would then need a third term, a contested decision.

3. **DS ¶5, L60.** *Original:* 'Writers on algorithmic accountability already hold that deploying an autonomous system does not lessen the deployer's responsibility (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5).' *Suggestion:* 'Kong (2026: 5) holds that deploying an autonomous system does not lessen the deployer's answerability, and Nissenbaum (1996) warned that, unless someone assigns it, answerability is lost among many hands.'

4. **DS ¶3, L56.** *Original:* 'We propose that the party which holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside the party that controls the operation'. *Suggestion:* 'We propose that the party a reasonable guest takes to be the host, and any party with decisive influence over the terms of the stay, answer to the guest'. Then add a short sentence of about 15 words saying that platform-liability law uses the same two tests (Filatova-Bilous 2021: 12–13).

5. **Split ¶1, L76.** *Original:* 'The split also takes different shapes across hospitality formats, so that a theory built on the hotel desk alone would not hold.' *Suggestion:* 'The split takes different shapes in hotels, rentals and restaurants.'

6. **Split ¶4, L82.** *Original:* 'Knowledge drifts towards the system, which can hold a guest's history, preferences, spending and loyalty status alongside the property's inventory and constraints (Rahman 2021; Calo and Rosenblat 2017).' *Suggestion:* 'Knowledge drifts towards the system, which, as Rahman (2021: 977) found for workers on a labour platform, can know the people it rates in ways closed to them.'

7. **Test, question 2, L91.** *Original:* 'Could the guest have anticipated it, and is the guest told when it changes?' *Suggestion:* 'Is the guest told the rule, and told when it changes?'

8. **Test, question 5, L94.** *Original:* 'Who must answer if it is disputed?' *Suggestion:* 'Can the guest reach, in time, someone who can change it?' (also R1, R2)

9. **Test ¶4, L99.** *Original:* '…the technology adds to an encounter whose rules stay steady and whose staff keep their discretion (Batat 2021), and the touchpoint passes.' *Suggestion:* '…the technology adds to an encounter that staff go on hosting (Batat 2021); since the projection decides nothing about the guest, the test has nothing to examine.' Better still, replace the case.

10. **Table 2, stage 4, L142.** *Original:* 'Establishes a forum before which an accountable actor with authority to change outcomes must answer, in real time where Table 1 requires it' / 'Accountability'. *Suggestion:* 'Names who can stop or change a decision before it takes effect, where Table 1 requires it, and who hears appeals after' / 'Intervention before; accountability after'.

11. **Design ¶4, L133.** *Original:* 'For organizations, the five principles line up as a roadmap of rising cost, which Table 2 sets out.' *Suggestion:* 'Table 2 sets out what an organization does, from mapping its touchpoints to keeping a real alternative; without stage 4, the earlier stages leave the guest no one to address.'

12. **Conclusion ¶2, L149.** *Original:* 'The role account asks a third question, what the holder of a public calling owes the person it has received. … The paper offers three things: first, …' *Suggestion:* 'The role account asks what the party that took up the role owes the guest it has received. The paper offers one argument, that the host's obligation and the guest's standing survive delegation, and one instrument, the test with its two tables.'

## Who I am

I referee for algorithmic-accountability and organization-studies venues, and I read this as the anonymous second reviewer the editor sends a paper to when the first two liked it. I came to it believing that 'hospitality-specific' theories of algorithmic decision-making usually restate the general accountability argument with a hotel lobby painted behind it, and that the innkeeper law would be decoration. I leave partly persuaded. The move from motive to role is a real contribution to this journal's argument about whether a commercial host is a host. The instrument is not new. It is contestability applied to hotels. The paper would be stronger if it said so and claimed only what it adds.

## The read

Ranked by damage. Word costs are net body words.

**1. The novelty claim rests on a straw figure of algorithmic accountability, and the instrument restates contestability-by-design.**

- *Claim:* algorithmic accountability ties answerability to harm (Introduction ¶2, L20; Conclusion ¶2, L149); the paper's duty differs in shape (DS ¶5, L60).
- *What fails:* Bovens (2007: 450), the Working Party (2018: 19, 27) and Lyons et al. (2021, §5.1), all already cited, are decision-centred, not harm-centred. Nissenbaum is misdescribed (pdf p. 11). Kong absorbs role-based grounds as control (p. 4). The six questions, Table 2's middle stages and 'reachable authority to reverse' overlap Lyons et al. and Alfrink et al. (2023, §§6.1.4, 6.2.1). The one real difference, a first-order duty owed from reception, never enters the dispute-triggered test.
- *Cost:* about +10 words net, as a replacement for four sentences of DS ¶5 (Best spend).
- *Tag:* **[FIX]** for the misdescriptions; **[CONSIDER]** for reframing the contribution.
- *Overlap:* the journal's R2.2 named this risk, and the letter answers it on paper; simulated R2 judged the Kong contrast adequate, and I disagree.

**2. Holding out does no work in the half of the paper that applies it.**

- *Claim:* holding out is how a party takes up the role and identifies who answers (HO ¶8, ¶11; DS ¶3; Conclusion ¶2).
- *What fails:* the term never appears in the touchpoint section, the test, either table or any case. In the 1956 Act it defines the establishment, and proprietorship fixes the holder (s. 1(1), (3)). It does independent work only for the franchise brand, where it is Crinkley's apparent agency, on reliance the court called 'marginal' (para 52) and against contrary North Carolina authority the opinion records (Hayman, fn 3). In the paper's own rental case, control picks out Airbnb (Bosma 2022: 605) and holding out picks out whoever lists the room, that is, whoever sells it. It also excludes hosts who choose their guests.
- *The repair is published:* platform-liability law already pairs how the platform appears to the customer with whether it has decisive influence; 'both approaches are equally supported by scholars and courts' (Filatova-Bilous 2021: 13, held in full in the library).

## Where it is strong

- **The move from motive to role** (HO ¶4–5, L34–36). It is the paper's contribution to this journal, and it answers the Lashley–Slattery debate on its own terms. It must survive any revision.

## Best spend

I would spend the words on the paragraph the paper lacks: what it borrows and what it adds. The draft below runs 142 words. It replaces sentences 2–5 of DS ¶5 (L60, 133 words), which misdescribe Nissenbaum and miss Kong's p. 4, so it costs about 10 words net and leaves the rest of the 150 for the definition of holding out (finding 2). > [SUGGESTED, drafted by Reviewer 2] Contestability research already asks what a person may contest, who answers for the decision and what review is owed (Lyons et al. 2021), and the guidance on Article 22 asks that the reviewer be able to change the decision (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). We take those questions over and add who owes the answer, and from when. Kong (2026: 4–5) keeps answerability with the deployer because it controlled the act of delegating, and indexes it to foreseeable harm. Our duty differs in content and timing: the party that took the guest into its charge (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: paras 23–24) owes it from reception, before anything goes wrong. Online reviews do not meet it: they let a guest judge a property in public (Scott and Orlikowski 2012), but reach no one able to change the decision in time.

## Verdict

**Major revisions.** The single most important fix is to rewrite the positioning (Introduction ¶2, DS ¶5 and Conclusion ¶2) so that it concedes the test's questions and the reviewer's authority to contestability research and claims only what the role account adds: who owes the guest a hearing, and from when. Then make question 5 ask that.
