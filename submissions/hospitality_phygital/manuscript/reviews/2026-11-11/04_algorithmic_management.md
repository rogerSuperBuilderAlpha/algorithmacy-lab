## Part 1. Rigour and argument, section by section

### Introduction

**Introduction ¶2 (line 20), Riordan.** The brief asks whether the paper leans on Riordan (2024) more than an abstract allows. It does not. It leans on it less, and in a way that misleads. The sentence reads 'later work describes guests without hosts and algorithmic hostility'. Placed beside 'guests without hosts', 'algorithmic hostility' reads as hostility towards guests. According to its abstract, Riordan's concept describes how 'platforms, restaurant staff, customers and others utilize digital technologies and existing social divisions to exploit contingent workers'. Its subjects are food-delivery workers, and the abstract counts customers among those who exploit them. Germann Molz (2026) also turns to labour: her abstract extends 'guests without hosts' to 'the replacement of human hospitality workers with robots and AI'. Both cards are at abstract depth. Both journal pieces the paper cites as its nearest neighbours therefore look at the worker, and the paper's frame leaves the worker out. One accurate clause would fix the misreading (Part 3, rewrite 9). Real engagement needs the full texts. (Also R3, on the parenthesis.)

**Introduction ¶3 (line 22).** The thesis says 'the duty, and the answering for it, stay with the holder'. It then says mediation removes 'the path from the guest to anyone with the authority'. Both sentences assume that someone with authority over the rule stands somewhere behind the holder, waiting to be reached. The paper's own sources put that authority partly outside the holder (see DS and Split below, and The read, finding 1).

### The host's power and the host's obligation

The section is outside my field and I found nothing here to dispute from my lens. One note for later: HO ¶6 (line 38) quotes Golubovskaya et al.'s 'standardized procedures and scripted interactions' (2017: 1295). The quotation stops before the clause that matters for the employee: the same sentence says these procedures 'foster power inequalities between customers and employees' (card, p. 1295). I use that clause under finding 3.

### Delegation and standing

**DS ¶2 (line 54), the four terms.** The separation of obligation, responsibility, authority and accountability is the paper's best analytic move (R2 says the same). Two of its definitions collide with how my field uses the words.

- *Authority.* 'Authority is the power, assigned by an organization, to change a decision'. Two paragraphs later the paper says the power over the rule drifts to 'whoever designs, configures or controls it' (Split ¶4, line 82), 'often a vendor'. A vendor's power is not assigned by the hotel. The vendor keeps it by contract and by code: Cobbe et al. find providers 'reserving rights to dictate terms of use and change, withdraw, or cancel products and services at will' (2023: 1195). Platforms likewise steer work through algorithms while avoiding the forms of command that would make them employers (Rahman 2021: 949, summarizing the literature). 'Assigned by an organization' therefore leaves out the very power the paper says is drifting. This is a third problem with the term, separate from the case-versus-rule problem in brief finding 2 (also R1, R3).
- *Responsibility.* The paper defines it as 'the holder's retained duty to see that the obligation is performed'. That is the legal sense, taken from Woodland. In hospitality HRM and platform research, responsibility is exactly what firms push down. Hales and Klidas found that hotel 'empowerment' 'amounted to increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice' (1998, abstract). Kaniadakis and Farmaki, whom the paper cites, describe responsibility 'removed from the digital platform owners … and transferred to individuals' (2022: 2). Their title says 'Responsibilisation'. A reader from either field will take 'responsibility cannot be delegated' as false. One clause saying which sense is meant would prevent that (finding 7).

**DS ¶3 (line 56), the allocation.** The paper proposes that a party 'which only arranges a service, such as a technology vendor, answers to the host'. That rule assumes the host has authority over the arranger. On platforms the arranger manages the host. Bosma reports that Airbnb requires self-check-in for a listing to be eligible for business travellers (2022: 605). He also describes how the platform creates 'a particular form of distributed agency' between owner and co-host (2022: 601). In Kaniadakis and Farmaki's interviews, hosts see Airbnb as treating 'guests as customers and hosts as Airbnb employees' (2022: 14, OnlineFirst page). Germann Molz reports the platform's claim to be 'not a company that employs hosts' (2018: 243). The paper then cites her approvingly for showing that this claim evades legal responsibility (AH ¶1, line 72). Its allocation rule would accept that self-description. (Also R2, who reaches the Airbnb result through Woodland para 25(3).)

The paper already holds the remedy. Its second criterion is 'the party that controls the operation'. Labour law classifies a working relationship by the control actually exercised, whatever the parties call it. Cobbe et al. report the same move in data-protection law: the CJEU holds that 'the factual situation outweighs contractual or other arrangements' (2023: 1193). A platform that sets the rules of arrival and directs co-hosts through its app controls the operation. On the paper's own test, then, it answers to the guest. One sentence would say so (Part 3, rewrite 10). R2 notes that Woodland para 24 sets 'control of the environment' aside. The paper should therefore say that its control criterion is a proposal of its own and is not taken from Woodland.

**DS ¶5 (line 60), Kong.** The paper draws its contrast with Kong on content: Kong's answerability is 'responsibility for harm', while the paper's duty is 'first-order' and owed 'whether or not anything goes wrong'. The card supports this: Kong's principle covers 'foreseeable types of outcome' within 'a domain of foreseeable moral risk' (2026: 5), and his scope is harm to third parties (2026: 2). But the contrast a reader of Kong will look for is on ground, and the paper leaves it out. Kong already has role-based answerability: one can answer for outcomes one did not author 'provided one occupied a role, undertook a task, or created a risk' (2026: 3). He then subordinates roles to control: 'Roles are not an alternative to control-based responsibility; they are how it is distributed across a division of labour' (2026: 4). That is a published rival to the paper's foundation, and the paper cites Kong without meeting it.

The paper has the answer to hand. Woodland, quoted in the same paragraph, says the defendant 'is liable not because he has control but in spite of the fact that he may have none' (2013: para 24, card). One sentence setting that against Kong p. 4 would show that the role account is a different ground, not a restatement.

Kong also offers what the panel's first converging finding needs. He separates being answerable from being held answerable, and argues only the first: his principle 'is a claim about where the demand for an account properly lands; institutional proposals follow only with empirical and prudential premises' (2026: 4). The paper's step from duty to hearing is the step from the first to the second. Citing Kong for the distinction would let the paper say openly that reachability is its own institutional proposal (brief finding 1; also R2, R3). (See finding 5 for the terminology collision this creates in 'authority–answerability split'.)

**DS ¶9 (line 68), Xiang et al.** This is the one place where the paper shows an employee carrying a guest's claim to authority, and the source supports it. The card records that 'after checking with hotel management, the hotel was willing to cover the cost' of the test. The same study shows how narrow frontline latitude was: 'the only thing we can do is explain the government's and subdistrict's anti-epidemic policies to guests again and again' (Interviewee H8, Findings). The paragraph is sound from my lens. (R1's point about Lugosi stands.)

### Assembled hosts at the touchpoint

**AH ¶1 (line 72), Petie et al.** The receptionist who was 'not equipped for complex problems' matches the card (MS p. 11, citing two inquiries). This is a second-hand finding, and the paper presents it as Petie et al.'s own description. Phrasing it as 'Petie et al. (2025), citing two official inquiries, report …' would cost four words.

### The authority–answerability split

**Split ¶2 (line 78).** The paper takes 'I am not a butler though nor am I a receptionist in a hotel' from Kaniadakis and Farmaki's p. 14. The same page has the 'Airbnb employees' line quoted above. If the paper cites the page, it should face the line. The pinpoint is also an OnlineFirst page, not an issue page (also R1).

**Split ¶3 (line 80), graded authority.** 'Authority in commercial hospitality has long been graded and organizational.' This is the right correction to Reviewer 3's R3.9. But the evidence offered is a manager's sentence (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1297), the commercial home and a franchise case. On the card, the manager's sentence sits under the authors' 'leadership' practice and ends '[…] just consider it as your house'. It is about encouraging staff to act as hosts, so the paper's 'because the hotel has its own standard' is its own reading. Hospitality management has direct evidence on exactly this point, and the paper does not use it:

- Hewagama et al. find that in thirty Sri Lankan hotels 'service recovery is carefully stage-managed and "staircased"', with empowerment 'deliberately graduated according to rank' (2019, abstract).
- Hales and Klidas split hotel empowerment into 'choice', meaning discretion over how work is done, and 'voice', meaning 'involvement in organizational decision-making' (1998, abstract). That is the paper's case-versus-rule distinction in the field's own terms. Their finding was that five-star staff got neither.

A referee who reads *IJCHM* or *IJHM* will notice both absences (finding 6). Both cards are at abstract depth. The letter says the graded-authority answer rests on 'sources held in full', which is presumably why the drafter left these out. The abstract-level findings above are still citable as they stand.

**Split ¶4 (line 82), the three drifts.** Four points.

1. 'Discretion acts on the single case and authority on the rule'. This is coherent as a distinction (the definitional problem is brief finding 2). But the conclusion drawn from it, that a guest can meet someone with discretion and reach no one with authority, understates the problem my field documents. Under algorithmic direction the guest may meet someone with neither. Möhlmann et al.'s drivers had 'virtually no wiggle room to diverge from algorithmic instructions' and could not accommodate 'special client requests' (2021: 2009). Hales and Klidas found little choice even before algorithms (1998, abstract).
2. 'Discretion splits, because staff keep some freedom to act'. The paper's evidence for retained freedom is Spektor et al. (2025). Their hotel is the counter-case: 'In most implementations of UpKeep, rooms are algorithmically assigned and enforced' (§5.1.1), and self-sequencing at the study hotel 'is actually a tacit workaround enabled by the union contract' (§6.1). The source shows a second thing the paper's account of authority omits: a path to authority over the rule that runs through collective bargaining, not through the holder's hierarchy (finding 9).
3. 'more of their day goes to reading decisions made elsewhere'. Neither source measures a trend. Pedersen and Pors support the workarounds (2023: 80, 89). Spektor et al.'s time cost is documentation, 'additional labor to update their progress and remain responsive within the app' (§5.3).
4. 'often a vendor that keeps the right to "change, withdraw, or cancel products"'. This sentence is correct to the card (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1195), and it is the most consequential sentence in the paper for my lens. If the vendor can change the rule at will, the holder may answer for a rule it cannot change. The paper never comes back to this (finding 1).

A smaller point: knowledge 'drifts towards the system, which can hold a guest's history' (Rahman 2021; Calo and Rosenblat 2017). Rahman's system knows the worker, not the guest: 'algorithms purport to "know" the individuals in the invisible cage' (2021: 977). The application to guests is reasonable, but it is the authors' own. The same system that holds the guest's history holds the employee's record. Saying so would cost a clause.

### Three conditions and six questions

**Test ¶3 and the list (lines 90–97).** Question 4, 'Who can override it?', is where the employee belongs. Asking whether the person the guest meets can reach whoever can override would also answer R1's request for a question about reachability (rewrite 11).

**Test ¶4 (line 99), the pricing case.** R1 and R3 show that Garcia et al. say nothing about the desk. Bendoly's study, which the paper does cite, is the hotel evidence the case needs. Its abstract says reservation agents 'are often given the latitude to accept rate bids below the pricing prescriptions of these systems'. Their deviation is 'influenced by their perceptions of how their actions will be viewed by higher levels of management'. That is discretion over the single rate, used in the shadow of authority over the pricing rule. The split is shown in one sentence, from a source the paper already cites, if the paper cites it for what it says (finding 2).

**Test ¶5 (line 101), the employee paragraph.** The employee's own position is treated in two paragraphs, Split ¶4 and this one, about 380 words of an 8,385-word body. The paper does not need to become a labour paper. But this paragraph carries the claim that the employee is the guest's path to authority, and four of its six citations do not say what the paragraph says.

- **Bendoly (2013).** The paper writes 'Skilled front-desk employees manufacture a guest's sense of control through small acts of discretion, appearing to defer while steering the options'. The card, at abstract depth, reads: 'SUPPORT: NO … It says nothing about front-desk staff, skill, small acts of discretion, or a guest's sense of control.' The study is 'a laboratory experiment' on reservation agents' pricing deviations. v6 has embellished the earlier sentence ('appearing to defer while steering the options') rather than correcting it.
- **Fuller and Smith (1991).** 'Staff are asked to produce a sense of control without holding the controls'. The card, at abstract depth, reads: 'NOT CHECKABLE, and doubtful'. The abstract is about 'how managers and employers use customer feedback to monitor, evaluate and discipline service workers'. It supports a different and more important point (finding 3). Möhlmann et al. support 'without holding the controls' for Uber drivers (2021: 2009–2010), and nothing about a 'sense of control'.
- **Jianu et al. (2025).** 'as managers carry the system's decisions to the people they affect'. The study is a Delphi forecast: it 'adopted a Delphi methodology to gauge consensus about the future implications' (p. 4). Nine of its 22 recruits were academics, and the translation it forecasts runs to frontline staff, not guests: 'to explain and justify otherwise opaque algorithmic decisions to frontline staff' (p. 10). The present-tense 'carry' turns a forecast into an observation. The other direction of 'translation in both directions', 'the employee at the desk carries the guest's request into terms the system will accept', has no source.
- **Hatherley (2025).** 'no one tells the employee or the guest'. Hatherley's users are clinicians. His concern is that updated models are unintelligible, not unannounced, and he doubts that announcing changes helps: the information 'may not reduce the epistemic or safety risks' (2025: 11). The same source is cited at Design ¶1 (line 127) for 'make significant rule changes visible', a remedy Hatherley judges insufficient.
- **Morrison (2014) and Cheng et al. (2025)** are both carded at abstract depth. Morrison's card holds no quotation, so 'when speaking up looks futile' cannot be checked from the library. Cheng et al.'s mechanism is psychological contract breach, which concerns the employee's relation to the employer, not futility about a guest's claim. Their title says 'frontline service employees', so whether the sample was in hospitality is VERIFY. Lin et al. (2026), held in full text, found that algorithmic directing, scheduling and monitoring reduce voice while algorithmic feedback raises it (card summary; no quoted locator). A bare 'employees fall silent' is too general.

This matters beyond the paragraph. The letter's R3.22 reply says that where the paper relies on an abstract, 'it says only what the abstract says'. Bendoly and Fuller and Smith are abstract-depth sources cited for claims their abstracts do not make. A referee who checks one of them will distrust that reply as a whole. This is a referee point answered only on paper.

The paragraph then closes 'It is useful, therefore, to ask which decisions need a person with authority'. Nothing in the paragraph leads to that (also R3).

### When a person must be reachable

**Table 1 (lines 113–118).** The admission row asks for 'A reachable person with authority before refusal takes effect'. In app-only and unstaffed operations, the person reached at night is usually a remote agent. That agent's discretion is set by scripts and measured by metrics, which is algorithmic management of the very person who is supposed to supply authority. This is my own reasoning, not a sourced claim. The paper's own Baum and Laux quotation in Design ¶2, the human who 'rubber-stamps without genuine judgment', covers it if the paper applies it here. Question 4 as rewritten (rewrite 11) would catch it in the test.

### Designing for interruptible hospitality

**Design ¶2 (line 129).** The paper writes 'The deploying organization is best placed to reverse a decision (Lyons et al. 2021)'. The card's sentence is 'Internal review processes are seen as practical because the entity deploying a system is likely to be in the best position to reverse that decision' (pdf p. 12, §5.1.4). The paper has dropped both 'likely' and 'are seen as'. The hedge matters for finding 1: where the vendor reserves the rule, the deployer may not be best placed. Lyons et al. report the alternative on the same page: submissions suggesting 'the entities that can influence a decision should be accountable for the decision' (§5.1.3).

**Design ¶4 (line 133) and Table 2 (stage 4).** The paper says 'the actor is the manager, operator or brand that holds the host's role'. That actor can answer and still be unable to change a vendor's rule. Stage 4's cost column reads 'Governance and staffing', which leaves out procurement. Where the rule is a vendor's, constituting accountability means writing override, configuration and response-time terms into the vendor contract. Table 2 stage 2 promises that employees can 'challenge', but nothing in the body says how an employee challenges a system's rule. Jianu et al. give the one hospitality image: managers who 'visibly intervene in the system to shorten the shift length', which 'would valorise employee voice' (2025: 11). That is a forecast panel's recommendation, and should be labelled as one. (The forum is described as a complaints channel: also R3.)

### Conclusion

**Conclusion ¶2 (line 149).** 'guest agency, employee discretion and relational judgement' presents employee discretion as something an arrangement protects. This is its first appearance as a value. The body treats discretion only as a channel for the guest's claim. Either one body sentence earns it, or the conclusion should drop it (related to R3 on new material in the conclusion).

## Part 2. Slop audit

The paper is free of the worst machine tell. I found one 'have been' in the body ('Hospitality scholars have been right', DS ¶1, line 52), and it names its agent. First person marks the authors' moves ('We read these accounts together', 'We propose'). The problems are in the shape of paragraphs, and they cluster in the passages I read most closely.

1. **Stacked findings, Test ¶5 (line 101).** Six consecutive sentences open on a new subject: 'Skilled front-desk employees', 'Under algorithmic direction', 'The translation', 'Staff', 'employees', 'frontline hospitality employees'. The fourth sentence chains three claims, each with its own citation. This is the one-finding-per-sentence list that the style guide's mechanism 8 exists to fix, and it is also where the source problems sit. Mis-citation tends to happen where evidence is stacked rather than argued. Group the evidence into one claim about the employee's position, and end on a hook into the next subsection.

2. **Wordplay standing in for evidence, Test ¶5 (line 101).** 'Staff are asked to produce a sense of control without holding the controls' and the opening teaser 'does work there that is easy to miss' are phrases that read like hooks for a quotation. Neither is supported by its source.

3. **Inventory openings, Split ¶4 (line 82) and DS ¶2 (line 54).** 'Three things that need not travel together come apart along it' and 'The single host holds together four things that need to be kept apart' announce a count before giving content (also R3). In DS ¶2 the count earns its place because the four terms are used throughout. In Split ¶4 it does not, because 'knowledge' never reappears in the test, the tables or the conclusion.

4. **Epigram closers in place of hooks.**
   - Split ¶4 (line 82): 'The problem is not the length of the chain but a break in it'. The next subsection then opens on 'To examine a touchpoint, then' (Test ¶1, line 86). The break is the subject of the next stretch, so the close should hand it on.
   - DS ¶8 (line 66): 'Competence is learned, while the occasion and the addressee for a claim are provided'.
   - Design ¶4 (line 133): 'may have automated its hospitality without deciding who answers for it'.
   - Conclusion ¶5 (line 155) (also R3).

5. **Mid-paragraph aphorism and citation string, Design ¶2 (line 129).** 'What guests need is not a human presence as such but someone' sits between three clauses that each carry their own citation (Lyons et al.; Shabnam et al.; Martin and Waldman) (also R3).

6. **Chiasmus, Design ¶3 (line 131).** 'A system built never to interrupt the guest can become one the guest cannot interrupt'. I would keep this one. It is the memorable form of the paper's interruptibility concept, and the next clause (Star and Ruhleder) supplies the reason. One chiasmus a paper can afford. The problem is that it shares a page with items 2 and 4.

7. **What works.** DS ¶4 (line 58) ends on 'a firm that no guest can reach', and DS ¶5 opens on 'the holder is the party the guest must be able to reach'. That is clean tail-head linkage and a model for the closers above. Split ¶3 (line 80) is also well built: a claim, then 'The split did not begin with algorithms', a short sentence after a dense one, then evidence grouped by setting.

## Who I am

I study algorithmic management in hotels, ride-hail and delivery platforms: how systems assign, rate and discipline workers, and when workers speak up or fall silent. I came to this paper with a specific suspicion. In my field, 'accountability to the customer' usually lands on the worker, through ratings, scripts and metrics, while the party that wrote the rule stays out of reach. I also came sympathetic to the paper's central move. Labour law, like innkeeper law, allocates duties by role and control rather than by motive, so a role-obligation account of the host is a natural partner to my field. I read the paper asking two things. Does its 'authority' match how authority actually runs in hotels and on platforms? And does the employee, whom it casts as the guest's path to authority, appear as a person or only as a pipe?

## The read: findings ranked by damage

**1. The holder can answer for a rule it cannot change, and the paper never says how the holder gets authority over the rule back.**
- *Claim.* The duty and 'the answering for it' stay with the holder (Introduction ¶3, line 22). The guest needs 'a reachable party with authority to change the decision' (Abstract, line 12). Stage 4 constitutes a forum with an actor 'with authority to change outcomes' (Table 2).
- *What fails.* The paper's own sources put authority over the rule with vendors, who keep the right to change products 'at will' (Split ¶4, line 82; Cobbe et al. 2023: 1195). Woodland holds the duty 'in spite of the fact that he may have none' (para 24). Duty without control is what the paper wants from Woodland, but it also means the holder may owe a hearing it cannot make effective. The definition 'assigned by an organization' (DS ¶2) hides the problem, because vendor power is not assigned. Lyons et al.'s hedge, which the paper drops ('likely to be in the best position', pdf p. 12), points to the same gap.
- *Why it matters.* This is the paper's thesis. If no one inside the holder has authority over the rule, then 'the path to anyone with authority' cannot be fixed by organizational design at the hotel. That is the gap a referee in algorithmic accountability will find.
- *Fix.* Say that reachable authority starts in procurement: the holder must keep the power to override or reconfigure the vendor's rule, or bind the vendor to answer to it within a set time. Add 'procurement' to stage 4's cost. Restore Lyons's hedge.
- *Cost.* About 60 words (see Best spend). Tag: **[CONSIDER]** for the argument; **[FIX]** for the definition (rewrite 6) and the Lyons hedge.
- *Overlap.* Related to brief finding 2 (R1, R3) and to R2's point on the control criterion. The vendor-contract gap is new.

**2. The employee paragraph cites four sources for claims they do not make, and that undercuts the letter's R3.22 reply.**
- *Claim.* Test ¶5 (line 101) carries the employee as the guest's path to authority.
- *What fails.* The cards say:
  - Bendoly: 'SUPPORT: NO' for the front-desk sentence, and v6 has embellished it.
  - Fuller and Smith: 'NOT CHECKABLE, and doubtful'.
  - Jianu et al.: a Delphi forecast, and the translation runs to staff, not guests.
  - Hatherley: 'no one tells' is the authors' addition, and he doubts that telling helps (p. 11). The same source is misused at Design ¶1.
  - Split ¶4's 'more of their day' trend is unmeasured in Pedersen and Pors and in Spektor et al.
  - Morrison and Cheng are abstract-only; Lin et al. (2026) complicate 'fall silent'.

**3. The remedy can turn the guest's voice into management by customers.**
- *Claim.* The guest's standing is exercised by 'asking, objecting or requesting an exception' (DS ¶6), through a chain that runs guest to employee to system (Split ¶4).
- *What fails.* The paper never asks what the guest's claim does to the employee who receives it. My field's evidence, some of it in the paper's own citations, is that it disciplines them:

  - Drivers are rated 'sometimes due to circumstances beyond their control' (Möhlmann et al. 2021: 2010).
  - Airbnb hosts 'have to have a stellar reputation' (Germann Molz 2018: 237).
  - Riordan's abstract counts customers among those who exploit contingent workers.

The guest's claim would then land on the person with no authority over the rule, which is the authority–answerability split moved down one level. Hales and Klidas's finding of 'increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice' (1998, abstract) is the same split at the employee level, before any algorithm.

**4. On platforms the arranger governs the host, so the rule that arrangers answer to the host runs backwards.**
- *Claim.* A party that only arranges, such as a vendor, answers to the host (DS ¶3, line 56).
- *What fails.* The evidence runs the other way:
  - Airbnb requires self-check-in for business-traveller eligibility (Bosma 2022: 605).
  - It creates 'distributed agency' between owner and co-host (Bosma 2022: 601).
  - Hosts see Airbnb as treating 'hosts as Airbnb employees' (Kaniadakis and Farmaki 2022: 14).
  - Germann Molz reports the platform's claim to be 'not a company that employs hosts' (2018: 243), a move the paper elsewhere criticises.
- *Fix.* Use the paper's own second criterion: a platform that sets the rules of arrival controls the operation, and so answers to the guest. This is the labour-law test of substance over label, and Cobbe et al. report the same move in data-protection law (2023: 1193).

## Best spend: 150 words

I would spend them on finding 1, with finding 3 folded in. Both drafts are mine and are suggestions, marked as such.

**[SUGGESTED, Reinholt; Split ¶4, replacing 'The problem is not the length of the chain but a break in it, …' (about 30 words)]**
> The holder's duty survives where it has no control (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 24), but its power to change the answer does not. A hotel whose vendor may change its product at will owes the guest a hearing it cannot make good. Reachable authority therefore begins in the contract: the holder must keep the power to override or reconfigure the vendor's rule, or bind the vendor to answer to it in time.

**[SUGGESTED, Reinholt; Test ¶5, replacing the Fuller and Smith sentence (about 15 words)]**
> The employee who carries the claim is also managed by it. Managers already use customer feedback to discipline service workers (Fuller and Smith 1991), and hotels grade the authority to put a failure right 'according to rank' (Hewagama et al. 2019). A claim that ends as a rating of the employee who could not change the rule has not reached authority.

## Verdict

**Major revision**, achievable in five weeks. The role account and the held/exercised distinction survive my lens. The paper's account of authority does not yet match how authority runs where algorithms are deployed. In hotels it is graded by rank. On platforms it is exercised through code while being disclaimed. Across the vendor chain it is kept by contract. The employee paragraph also rests on sources that do not say what it says.

**Single most important fix:** say where the holder's authority over a vendor's or a platform's rule comes from: contract terms with the vendor, or the paper's own criterion of control applied to platforms. Without that, the thesis promises the guest a reachable party 'with authority to change the decision' whom the paper's own sources place outside the holder.
