### Introduction

**¶2 (L20).** The paragraph lists this journal's platform work in one sentence of three clauses: Bialski, Germann Molz (2018), then "later work describes guests without hosts and algorithmic hostility (Germann Molz 2026; Riordan 2024)". The quotations are accurate (Bialski p. 257; Germann Molz 2018 p. 244). Two things fail.
- **"Showed" overstates the 2018 source.** Germann Molz calls her article "exploratory reflections … meant to be illustrative, not comprehensive" (p. 235). 'Argued' is accurate.
- **Germann Molz (2026) is the paper's nearest neighbour, and it gets a parenthesis.** On its abstract (the only part read; card at abstract depth), the article extends 'guests without hosts' to "the replacement of human hospitality workers with robots and AI". Its title nearly inverts this paper's. A reader of this journal will expect the paper to say what it adds to that article, and it never does (also R3, item 5).

**¶3 (L22) and the Abstract (L12).** The thesis says algorithmic mediation "rarely removes the host's obligation". On the paper's own theory the duty cannot be delegated, so mediation never removes it. 'Rarely' is either a frequency claim the paper has no data for, or a concession of cases it never names. The Conclusion's "where it takes anything" (¶2, L149) has the same wobble. [FIX]

**¶4 (L24).** The paper claims the debate "has had no way to say where it goes once no person does the hosting". Germann Molz (2026, abstract) asks that very question of robots and AI, and Ganesh (2018, abstract) has already joined innkeeper law to the philosophy of hospitality (finding 2). Narrow the claim to what the paper adds: a holder and a trigger for the obligation (also R3, item 5).

### The host's power and the host's obligation

**¶1 (L28).** This is a stacked paragraph: Golubovskaya twice, Lynch et al., O'Gorman, Varley et al. reporting King, and Shryock, one source to a sentence (Part 2). Two of its claims fail their cards.
- **Lynch et al. (2011).** The paper says the founding editorial finds "a duty to the stranger that was sacred and reached beyond shelter to protection". The card holds three quotations, and none says this. VERIFY (finding 3).
- **O'Gorman (2009).** "Commercial houses made a similar promise" rests on one sentence in O'Gorman's conclusion: crossing the threshold of commercial hospitality "guaranteed and provided physical protection, sanctuary and security" (pdf p. 11). That sentence cites nothing, and the body's evidence concerns caravanserais that rulers and religious donors built. O'Gorman's located legal evidence runs the other way. Mesopotamian tavernkeepers had to "report all customers who were felons" on pain of death (pdf p. 4). Quote his sentence as his conclusion and drop 'promise'. [FIX]

**¶2 (L30).** This is the paper's best passage of hospitality theory. Pitt-Rivers ties the host's command to the duty to protect (2012: 513, 516), and the quotations are exact. The closing move, "We read these accounts together as saying that the party who admits the guest …" takes on the guest's safety, domesticates Bulley. His argument is that the host's power continues past the threshold as discipline and categorization. Guests are assessed "to enable their better categorisation and thus the management of their behaviour once inside" (Bulley 2015, PDF p. 15; card confirms). That is a warning about power, and the paper reads it as a warrant for duty. It also gives the paper its sharpest hospitality-theory lens on the check-in case in Test ¶4 (L99), a system "that weighs the guest's history to decide how they will be received". The paper never uses it there. [FIX] (Rewrite 2.)

**¶3 (L32).** Two more Lynch et al. (2011) attributions, Aramberri's claim and the editors' answer that hospitality still describes relations between strangers in the commercial realm, are not on the card. VERIFY. One irony is worth fixing. Lashley co-wrote that 2011 answer (card: author list), and ¶4 then makes him the voice of the motive side. That should tell the authors the field holds the two views together. They are not two camps.

**¶4 (L34): the motive foil.** "The debate, on both sides, turns on the host's motive." This is the claim the whole turn to role obligation rests on, and single citations from the paper's own library refute it:
- **Lashley (2015: 4):** "the duty to provide hospitality, to act with generosity as a host, and to protect visitors is more than a matter left to the preferences of individuals" (card, full text).
- **Blain and Lashley (2014: 1):** "the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome". On the same page they extend host and guest relations to "tour guides, air stewards, conductors and drivers on coaches", which extends the obligation to service roles by metaphor.
- **Golubovskaya et al. (2017: 1284):** they list approaches that regard hospitality "as a right, an obligation, a duty, a law".
- **Lugosi (2009, section 'Hospitality'):** "hosts have duties to ensure the wellbeing of their guests" (accepted manuscript, no page).

The 'externally imposed' quotation in ¶4 is also narrower than v6 makes it. Blain and Lashley say religious obligations "may be at odds with Telfer's notion of hospitableness" (p. 2), not that obligation as such opposes hospitableness. So hospitality studies already has a host's obligation. What it lacks for the commercial case is a holder and a trigger. Lashley holds that offering hospitality was a moral obligation "until the advent of mass travel and commercialism", after which it no longer carried "the same moral obligation it once had" (2015: 3). That is the argument v6 should have. The law of innkeepers shows Lashley's cultural-domain duty surviving commercialization for one trade (Lashley 2015: 3–4 names the three domains). Put that way, the turn to role obligation says something about hospitality: it disputes a named scholar's history of the obligation. Put v6's way, it replaces hospitality theory with legal doctrine and caricatures the theory it replaces (partly also R2, item 6). [FIX], +25 words.

**¶5–6, ¶9 (L36, L38, L44): what the law is evidence of.** v6 reports Bogen's dismissal of holding out (p. 52) and his "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment" (p. 75). It leaves out Bogen's own thesis: "concern for criminal activity in the inn … led to the obligation to serve the public" (p. 53). It also leaves out his conclusion that the duty's "main significance … today is to protect against discrimination" (p. 91). Sandoval-Strausz, whom ¶9 calls "the most persuasive account", is an article titled "Travelers, strangers, and Jim Crow". The law the paper uses as evidence has two faces.
- **To the guest,** it is protection.
- **To the state,** the host answers for the guest (O'Gorman's felons; Bogen's crime control).
- **In modern law,** it is the ground on which exclusion by race was fought (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 54, "its duties and privileges had to be turned into rights").

Critical hospitality studies treats that doubleness as hospitality's defining feature: the conditional law that admits some and polices all. v6 keeps the protective face only. The cost shows downstream. The six questions (Test, L90–95) ask nothing about whom an arrangement turns away, and the paper has dropped the discrimination evidence its library carries (Edelman et al. 2017, full text: applicants with "distinctively African-American names are 16% less likely to be accepted"; Cui et al. 2020, abstract). [CONSIDER] (finding 5).

**¶7 (L40).** The distinctive object, "the stranger's person and belongings, overnight, in a house that was not the stranger's own", is hospitality's classical object, and the paragraph is right to land there. Two facts in the paper's own sources bear on it.
- **King (1995).** The field's most cited source on the host's obligation was written because "health care organizations … have tried to redefine their customers as guests" (King 1995, abstract). The obligation was being exported to hospitals before R2 raised them.
- **Restaurants.** Title II of the Civil Rights Act, which the paper cites, covers restaurants (§ 2000a(b)(2), per the card's note; the card quotes only (b)(1) and the chapeau, so VERIFY). That gives the paper a statutory route beyond the inn for the duty to receive, though only as a duty not to discriminate. It answers part of the restaurants objection (also R1, R2, R3) at a cost of one clause. [FIX]

**¶8 (L42).** The Hotel Proprietors Act's definition reads as conditional hospitality in statute: the traveller must appear "able and willing to pay" and be "in a fit state to be received" (s. 1(3)). This journal's founding statement lists "conditional/unconditional" among the oppositions that organize the field (Lynch et al. 2011, card). Bulley's article, which v6 cites, is subtitled "beyond Derrida". v6 never says that its role account is the conditional law of hospitality, and Derrida does not appear in the body. One sentence would place the paper in the journal's conversation. The Derrida card is at metadata depth, so cite the distinction, not a page. [FIX], +25 words.

**¶9 against ¶8: the ground and the trigger point different ways.** ¶9 grounds the duty in the traveller's exposure: people "away from their home communities and in unfamiliar surroundings" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). Nobody fits that ground better than an asylum seeker in a contracted hotel or a refugee in a camp. ¶8's trigger, holding out to a traveller able and willing to pay, excludes both. That tension is the paper's to resolve (finding 1).

**¶10 (L46).** The account of the guest's duties is accurate. It stops one sentence short at p. 517, though. Pitt-Rivers says either party, if slighted, may "relinquish his role and revert to the hostility which it suppressed", and "Once they are no longer host and guest they are enemies, not strangers". That sentence gives Table 1 its hospitality ground. Admission and removal (Table 1, rows 1–2) are the two thresholds at which hospitality theory turns a stranger into a guest and back. Riordan's 'algorithmic hostility' (abstract) names the digital form of the second. [CONSIDER] (Rewrite 10.)

### Delegation and standing

**¶1 (L52).** "We now push beyond a second part of their account", namely the host as one person. Two paragraphs later (¶3, L56) the paper says "This journal already has words for the result" and names the assembled host and the macro-host. Both cannot be true. The field has named the divided host. What it has not asked is which part answers to the guest, and ¶3 says exactly that. Align ¶1, and consider the heading, whose 'Beyond' echoes Bulley's title while claiming to go past a position Bulley left behind. [FIX] (Rewrite 4.)

**¶2 (L54).** Authority is defined as "a sense we stipulate because the word carries technical meanings elsewhere". Hospitality theory has its own sense, and the paper cites the page. "A host is host only on the territory over which on a particular occasion he claims authority" (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 514). On that sense the split in Split ¶1 becomes a hospitality claim. In the classical host, territory and authority coincide. In a phygital arrangement the vendor holds authority over the rule and has no territory, so it never becomes host, while the property holds the territory without authority over the rule. That does not settle the two senses of authority (also R1, R3, item 2). It does give the term a ground in this journal's tradition instead of a stipulation. [CONSIDER], +40 words.

**¶3 (L56): the assembled host.** See finding 4. In brief, v6 describes accurately whom Bulley calls the assembled host: the agencies that sort and manage refugees "once over the formal threshold" (PDF p. 14). It drops what he meant by the term.
- **The assembled hosts are themselves guests.** They perform the host's role "while being themselves guests in a space to which they do not formally 'belong'" (PDF p. 14).
- **The term names an apparatus of "care and control".** It is not a set of duty-holders (PDF pp. 14–15).
- **Bulley's evidence shows the paper's split.** The assembled hosts "are separated in compounds, reducing or annihilating the space where hosts and guests can meet or mix" (PDF p. 16, drawing on Hyndman).
- **Bulley never asks which member answers to the guest.** That is v6's question, and a good one, but it is v6's.

**¶3 (L56): micro-hosts and macro-hosts.** v6 attributes the distinction to Lynch et al. (2011), "after Robinson and Lynch", with a train company as the example. The Lynch et al. (2011) card does not record it. The project's bibliography attaches the distinction to Lynch (2017), 'Mundane welcome', with airlines as the example (`literature/references.bib` l. 1041; `literature/FOUNDATION.md` ll. 570–572), and the Lynch 2017 card is at metadata depth, marked "NOT CHECKABLE". VERIFY before resubmission (finding 3).

**¶4–6 (L58–62).** The core of the theory runs on Woodland, Morgan, Bovens, Darwall, Thompson, Kong, Nissenbaum, Kropf, Crinkley and Cobbe. Hospitality theory supplies two labels in one sentence. That is the clearest sense in which v6 replaces hospitality theory with legal and moral doctrine. The fix need not be large. Standing held is what Pitt-Rivers calls the guest's right "to respect" (2012: 514). Standing exercised is what the classical law withholds, since a guest who "makes claims or demands" usurps the host (p. 515), and what the commercial relation licenses. v6 already makes that second point in ¶7, and it is the paper's real contribution to hospitality theory. One clause in ¶6 joining the two would make the standing distinction a claim about hospitality. [FIX], +20 words.

**¶4 (L58): Woodland and the placed guest.** R2 is right that Woodland's criteria fit an adult paying guest poorly. The court's factors are "the vulnerability of the claimant" and a relationship giving the defendant "a degree of protective custody over him" (para 12). Para 23(2) requires that the claimant be in "the actual custody, charge or care of the defendant". Para 23(3) adds that the claimant has "no control" over how the defendant performs. Those criteria describe an asylum seeker placed in a contracted hotel far better than a business traveller. The structure v6 borrows fits best the guests its trigger excludes (finding 1).

**¶7 (L64).** This is the strongest paragraph for my field. It sets the classical restraint on complaint (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 513–514; Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 333) against the commercial licence to complain. Two problems remain.
- **Moysidou and Stanley's thesis.** They "reject a model that assumes, a priori, that hosts are powerful and guests are powerless", and they argue that "mobilities seem to operate as proxy for power" (accepted manuscript p. 15). The paper cites their line on complaint and leaves out their central claim. That claim is the exit side of the paper's own argument: Hirschman in Test ¶3 and stage 5 of Table 2.
- **The au pair is a placed guest.** She works for her hosts and "cannot leave". She is the kind of guest the role account excludes.

**¶9 (L68).** The Lugosi sentence does not show a request reaching authority (also R1). Lugosi says what v6 needs elsewhere in the same article: "those involved in transactions negotiate their roles alongside their dominance or subservience" (section 'Hospitality'). That sentence is the hospitality literature on negotiation that Reviewer 1 asked for in round one (R1.2). The card warns that it carries no page from the version of record.

### The authority–answerability split, the test, and Table 1

**Split ¶1 (L76).** The relay, "physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts", is fresh relative to the phygital literature. In this journal it sits close to Riordan's (2024) "new virtual domain - which accounts for exchanges at the threshold of material and virtual contexts" (abstract). One clause setting the two apart is enough, and the full text should be read first. [CARRY FORWARD]

**Test ¶4 (L99).** The check-in case's "one that weighs the guest's history" is, in hospitality terms, Bulley's categorization of guests to manage them once inside (PDF p. 15). The test asks who can override and who must answer. It never asks who is sorted out. A critical-hospitality reader will want a seventh question, or a clause in question 1: whom does the arrangement turn away, and on what grounds? [CONSIDER], +12 words.

Both are in-journal evidence for the paper's claim, and neither is used.

**Reachable ¶4 (L123).** The paper treats the five-room, owner-occupied exemption as the place where "the role obligation we describe is at its weakest". The exemption takes such houses outside Title II's bar on discrimination by race, colour, religion or national origin (§ 2000a(b)(1) quoted on the card; the grounds come from the card's summary of § 2000a, so VERIFY the wording). The edge of the role account is therefore also the place where the law lets a host refuse on those grounds. A critical-hospitality reader will see the 1964 Act cited only for its exemption and not for what it was enacted to do. Also R2 on the exemption's character. [FIX] (Rewrite 11.)

### Designing for interruptible hospitality

**Design ¶3 (L131).** Interruptibility is the paper's strongest design idea, and its hospitality pedigree is better than seamful design. Bulley ends his article on a wager: 'better' forms of hospitality would "keep that welcome open once the threshold is passed", through "a negotiation between host and guest" (PDF p. 19). Varley et al. report the commercial version, that to be a good host "one must be unconditionally available to the guest", and that commodified hosting "creates a similar imperative" (section 2). Weaver (2026) is cited for a claim, that the journal "traced the push for speed … to large corporations", which rests on the card's summary of an abstract, not on a quotation. VERIFY.

### Conclusion

**¶2 (L149).** "The argument catches an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one". Beyond the evidential gap (also R1, R2, R3), critical hospitality studies doubts the phrase itself. An arrangement that sorts guests can harm a class of them while each transaction looks harmless (Edelman et al. 2017). The test "measures neither satisfaction nor harm" (Test ¶1, L86), so 'harms no one' is outside the paper's own instrument.

**¶4 (L153).** The first proposed study, following a complaint to the party with authority over the rule, would be sharpest in contracted asylum hotels, where payer and guest differ (Petie et al. 2025). Adding that clause would turn the reach problem into an agenda. [CONSIDER], +20 words.

## Part 2, slop audit

The prose reads as competent scholarship. What marks it as made, not written, for this journal is mostly structural.

1. **The vocabulary moves out of hospitality.** In 8,469 words of body the paper uses 'welcome' twice, 'hostility' once and 'stranger' four times. It uses 'authority' 33 times, 'standing' 24, 'arrangement' 17, 'touchpoint' 15 and 'holder' 15. It never uses 'Derrida', 'conditional hospitality', 'exclusion' or 'race'. A reader of this journal hears a legal-managerial paper that cites hospitality scholars.
2. **"This journal" as a credential.** The phrase appears seven times (L20 twice, L28 twice, L56, L64, L131): "Writing in this journal", "This journal already has words". Each time it introduces a scholar by venue rather than by position. Name the scholar's claim instead, and the venue takes care of itself.
3. **A checklist march in HO.** ¶4 announces "a content, a moment at which it comes into force, and a holder". Then ¶5 opens "The obligation belongs to a role because", ¶6 "The obligation also has a content", ¶8 "The role has a beginning as well as a content" and ¶11 "The role has one further property". The paragraphs open on the paper's scaffolding, not on a scholar or a case. This is the inventory habit R3 points to, at its densest.
4. **Stacked sources.** HO ¶1 (L28) gives six sentences to five sources, one finding to a sentence, and Intro ¶2 (L20) carries fifteen citations in five sentences. Group them under the claim they share and argue with one.
5. **Aphoristic closes** (also R3): "To host, on these accounts, is to take the guest into one's charge" (L28); "it belongs to whoever occupies the role" (L36); "it changes who holds the host's role, not whether the role exists" (L40); "what an organization must keep for its guests to remain guests" (L24). The last reads as a slogan because 'guests' carries two senses in nine words.
6. **Paragraph shape.** DS ¶3 (293 words) and Conclusion ¶2 (287) carry the allocation and the contribution, and both read as stuffed. DS ¶8 (76 words) is a fragment and could fold into ¶7.
7. **No em-dashes in the body.** Glosses are welded with commas ("Obligation is what the host owes the guest, the duties set out …", L54), which flattens the definitions into lists. A few appositive dashes in DS ¶2 would let the four terms read as definitions.

The paragraphs I would keep as they stand for their voice are HO ¶2 (L30, apart from its last sentence), DS ¶4's closing pair (L58, "A servant who erred could be found and named …") and DS ¶7 (L64).

## Who I am

I read and publish in *Hospitality & Society* on conditional hospitality, on Lashley's domains and on the host's power: Derrida's laws of hospitality, Bulley's turn beyond him, Pitt-Rivers and Shryock on command and sovereignty, Germann Molz on network hospitality. I came to this paper sympathetic to its main move. I have long thought the hospitableness literature's focus on the individual host's motive leaves commercial hospitality without an account of what is owed. I was wary, though, of papers that bring law or management into this journal and keep hospitality theory as a set of epigraphs. My field's ethical centre is the guest who did not choose the host and does not pay: the refugee, the asylum seeker, the person placed. I read for whether an account of hospitality holds for that guest.

### 1. The role account excludes the guests whose cases supply its evidence from this journal, and it leaves the state out of the assembled host. (also R3; R1, R2 in part) [CONSIDER], with a [FIX] fallback

- **Claim.** The obligation is triggered when a house holds itself out to a traveller "able and willing to pay" (HO ¶8, L42), and the holder may be owner, operator or brand (HO ¶11, L48; DS ¶3, L56).
- **What fails.** Three of the paper's in-journal sources concern guests outside that trigger:
  - Bulley's refugees, from whom the paper takes 'assembled host' (L56);
  - Petie et al.'s asylum seekers, "the Home Office who are paying customers" (MS p. 4), the opening case of the application section (L72);
  - Moysidou and Stanley's au pair, who "cannot leave" (L64).

  The paper's preferred ground for the duty, the traveller's exposure (HO ¶9, L44), applies to these guests with most force, and its trigger excludes them. The Woodland structure it borrows fits them better than it fits a paying adult (paras 12, 23(2)–(3)). The allocation of DS ¶3 has no place for the state, although Shryock, whom the paper cites, hands the modern care of strangers to the industry and to "the government bodies that control immigration and naturalization" (2012: S30). Xiang's hotel was enforcing "the government's and subdistrict's anti-epidemic policies".
- **Why it matters.** These are the guests critical hospitality studies cares most about, and a referee from this field will notice that the paper uses their cases for an account that does not reach them.
- **Cost.**

## Where it is strong

- **The commercial licence to complain.** DS ¶7 (L64) sets the classical guest's restraint (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 513–515; Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 333) against the paying guest's licence. It locates what commercial hospitality adds in what makes a claim proper to press. That is the paper's real contribution to hospitality theory, and every revision should keep it.
- **Command brings duty.** HO ¶2 (L30) states the classical link plainly and quotes it exactly.
- **The guest's duties.** HO ¶10 (L46) gives an accurate account of them, from the anthropology to the statute.
- **The distinctive object of hospitality.** HO ¶7 (L40) concedes that the mechanism is general and then names hospitality's distinctive object: the stranger's person, overnight, in another's house. That is a hospitality claim, not a legal one.
- **A servant against a system.** DS ¶4's closing pair (L58), on the servant who "could be found and named" against a system supplied by a firm no guest can reach. It is concrete, short and true.
- **Interruptibility, defined with a counterparty** (Design ¶3, L131). Grounded in Bulley as well as in seamful design, it is the paper's best idea for this special issue.

## Best spend

If I had 150 of the roughly 600 spare words, I would spend them where the paper meets its own guests. The three passages come to about 150 words, or about 130 net of the clause the first replaces. All three are drafts for the authors to weigh; none is the authors' wording.

> **[SUGGESTED by panelist 01, DS ¶3 (L56), replacing the Bulley clause; about 55 words]** Bulley (2015) calls the agencies that run a refugee camp, once refugees are past its formal threshold, an assembled host. They host in a space that is not formally theirs, sort the people they receive into zones, and live apart from them in compounds, so that the space where host and guest might meet shrinks.

> **[SUGGESTED by panelist 01, Design ¶3 (L131), after the definition of interruptibility; about 40 words]** Bulley ends on the same wager from inside hospitality studies: a better hospitality would seek to 'keep that welcome open once the threshold is passed', through negotiation between host and guest rather than control of every aspect of the guest's behaviour.

> **[SUGGESTED by panelist 01, Reachable ¶4 (L123), after the five-room sentence; about 55 words]** The account also stops short of guests who are placed rather than received, such as asylum seekers housed in hotels the Home Office pays for (Petie et al. 2025). The traveller's exposure that grounds the duty applies to them with most force, yet no hotel held itself out to them. Their host is whoever placed them.

The quotations in the second passage need their printed page (VERIFY). To fund the three, cut the Conclusion's human-centricity sentence (L149, about 35 words; also R3, item 6) and the "this journal" credentials (about 20 words), and fold DS ¶8 into ¶7 (about 30 words).

## Verdict

**Major revision** from my lens. The argument is coherent and much improved. It does not yet hold for the guests this journal's critical tradition cares most about, and it uses three of their cases as evidence. It also borrows the field's terms ('assembled host', the motive debate) without what their sources meant. None of this needs new data. Most of it is [FIX] work within the word budget, and the reading (Ganesh, Germann Molz 2026, the Lynch et al. 2011 text) fits in five weeks.

**The single most important fix:** scope the role account against the placed, non-paying guest, and re-read the three in-journal sources that describe such guests (Bulley, Petie et al., Moysidou and Stanley) for what they show. They show the paper's split in this journal's own terms.
