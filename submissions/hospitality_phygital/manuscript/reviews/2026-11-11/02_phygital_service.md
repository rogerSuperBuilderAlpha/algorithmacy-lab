## Part 1, rigour and argument

### Introduction

**The absence claim fails against sources the paper itself holds.** Introduction ¶2 (l. 20) says the phygital literature describes systems "without saying what the party that deploys them owes the guest". Four sources in the library say what deploying firms should do:

- **Shabnam et al. (2026)**, cited in v6, state that "algorithmic governance must remain contestable. Digitally enforced rules should incorporate escalation pathways and human oversight" (PDF p. 14). They also say phygital systems "redistribute decision authority, embed governance within code" (PDF p. 2). Their escalation example, consumers who meet chatbots "before escalating to human agents", illustrates "how expertise, authority and responsibility are redistributed" (PDF p. 12). The card holds all three, and I re-found them in the retained full text.
- **Gursoy (2026)** is carded at full text and flagged `must-engage`, yet v6 drops it. He writes that "Algorithmic governance shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics, raising questions about accountability when harm, bias, or exclusion arise" (p. 160). He also recommends hybrid systems "that allow guests to choose their preferred level of human or AI engagement" (p. 157). That is the paper's drift of authority, and its fifth principle, already in a hospitality journal.
- **Pigac et al. (2026)** appear in v6 only for the timing of disclosure. They report that "Systems that allowed concierges to override or explain AI decisions were described more positively" (p. 291). They also advise firms to "Train staff as transparency mediators ... override where necessary" (p. 292). Both are confirmed in the retained text.
- **Batat and Hammedi (2023)**, cited in v6, write that "companies should never replace services delivered through humans by virtual agents, even if they are highly humanized" (p. 18).

A narrower gap survives, and it is worth stating. These works prescribe what firms should do for the sake of trust, fairness, empathy or well-being. None says the safeguard is owed to the guest, and none asks which of several firms must provide it. Gursoy's accountability is tied to harm (p. 160), which puts him on the paper's own map beside Nissenbaum and Kong. Stated that way, the gap cannot be refuted from the paper's own reference list. (Partly also R1, item 8, which raises Shabnam and Batat 2024: 1238. Gursoy, Pigac and Batat and Hammedi are new here.) Rewrite 1 drafts it for +32 words.

### Assembled hosts at the touchpoint: the authority–answerability split

**The relay is PH-CX's two processes, renamed and uncredited.** Split ¶1 (l. 76): "The movement can as accurately be called a relay". Its gloss is "physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts". Batat (2024) names these movements: "shifting from physical to digital ('digitalization') and from digital to physical ('physicalization')" (pp. 1230–1231). The card has the passage, and I found it in the retained text. Anyone who knows PH-CX, the special issue's editors among them, will see the relay as the framework's two processes without the citation. Crediting it costs ten words. [FIX] (New.)

The relay is also the paper's best idea for this special issue, and the paper uses it once. (Also R1, item 8: "the paper's freshest idea".) What it adds to PH-CX can be stated exactly. Batat follows consumer value through the shift: the framework's aim "should be to identify the dynamics of consumer value in the shifting process" (p. 1238). The relay follows authority through the same shift. The guest meets a ruling on the physical side, as a key card that does not open or a price at the desk, and that is where a claim arises. The power to change the ruling sits on the digital side, often inside another firm. That sentence links phygital theory to the authority–answerability split, and v6 leaves it implicit. State it, and carry the relay into the conclusion's list of contributions. [CONSIDER], +34 words (Best spend, S2).

**The smoothness sources are presented as hospitality accounts, and one is misdescribed.** Split ¶1 (l. 76) cites Batat (2024: 1220, 1233) for "Accounts of phygital hospitality describe the movement between physical and digital touchpoints as fluid". PH-CX is a general customer-experience framework. Hospitality appears in it only in passing: in a list of sectors, and in sensory marketing "in stores, restaurants, and hotels". On p. 1233, "seamless customer experience" concerns the fit between merchandising and a website. Write 'accounts of phygital customer experience'.

The next sentence is worse. It says "good technology removes queues, repetition, uncertainty and wasted effort" so that the guest moves "without friction (Batat and Hammedi 2023)". The card records none of the four items. In the retained full text I found no 'queue', no 'wasted' and no 'friction'. 'Uncertainty' appears only in a research question about developing new services. The source's own words are close and available: "Flawless interactivity and connectivity are thus necessary" (p. 14), and a phygital perspective "smooths customers' transitions" (p. 24). Quote those (Rewrite 2). [FIX], −3 words. (New.)

### Three conditions and six questions

**The Batat 'parallel' points the other way.** Test ¶1 (l. 86): "Batat makes the parallel claim within the phygital framework". The quotation that follows is accurate, but Batat's passage opens with its other half. In PH-CX, "the negative effects generated by the three connectors, including media, digital, and the physical, can be fixed by positive human interactions" (p. 1234). Batat treats the human as the repair for everything else. The paper argues the opposite twice. Design ¶2 (l. 129) says "What guests need is not a human presence as such". Conclusion ¶2 (l. 149) says "keeping humans visible in the encounter is neither necessary nor sufficient". Calling Batat's claim 'parallel' invites the editor to read the conclusion as a quiet rebuttal of the human connector.

The better move names the difference: what cannot be replaced is the authority a human connector may carry, not the human. That is a contribution to PH-CX, it costs about seventeen words (Rewrite 4), and it gives the conclusion's sentence on human-centricity a named interlocutor. [FIX] (New. R3, item 6, flags the human-centricity sentence as new material but does not connect it to PH-CX.)

**The restaurant case falls outside the paper's own definition.** Test ¶4 (l. 99) says "the technology adds to an encounter whose rules stay steady" and concludes "and the touchpoint passes". The paper defines an algorithmic system as "software that takes or shapes a decision about a guest" (l. 18). Le Petit Chef is a projection, and it decides nothing about the diner. The case passes because the test has nothing to ask of it. That shows the edge of the test's domain, not a pass.

Two further problems sit on the card. First, "rules stay steady and staff keep their discretion" is not in Batat (2021); the card rates it the authors' reading (PARTLY). Second, the study is silent on whether a diner can decline the projection, which is the sixth question. Batat also reports that "in some situations, such as a business diner or a romantic diner, the use of AR could be a distraction" (2021: 9). By the paper's own test, then, the case is open, not passed. It is the only restaurant case, so one of the three named formats rests on a touchpoint that takes no decision.

Two fixes are possible. The smaller one marks the reading as the authors' and names the open sixth question (Rewrite 5, +17 words) [FIX]. The larger one replaces the case with one where a decision is taken and someone with authority can be reached [CONSIDER]. ### Designing for interruptible hospitality

**Interruptibility is seamful design plus contestability by design, with a time and an addressee added. The paper credits the first and not the second.** Design ¶3 (l. 131) defines interruptibility as "a point at which the guest can stop the process, ask, redirect". It then sets the term against seamful design: "a visible seam is of no use to a guest unless someone with authority". Against Chalmers and Galani (2004), whose seams exist to support "social interaction", the distinction is right.

It is not new against Alfrink et al. (2023), which the library holds at full text and v6 dropped. Contestable AI by design already requires that "human controllers who respond to intervention request should have the authority and capability to actually change previous decisions" (§6.1.4, a sentence in which Alfrink et al. cite Brkan). It also says that controllers "must make room within their organizations for receiving, evaluating and responding to disputes" (§6.1.4). So pairing a visible seam with a person who has authority is already published. Two things are the paper's own. One is the time, which Table 1 scopes by decision on Baum and Laux's criterion. The other is the addressee, the holder of the host's role. Say so in one sentence (Rewrite 9, +27 words). Interruptibility then becomes a defensible construct: contestability owed to the guest, and made synchronous where the decision cannot wait. [FIX] (R1, item 7, notes that the term is defined only here. The collision is new.)

The term itself may collide with established uses. I believe human–computer interaction uses 'interruptibility' for a person's availability to be interrupted by a system, the reverse of the paper's sense. I also believe AI-safety work uses 'safe interruptibility' for an operator's power to halt an agent, which is close to the AI Act's "stop" button that the conclusion quotes. Neither literature is carded, and I have not checked either. **VERIFY** before the term stays in the keywords.

**The friction literature has dropped out, and it holds the sharpest foil.** Design ¶3 says "frictionless design can remove the occasion for a claim along with the annoyance" (l. 131). The library holds Padigar et al. (2025), read in full, who sort friction by type and observe that "businesses have been mindlessly removing constructive friction" (p. 32). The friction they would keep sits in security, authentication and privacy tasks (pp. 28, 32). They put contacting customer service on the other side: that friction can be "frustrating friction", and "reducing or eliminating friction in these tasks can enhance customer experience" (p. 32). So consumer research already keeps some friction, and it files the guest's complaint under friction to remove. No source in my field states more clearly the position the paper argues against. It serves the paper better than Weaver does, because it shows the cost of smoothness falling on the claim itself. [CONSIDER], +43 words (Best spend, S3). (New.)

**Pigac et al. are cited against their own finding.** Design ¶1 (l. 127) says "explain a decision where it lands, since guests prefer little disclosure at check-in", and then "fuller explanation later in the stay (Pigac et al. 2026: 286)". Pigac et al. report "minimal disclosure during check-in; richer, retrospective transparency in loyalty apps" (p. 286). They add that "Post-stay feedback and loyalty apps were seen as appropriate venues for deeper algorithmic explanations" (p. 291). The fuller explanation comes after the stay, not later in it. The 'since' clause therefore argues for explaining a decision somewhere other than where it lands. The sample is 50 guests with a recent five-star stay (p. 287), so 'guests' should read 'luxury-hotel guests in one study'. Either rename the principle (Rewrite 7) or find it another source. [FIX], about −1 word. (New.)

The same paper offers what Design ¶1–2 lack: hospitality evidence. "At check-in, respondents preferred minimal interruption and trusted hotel staff to mediate technology", and one guest said "The AI is fine—as long as the people can still step in" (p. 291). That evidence could replace the Mosca pair that opens Design ¶1 (also R2, R3, who call it weak) and save 25 words (Rewrite 6). It also gives the unsourced line "What guests need is not a human presence as such" (l. 129) a guest to say it.

**Lyons et al. are quoted as if they were the authors of a submission's view.** Design ¶2 (l. 129) says "The deploying organization is best placed to reverse a decision". The card notes that §5.1.4 summarizes submissions to the Australian AI-ethics consultation: "Internal review processes are seen as practical because the entity deploying a system is likely to be in the best position to reverse that decision". Attribute the claim to the submissions (Rewrite 8). [FIX], +7 words. (New. Martin and Waldman, in the same sentence, is also R3.)

## Part 2, slop audit

In my sections the prose is cleaner than in round one, and paragraph lengths sit near the target (96–235 words in the touchpoint section). The machine tell is the shape of the paragraph, not the sentence: a claim, three citations, then an aphoristic close. That shape repeats across Split ¶1 and ¶4 and Design ¶1–4. In Design ¶1–4 no sentence falls below ten words, so the density never breaks. The passages below are the ones a scholar in my field would stop at.

1. **Introduction ¶2 (l. 20).** Five sentences carry 235 words, a mean of 47. Two run to 59 and 60 words. Three literatures are stacked into two sentences, and the phygital literature, the paper's main interlocutor in this special issue, gets half of one sentence and six citations. The paragraph closes on a sorting summary: "so the host's obligation is sought either in an injury or in a person's motive". It reads as a list joined by commas. Give the phygital literature its own two sentences, with named scholars as subjects (Rewrite 1).
2. **Split ¶1 (l. 76), last sentence:** "The split also takes different shapes across hospitality formats". The sentence continues "so that a theory built on the hotel desk alone would not hold". It answers Reviewer 3 (R3.8) rather than the reader, and the letter quotes it as the change. Cut it; the next paragraph shows the formats.
3. **Split ¶1 (l. 76):** "The movement can as accurately be called a relay". The hedge stages a renaming, and the next sentence personifies: "A relay raises a question that fluidity hides". A coinage that appears once and then disappears reads as ornament, not as a construct. Credit the relay and carry it (Rewrite 3; Best spend, S2).
4. **Split ¶4 (l. 82), closer:** "The problem is not the length of the chain but a break in it". The sentence continues through two nested clauses ("at which the point where ... comes apart from any point where") that cannot be read aloud. Plainer: "What matters is a break in the chain, where the place a decision is made and the place the guest can be answered come apart."
5. **Test ¶5 (l. 101), closer:** "It is useful, therefore, to ask which decisions need a person with authority". 'It is useful' is a cousin of the banned 'It is worth', and 'therefore' does not follow from employee silence. Use a hook instead: "Whether that work can carry a claim depends on when the decision takes effect."
6. **Reachable ¶4 (l. 123):** "The framework explains most where the guest is on site". Three sentences in a row ("explains most", "explains less", "reaches its edge") echo the wording of R2.5. The paper answers the referee in the referee's words, and 'framework' survives (also R1, R3).
7. **Design ¶1 (l. 127):** "Concealment makes the system harder to see and leaves the unease where it was". An aphorism stands where evidence should be; Pigac et al. supply the evidence (Rewrite 6).
8. **Design ¶2 (l. 129):** "What guests need is not a human presence as such". The sentence is a not-X-but-Y with no source. Because it sits between two cited sentences, it reads as cited.
9. **Design ¶3 (l. 131):** "A system built never to interrupt the guest can become one the guest cannot interrupt". The chiasmus carries the argument, and it is the section's most quotable line. Keep it. But it is the kind of line R3 objected to in round one ("condemns the first … blesses the second"), so allow one such line per section.
10. **Design ¶4 (l. 133), closer:** "A property that stops after better interfaces and better-trained staff may have automated its hospitality". The epigram falls just before Table 2. Cut it, or turn it into the call-out for the table.
11. **Conclusion ¶2 (l. 149):** "The argument catches an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one". The sentence grades the reach of the paper's own argument. It also repeats the refrain from Test ¶4, "satisfy guests and harm no one" (refrain also R3).
12. **Conclusion ¶5 (l. 155):** "the framework is meant to help organizations keep those gains". The closer is empty, and 'framework' survives again (also R1, R3). The paper's last sentence then brings in a new metaphor: "the obligation of hosting still has an address".

## Part 3, line-level rewrites

These are suggestions, drafted by Claude, for the authors' read-aloud; none should be applied unread. Word changes are body words.

**1. Introduction ¶2, l. 20 (+32).**
Original: "The phygital literature presents such systems as means to a smoother and more personal experience (Batat 2024, 2026; Brochado et al. 2026) and describes them by what they do, as tools, mediators, facilitators and co-creators (van Doorn et al. 2017; Li et al. 2021; Xing and Zhang 2026), without saying what the party that deploys them owes the guest."
Suggestion: "Phygital research treats such systems as means to a smoother experience (Batat 2024: 1220) and names them by what they do, as social presences, mediators, facilitators and co-creators (van Doorn et al. 2017; Li et al. 2021; Xing and Zhang 2026). Its agendas ask firms to keep algorithmic rules contestable, through escalation and human oversight (Shabnam et al. 2026), and Gursoy (2026: 160) observes that algorithmic governance 'shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics'. Neither says to whom these safeguards are owed, or which of several firms must provide them."
(Gursoy needs a reference entry; Brochado and Batat 2026 leave this sentence.)

**2. Split ¶1, l. 76 (−3).**
Original: "Accounts of phygital hospitality describe the movement between physical and digital touchpoints as fluid and make smoothness a goal of design (Batat 2024: 1220, 1233). On these accounts good technology removes queues, repetition, uncertainty and wasted effort so that the guest moves through the journey without friction (Batat and Hammedi 2023)."
Suggestion: "Accounts of phygital customer experience describe the movement between physical and digital touchpoints as fluid and make smoothness a goal of design (Batat 2024: 1220, 1233). For Batat and Hammedi, 'flawless interactivity and connectivity are thus necessary' (2023: 14), and a phygital perspective 'smooths customers' transitions' (2023: 24)."

**3. Split ¶1, l. 76 (+2; the developed version is Best spend, S2).**
Original: "The movement can as accurately be called a relay: physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts, a key card that opens or does not, a price on the screen at the desk. A relay raises a question that fluidity hides, namely who holds authority, and who keeps room for judgement, at each handoff."
Suggestion: "Batat (2024: 1230–1231) calls the two directions digitalization and physicalization. Read as handoffs, they form a relay: physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts, a key card that opens or does not, a price on the screen at the desk. Batat (2024: 1234) finds a similar asymmetry in the phygital framework, where failures by human connectors 'cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors'; on our account, what cannot be replaced is the authority a human connector may or may not carry."

**6. Design ¶1, l. 127, opening (−25).**
Original: "Researchers disagree about whether guests should see the technology at all. ## Who I am

I work on phygital customer experience and transformative service research. My questions are how firms blend the physical, human and digital parts of a service, and what that blend does to customers' well-being. I use PH-CX and the extended-reality framework in my own studies, and I treat smoothness as a design goal that needs a reason, not as a value in itself. I came to this paper expecting two things. I expected a hospitality-theory paper that would caricature my field as naive about seamlessness. I also expected 'phygital' to be a label laid over a paper about algorithms. The paper persuaded me that its core question, to whom a safeguard is owed and which firm owes it, is one my field has not asked. It also irritated me, because it states that gap in a form my field can refute from the paper's own reference list.

## Where it is strong

- **The question.** To whom a safeguard is owed, and which of several firms owes it, is a question phygital service research and TSR do not ask. Gursoy stops at accountability for harm (p. 160). Shabnam et al. stop at escalation for fairness and trust (PDF p. 14). Once it is stated precisely, this is a real contribution to my field, not only to hospitality theory.
- **A test that measures neither satisfaction nor harm** (Test ¶1, l. 86). This separates the paper cleanly from service-quality and customer-experience instruments. Keep the sentence.
- **Knowledge, discretion and authority as three things that come apart** (Split ¶4, l. 82). A service designer can use this decomposition directly when building a service blueprint.
- **Table 1's rule on timing.** It ties the need for a reachable person to whether a decision can be undone in time (Baum and Laux). That is more precise than anything in the phygital agendas, which say that escalation should exist but never when.
- **The relay**, once credited and developed. It is the passage that makes this a phygital paper.
- **The Wilson-Nash material**, which is accurately quoted and paged (also R1).

**Rewrite 1, +32 words.** This narrows the absence claim in Introduction ¶2; see Part 3.

> [SUGGESTION] Batat (2024: 1229) defines phygital as an ecosystem of physical, human, digital and media elements. A phygital arrangement adds the firms that own, brand, operate and supply those elements, because one of them holds the host's obligation.

**S2, Split ¶1 (l. 76), replacing the two relay sentences. +34 words net.**
> [SUGGESTION] Batat (2024: 1230–1231) calls the two directions of that movement digitalization and physicalization. Read as handoffs, they form a relay: physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts, a key card that opens or does not, a price on the screen at the desk. The guest meets the ruling on the physical side, where a claim arises; the power to change it sits on the digital side, often in another firm. Where the phygital framework follows consumer value through each shift (Batat 2024: 1238), the relay follows authority.

**S3, Design ¶3 (l. 131), after "along with the annoyance". +43 words.**

## Verdict

**Minor revision, at the heavy end.** The argument stands, and in my lane every repair fits within the budget. But the paper presents itself to a phygital special issue through an absence claim that its own sources refute, a central idea borrowed from the lead framework without credit, and several of the editors' works described as they do not read.

**The single most important fix:** replace the absence claim in Introduction ¶2 with the narrower gap the sources support, and correct or remove the phygital citations that misdescribe their sources (Batat 2026, Batat and Hammedi 2023, Brochado et al. 2026, van Doorn et al. 2017), before the editors read it.
