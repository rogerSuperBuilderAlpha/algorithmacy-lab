## Part 1. Rigour and argument, section by section

### Introduction

Introduction ¶1 is the best-calibrated paragraph in the paper on technology. It separates the technologies and their jobs. It also gets revenue management right: "Revenue-management systems recommend the price a guest is quoted" (line 18) is exactly what Garcia et al. found, since their algorithm advises "human managers who retain the final decision right" (card, working paper p. 5). The trouble is that Test ¶4 forgets this sentence (see below). The Belanche clause, "who hold the firm responsible when the robots fail" (line 18), turns vignette respondents into guests. Belanche et al. ran scenario experiments with 331 US participants imagining a hotel reception (pp. 273–274). The firm effect is a modest main effect, and the robot effect reaches significance only in the failure condition (pp. 275–277). In Study 2 it disappears for analytical robots (p. 282). The fix costs no words: "respondents asked to imagine a robot at a hotel desk held the firm more responsible".

Introduction ¶2 makes the paper's absence claim: the phygital literature describes systems "without saying what the party that deploys them owes the guest" (line 20). Two of the three works cited for it, Li et al. (2021) and Xing and Zhang (2026), are carded at abstract depth only, so the absence is unchecked for them. More damaging, the 2025–2026 hospitality-technology literature has come closer than the sentence allows, and v6 has dropped the papers that show it. I take these up under Design and in The read, finding 4. R1 made the parallel point for Shabnam et al. (also R1, issue 8).

Introduction ¶4 sets aside whether a machine can bear a host's duties, citing only Kropf et al. (2026: 7), a paper on care robots. A referee from hospitality technology will ask why the paper cites social robotics here and not Sharma and Mattila (2026), a *Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research* essay on the rights and responsibilities of hospitality service robots. v4 cited it, and its card is full-text. Sharma and Mattila frame robot responsibility as "business governance and risk management" (p. 915), aimed at "clarifying accountability (liability, safety, data protection, consent)" (p. 904). That framing is the harm-indexed account the paper sets itself against in Introduction ¶2. Restoring it costs about 15 words and strengthens the contrast.

### The host's power and the host's obligation

This section is outside my field, and I leave the legal history to panelist 05. Two points touch it from mine.

First, HO ¶10 generalizes one hotel into an industry practice: "hotels ask guests to leave who will not follow the stay's rules" (line 46). Xiang et al. studied one upscale hotel in south-eastern China, under state-imposed pandemic rules. Its card says to cite the study "for the form of the encounter, not for guest compliance norms". R3 flagged only the word order. The substance is that the sentence needs "in one hotel under pandemic rules". That costs about six words.

Second, HO ¶8 holds an unused key to the paper's own revenue-management case. Holt's dissent in Lane v. Cotton allows that "If the Inn be full ... the Action would not lie for such Refusal" (card, p. 485; long s modernized), and line 42 cites it. In a modern hotel the commonest way an algorithm refuses a confirmed guest is, in my experience, the oversold arrival. A revenue-management system sets an overbooking level, and the desk turns the guest away on arrival, usually in the evening. Holt's excuse assumed a house that filled. A house that a pricing system fills beyond capacity by design is a different case, and the role account can say why. A search of `library/cards/` for "overbook" and "walk" finds only the 1956 Act's card, and nothing there covers overbooking or the industry's practice of "walking" a guest to another hotel at the hotel's cost. A sentence on it needs a source, so I tag it [CARRY FORWARD] in The read, finding 7.

### Delegation and standing

DS ¶9 describes Xiang et al. accurately. In v6 the manager-approved step is a compensation, with the hotel paying for the test, and not a waiver of the rule. The card's correction has been carried into the text, and the sentence should stay as it is.

DS ¶5 argues that the duty stays with the holder while a machine performs the work, and it cites only philosophy for this (Kong; Kropf et al.). The paper already cites evidence from my field that would support the argument, and that evidence sits unused in Introduction ¶1. Belanche et al. explain their firm-attribution result by saying that "robots are perceived to be more representative of a firm than employees" (p. 281). On their evidence, people already look past the machine to the party that deployed it. That is the role account's prediction, observed. It costs about 40 words, and I draft the sentence under Best spend.

### Assembled hosts: The authority–answerability split

Split ¶1's relay is the paper's most accurate sentence about operations. "Digital rulings come back as physical facts, a key card that opens or does not" (line 76). Anyone who has worked a front desk will recognize it. It should survive every revision, and R1 is right that it belongs in the conclusion (also R1).

Split ¶3, "Authority in commercial hospitality has long been graded and organizational" (line 80), is correct and answers R3.9. It rests on a manager's quotation (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1297), the commercial home and Crinkley, and hospitality management's own literature on this point is missing. Hewagama et al. (2019), in *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, find that service recovery in hotels is "carefully stage-managed and 'staircased'", with empowerment "deliberately graduated according to rank" (abstract; card abstract-only). That is graded authority, measured in hotels. Hales and Klidas (1998) separate empowerment as "'choice'", more discretion over how work is done, from "'voice'", "greater involvement in organizational decision-making" (abstract; card abstract-only). That is very nearly the paper's split in Split ¶4: "Discretion acts on the single case and authority on the rule" (line 82). A referee who knows the empowerment literature will read the discretion/authority distinction as choice/voice renamed. Citing Hales and Klidas also mends the two senses of authority that R1 and R3 found, because it gives the two levels names the field already uses. Its card warns, rightly, that the paper "should not claim Hales and Klidas made it" (the parallel). The cost is about 35 words for both sources. Both are abstract-only, so the sentences must claim no more than the abstracts.

Split ¶4's "more of their day goes to reading decisions made elsewhere" (line 82) is supported by neither source cited. The Pedersen and Pors card says the trend clause "is not measured or claimed in the paper". The Spektor et al. card says the time cost in their hotel was documentation labour, "not reading decisions". The Spektor card adds that the study hotel is a counter-case. There, the union contract secured housekeepers' right to sequence their own rooms (§5.1.1, §6.1). Cut the trend clause. That saves about six words. (Panelist 04 may make the same point.)

The knowledge-drift sentence lists what a hotel system holds: "a guest's history, preferences, spending and loyalty status alongside the property's inventory". The list is accurate to a property-management and CRM stack. But its sources are a study of freelancers on Upwork (Rahman 2021) and an abstract-only law-review article on Uber (Calo and Rosenblat 2017). The Rahman card calls the hotel particulars "the authors' application". This is acceptable if the sentence owns the application.

**The cases (Test ¶4, line 99).** This paragraph is where the paper meets practice, and in all four cases the sentence says more than the source. The referees caught two of them; I add two more and a structural problem.

The pricing case has three problems beyond the desk gloss.

*It cannot tell algorithmic pricing from the pricing that came before it.* Question 2 asks "Could the guest have anticipated it", and whether the guest is "told when it changes". No hotel guest has been able to anticipate a demand-based room price, with or without an algorithm. So pricing fails predictability whatever system sets it, and the case shows nothing about algorithms. A revenue manager would say the predictable thing for a guest is the rate's terms: what is included, what cancellation costs, and what will be charged. The rule that produced the number is not. The predictability condition needs that distinction for pricing (about 25 words).

*The legal clause misfits.* "although its legal weight is moderate unless the price bars a guest" (line 99) cites Article 29 Working Party (2018: 22). That page concerns "differential pricing based on personal data or personal characteristics", and it says such pricing "could" be significant. The card records that the guidance "makes no negative claim", so "moderate" is not the regulators' word. Garcia's manager also keeps the final decision, so the decision is not "solely automated" in the sense of art. 22(1) at all. Cut the clause, which saves about 20 words, or say that Article 22 does not reach a price a manager sets.

*Read properly, the case shows the split better than the gloss does.* Authority over the price exists. It sits with a revenue manager who keeps the final decision and reprices about once a month (WP p. 14). In chains that manager is often off property. That last point is my experience, not the source's. The question is whether a guest can reach that manager. R1 suggested this reframing, and I endorse it. It also shows graded authority without any algorithm, which is the paper's own point in Split ¶3.

The check-in case's verdict is wrong on the paper's own questions. For a biometric system, question 3 ("Who can see how it was made?") and question 6 ("What does refusing the system cost the guest?") bear hard on the "clerical" variant too. A guest who declines facial recognition either gives up a face template or queues at a slower desk. Wilson-Nash et al. call an analogue route that is "retained but downgraded" a trigger of captivity (p. 978). Both carded hotel studies of facial-recognition check-in are framed around privacy (Xu et al.; Boo and Chua; both abstract-only), and the paper never mentions privacy. A sentence that confined facial recognition to clerical and admission uses would misdescribe what its literature studies. The fix is to drop "and barely on the first", which saves about five words. Better, say that the confirming system still owes question 6 an answer.

The restaurant case does not test what the paper says it tests. Introduction ¶1 defines an algorithmic system as "software that takes or shapes a decision about a guest". Le Petit Chef is a projection whose purpose, per its producers, is "to make the waiting moments in restaurants more pleasant" (Batat 2021: 4). It takes no decision about any diner, so on the paper's own definition it is not an algorithmic system, and its "pass" is empty. Batat's own caveat does raise a question-6 problem: "in some situations, such as a business diner or a romantic diner, the use of AR could be a distraction" (p. 9). Yet the source cannot say what declining the show costs. A restaurant case that tested the six questions would need a decision. Examples are a booking platform's deposit and no-show charge, a fixed table-time limit, or QR ordering that removes the server. These are my examples, and none is carded. So either relabel the AR case as a contrast in which nothing is decided, or replace it with a sourced restaurant decision [CONSIDER] (also R3, who noted that restaurants rest on one AR case).

The Bosma case improves if it uses what Bosma reports. On p. 601 the platform splits "host" into "primary hosts", "listing admins or owners" and "co-hosts". On p. 605 smart-lock providers integrate with the platform. A guest locked out at night therefore faces a real question with four possible answers: owner, co-host, lock provider or platform. That is the assembled host from DS ¶3 in a form an operations reader would recognize. R3 adds Bosma's business-traveller requirement as evidence that the platform holds the rule (also R3).

**The employee paragraph (Test ¶5, line 101).** Almost every hotel-specific claim in this paragraph misdescribes its source. None of the three referees checked these sources.

- *Bendoly (2013).* v6 cites it for "Skilled front-desk employees manufacture a guest's sense of control". Bendoly's abstract describes a laboratory experiment with reservation agents who may accept "rate bids below the pricing prescriptions" of a revenue-management system. Their deviation is "influenced by their perceptions of how their actions will be viewed by higher levels of management." The card is abstract-only. It calls the front-desk use "a mis-citation at abstract level" and records no open copy against which to check the full text. v6 has added "appearing to defer while steering the options", which is more specific than the v4 sentence the card already rejected. Any revenue-management referee will know Bendoly. Worse, Bendoly's real finding cuts against the pricing case. It shows reservation agents given sanctioned latitude below the system's price, graded by what management will tolerate. Some people near the desk do have authority over the single price. That finding belongs in the pricing case.
- *Jianu et al. (2025).* "managers carry the system's decisions to the people they affect" reads as observed practice. The study is a three-round Delphi forecast. Its panel of 22 recruits included nine academics (pp. 4–5). The translation it forecasts runs to "frontline staff" (p. 10), not to guests. The card marks this use "PARTLY", and the pairing with the desk employee in v6 implies guests. Write "experts expect line managers to explain algorithmic decisions to frontline staff (Jianu et al. 2025: 10)" (no extra words).
- *Hatherley (2025).* Hatherley supports "the knowledge it rests on loses its value overnight" (pp. 5, 7). But "no one tells the employee or the guest" is the authors' addition, since his users are clinicians, and his concern is change they cannot follow even when told: users "are informed about changes" under model reporting (p. 10), yet that "may not reduce the epistemic or safety risks of update opacity" (p. 11).
- *Fuller and Smith (1991).* The card has only the abstract, which is about managers using customer feedback to discipline workers. It judges the "sense of control" use "NOT CHECKABLE, and doubtful". VERIFY or cut.
- *Cheng et al. (2025).* This is the only hospitality finding in the paragraph, and it is abstract-only.

Hotel evidence that would carry the paragraph is already carded. Hewagama et al. found authority over recovery staircased by rank (abstract). Spektor et al. report a housekeeping platform whose configuration a union contract had to win back (§5.1.1). In Xiang et al., a front-office employee says "the only thing we can do is explain the government's and subdistrict's anti-epidemic policies to guests again and again" (Interviewee_H8). Rebuilding Test ¶5 on these sources is close to word-neutral, and R2 has already asked for the paragraph to be shortened [FIX].

### Assembled hosts: When a person must be reachable

Reachable ¶1 takes the right axis from Baum and Laux: whether intervention can come "before the token output becomes effective" (2026: 120), not elapsed time. That is the axis a front-office manager uses without naming it. Can it be fixed before the guest is standing in the corridor? This criterion should survive.

**Table 1, read by a revenue manager and a front-office manager.** The column "Reversible in time?" runs together two things operations keep apart: undoing a decision and compensating for it. Most rows fail on that confusion.

- **Admission; a booking not honoured** (line 113). The recourse, "A reachable person with authority before refusal takes effect", is right. But the row lumps a door refusal with an oversold arrival, and the industry already answers the second with a staffed procedure: relocate the guest at the hotel's cost. This is my operational knowledge, not carded. The duty to receive, applied to an oversold house, is a duty to find the guest a bed, not only to hear them. On cancellations made before arrival, see R2.
- **Safety and access** (line 114). The row says "Often not; some too fast for any person". A lock-out is not too fast for anyone. It is slow and it persists, lasting until someone acts, at any hour. It belongs in the reachable-person class with a response measured in minutes. Fire and life-safety releases are too fast, but codes govern them, not the host's discretion. Ejection is never too fast. It is a staffed decision in practice, and it is where the guest's duties in HO ¶10 matter (also R3, who found those duties unused). Baum and Laux's "too fast" example is emergency braking in a vehicle (card, p. 122). The paper gives no hotel example, and I doubt there is a common one. Split the row (about 25 words).
- **Identity verification** (line 115). The row reads "Yes, if a staffed route exists". The staffed route is reversible only if it is independent of the system that failed. A night auditor who cannot cut a key without the verification step is not a fallback. In app-only and select-service hotels the staffed route at night is often a remote support line run by a vendor. On the paper's own allocation in DS ¶3, that vendor is an arranger that "answers to the host", not to the guest. Biometric capture also cannot be undone once taken. That is what the hotel facial-recognition literature studies (both carded abstracts concern privacy). "Low as verification" also ignores that some jurisdictions oblige hotels to register guests' identity, so a failed check can lawfully block admission. That point is VERIFY: it is not carded.
- **Room assignment** (line 116). "Mostly" reversible is true only while inventory remains. At a full house at eleven at night, a room that a guest's need makes unusable cannot be swapped. "A later appeal by default" can then only compensate, because the night is spent. Recourse should be a reachable person the same evening, with "Mostly" defined as "while rooms remain" (also R3, who asked for "Mostly" and "Partly" to be defined).
- **Pricing** (line 117). The row says "Yes" reversible, and that is the column's clearest error. A price can be changed, but a guest who booked a non-refundable rate at it, or booked elsewhere, cannot be restored. A refund compensates; it does not reverse. "Rules the host can explain" raises the problem I set out under Test ¶4: the explainable party is the revenue manager, and the rule is not what a guest can use. On a price that bars access at night, see R2.
- **Routine requests and information** (line 119). "Automation, under rules set in advance" assumes the class of request is known before the system acts. In practice a chatbot or messaging system does the triage. A broken lock reported in chat can be filed as a routine request. Who classifies the request is itself a decision, and it needs a way out to a person. Shabnam et al. describe consumers who "may first interact with chatbots or recommender systems before escalating to human agents" (PDF p. 12). The table needs a clause on misrouted requests, about 20 words.

**"Where a staffed fallback works."** The paper never says, and the evidence it has, or could have, is all from settings where staff are already present. Pigac et al.'s luxury guests valued systems "that allowed concierges to override or explain AI decisions" (p. 291). Schmidt et al. stationed a robot at a working hotel's reception "offering guests the choice to interact with it or human staff" (p. 565). They found "indifferent effects" on the hospitality experience (p. 571), and they add that robots "are not yet ready for all hospitality tasks, such as handling payments or managing check-ins" (p. 572). The staffed fallback is evidenced in luxury and full-service daytime reception, where it is least needed. Night shifts, select-service hotels, app-only hotels and self-check-in rentals are where the paper needs it, and there it has no evidence. Table 1 should state four conditions for a real fallback:

1. Someone is reachable at the hour the decision lands.
2. The fallback is independent of the failing system.
3. It is staffed by someone with authority to complete the process (cut a key, admit, comp a night).
4. It is not downgraded in price, time or dignity (Wilson-Nash p. 978; also R1, issue 5).

That costs about 40 words [FIX].

**An absence claim one citation refutes.** Reachable ¶3 says "no study makes them for hospitality" (line 121). Ivanov and Webster (2024), in *Technology in Society*, asked 130 Bulgarian hoteliers whether 23 decisions in 8 hotel departments should be taken by "'human only', 'human-in-the-loop', 'human-on-the-loop' and 'human-out-of-the-loop'" (abstract; card abstract-only). That is the loop vocabulary the paper takes from Baum and Laux, applied to hotel decisions. Its criterion differs: "decisions that do not require much emotional intelligence or interactions with customers/employees" go to AI (abstract). Table 1's criterion is the guest's recourse. A referee in my field will know Ivanov's work. Replace the absence claim with one sentence that cites the study and states the difference (net about +25 words) [FIX]. The card says to read the open-access PDF before relying on more than the abstract.

**The legal floor (Reachable ¶4).** "The law therefore secures a later review, not a person at the threshold" (line 123) holds only inside the art. 22(2) exceptions. Outside them the Working Party reads art. 22(1) as "a general prohibition" that "applies whether or not the data subject takes an action" (WP29 p. 19). A solely automated facial-recognition refusal outside the contract exception would then be barred, not merely appealable. Add "Where a decision falls within these exceptions" (about seven words). (Panelist 05 may make the same point.)

### Assembled hosts: Designing for interruptible hospitality

Design ¶1 misdescribes Pigac et al. "since guests prefer little disclosure at check-in and fuller explanation later in the stay" (line 127) has three faults.

- The locator, p. 286, is the authors' Dynamic Transparency Protocol, a design they propose. It is not a finding about guests. The guest finding is on p. 291.
- "Later in the stay" is wrong. The source places deeper explanation in "Post-stay feedback and loyalty apps" (p. 291) and calls it "retrospective" (p. 286).
- The evidence points against the principle it is offered for. "Explain a decision where it lands" is followed by evidence that at check-in, where several of the paper's decisions land, guests "preferred minimal interruption and trusted hotel staff to mediate technology" (p. 291).

Pigac et al. interviewed 50 guests of four luxury brands (p. 287) and concern personalization, not adverse decisions. The principle survives if it becomes "explain a decision when the guest can use the explanation". That costs no extra words; see Part 3.

The same paper is a 2026 collision the draft does not engage. Pigac et al.'s guests accept AI "as long as the people can still step in" (p. 291), and the authors advise firms to train frontline staff to "interpret AI behaviors, override where necessary, and reassure guests" (p. 292). That is the paper's first institutional principle, "reachable authority to reverse", already published in *Cornell Hospitality Quarterly*. The card supplies the difference: Pigac et al. have "zero hits for contest, appeal, dispute", and their override exists to reassure guests. In this paper the override answers to a guest with standing. Saying so costs about 45 words and turns a rival into support. I draft the sentence under Best spend.

Design ¶1 also cites Hatherley for "make significant rule changes visible". The card records that Hatherley judges announcing updates insufficient: such information "may not reduce the epistemic or safety risks of update opacity" (p. 11). The source argues against the remedy it is cited for. Keep the principle and concede the limit (about eight words).

Design ¶2. "not a human presence as such but someone with the power to change the answer" (line 129) is an empirical claim about guests, and the sentence states it without support. The library has the support. Martin and Waldman found that "human in the loop" lowered perceived legitimacy relative to mere notice, while appeal to an internal review board raised it (card, Results). Pigac et al.'s Theme 4 is guest testimony to the same effect (p. 291). One of these citations, placed here, would turn the aphorism into a finding. On the Martin and Waldman wording, see R3. On Wilson-Nash, the quotations are exact (pp. 976, 978, 979, 981), and R1 confirmed them. One thing is missing for hospitality. Wilson-Nash's captivity applies where "the consumer cannot walk away" (p. 982), and a hotel guest choosing where to stay can walk away; the source calls that switching or forgoing (p. 975). A guest is captive once booked, prepaid and on site, at night. That is exactly Table 1's boundary, and one clause could join the two (about 15 words). The article's one hospitality mention, "self-service hospitality check-ins" as a digital-only route (p. 974), would anchor the check-in case (also R1).

Design ¶3 defines interruptibility, and a 2025 paper collides with it directly. Padigar et al. (2025), in *Psychology & Marketing*, class contacting customer service "when there is a problem" among tasks that are "relatively low task value", where reducing friction "can enhance customer experience" (p. 32). On their typology, the guest's interruption is friction to remove. A marketing referee will put this to the paper. One sentence can concede the classification and argue that the complaint's value lies with the guest's standing, not with the task (about 30 words) [CONSIDER]. (Panelist 02 may make the same point.)

Design ¶4 and Table 2: on stage order and the forum, see R1, R2 and R3. From operations I would add one thing. The cost ordering is asserted and may be wrong. A delegated-authority matrix, the limits within which a desk agent may comp a night or move a guest, is cheap, and most full-service hotels already have one. That is my experience, not carded. Keeping a staffed alternative around the clock (stage 5) is the costliest item on any property's labour budget. If the order is cost, stage 4 may come before stage 3. If the order is not cost, Table 2's title should not say it is.

### Conclusion

Conclusion ¶2 says "the Act lists no hospitality use as high-risk" (line 149). This is true of guest-facing uses in Annex III's text (card, PDF p. 127). But Annex III point 4(b) covers systems used "to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits or characteristics or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons" in work relationships. On its wording, that may reach the housekeeping platforms the paper cites from Spektor et al., which document worker progress in the app (§5.3). The card calls staff-facing systems "the textual hook". The qualification costs about 20 words and helps the employee-side argument. Whether a given housekeeping system meets 4(b) is a legal question: VERIFY. R1 and R2 raised the separate question of biometric categories (also R1, R2).

Conclusion ¶4 says "the studies of unstaffed hotels we found measure acceptance and satisfaction" (line 153). No card in `library/cards/` covers an unstaffed or app-only hotel study; my search of the cards for "unstaffed", "staffless" and "contactless" found only Bosma. R3 asked for the studies to be cited (also R3). My addition is that they are not on record at all. A referee in my field will know the literature on "unmanned" and smart hotels. The authors must card the studies they mean and cite at least one [CARRY FORWARD] (about 10 words).

## Part 2. Slop audit

I count tics only to find the passages. The question is whether each passage reads as a scholar's.

**Test ¶4 (line 99) reads as a verdict generator.** Each case gets one sentence of setting and one of verdict, and the paragraph ends "Together the cases show". A scholar writing for this journal would give each case one concrete fact from its source: Garcia's 35-day repricing against weekly recommendations, Bosma's business-traveller rule, Batat's diners who found the show a distraction at a romantic dinner. Then the verdict would follow from the fact. At present the verdicts outnumber the facts. That ratio, more than anything lexical, makes the paragraph read as machine-made.

**Test ¶5 (line 101) is a list with full stops.** It has six subjects in six clauses: skilled employees, that work, the translation, staff, employees, frontline employees. Each carries a separate citation and no grouping. This is mechanism 8 failed, and most of its citations are the misdescribed ones above. Its last sentence, "It is useful, therefore, to ask", does not follow (also R3).

**Aphoristic closers that carry unproved claims.** Three paragraph endings sound like findings but are not supported:

- "not a human presence as such but someone with the power to change the answer" (line 129) is an empirical claim stated as a maxim.
- "A system built never to interrupt the guest can become one the guest cannot interrupt" (line 131) is a chiasmus. It is memorable, but it does the work an argument should do.

## Who I am

I study hospitality technology: service robots, self-service and biometric check-in, revenue-management systems, and how guests and staff accept them. Most of my work sits in the acceptance tradition that this paper argues past: technology-acceptance models, privacy calculus, vignette experiments on robot failure. I came to the paper believing that tradition has a blind spot. It measures whether guests will use a system and never asks who answers when the system says no. I am sympathetic to the paper's question for that reason. I also came believing that conceptual papers about hotels often get the operations wrong. They picture a front desk with powers it never had, and they treat a price, a room or a key as more reversible than any duty manager would. I read for both.

## The read: findings ranked by damage

**1. The four cases attribute the authors' scenarios to their sources** (Test ¶4, line 99). The paper presents the cases as illustrations drawn from published studies. In every case the decisive sentence is the authors' own:

- Garcia et al. never mention the desk.
- Xu et al. describe no system that weighs a guest's history.
- Bosma says nothing about codes failing at night.
- Batat does not address rules or staff discretion.

The pricing case also cannot tell algorithmic pricing from the pricing that came before it, because predictability fails for any demand-based rate. Its legal clause misapplies Article 29 Working Party (2018: 22) to a decision a manager takes. The restaurant case involves no decision about a guest, so on the paper's own definition it has no algorithmic system to test. A referee from hospitality technology will find all of this on a first read, and it is the paper's only contact with practice. *Cost:* close to word-neutral; rewrites 3–6 in Part 3 replace sentences one for one, and cutting the WP29 clause saves about 20 words. **[FIX]** for the attributions. **[CONSIDER]** whether to replace the AR case with a sourced restaurant decision. (Garcia and Xu: also R1, R2, R3. Bosma, Batat, the predictability problem and WP29: new.)

**2. The employee paragraph misdescribes its sources** (Test ¶5, line 101).

- Bendoly (2013) is cited for front-desk staff "appearing to defer while steering the options". The study is a laboratory experiment on reservation agents' latitude below the revenue system's price; its card calls the use a mis-citation.
- Jianu et al. (2025) is cited as practice; it is a Delphi forecast about translation to staff.
- Hatherley's "no one tells" is the authors' addition.
- Fuller and Smith is doubtful on its own card.

Bendoly's actual finding also undercuts the pricing case, because it shows sanctioned price discretion near the desk. *Cost:* word-neutral or a saving if the paragraph is rebuilt on Hewagama et al., Spektor et al. and Xiang et al.'s H8. **[FIX]**. (New. R3 flagged only the closing "therefore".)

**3. Table 1's reversibility column would not survive an operations reader** (lines 109–119). It confuses undoing with compensating. Pricing is not reversible once a guest books or leaves. Room assignment is reversible only while rooms remain. A lock-out is slow, not "too fast for any person". Biometric capture cannot be undone. The "staffed fallback" is unspecified, and in the hotels where it matters it is often a vendor's remote line. On the paper's own allocation that vendor answers to the host, not the guest. The routine-requests row ignores misrouted triage. *Cost:* about 40 words for the four fallback conditions, about 25 to split the safety row, and redefinitions within the cells. **[FIX]**. (Partly also R1, issue 5, the downgraded fallback; R2, the price that bars access; R3, define "Mostly" and "Partly". The undo/compensate confusion, lock-outs, independence of the fallback, the vendor line and triage are new.)

**4. The 2025–2026 hospitality-technology literature collides with the positioning and is not engaged.**

- Gursoy (2026), in *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management*, writes that algorithmic governance "shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics, raising questions about accountability when harm, bias, or exclusion arise" (p. 160). He recommends "human-led resolution" and letting guests "choose their preferred level of human or AI engagement" (p. 157). Those are roadmap stages 4 and 5 in a hospitality journal's words. The card flags Gursoy "must-engage". v4 cited him; v6 does not.
- Pigac et al. (2026) already recommend staff who "override where necessary" (p. 292), and v6 misdescribes the one thing it takes from them.
- Sharma and Mattila (2026) frame hospitality robots' responsibilities as governance (pp. 904, 915) and were dropped.
- Padigar et al. (2025) class contacting service about a problem as friction that can be reduced (p. 32).

The paper's difference from all four survives. They tie accountability to harm, trust or governance, and none asks whether the guest can reach the holder. But the paper must say so, or the Introduction ¶2 absence claim falls. *Cost:* about 80–120 words for Gursoy, Pigac and one of the other two. Paid for by cutting the staged Mosca debate in Design ¶1 (about 65 words). **[FIX]** for Pigac's misdescription; **[CONSIDER]** for which collisions to engage. (Partly also R1, issue 8, on Shabnam; the rest is new.)

## Verdict

**Major revision**, though nearly all of it is a careful author's week. The argument does not need new data from my field. It needs its cases and its table to say only what their sources and hotel operations support. As they stand, a referee from hospitality technology would find a misdescribed source in almost every sentence that touches practice.

**The single most important fix:** rewrite Test ¶4 and Test ¶5 so that each case states one fact its source reports and labels every scenario as the authors' own. Reframe the pricing case as authority that exists, with the revenue manager, but cannot be reached from the desk.
