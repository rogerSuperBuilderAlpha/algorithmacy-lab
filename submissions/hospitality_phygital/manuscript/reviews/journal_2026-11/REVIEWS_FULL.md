Reviewer 1 : Hospitality and Society 
Thank you for the opportunity to review the manuscript “Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality”. The conceptual manuscript builds on the concepts of phygital experiences and the roles of hosts and guests within hospitality. It addresses the topical phenomenon of phygital (hybrid) journeys, which are increasingly part of service design and delivery. The manuscript questions the implications of phygital for hospitality — specifically, it highlights how phygital arrangements augment the traditional roles of hosts (duties and obligations) and guests (standing, expectations and rights to negotiate), and the redistribution of agency, represented in this manuscript as knowledge, discretion and authority. The manuscript, therefore, boldly offers a counter-rhetoric to phygital hospitality arrangements, arguing that the goal of providing seamless guest experiences can strip the central concept of hosting in hospitality studies of its core, eroding the very aspects that drive value creation for guests. The author(s) introduce an interesting concept of “interruptability”. 
The authors provide a guideline/litmus test to distinguish when phygital touchpoints can be used to help or replace traditional hosts in hospitality contexts/institutions. The authors further contribute a meso-level 5-stage roadmap for integrating phygital touchpoints, proposing the need for institutions to strike a balance between automation and preservation of meaningful alternatives in ongoing institutional operations. Collectively, contributing to our theoretical understanding of phygital hospitality. In summary, the manuscript does contribute to theory and has implications for practice, making it suitable for publication. 
The article is generally well structured, and concepts used are suitable and well synthesised. Therefore, my suggestions for improvement are minimal. The article cites a good amount of prior work to support its conceptualisation, drawing on key literature in phygital, hospitality, and services. However, there is an article not mentioned in the reference that may be useful to include in the discussion: Wilson-Nash, C., Angell, R., Qiu, Y., Pavlopoulou, I., & Kolyperas, D. (2026). No App, No Entry: Conceptualizing Digital Technology Captivity in Service Access. Psychology and Marketing, 1–12. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.70098. 
Although, there are articles that reference hospitality, the manuscript could benefit from articles that reference host-guest negotiation inherent in the hospitality literature. To better highlight the interactions between host and guest that are more than functional and add to the creation of value for guests. This can perhaps be inserted in Section 3: The missing counterparty.   
In Section 4 the author(s) discuss of the “split”, given the gap between the first and later mentions, it may be helpful to remind the reader what is being referenced as the split. 
Section 5 is an important section that provides a framework/test for deploying/integrating digital touchpoints, where it can either replace or augment roles of hosting in hospitality. Some discussion around the test and the purpose of the test will be helpful. At the moment it mentions a test but provides the reader with little context/preamble. Given that this is a key contribution the presentation of the test in the abstract can also be improved. 
The author(s) describe a framework in the fourth paragraph of Section 8 Discussion, but it is not clear what is being referred to. Is there a framework that is meant to be included, or is it in reference to one of the tables? The reader cannot quite connect with what is being discussed. There are no words that help signal connection to any of the tables/framework provided. The word framework is repeated again in the second last paragraph of Section 8, but the reader suspects this is in reference to Table 2.    
A complete round of proofreading is highly recommended. There were parts where the author(s) use the terms “it” and “this”, with little follow-on context for the reader, making it difficult to decipher the intentions of the author(s). 
I hope that the feedback is useful. 

Reviewer 2 : Hospitality and Society
1. I believe the paper needs to explain more clearly where the host’s obligation comes from. This is key to the argument, especially in Figure 1. The manuscript argues that hosting functions can move to algorithms while the obligation to the guest remains with the host, but it does not fully explain why that obligation remains, who actually holds it, or what makes it non-transferable. In commercial hospitality, this becomes especially important because responsibility may sit with the hotel company, operator, employee, platform, vendor, or several actors at once. 
2. In its current version, the paper's contribution depends on the claim that functional delegation does not remove answerability. I suggest developing a clearer theory of role obligation in commercial hospitality, concerning what activates the obligation, who initially bears it, what can and cannot be delegated, and how responsibility is shared when multiple actors control the guest experience. Without this foundation, the argument risks looking like a more familiar problem of organizational delegation or algorithmic accountability rather than a specific hospitality theory. 
3. At the moment, standing seems to mean that someone is obligated to answer the guest, while accountability also means that someone must answer a guest who has standing. This creates some circularity. It would also help to distinguish between a guest having standing and being able to exercise it. 
4. I urge the authors to show more clearly why hospitality itself matters to the mechanism. Many of the problems discussed here, such as inaccessible decision makers, limited voice, algorithmic opacity, weak appeals, and costly exit, also appear in banking, healthcare, e-commerce, and other service settings. The manuscript therefore needs to explain what is specifically unique about hospitality. 
5. The paper should also clarify the boundary conditions of the framework. In particular, which types of hospitality decisions require immediate interruptibility and answerability, and in which situations are these less important? It would also help to specify which hospitality formats such as hotels, short-term rentals, or highly automated services the framework explains best, and where its explanatory power is more limited. 

Reviewer 3 : Hospitality and Society 
Reviewer notes for both Author and Editor:
Thank you for the chance to read this piece. I think it holds promise but several areas deserve substantial attention. Stylistically, I found it an unusual piece. At times, I felt the conversational style was engaging, but the rhetoric often felt jarring. I usually put points concerning style at the end of my reviews but, in this case, it is helpful to state them at the outset. In the end I wasn’t sure whether AI assistance was the problem or a possible solution. The point is, the style and rhetoric seemed to obscure rather than help the messaging. Opening with the anecdote, asking about how “we talk” about technology (p2), stating that “put that way, the answer is boring” (p2), arguing that the test “condemns the first…blesses the second” (p13); pointing to the “bite” of the six question (p13) and referring to “sitting ducks” (p14) seemed to undermine the paper’s authority rather than add to it. The sentence on page 18 goes on for six lines. 
The point on page 19 regarding the Airbnb experiment and its results, and the closing of the gap was difficult to comprehend. These are just some examples, but there are a lot more where serious editing is required. Just as an observation, I also wasn’t convinced by the presumed ‘boringness’ of the answer on page 2. Sometimes, when referring to the general “we”, the evidence for the claims were unclear. It also revealed all sorts of assumptions by the author(s) that were often unwarranted. For example, when referring to the various conceptions of AI on page 2, you cite Bitner et al. 2000, but I wasn’t persuaded that this citation was credible. There were other leaps and assumptions across the paper. For example, many of the arguments in the front of the paper seemed to treat technology reductively, rather than recognising that it takes multiple forms, especially in a phygital context. This seemed to be better recognised later in the text. You also seem to equate hosting with the hotel reception role, especially at the front. You eventually branched out into other examples, but across the paper, I wondered whether the various claims held up across other types of host(ing) contexts and encounters in the wider hospitality sector. Other assumptions and leaps were present later. For example, you positioned authority as being absolute, whereas that authority is always organisationally constrained. There were other parts where the points were unclear and insufficiently evidenced. For example, the points on page 6 (second paragraph, just before part 3) regarding what guests owe or do were highly questionable. The measures of what “they feel they gain from meeting visitors” seemed irrelevant. And the following points about studying or “measuring guest’s standing” were difficult to decipher. 
On page 8, in reference to the “single duty [that] defines the role” seemed to ignore that welcome does not operate in isolation from its social or organisational context, and is not therefore a singular thing. Parts 6 and 7 meandered and I struggled to follow the thread or point of your arguments in the context of the wider aim of the paper. Again, this is partly a matter of style and sufficient signposting, but there is a bigger point here about the coherence of the argument and its construction. We emerge at the other end of this swirl of rhetoric on page 20, with reference to a “roadmap of rising commitment” and “forum”, both of which remain unqualified. Table 2 needs better and longer explanation to justify its value, and its connection to the previous discussion should be clear. This is key to articulating a contribution and working backwards to ensure the rest of the content builds to this through appropriate evidence and clear arguments. I was unconvinced by the contributions stated in section 8 (p21). There was reference to an emergent question, this time in reference to AI (as opposed to technology), algorithmic systems etc., but it is unclear whether this was the question we were working with throughout the piece or whether this was sufficiently addressed at all. In general, I was left unconvinced that the stated contributions were achieved. On page 22, the discussion mentions that “these distinctions touch well-being.” Firstly, the distinctions are unclear, and the well-being link is not clear at all. The reference to a “direction of travel for research rather than a finished theory” also left me confused. The subsequent reference to the “two levels” also needs clarification. The final paragraph of section 8 on limitations and implications for future research also need to be more detailed and clearer in the agenda they suggest. Similarly, the conclusion should offer a clearer sense of what this paper has achieved in terms of its original questions and set a stronger agenda for what readers should take from this piece.
I found this a difficult paper to review. Usually, the feedback to authors can be clear and precise, but the style and structure of this piece make advice difficult in this instance. I think it has the potential to contribute to knowledge, especially in the context of this SI, but it needs serious revisions to clarify its purpose, provide consistent and convincing evidence, identify its contributions and implications and, importantly, to ensure the message is comprehensible. Right now, it struggles to achieve all these things. I wish the author(s) luck with revising this piece.
