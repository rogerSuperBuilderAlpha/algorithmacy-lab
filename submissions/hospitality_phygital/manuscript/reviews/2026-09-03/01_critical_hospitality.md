## Part 1 — Rigour and argument, section by section, from the critical-hospitality lens

The question I came to answer is whether this paper has said something about hospitality or has borrowed the word to dignify an argument about algorithmic governance. My answer is that it has said something about hospitality, and that it has said it in places where the field's own apparatus would say it better, and that in two of those places it has cited the apparatus and left it idle. The core move, that a party can take on a host's functions without inheriting a host's obligations, I accept. What follows is where the move is exposed and what it would take to cover it.

### §2 From service recipient to guest

This is the section my lens cares most about, and it is mostly sound. The standing paragraph (l.33) does the construct-collision work the last review praised and I have nothing to add to that judgment. The four-word taxonomy at l.35 is honest about which term is borrowed (Bovens) and which are stipulated. The Pijls passage is a good argument in shape.

Now the definition of welcome (l.37): 'recognition, admission, responsiveness, and the capacity for a negotiated exception, all held together.' Is this a definition this journal would recognize? Partly. It is recognizable as a stipulation and the paper says it is one ('in the sense we need'). The field's own vocabulary is close: Lynch et al.'s (2011) antonym pairs (welcome/non-welcome, inclusion/exclusion, conditional/unconditional) map onto admission and exception; Lugosi's (2008) 'hospitable interaction is an acknowledgement of the other' (verified on the card, full-text) is recognition; Derrida's threshold question is the negotiated exception. What the definition leaves out is what this journal's readers would put first: the hospitableness tradition, the host's care for the guest's wellbeing and pleasure in their company (Telfer's chapter in the same Lashley and Morrison volume the paper already cites; no card, so I do not ask you to cite it tonight), Hemmington's generosity and 'security of strangers' (on the card), the affective and embodied welcome in Lugosi and in Lynch's mundane welcome. The paper has not forgotten these. It has moved 'care' into a different duty at l.61 ('Care is owed because someone is in need'), which is a defensible analytic separation, but it never says that it has done so or why. A reader from the hospitableness tradition will feel that the paper's 'welcome' is answerability plus admission, a governance virtue wearing the field's oldest word.

The fix is one sentence that names what is set aside and gives the reason, and the reason is the paper's own thesis: a machine can imitate warmth and cannot confer standing. **[CONSIDER]**, +30 words, rewrite in Part 3. This is the sentence that turns the definition from a stipulation into a position in the field's conversation, and I think it is worth the words.

l.41, Derrida: 'Ethical traditions stress the mutual bond between host and guest, even when it is unequal or brief (Derrida and Dufourmantelle 2000; Lashley 2000).' Here is the answer to the brief's third question. Derrida is decorating. 'Mutual bond' is not his theme; his themes are the aporia between the unconditional law of hospitality and the conditional laws, the host's mastery of the house, and the foreigner who is asked for a name. The card says exactly what the paper wanted Derrida for: 'every real hospitality sets conditions, and the question is who sets them and whether they remain open to discussion', and that use is nowhere in the text. It belongs at l.61, where the paper says a welcome 'asks nothing of the guest except that they be there' and needs the conditional tradition to stop a referee. See §4 below; this is my single most important fix. At l.41, drop Derrida from the parenthesis and let Lashley carry the mutual-bond claim, which is his (the social domain, the obligations of host and guest).

l.41, Pijls: 'It also tried two questions about agency, whether the guest had a choice and whether she felt independent, and dropped both when the scale was tested (Pijls et al. 2017). The field did not simply overlook the guest's standing. It tried to measure it and the questions did not survive.' No card for Pijls. The arm's `CLAIMS.md` (row 10) records: 'Pijls is the paper's most exposed citation: an affect scale, not an agency instrument, whose predecessor "autonomy" dimension = NOT having to ask.' The hedged description of the items is honest. The claim that follows, that the field 'tried to measure it', is not. **[FIX TONIGHT]**, +3 words: 'It tried to measure something next to it, and the questions did not survive.' VERIFY the item wording against Pijls et al. 2017.

The card (abstract plus the conference precursor read in full) says the authors position the reciprocation as 'driven by gratitude rather than by obligation or indebtedness'. ### §4 Redistribution in the phygital arrangement

Mostly holds, and the arrangement-not-gadget move is right. Then l.61, the four-duty passage, which is where my lens bites hardest.

'A welcome is owed for one reason only: someone has arrived. It is the only one of the four triggered by bare presence, asking nothing of the guest except that they be there.'

Does this survive the conditional-hospitality tradition? As written, no. Commercial hospitality is conditional hospitality in its purest form: the arrival is a guest because they have booked, can pay, and can show a name. Derrida's whole point is that every hospitality that is actually practised asks the foreigner for a name and sets terms; only the unconditional law, which no institution can enact, asks nothing. Lashley's commercial domain is conditional on exchange. The paper's own evidence knows this: §3 cites guests 'shut out on the basis of records they never wrote' (Edelman), §5 asks 'what does refusing the system cost the guest', and the vignette's guest has a booking. So 'asking nothing of the guest except that they be there' is false for the domain the paper is about, and a referee raised on Derrida and Bulley will say so in the first paragraph of the report.

The claim underneath is sound and can be rescued in a sentence, because the paper is not really making a claim about what grounds a welcome. It is making a claim about what triggers one: among the four duties, welcome is the one set off by arrival, where care is set off by need, fiduciary duty by trust, and governance by risk. That distinction holds under conditions. And the conditional tradition does not merely tolerate the paper's argument; it supplies its critical edge, which is what the Derrida card says it is there for. Every real welcome sets conditions. The question hospitality theory has always asked is who sets them and whether the arrival can still contest them. That is the paper's 'negotiated exception' and the paper's 'standing' in the field's own words, and it converts the objection into support.

### The core move, accepted and sized

A party can take on a host's functions without inheriting a host's obligations. I accept it. The field already separates the provision of hospitality from hospitableness (the disposition), and the paper's contribution is a third separation: performance from obligation, stated as a relation rather than a disposition. That is why it can answer Lee and Lu and Liu et al. (does the machine seem hospitable?) with a question they do not ask (what does it owe?), and l.61 states the difference cleanly: 'Whether a party seems hospitable and whether it occupies a position that owes the guest something are two different matters, and only the second is independent of what the guest happens to believe.' That sentence is the paper's hospitality contribution in one line and it should survive any edit.

The move is exposed at one point only, the conditional tradition, and the exposure is repairable tonight with the source already in the reference list.

## 1. Who I am

I read and publish in this journal, and I came to this paper expecting what a critical hospitality reader usually finds when a management framework visits: the word 'hospitality' in the title, Derrida in the second section, and an argument that would run unchanged if the word were 'service'. That is not what I found. This paper asks what the host owes, which is the field's question, and it asks it of a party the field has been describing for a decade without asking. I believe the conditional tradition (Derrida, and Bulley's correction of him in these pages) is the field's sharpest instrument and I watch for papers that cite it and do not use it. I believe hospitableness is more than answerability and I watch for papers that quietly reduce it. And I believe a paper about algorithms belongs in this journal only if it changes what we can say about hosting; this one does, at l.61 and l.130, and it can say so more plainly than it does.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revisions.** The paper says something about hospitality, says it in the field's own terms at its two best moments, and is exposed at exactly one point where the field's sharpest instrument is already in its reference list and idle. **The single most important fix is l.61:** replace 'owed for one reason only … asking nothing of the guest except that they be there' with the trigger claim under conditions, and put Derrida there instead of at l.41, so that the sentence a Derridean referee would reject becomes the sentence that enlists him (R1 and R2, +20 words). Do that, restore Bulley to his argument (R3), size the three §2 claims to their evidence (R5–R7), and add Hirschman (R16), and the paper goes to referees with no hospitality-theory door left open.
