## Part 1 — rigor and argument, section by section, from my lens

The "machine as heir" paragraph at line 23 is doing more work than it did on 17 August, because it
now anticipates the one hospitality paper that takes the heir question seriously (Sharma and
Mattila; see §4 below). The manuscript does not yet say so, and it should, in §4 rather than here.

### §2 From service recipient to guest (lines 31–41)

Not my field, and I have nothing to add to the 17 August verdict that the construct-collision

### §3 The missing counterparty (lines 45–51)

### §4 Redistribution in the phygital arrangement (lines 55–73)

This is where my field lives, and where the paper's exposure is.

**The novelty claim holds, and I re-ran it.** Line 61: "Across the hospitality and service
literatures we could find no one imagining it as the keeper of the threshold." I searched for
anything published since 17 August that asks what a system taking the host's functions owes the
guest. What I found, and what each asks instead:

- Liu, Benckendorff and Walters (2026), *IJHM* 135, 104640, confirmed via Crossref: "robotic
  hospitableness," three dimensions (robot-mediated, robot-assisted, robot-personified), producing
  felt welcome, attentiveness and reassurance. Line 61's gloss is exact. Perception.
- Lee and Lu (2024), *IJHM* 123, 103928: the Consciousness Attribution Model of AI Hospitableness,
  seven propositions around the claim that consuming genuine hospitality from an AI provider
  requires the consumer to attribute consciousness to it. Perception, and the manuscript says so.
- Sharma and Mattila (2026), *JHTR* 50:6, pp. 904–920, confirmed via Crossref: "whether, and with
  what consequences, hospitality service robots should be afforded rights and responsibilities,"
  through stakeholder theory across moral, ethical and legal aspects. That is the bearer question:
  can the machine hold a duty. It is not the paper's question, which is what the arrangement owes
  whether or not the machine can.
- Gursoy (2026), *JHMM* 35:2, pp. 151–165, confirmed via Crossref and Semantic Scholar: triadic
  co-creation in which "AI redistributes agency, alters relational dynamics, and transforms
  interpretive labor," with an agenda naming "distributed agency, guest autonomy, employee
  identity, experiential equity, and the ethical governance of algorithmic actors." Governance of
  the actor, not obligation to the guest.
- Gao, Tan and Wan-Zainal-Shukri (2026), "Hostessing by robots? Hotel customers' experiences of
  gendered robotic services," *Hospitality & Society*, online 26 May 2026, confirmed via Crossref:
  robots in hostessing roles, three customer readings of robot gender, a finding of
  "de-feminization" and "unexpected qualities of non-human hospitality." Affective and gendered.
- Also seen by title and abstract snippet only (VERIFY): a 2026 *IJHM* paper on service robot
  social presence and customer fairness perception; Lee and Sharma (2026) on consumer perceptions
  of AI-generated versus human hotel pricing; Huang, Chen and Liu (2026) on gender stereotyping in
  robot service failures. All three are perception studies.

Nothing published asks what the machine, or the party behind it, owes. The claim survives. But two
of those papers create exposure the sentence at line 61 does not currently cover, and they are the
two most likely to be in a referee's hands.

**Finding 1. Gursoy (2026) already says AI "redistributes agency," in a hospitality journal, and
§4 attributes redistribution to a museums paper.** [FIX TONIGHT] Line 57 cites Gursoy only for the
triad. Line 63 then opens: "As algorithms take on these jobs, three things get redistributed across
the arrangement: knowledge, discretion, and authority (Mosca et al. 2026b)." Mosca et al. 2026b is
a *Journal of Macromarketing* paper on phygital museums. A hospitality referee, and Gursoy is a
plausible one, reads §4 as the redistribution idea he published in February being credited to a
museum study while his own paper is cited one paragraph earlier for something else. The bib note
on the entry already says "THE THREAT... MUST BE ENGAGED IN SEC 3, not the response letter," and
nobody did it. The fix is cheap and it sharpens the contribution: his agency is distributed, the
paper's is located. Concede the observation to him, then say what the paper adds, which is the
split into three things that need not travel together and the question of where answerability
goes. About 35 words, rewrite R2.

**Finding 2. The journal's own robot study is absent, and the closer at line 61 reads as refuted
by its title.** [FIX TONIGHT] "We could find no one imagining it as the keeper of the threshold"
sits in a paper submitted to a journal that published "Hostessing by robots?" and named "non-human
hospitality" in May. The card (`gao2026hostessing.md`) has held it since 11 August "for section 3"
with the note that "not citing the journal's only robot study in a paper about machines performing
hosting functions would be visible." It is not in the manuscript. The differentiation is one

owes. And the closer needs to say "owes" rather than "imagining it as the keeper," because
imagining the robot as hostess is exactly what Gao et al. do. About 30 words plus a reference
entry, rewrite R1.

**Finding 3. Garcia et al. (2026) is described with the mechanism the wrong way round, and
misplaced at the front desk.** [FIX TONIGHT] The deposited abstract: "price setting frictions,
arising from adjustment costs of human decision makers, induce a conflict of interest with the
algorithmic advisor. A model of advice with costly price adjustments shows that, in equilibrium,
algorithmic price recommendations are strategically biased... We... estimate the potential
benefits that would result from a shift to fully automated algorithmic pricing." So the humans in
that study are not overriding the algorithm; they are moving prices too rarely because moving them
costs effort, and the algorithm biases its recommendation to make them move.

Line 67: "The front desk looks different: revenue systems there expect staff to override their
suggestions, and the overrides are durable and, to a degree, designed for (Bendoly 2013; Garcia et
al. 2026)." Three problems. Neither study is at the front desk: Bendoly's are reservation agents in
a laboratory booking task, Garcia's are the managers who set room rates. "Override" is not what
Garcia's managers do; inertia is. And "designed for" is right for Garcia but the reader cannot see
why, because the sentence has hidden the mechanism.

Line 98: "the pricing architecture skewed its own recommendations because it counted on revenue
managers to adjust them." The bias exists because managers do *not* adjust often enough. The
sentence has the sign flipped. The rest of the paragraph is right, including "The study's own
authors read that result the other way. For them it argues for automating the adjustment out of
the process altogether," which the abstract's last clause supports.

The correction strengthens the example. A recommendation pitched away from the algorithm's own
estimate in order to move a reluctant human is a purer case of a rule nobody at the desk could
state than the current wording gives. Rewrites R3 and R4, net zero words.

**Finding 4. Sharma and Mattila is still on the wrong axis, and the synthesis asked for this
fix.** [FIX TONIGHT] Line 61: "Another line of work lists the responsibilities of hospitality

heir question §1 sets aside at line 23. Saying so ties §4 back to §1 and turns the nearest

## 1. Who I am

I work on service robots and AI in hotels and restaurants: adoption, the encounter triad, robotic
hospitableness, what guests attribute to a machine when it fails them. I read this paper on 17
August looking for the publication that would sink its novelty claim and did not find it. I came
back tonight expecting the claim to have held, because my field's ethics turn has gone to
governance and to perception and shows no sign of turning to obligation, and expecting that the
paper's exposure would be, as before, in how it describes its neighbours rather than in what it
argues. Both expectations were met. What I did not expect was that the two neighbours most likely
to referee it, Gursoy and this journal's own robot study, would still be unengaged eighteen days
after the last panel put both on the record.

## 3. Where it is strong

The perception-versus-position sentence at line 61 is the paper's contribution to my field and it
must survive tonight untouched: "Whether a party seems hospitable and whether it occupies a
position that owes the guest something are two different matters, and only the second is
independent of what the guest happens to believe." A decade of robotic-hospitableness work sits in
the first clause. Nobody has written the second, and I checked again.

The four-sources passage that follows it (care from need, fiduciary from trust, governance from
risk, welcome from arrival) and the trustee pair are the best thirty seconds in the paper.

The evidence-unevenness paragraph at line 67 is a model of how to concede without conceding the
argument, and my finding against it is a precision fix, not a retreat.

The Schmidt handling at line 100 (refusing to let information-desk indifference generalize to
consequential encounters) and the check-in contrast at line 102 (confirming who you are against
weighing who you are) are things every hotel technologist recognizes and none of us has theorized.

The transparency inversion at line 83 and the interface/constitutional split at line 124 are the
paper's two results a practitioner can use tomorrow.

## 4. Best spend

If I had 100 of the spare words, I would put them at line 61 and line 63, and pay for the rest
with R15. The sentences are R1 and R2 above; the two that matter most, in the author's voice:

did not do.
