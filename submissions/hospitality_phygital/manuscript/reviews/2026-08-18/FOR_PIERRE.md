# *Who Hosts the Guest?* v3 — claims needing more argument or a different citation

Prepared 2026-08-18. Submission window closes **4 September**.

This follows a source-side audit of all 124 references: each one checked against the publisher's own
record, and the full text obtained wherever it could be reached. That audit had never been run on
this project. It found no fabricated references — every one is a real, published work — but it found
a great deal about what those works actually say, and some of it does not match what we say they say.

Below, three kinds of problem, in the order a referee would meet them.

**Part 1** is citations that do not support the sentence they are attached to. These are the ones
that lose us the paper if a referee checks, and several are in load-bearing positions.
**Part 2** is claims that need argument rather than a citation — mostly the new v3 material, which is
strong and, being new, has never been audited.
**Part 3** is the absence claims, where one counter-citation is enough to embarrass us.

Where the fix is obvious I have said so. Where it is your call, I have said that instead.

## Part 1 — Citations that do not support the sentence

### 1.1 Folger (1977), §7 — must change before submission

We write that the value of speaking up "does not rest entirely on whether the guest gets their way
(Lind et al. 1990; Goodwin and Ross 1992; Folger 1977)."

Folger will not carry this. The paper is closed on every route we have tried since 8 August, but we
read **Lind, Kanfer and Earley (1990) in full**, and it classifies Folger 1977 among the **frustration
effect** studies — the finding that voice *without* influence can make things worse, not better. Our
reading is contradicted by everything reachable and supported by nothing.

**The good news is that the replacement is verified.** Lind, Kanfer and Earley genuinely carries the
claim: post-decision voice, delivered with an explicit statement that the decision would not change,
and with it in fact unchanged, still raised procedural fairness (2.43 → 3.15) and outcome fairness
(2.11 → 2.72). That is exactly our sentence.

But it comes with conditions we should state rather than have a referee state for us: pre-decision
voice beats post-decision; a perceived-control confound absorbs much of the effect; and Lind et al.
themselves warn that voice effects can be abused to manufacture fairness perceptions. **That last
point is useful to us** — it is precisely why §7 argues for interruptibility with a real counterparty
rather than for the appearance of voice.

**Recommend:** drop Folger, keep Lind, and add one sentence carrying the boundary. If you want Folger
retained, we need Bentley's PsycARTICLES access to read it first.

### 1.2 Pijls et al. (2017), §2 — the instrument cannot bear the weight

We cite Pijls for the field measuring "whether guests feel welcomed, satisfied, comfortable, or cared
for" while being "much less curious about what flows the other way."

We obtained and read it. The validated EH-Scale measures inviting, care and comfort — and its **two
agency items, "having choice" and "feeling independent," were dropped during confirmatory factor
analysis for low loadings**.

This is better for us than it looks. The instrument did not merely neglect guest agency; it *tried to
measure it and the items did not hold*. That is a stronger version of our own point and we should
make it, with the CFA detail, rather than the vaguer claim we make now.

### 1.3 Lin et al. (2026), §6 — we state a quarter of the result backwards

We write that employees who see how little pull they have "may grow less willing to talk about
algorithmic decisions with guests or managers (Cheng et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2026; Duggan et al.
2026)."

Full text read. Lin et al. find algorithmic **directing (−0.650), scheduling (−0.595) and monitoring
(−0.515)** suppress voice through reduced felt responsibility — but algorithmic **feedback raises it
(+0.345)**. Citing the paper flat for silence misstates a quarter of its finding, and it is
open-access, so a referee can check in one click.

**Recommend:** state the split. It also strengthens §7, because feedback is a design feature we
recommend.

### 1.4 Christin (2017), §4 — her finding is the opposite move

We cite Christin among sources showing staff time going to "reading decisions made elsewhere."
Christin's actual finding is **decoupling and buffering**: practitioners blunt and work around the
algorithms. Cite her for contest over discretion, not for discretion having already moved.

### 1.5 Garcia et al. (2026), §5 — the punchline runs against us

Our clearest worked example. The study is correctly described, but its own conclusion **favours fuller
automation** — the human overrides are the problem it identifies, not the safeguard. A referee who
knows it will say we have taken the setup and left the finding.

**Recommend:** keep the example, which is genuinely apt for one-sided predictability, and add half a
sentence acknowledging that the authors read their result as an argument for automation while we read
the same mechanism as an accountability gap. Owning it is stronger than being caught.

### 1.6 Manfreda and Harkison (2025), §2 — "gratitude" is not their frame

We say the luxury-lodge study is "careful to call this gratitude, not obligation." The publisher
abstract frames the reciprocation as **altruism, generosity and fictive kinship**. Gratitude is our
word, not theirs. Elsevier blocks the full text, so either we soften to what the abstract supports or
we obtain it.

### 1.7 Spektor et al. (2023), §4 — two overstatements

Full text read. The rejection affordance we lean on was a **worker-proposed prototype that the workers
themselves rejected**, and the withholding of the full task list happened "only in the most limited
configurations" — many systems let attendants see their whole board. Our sentence generalizes both.

Related: **Spektor et al. (2025) studies a housekeeping department**, peer relationships among
workers. It is not evidence about guest-facing work, and our §4 sentence should not imply it is.

### 1.8 Zhou et al. (2025), §6 — the hinge rests on one weak item

This is the passage where we argue that a competence scale cannot capture whether anyone is obliged to
consider an appeal. It is the hinge of the paper and I want it to hold.

The scale has three validated remediating items: solving the problem oneself, asking peers, and using
the platform's in-app reporting and appeal functions. **Only the third mentions appeal, and it carries
the weakest loadings.** The "gathering evidence in order to appeal" phrasing we use comes from an
interview exemplar in the scale-development table, not from a validated item.

**This makes our argument stronger, not weaker.** Two of the three validated items are workers routing
*around* the institution rather than through it — which is exactly what you would expect when no one
is obliged to answer. We should say that instead.

### 1.9 Martin and Waldman (2022), §7 — a caution for principle four

Their result: **appeals raised perceived legitimacy; oversight and audit lowered it.** Our fourth
principle asks for a reachable person with authority to reverse. That is closer to appeal than to
oversight, so the finding supports us — but only if we say which of the three we mean.

### 1.10 Smaller ones

- **Huang and Lo (2025)** — our bibliography carried the wrong title, and the paper shows a humanness
  *penalty* in process failures rather than a preference for humans. Check the sentence.
- **Möhlmann et al. (2021)** — matching and control are two **parallel** dimensions, not a temporal
  conversion from one to the other.
- **Fink (2025)** — still unpublished (a forthcoming chapter), so it should not anchor the authority
  distinction alone. Also: **"liability sponges" is Fink quoting Crootof, Kaminski and Nicholson
  Price**, not her coinage.
- **De Vos et al. (2023)** — this is a **one-page conference abstract**. It currently carries our claim
  about employees reformulating guest requests into system categories, which is a substantive
  ethnographic claim. It cannot.

## Part 2 — Claims that need argument, not a citation

The v3 material is the strongest writing in the paper and none of it has been audited, because it did
not exist when the claim audit ran. These are the places a good referee will press.

### 2.1 The uniqueness claim about welcome — §4

> "A welcome is owed for one reason only: someone has arrived. And it is the single duty that defines
> the role of the party who owes it."

The first sentence holds and is the best sentence in the paper. **The second is false, and its
counterexample is one clause earlier in our own list**: fiduciary duty is the standard case of an
obligation that constitutes its bearer's role — a trustee is a trustee *because* of the duty. Care and
governance survive the test; fiduciary does not.

There is a version that works, on a different axis: welcome is the only duty triggered by **bare
presence**, and the only one whose *ownership* — as distinct from its performance — cannot be
delegated without transferring the role itself. That is what §4 actually needs, and it sets up the
Bovens move better than the current wording does.

### 2.2 Standing, and its four neighbours — §2

The differentiation of standing from consumer rights, procedural justice, contestability and customer
participation is new, and it is the conceptual core of v3. It is currently **asserted rather than
grounded** — no source appears in the passage.

That is defensible for a definition, but it is where a theory referee will push hardest, because each
of those four has a literature that thinks it owns the ground. We have Bovens verified in full for
accountability, but obligation, responsibility and authority are stipulated. Either anchor them or say
plainly that we are stipulating them for the paper's purposes.

### 2.3 The three conditions, re-founded — §5

> "they are best introduced not as imports from the study of organizational coordination but as what
> the host–guest relationship itself requires"

This is a real move and I like it, but it is doing something delicate: taking Okhuysen and Bechky's
integrating conditions and re-deriving them from hospitality rather than borrowing them. The three
conditions are **confirmed verbatim** as theirs. What is not yet argued is *why* the host–guest
relationship requires exactly these three and not others. One paragraph would close it.

### 2.4 Interruptibility — §7

The paper's design conclusion, and it carries no citation at all. That is fine if it is our coinage,
and it may be the most quotable thing in the paper. But it should be introduced as ours explicitly,
and distinguished from the seamful-design tradition already in our reference list (Chalmers and
Galani; Ehsan et al.), which argues for revealing seams. Our claim is different — the seam must have
*someone behind it* — and saying so is what makes it a contribution rather than a transposition.

### 2.5 The priority claim — §3

> "Hospitality scholarship, in other words, noticed that a third party had unsettled the question of
> who hosts before the service literature had even named the triad."

A dating claim, and dating claims are checkable. Worth softening unless we want to defend the
chronology.

### 2.6 Lynch et al. (2011) already contemplated non-human hosts

We read the journal's founding editorial in full for the first time. It contains a **"Hospitality and
virtuality"** research-agenda item and a Ciborra discussion of technology as guest and host —
"hostages of each other."

This is our strongest fit warrant for this journal, and it is also a caution: the editors already
raised the question we say has not been asked. **Engaging it directly makes the paper stronger.** We
are answering a question the journal itself posed fifteen years ago, which is a better framing than
implying nobody posed it.

## Part 3 — Absence claims, where one citation ends the argument

Every one of these is a sentence a referee can kill with a single counter-example. Each needs either a
narrowing clause or a footnote recording the search that justifies it.

1. §2 — "we know of no instrument that measures conduct running in that direction." Service research
   does measure customer-side conduct in value co-creation and citizenship-behaviour scales. Narrow to
   what the guest *owes a commercial host*, which is the real and defensible claim.
2. §4 — "It has not yet imagined the machine as the keeper of the threshold." Our own audit found this
   holds against everything we checked, but the claim is stated absolutely. Scope it to the
   hospitality and service literatures.
3. §6 — "no one has tested that interaction" (silence climate × algorithmic direction). Verified:
   Lin et al. include no climate construct. Safe as stated, and worth a footnote saying how we
   checked.
4. §7 — "smoothness itself has stayed a goal rather than a suspect." **Verified against all four named
   works**, including Mameli et al.'s full text, where "friction" never appears and "seamless" appears
   once, affirmatively. This one is solid. Keep the narrow phrasing.
5. §8 — "we know of no such study" (guests' working theories of hospitality systems). Plausible and
   unverified. Either a search footnote or soften.

## One sentence in §1 carries two problems, and one fix closes both

The sentence beginning "This journal has already asked…" does two things worth changing.

It signals the target venue. That is a mild issue rather than a serious one — it does not reveal
authorship, which is what double-blind protects, and the construction is ordinary in published work.
But the Notes for Contributors ask for strict anonymity in both directions and the change costs
nothing.

More substantively, the same passage reads Germann Molz as **erasure**. Her abstract shows the phrase
"guests without hosts" was coined for the **blurring** of hosting and guesting, extended in two
directions, with erasure one of three operations.

One replacement closes both. It runs from "This journal has already asked…" through "…That work asks
what happens as the host is erased," and your next sentence carries straight on:

> That gap matters more than ever now that platforms and automated systems are taking over jobs human
> hosts used to do. Hospitality scholarship has already asked what happens when platforms push the
> human host out of view, and has named the resulting condition algorithmic hostility (Riordan 2024).
> The phrase "guests without hosts" was coined for something adjacent, the blurring of hosting and
> guesting in network hospitality, and has lately been extended to an algorithmic governance that
> holds hosts and guests in extractive relations, and to the replacement of human hospitality workers
> by robots and AI (Germann Molz 2026). Both ask what becomes of hospitality as the human host
> recedes. We ask the next question.

"Erased" becomes "recedes," which is what her abstract supports, and naming the blurring origin shows
a referee we are reading her accurately rather than selectively.
