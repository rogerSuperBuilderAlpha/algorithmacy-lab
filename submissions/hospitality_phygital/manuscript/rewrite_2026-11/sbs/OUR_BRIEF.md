# Our brief

## The research question, and where v6 states it

1. **As an aim**, introduction ¶3: the article seeks to keep the field's account of what a host owes, and to say where that obligation goes when the work of hosting is delegated to algorithmic systems spread across several firms.
2. **As what the field must attend to**, introduction ¶4: who answers to the guest.
3. **As the driving question**, second section ¶5, in the model's form: 'But what becomes of the host's obligation once the work of hosting is handed on?'
4. **As the case question**, end of the second subsection of the third section: which decisions need a person with authority at the moment they are taken.
5. **Answered**, conclusion ¶2.

## The thesis, and its shape

- **Claim.** The commercial host's obligation stays with whoever took up the role of receiving the guest. Algorithmic mediation rarely removes the obligation; what it removes is the path from the guest to anyone with the authority to answer for it.
- **Shape: with the field's account of the host, then two steps beyond it, then the touchpoint.**
  1. Grant the field its account: the host has power over the guest and owes the guest protection.
  2. Name two limits: the field looks for the obligation in the host's motive, and it pictures one host.
  3. First step: the obligation is a role obligation, with a content, a moment at which it comes into force, and a holder.
  4. Second step: hosts are assembled; the work is delegated and the duty is not; the guest's standing survives and its exercise needs a reachable party with authority.
  5. Show both steps at phygital touchpoints across formats.
  6. Close on limits, three studies, and what organizations can do.

## Contribution moves, in order

1. Reaffirm the field's account of the host's obligation (first section ¶1–2).
2. Move the debate about commercial hospitality from motive to role (first section ¶3–5), with the law of innkeepers as evidence for content, moment and holder (¶6–10).
3. Say what is distinctive about hospitality by what it is not, and dispute Slattery by name (first section ¶7).
4. Name the several-firm host in the journal's words and propose who among its members answers to the guest (second section ¶3–4). Marked as our proposal.
5. Separate what can be delegated from what cannot, against the harm-indexed account (second section ¶5–6).
6. Define guest standing from the obligation, and separate holding it from exercising it (second section ¶7–9).
7. Name the authority–answerability split and show it across formats (third section, first subsection).
8. Offer three conditions and six questions; a typology of when a person must be reachable; five principles and five stages (third section, second to fourth subsections).

## Register commitments

Taken from the model and checked against the venue (`MODEL_BRIEF.md`).

1. **Verbs.** Literal verbs of saying, showing, holding and answering: argue, hold, owe, receive, answer, attach, delegate, reach, press, grant, refuse, examine, show, state. A verb that reaches is allowed where the model's would ('push beyond', 'ward off'), never extended.
2. **Metaphor.** None developed. v5's 'relay' is kept once, because it is argued for against 'fluidity' and names a thing. 'bends the line', 'dropped in between', 'bites hardest', 'bought back', 'live at the interface' are not carried over.
3. **Key terms repeated.** As above.
4. **Abstraction.** Each abstract stretch reaches a case or a quotation within the paragraph.
5. **First person.** 'We argue', 'we propose', 'we disagree', 'we read', 'on our account': about seven to nine across the paper, on the thesis and on proposals that are ours. 'We' means the two authors.
6. **Hedges** sit on our own proposals (who answers; the typology), not on reported facts.
7. **Never.** No sentence grading the argument. No anecdote. No fragment. No second person. No rhetorical question except the driving question and one self-posed question. No bare 'it' or 'this' as subject where the referent is more than one clause back (Reviewer 1.6).
