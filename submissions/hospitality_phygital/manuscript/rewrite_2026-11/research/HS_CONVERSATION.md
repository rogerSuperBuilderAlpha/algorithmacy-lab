# The conversation v6 joins

Depth tags: **[full]** read in full, this session or on a full-text card; **[abstract]** abstract only; **[via X]** known only through X, which was read in full.

## 1. The host holds power, and owes protection

The field's canon states both halves.

- **Power.** Derrida ties hospitality to the host's mastery of the home [via Bulley 2015: MS pp. 8–9; via Shryock 2012: S30]. Shryock: 'sovereignty is manifest in the ability to act as host' (2012: S25) [full]. Pitt-Rivers: 'It is always the host who ordains, the guest who complies' (2012: 513) [full]. Di Domenico and Lynch find the balance of power resting 'firmly' with hosts in commercial homes (2007: 332) [full].
- **Protection.** The good host can 'protect guests from harm' (Shryock 2012: S20) [full]. The host infringes the law of hospitality 'If he fails to protect his guest' or 'to show concern for their needs and wishes' (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 516) [full]. Historically hospitality 'entailed a sacred obligation … to protect the stranger who arrived at the door' (Lynch et al. 2011, Introduction ¶2) [full]. 'hosts have duties to ensure the wellbeing of their guests, while guests have obligations to respect the rules of the host' (Lugosi 2009) [full]. Commercial thresholds 'guaranteed and provided physical protection, sanctuary and security' (O'Gorman 2009: pdf p. 11) [full]. 'Historically, the host had an obligation not only to provide a place for and assure the safety of their guests, but also to assure their comfort' (Varley et al. 2018, §2 ¶2, on King 1995) [full; King via Varley].
- **Bulley's step.** Power does not stop at the door: the distinctions of hospitality 'need to be policed, managed and controlled, even after the threshold of the home is crossed' (2015: MS p. 2) [full]. He asks 'what becomes of the power of hospitality once this threshold is crossed?' (MS p. 10). He does not ask what becomes of the obligation.

**Use in v6.** The paper's question is Bulley's question turned over: what becomes of the host's obligation once the work of hosting is handed on.

## 2. Whether a commercial host is a host at all

- **The doubt.** Commercial hospitality 'cannot deliver true hospitableness' because it is offered at a price and 'would be withdrawn if the payment could not be made' (Lashley 2015: 5) [full]. The obligation to the stranger 'no longer carries the same moral obligation it once had' (Lashley 2015: 3) [full].
- **The denial.** 'The buyers are not guests, they are customers. The relationship is not philanthropic it is economic' (Slattery 2002: 21) [full]. Aramberri says the host should 'get lost' [via Lynch et al. 2011, 'Social control' ¶2; via Germann Molz 2018: 236].
- **What happened on the ground.** Among 22 hotel frontline staff, 'service' prevailed over 'hospitality' as the default vocabulary (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1296) [full].
- **The journal's answer.** 'hospitality remains a powerful term for describing social arrangements amongst strangers both within and beyond the commercial realm' (Lynch et al. 2011) [full]. Telfer allows that a commercial host can be hospitable [via Lynch et al. 2011; via Lashley 2015: 5].
- **Slattery's own point about firms.** Chains 'outsource a range of services', and 'The corporate context has no parallel in the social or private domains' (2002: 25) [full].

**Use in v6.** The debate asks whether the commercial host has the *motive* of a host. v6 moves the question from motive to role: the law has long treated the one who receives travellers as a host whatever the motive. Slattery's corporate context is granted and used.

## 3. More than one host

- **'Assembled host'.** In refugee camps, admission is decided at the border by officials, and after it a 'variety of agencies … perform the role of an assembled host' (Bulley 2015: MS p. 13) [full].
- **'Micro-hosts' and 'macro-hosts'.** The seated passenger, the guard and the train company (Lynch et al. 2011, 'Exchange' ¶3) [full; Robinson and Lynch 2007 via Lynch et al.].
- **Person and organization.** 'asymmetric hospitalities', where the relationship is 'between individuals and broader, more ambiguous, entities' (Lugosi 2011 ¶2, citing Lugosi 2009) [full for 2011 and 2009].
- **Scale.** Bad hospitality 'accumulates in the gaps created by shifts in scale' (Shryock 2012: S20) [full].
- **The divided host in practice.** In the Glasgow hotels that housed asylum seekers during the pandemic, outsourcing made 'a web of hosts'; inquiry reports found 'a lack of clarity around what each different agency was responsible for', residents 'did not know where to turn for help', and the receptionist they often turned to first lacked the skills for complex problems (Petie et al. 2025: MS p. 11) [full, accepted manuscript, CC BY; read 11 Nov 2026].

**Use in v6.** The several-firm host is already named in the journal, and Petie et al. (2025) show what its unsettled division costs a guest. v6 adds what the journal has not: which of the assembled hosts answers to the guest.

## 5. The gap search

- **Where:** all 282 records of *Hospitality & Society* in OpenAlex, 10 Nov 2026 (`hs_screen.tsv`). 21 have no abstract there and were screened by title.
- **Terms and counts:** technology family, 37 records; obligation family, 12; voice family, 20.
- **What the 12 obligation records concern:** workers' obligation to stay hospitable under harassment; service as sacred duty; Shari'a in luxury hotels; a labour accountability database; Polynesian hosting; nineteenth-century innkeepers in travel writing; Poulston (2015) on hospitality as 'essentially a moral duty'; Lugosi (2014) on hospitality used to 'invoke obligations' within organizations; three others unrelated.
- **What the 37 technology records concern:** CouchSurfing and Airbnb as network hospitality; platform labour; robots and gender; speed; online reviews and marketing; virtual reality.

**Statement.** In the titles and abstracts of these 282 records I found no article that asks where the commercial host's obligation to the guest sits when the work of hosting is delegated to software or spread across firms, and none that treats the guest's ability to press a claim on a host as its subject. The nearest are Germann Molz (2018, read in full), who shows a platform disclaiming responsibility and asks that scholars watch whether it is held accountable, and Germann Molz (2026, abstract only), on the erasing of hosts.

**Correction after a full read (11 Nov 2026).** Petie, Phillimore and Allsopp (2025) is the nearest work in the journal to v6's question, and the screen missed it: `hs_screen.tsv` codes it 0 on all three families, because its abstract speaks of hoteliers, housing companies and home, not of obligation, technology or voice. Read in full for the journal study, it describes hosting divided by outsourcing in which no one could say which agency was responsible and residents could not tell where to seek help (MS pp. 3, 11). Its question is how asylum seekers experience hospitality and home, not where the host's obligation sits once hosting is divided, so the statement above stands as worded; Petie et al. now belong among the nearest works, beside Germann Molz (2018), and v6 cites them (S-150b). The miss is a limit of a term-based screen over abstracts, recorded here so the statement is not read as stronger than it is.

**Limits of the statement.** It rests on titles and abstracts, not full texts, for all but eight records. It covers this journal only; the wider gap search is in `revision_2026-11/research/` (WS_H1 to WS_H5).
