# What the research changes

## The thesis does not change

v6 argues what v5 argues: the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation; the law imposes it on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers; holding out is how the role is taken up; the work can be delegated and the duty cannot; the guest keeps standing but may be unable to exercise it.

## What v6 gains

1. **A way in through the field.** The canon holds that the host has power over the guest and owes the guest protection (Derrida as Bulley and Shryock read him; Pitt-Rivers; Shryock; Lynch et al.). v5 reached the obligation through law. v6 reaches it through these writers and brings the law in as evidence.

2. **The question in the journal's own form.** Bulley (2015) asked what becomes of the host's *power* once the threshold is crossed. v6 asks what becomes of the host's *obligation* once the work of hosting is handed on. The paper becomes a second step in a line the journal already recognizes: Kekstaite (2022) took the first.

3. **A sharper contrast with the field's debate about commerce.** That debate turns on motive: whether a host who charges can be hospitable (Lashley 2015; Slattery 2002; Telfer as reported). v5 already set motive against role in §2.1. v6 can now say that the whole debate shares the premise v6 leaves behind.

4. **The several-firm host, in the journal's words.** Bulley's 'assembled host'; the 'micro-hosts' and 'macro-hosts' of Lynch et al.; Lugosi's 'asymmetric hospitalities' between a person and an organization; Slattery's chains that 'outsource a range of services'. v5 built this from *Crinkley* and Cobbe et al. alone.

5. **A stated difference from 'guests without hosts'.** The journal's latest account is that platforms erase the host (Germann Molz 2026, at abstract depth, and as its special-issue editors summarize it). v6's claim is that the role still has a holder, and that what goes missing is the guest's path to it. v6 states this as a difference of question, and does not characterize her argument beyond the abstract.

6. **Two warrants from the journal.** The founding editorial asked for a hospitality perspective on 'human–machine interactions' (Lynch et al. 2011). Germann Molz (2018: 246) asked scholars to watch 'how, or whether, companies like Airbnb are held accountable'. v6 answers both, and its introduction can say so.

7. **Evidence on the guest's voice.** Moysidou and Stanley (2023) treat a guest's confidence that a complaint will be acted on as a form of power, and show a guest who cannot leave staying silent. This supports v5's sixth question (what refusing costs) from inside the journal.
