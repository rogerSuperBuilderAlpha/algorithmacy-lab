## 1. Review of the new literature

The search set out to answer the editor's four questions about the host's obligation: why it remains, where it comes from, whether it can be transferred, and what separates it from organizational delegation and algorithmic accountability. It also asked what standing is, and what makes hospitality more than service. The answer that came back is more useful than the one the paper went looking for. The obligation itself is not new. What the literature lacks is an account of its *address* — who the guest can reach when the work of hosting has moved into a system. That shift reorganizes the paper, and the review below is arranged by the questions it settles.

### 1.1 Hospitality already names the host's obligation

Hospitality scholars have long held that the commercial host owes the guest something beyond the transaction. Xiang, Huang, Gao and Lai (2022), drawing on King (1995), state it plainly: guests are "guests" and hotels, "as 'hosts,' are obliged to provide care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety to the guests" (Literature review). Their study of COVID-era hotels frames itself against service management — "existing research on host-guest interactions in hospitality largely relied on service management and marketing literature" — and claims a hospitality paradigm "as the relationship between hosts and guests" (Conclusions). The paper cannot claim the obligation as its discovery. It can claim what happens to the obligation's address under algorithmic mediation, which none of these sources addresses.

Shryock (2012) supplies the account of who holds the obligation. Hospitality "is also a test of sovereignty" (p. S20); "sovereignty is manifest in the ability to act as host" (p. S25); and the good host is one who can "protect guests from harm" (p. S20). Guests "depend on their hosts for protection and respect" (p. S23). Bad hospitality arises when host and guest "cannot agree on who controls the space of interaction, who is sovereign, who belongs, and who owes or should offer respect" (p. S30). He also names the modern holder outright: the answer to "Who does so?" is "the hospitality industry (the market in hotels, restaurants, and other sites of temporary accommodation)" (p. S30). The obligation belongs to whoever holds the space, and in commercial hospitality that is the institution. O'Gorman (2009), read in full but not yet carded, gives the commercial history: "Crossing thresholds of commercial hospitality also guaranteed and provided physical protection, sanctuary and security" (PDF p. 11).

Slattery (2002) is the rival the paper must answer, and he is blunt. "It is inaccurate and misleading to assume that in the hospitality business the critical relationship is between host and guest" (p. 21); "The buyers are not guests, they are customers. The relationship is not philanthropic it is economic" (p. 21). His alternative locates the industry's work in "the creation of the context of consumption and the management of consumption" (pp. 25–26). Golubovskaya, Robinson and Solnet (2017) show that Slattery's view has largely won on the ground. Only "the minority (6 of 22 participants)" of their hotel frontline staff "were able to define and describe hospitality" (p. 1296), and "service management terminology overrides hospitality within the commercial context" (pp. 1299–1300). Their own concept of hospitality is an attitude, "the attitude and spirit with which service is performed" (p. 1287). They note obligation among the definitions in the field without adopting it (p. 1284). Golubovskaya et al. therefore support the paper's diagnosis that service language displaces hospitality's. They do not support its positive claim.

### 1.2 Delegation moves the work but not the answering

The delegation literature answers the transfer question more sharply than the plan expected. Nissenbaum (1996) treats accountability as "closely akin to 'answerability'" (PDF p. 3). Blaming the computer is a barrier to it "because, having found one explanation for an error or injury, the further role and responsibility of human agents tend to be underestimated" (PDF p. 10). Answerability "cannot be spread, thinned and offset" (PDF p. 15). But she does not say it survives by default. "Unless alternate lines of accountability are worked out, accountability for these important functions will be lost" (PDF p. 11). The deploying organization is one of her three candidates, alongside the designers and the human experts (PDF p. 11).

Kong (2026) states the principle the paper needs and grounds it. When an agent delegates a task to a process, human or artificial, "the agent's answerability for the foreseeable types of outcome within that domain is neither divided nor diminished by the autonomy of the process. It is relocated to the prior act of delegation" (p. 5). "One who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it" (p. 10). A diminished answerability still located somewhere "is not a gap. It is a smaller quantity with an address" (p. 10). Kong's ground is control over the decision to delegate rather than the role: "A role matters derivatively rather than intrinsically" (p. 4). Roles still matter, though, because they specify "the domain over which control and knowledge are exercised, and thereby of the outcomes that are hers to explain" (p. 4). Kong sets fiduciary duty aside as "a different matter again" (p. 4). His principle is also indexed to foreseeable harm, so it describes answering *after* something goes wrong.

Kropf, Spöck and Werner (2026) were cast as the opposition, and they turn out to be the paper's best ally on the machine side. A care robot can hold a narrow role responsibility, "thin blame", without accountability, "thick blame" (p. 7). But "the answerability of care robots is currently inconceivable" (p. 9). Thick blame "lies with the moral – and therefore also human – actors" (p. 10), and holding robots to account "could lead to a diffusion thereof, allowing humans to evade moral responsibility" (p. 12). Their robot's role is explicitly not a carer's: "A care robot in a nursing home does not have the same role obligations as a nurse" (p. 4). In other words, the role can move to the machine and the answering cannot. That formulation is better than the plan's "ownership moves only with the role", which no source supports.

Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) contribute less than v4 claimed, and one sentence of v4 must change. Their paper divides the responsibility gap into culpability, moral accountability, public accountability and active responsibility. It contains no typology of duties by trigger: "fiduciary" never appears. What they do supply is the institutional version of the holder: a public organization's own officers, "not IT experts and private companies", must keep the capacity to explain "the behaviour of their institution … no matter how much technology is involved in the decision" (§4.3 ¶1). They also warn that legal personhood for artificial agents shifts attention away from responsibility (§3.2.2 ¶3).

Germann Molz (2018) shows that the risk is real in hospitality. Airbnb "exempts itself from more binding regulation" (p. 244), and investors profit "while transferring legal and tax liabilities onto homeowners and regulatory obligations onto municipalities" (p. 245). The platform case is a precedent for the problem: there, the obligation was moved without its owner's consent. It does not support the paper's conclusion. Gursoy (2026) supplies the field's nearest statement of the mechanism: "Algorithmic governance shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics, raising questions about accountability when harm, bias, or exclusion arise" (p. 160). He does not ask who owes the guest the welcome.

### 1.3 Standing has a home, and the paper adds a condition to it

Hohfeld (1913) gives standing its formal skeleton. "A duty is the invariable correlative of that legal relation which is most properly called a right or claim" (p. 33); if X has a right against Y, "the correlative (and equivalent) is that Y is under a duty toward X" (p. 32). Darwall (2013) gives it its moral content. Bipolar obligations relate "an agent who is obligated (the obligor) and an individual … to whom she is obligated" (p. 20), and they "always involve an assumed individual authority or standing that the obligee has with respect to the obligor that others do not have" (p. 27). The obligee holds "a distinctive discretionary second-personal authority … to make claims and demands of obligors and hold them personally responsible" (pp. 31–32). His apology example captures the paper's problem exactly: an admission of guilt found in a diary is not an apology, because an apology "is, by definition, addressed to someone who receives it" (p. 31).

Two cautions follow. First, in Darwall and Hohfeld the guest's standing is entailed by the obligation itself. It does not lapse when no one answers. The paper's condition — that standing can be *exercised* only while a counterparty is reachable — is the authors' own addition, and it should be argued as one. Second, "standing is conferred" sits awkwardly with Darwall, for whom the obligee's authority comes with the obligation rather than from an institution's grant. The repair is precise. The guest holds standing because the host is obliged. What an institution confers, or withholds, is the *occasion and the addressee* for exercising it.

Metcalf, Singh, Moss, Tafesse and Watkins (2023) mark the legal sense the paper must set aside. For a plaintiff to have standing, "plaintiffs seeking redress must demonstrate a reasonable connection between another party's action or inaction and material harm" (p. 1455). Their relational approach asks for forums where publics can address "regulators and developers who are obligated to listen" (p. 1453). Coeckelbergh (2019), read in full, is the nearest rival. He puts the people affected by AI on the addressee side of answerability, the "responsibility patient" (section 'Include Responsibility Patients'). He concludes that "developers and users of AI owe those affected by AI an answer". He credits the "to whom" question to Duff and never mentions Darwall, Hohfeld or second-personal standing. I found no work that applies second-personal standing or Hohfeld's correlativity to guests. Nor did I find any that makes a decision subject's standing depend on a reachable counterparty. The searches covered Consensus, Crossref and OpenAlex in November 2026; Scholar Gateway refused access throughout.

### 1.4 Negotiation is the practice of standing, and presence alone does not secure it

The host–guest negotiation literature that Reviewer 1 asked for exists, and it cuts both ways. Xiang et al. (2022) show negotiation as staff work: guests who resist "do not fully participate in the ritual, which requires more negotiation efforts from the staff" (Findings). In their clearest case, a guest arrived without a test report and the front-office employee, "after checking with hotel management", had the hotel cover the cost of the test (Findings, Stage II). The rule was not waived; the hotel compensated. The case shows a management-approved accommodation reached through an employee who could get to the authority that approved it.

Di Domenico and Lynch (2007) supply the baseline. In commercial homes, where the host is physically present, "the balance of power in the host/guest relationship is perceived by hosts to rest firmly with them" (p. 332). Guests "lack power in deciding the social control of initial access" (p. 333). One guest-researcher's note is telling: "in a hotel one might complain about the beds being uncomfortable, but as the guest in the home one does not" (p. 333). So a copresent host does not by itself let a guest press claims. That is what the paper needs: the problem is reaching an addressee with authority, not the presence of a human face. Lugosi (2014), read only as an abstract, argues that hospitality "can be used purposefully to establish power relations and invoke obligations", and can also "reconfigure power relationships" (abstract). It belongs in §3 once read in full.

### 1.5 Refusal has a price, and the law addresses the deployer but not the hotel

Wilson-Nash, Angell, Qiu, Pavlopoulou and Kolyperas (2026) name what the fifth principle protects against. Digital technology captivity is "the condition in which consumers feel constrained when services move to digital access, and … perceive that they have few resources and options" (p. 975). It "centers on coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable", unlike exclusion or withdrawal (p. 976). Three findings serve the paper:
- An alternative that is "retained but downgraded and more difficult to access" still triggers captivity (p. 978).
- Abandoning the service "may carry a personal cost and loss" (p. 979).
- The mitigation is "allowing consumers to choose modes of access (e.g., digital, analogue)" (p. 981).

They frame the stake as stress and vulnerability rather than standing, so the paper extends their concept rather than borrowing its conclusion. "Captivity is different: the consumer cannot walk away" (p. 982).

The EU AI Act already addresses the deployer, which fits Kong's relocation of answerability. Article 86 gives "any affected person" subject to a deployer's decision based on a high-risk Annex III system "the right to obtain from the deployer clear and meaningful explanations" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 86(1)). But Annex III names no accommodation or hospitality use. Guest-facing hotel decisions — admission, pricing, ejection, ratings — fall outside its listed areas, except where they involve emotion recognition or profiling (Art. 6(3)). The Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, also moves the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027. It leaves Article 86 and the general application date of 2 August 2026 unamended. The law has located the addressee, then. It has not reached the hotel.

## 2. The new flow of the argument

The argument now turns on one distinction the new literature makes available: between the *role* of hosting, which can move, and the *answering* that belongs to hosting, which cannot. Kong (2026) shows that delegation relocates answerability rather than dissolving it. Kropf et al. (2026) show that a machine can take a role without taking the answering. Nissenbaum (1996) shows that the relocated answerability is lost unless someone builds the line to it. Hospitality theory supplies what the delegation literature lacks. Its obligation is owed to a particular arrival before anything goes wrong, and it includes the power to make an exception. Standing theory supplies the guest's side: a claim exists only as a claim addressed to someone. Put together, these yield the thesis:

> When phygital arrangements move the work of hosting into algorithmic systems, the obligation of hosting stays with the institution that admitted the guest, because delegation relocates answerability to the act of delegating. Hospitality fails when that relocated answerability has no address the guest can reach.

The flow runs in eight steps, each taking its premise from the step before.

1. **The obligation exists and has a holder.** The commercial host owes the guest care, welcome and safety (Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995). The holder is whoever holds sovereignty over the space (Shryock 2012: S25, S30) — the institution, not the employee. Against Slattery (2002), the paper sides with Lashley, and Golubovskaya et al. (2017) show why the question is urgent: service language is displacing hospitality's.
2. **Delegation relocates the answering.** When the institution hands recognition, pricing or admission to a system, answerability moves to "the prior act of delegation" (Kong 2026: 5). A machine can take the role but not the answering (Kropf et al. 2026: 7, 9). Without deliberate lines, the answering is lost (Nissenbaum 1996: PDF p. 11).
3. **Hospitality's obligation differs from harm-indexed accountability.** Kong and Nissenbaum index answerability to foreseeable harm and malfunction. The host's obligation is owed to this arrival from the threshold onward, before any harm, and it includes the power to make an exception. That content is hospitality's distinct contribution, and it is the authors' own move.
4. **The guest's standing is the obligation seen from the other side.** Every duty has its correlative claim (Hohfeld 1913: 32–33). The guest holds the obligee's standing to address claims to the host (Darwall 2013: 27, 31–32). Standing is held because the host is obliged; what an institution supplies is the occasion and the addressee for exercising it. Negotiation is the practice of standing (Xiang et al. 2022), and a host's mere presence does not secure it (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007).
5. **Phygital arrangements separate authority from answerability.** Knowledge drifts to the system, staff contest discretion, and authority drifts to whoever configures the rules (Gursoy 2026: 160). The *authority–answerability split* is the result: the party who can change the rule is not a party the guest can reach. Germann Molz (2018) shows the platform version, in which the obligation is shed onto others.
6. **Three conditions test whether a touchpoint keeps the address.** A welcome holds recognition, negotiation and standing together, so a touchpoint needs common understanding, predictability and accountability (names from Okhuysen and Bechky 2009). Accountability cannot be bought back with the other two; Batat (2024: 1234) makes the parallel claim for human connectors. Six questions apply the test.
7. **Skill cannot supply the address.** Guests and staff learn to read and even bend systems (Lin 2025: 8–9), but no skill creates an addressee (Lin 2025: 11; Vaccaro et al. 2020). Competence is learned; the conditions for exercising standing are provided by institutions.
8. **Design for interruptibility, and build the address.** Interface conditions (explanation, adjustment, notice of change) secure the first two conditions. Constitutional conditions — reachable authority to reverse (Shabnam et al. 2026; Lyons et al. 2021) and a real alternative (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026) — secure the third. Seamlessness becomes suspect where it removes the occasion for a claim. The roadmap orders these by cost. The law has begun to name the deployer as addressee (AI Act Art. 86) but has not reached the hotel.

The discussion then claims three contributions, each demonstrated in one step: the address of hosting's obligation under delegation (steps 1–3), standing as correlative and exercisable (steps 4 and 7), and the test with its roadmap (steps 6 and 8).

## 3. Proposal (two pages)

**Working title.** Who Hosts the Guest? Algorithmic Mediation, Standing, and the Address of Hospitality's Obligation

**The problem.** Phygital hospitality hands the work of hosting to algorithmic systems. Systems now recognize the guest, set the price, admit or refuse, and route the request. Research on these systems asks what they do for the guest and whether guests accept them. Germann Molz (2026) asks what happens as the platform erases the human host. No one has asked what becomes of what the host owed. That question matters now because hospitality's own field is losing the vocabulary to pose it. In Golubovskaya et al.'s (2017) hotels, most frontline staff could not say what hospitality was, and "service management terminology overrides hospitality within the commercial context" (pp. 1299–1300).

**What is known, and what is not.** Hospitality scholars hold that the commercial host is obliged to the guest (Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995; Lashley 2000). They locate that obligation with whoever holds sovereignty over the space (Shryock 2012). Philosophers of technology hold that delegating a task to a machine does not dissolve the duty to answer for it (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026; Kropf et al. 2026). Theorists of obligation hold that every duty carries a correlative claim held by the one to whom it is owed (Hohfeld 1913; Darwall 2013). These three literatures have not met. Hospitality theory has no account of delegation, and the delegation literature indexes answerability to harm, so it has nothing to say about an obligation owed at the threshold before anything goes wrong. Neither has an account of the guest's standing. I found no work that applies standing theory to guests, or that ties a decision subject's standing to a counterparty they can reach.

**Research question.** When phygital arrangements delegate the functions of hosting to algorithmic systems, where does the obligation of hosting go, and what must an arrangement preserve for the guest to remain a guest?

**Thesis.** The obligation stays with the institution that admitted the guest, because delegation relocates answerability to the act of delegating rather than dissolving it (Kong 2026: 5). A machine can take the role of hosting but not its answering (Kropf et al. 2026: 9). The characteristic failure of phygital hospitality is therefore not the loss of a human face. It is the loss of an *address*: the guest keeps standing in principle while no party with authority over the decision is placed to answer.

**The argument.**
- **The obligation and its holder (§2).** The paper shows that the commercial host's obligation has a holder, the institution that opens the threshold. It argues that delegation moves the answering to the act of delegating.
- **What makes it hospitality's (§2).** It distinguishes the host's obligation from harm-indexed accountability: the obligation is owed to an arrival before any harm, and it includes the capacity for a negotiated exception.
- **Standing (§3).** It defines the guest's standing as the correlative of that obligation and treats negotiation as the practice of standing. A host's presence does not secure standing; a reachable addressee does.
- **The split (§4).** It traces how phygital arrangements pull authority away from answerability, and names the result the authority–answerability split.
- **The test (§5).** From the elements of a welcome it derives a test of three conditions: common understanding, predictability, and accountability, the last of which cannot be traded for the other two. It applies the test through six questions and two cases: algorithmic hotel pricing, and check-in that confirms identity against check-in that scores a profile.
- **Competence is not standing (§6).** It shows why skill cannot close the split.
- **Design and roadmap (§7).** It proposes design for interruptibility in place of seamlessness, a distinction between interface and constitutional conditions, and a five-stage roadmap ordered by cost.

**Contributions.**
1. *A theory of where hospitality's obligation goes under delegation.* It answers the editor's questions: why the obligation remains, where it originates, whether it transfers, and how it differs from organizational delegation and algorithmic accountability.
2. *A concept of guest standing.* Standing is the correlative of the host's obligation, exercisable only where an addressee is reachable, and it separates what guests and staff can learn from what institutions must provide.
3. *A test and a roadmap that follow from the theory.* Each condition and each stage traces back to an element of the welcome, and the roadmap ends in the institutional stages that interface fixes cannot reach.

**Why hospitality, and not service management.** Service management asks whether the encounter delivers value. Algorithmic accountability asks who answers when a system causes harm. Hospitality asks what is owed to a person because they have been admitted, and it treats the exception as part of what is owed. This lens catches a failure the other two miss: an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one, yet leaves the guest with no one who must answer.

**What the paper does not claim.** It does not claim that machines can or cannot be moral agents; it follows Kropf et al. in letting the machine hold a role without answerability. It does not claim that guests prefer humans. Belanche et al. (2020) and Schmidt et al. (2025) show that preferences depend on what is at stake. Nor does it claim that a visible human is necessary: Di Domenico and Lynch (2007) show that presence alone does not secure standing. It is a conceptual paper, and it tests nothing.

**Fit with the special issue.** The paper takes the special issue's central concept, phygital hospitality, and asks what its promise of fluidity costs (Batat 2024). It argues on the editor's terms: Batat's own framework holds that failures by human staff "cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors" (Batat 2024: 1234), which is the paper's non-compensability claim.

**How the revision answers the decision.**
- **Reviewer 2's questions** on why the obligation remains, where it comes from, whether it transfers, and how it differs from accountability get a dedicated §2.
- **Guest standing** gets §3, anchored in Hohfeld and Darwall.
- **Hospitality's distinctiveness** becomes an argument against Slattery's denial, rather than an assertion.
- **Reviewer 3's concerns** about drift, tone, and frameworks that don't follow from the argument are met three ways: by one line of reasoning in which every section takes its premise from the last, by a derivation table, and by rewriting the flagged phrases.
- **Reviewer 1's six requests** each have a located change; the concern map is in `REVISION_PLAN.md`.
- **The length** fits within 9,000 words all-inclusive.

### Abstract (150 words)

Five moves, plain test.
- **Phenomenon.** Phygital hospitality delegates hosting to algorithms.
- **Tension.** The obligation does not travel with the work.
- **What the paper does.** It locates the obligation, defines guest standing, and derives a three-condition test.
- **Payoff.** Accountability cannot be traded for transparency or predictability.
- **Implication.** The fix is constitutional, not only interface design.
- State the test in one sentence (R1.4).

### 2. The host's obligation and its address (1,000)

The new theoretical core. It answers Reviewer 2 and the editor's distinctiveness question.

**2.1 What the commercial host owes, and who owes it.**
- **The obligation.** Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995; Lashley 2000 (v4).
- **The holder is the institution.** Shryock 2012: S20, S25, S30; O'Gorman 2009 (card it before citing).
- **The rival.** Slattery 2002: 21. Lashley's reply as quoted there (p. 22; cite Lashley directly).
- **The displacement.** Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1296, 1299–1300.
- **The employee is not the host** (v4 §1 ¶4). The contract point stays, now grounded in the institution as holder.

**2.2 What delegation does to answering.**
- Nissenbaum 1996: PDF pp. 3, 10, 11, 15.
- Kong 2026: 4, 5, 10.
- Kropf et al. 2026: 4, 7, 9, 10, 12.
- Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021: §4.3 ¶1 for the institutional holder, *not* for a duty typology.
- **State the premise.** The role can move; the answering cannot.
- **State the normative claim as the paper's own.** The answering *ought* to stay with the institution that delegated. Nissenbaum shows it is otherwise lost.
- **Precedent for the stakes.** Germann Molz 2018: 244–245.

**2.3 What makes this obligation hospitality's.**
- **Prior to harm.** Kong's answerability is indexed to foreseeable harm (p. 5); Nissenbaum's is worked out on malfunctions. The host's obligation is owed to an arrival from the threshold onward, before any harm.
- **Includes the exception.** The obligation includes the power to make one. This is the authors' move; Xiang et al. show it in practice as a management-approved accommodation.
- **Replace v4 §4 ¶5's trigger typology** with this contrast. Either source the care/fiduciary/governance analogy elsewhere or present it as the authors' own.

**2.4 Four terms.** Obligation, responsibility, authority, accountability (v4 §2 ¶3), with their unity in the face-to-face host as the baseline.

### 3. Guest standing (650)

- **Claim.** Standing is the correlative of the host's obligation: the guest's authority to address claims to the institution that owes the welcome.
- **The tradition.**
