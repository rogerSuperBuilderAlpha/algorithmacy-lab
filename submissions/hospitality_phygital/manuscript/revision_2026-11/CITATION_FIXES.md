# Citation fixes for the revision

8 November 2026. Drafted by Claude from research run rr-2026-11. Every item was checked by two independent verifiers and a final Fable read against the source. Full detail and quotations are in `research/FABLE_fable_1.md` to `FABLE_fable_4.md`. Line numbers refer to v4 as submitted.

## 1. v4 sentences the source does not support — fix before resubmission

Ranked by how much the argument leans on each one.

1. **§4 ¶5, duty typology (Santoni de Sio & Mecacci 2021).** "Care is owed because someone is in need. Fiduciary duties … trusted. Governance duties … risk." The paper has no such typology; "fiduciary" never appears. The new §2 *Origin* builds on this sentence. Find another source or state it as your own argument.
2. **§7, the "limit" sentence (Lind et al. 1990).** "An invitation to speak that was never going to bear on anything lowers those judgments." Lind found the opposite: voice that changed nothing *raised* both kinds of fairness (pp. 955–956). The preceding sentence ("voice offered after a decision … still raised …") is fully supported. A *conditional* lowering effect can be cited: Folger's frustration effect (Lind et al. p. 953), or Lind & Tyler 1988 as quoted in Vaccaro et al. (p. 167:16). Move Goodwin & Ross off the earlier sentence; at most it supports "a lesser effect, sometimes lower".
3. **§7, roadmap (Bovens 2007).** "constituting a forum that is required to answer challenges" reverses Bovens: the actor answers, the forum questions (p. 450). "Someone in that forum has real authority to act" does fit Bovens (p. 452). "A meaningful alternative" is not in Bovens. Wilson-Nash et al. (2026, pp. 978–981) carry it instead.
4. **§7, appeal/oversight/audit (Martin & Waldman 2023).** The study compared five mechanisms, not three. Only appeal raised legitimacy. Impact assessment, a human in the loop and audit scored below mere notice, in a survey with no bad outcome. Suggested wording is in FABLE_fable_2 item 2.
5. **§7, "a person must stay reachable" (Yurrita et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2019).** Yurrita found no difference between appeal to a different human and appeal to the same system (p = 0.45). Lee supports reversibility, not reachability. Shabnam et al. (2026) recommend "escalation pathways and human oversight" (PDF p. 14); cite them here.
6. **§6, the hinge (Lin 2025).** "Users who understand a platform's curation well still cannot bend it." Seventeen of 31 did bend non-political curation (pp. 8–9). What they could not move was political curation and the platform's power. Keep the second clause; rewrite the first. Do not use Eslami et al. to shore it up: their remedy is the user-education fix §7 rejects.
7. **§4, pricing desks (Jianu et al. 2025).** "the managers who run such desks describe a running negotiation…" This is a Delphi forecast (22 experts, nine academics), not managers describing practice. Its "translation" runs to staff, not guests. Reword ("a hotel Delphi panel expects…") or drop.
8. **§4, front-desk craft (Bendoly 2013; Fuller & Smith 1991).** "Skilled front-desk employees often manufacture a guest's sense of control." Bendoly is a lab study of reservation agents' pricing. Fuller & Smith is about managers using customer feedback to discipline workers. Neither supports the sentence. Möhlmann et al. support only the second half, for Uber drivers.
9. **§7, seamlessness as unquestioned (Batat & Hammedi 2023; Batat 2024; Mameli et al. 2026).** Both quotations are word for word with the right pages, but three glosses go too far:
   - "describes its purpose as": the p. 10 sentence sits under "Findings"; the goal sentence is p. 11.
   - "aims at 'fluidifying'": in the abstract fluidifying is the means; the goal wording is p. 1235.
   - "no discussion of friction at all": the word never appears, but Mameli discusses risks and drawbacks (pp. 13–14, 22, 26). Say "never names friction".
   A reviewer close to the editor may cite Batat (2024: 1238): a positive phygital experience should not be "the aim".
10. **§7, what to keep (Padigar et al. 2025).** They never discuss recognition, negotiation or exceptions, and they treat contacting customer service about a problem as friction to reduce (p. 32). Argue the extension yourselves.
11. **§7, "explain a decision where it lands" (Pigac et al. 2026).** Guests wanted minimal disclosure at check-in and fuller explanation later (p. 286). The paper never mentions contest or appeal.
12. **§7, "make rule changes visible" (Hatherley 2025).** He says telling users about updates "may not reduce" the risk (p. 11). He supports the depreciation of learned knowledge (p. 5), not the remedy.
13. **§2, guest agency items (Pijls et al. 2017).** "Choice" and "independence" were dropped (pp. 130–131), but a third autonomy item, "experience freedom", was kept, and the paper's word is "autonomy". "The questions did not survive" goes too far.
14. **§4, withheld task list (Spektor et al. 2023, 2025).** The 2023 paper supports it almost verbatim (pp. 628–629). The 2025 paper is a counter-case: there, the union secured self-sequencing. Write "configured to lock".
15. **§2 ¶5, Kropf et al. 2026.** "the role responsibility of a carer": the paper says the robot's role is explicitly *not* a nurse's (pp. 4, 6). Write "of a care robot".
16. **Smaller wording fixes:**
    - Duggan et al.: "rarely", not "at all" (p. 1448).
    - Zientara et al.: *intend* to leave rather than join a union.
    - Beatty et al.: the sample is oil-change and pest-control technicians.
    - Liu et al.: "ask whether", not "test".
    - Batat 2021: the "rules stay steady / staff keep discretion" half of the restaurant case is your reading.
    - Ananny & Crawford: they carry "transparency alone is not enough"; "obliged to answer" is Bovens.
    - The §8 well-being "four lines" are Batat 2022 alone, and her object is luxury.
    - Raisch & Krakowski and Parasuraman et al. are positions argued past, not support.
    - Odekerken-Schröder et al. never use "host".
    - Bulley: the continuing commercial obligation is your inference.

## 2. Revision-plan moves that need rewording

- **§2 Transfer, "ownership moves only with the role".** Neither Kong nor Kropf grounds answerability in the role.
  - Kong grounds it in control over the decision to delegate (p. 4). Roles only mark where answerability lands across a division of labour.
  - Kropf et al. let the role pass to the machine while answerability stays human (pp. 7, 9–12).
  - Better premise: *the role can move; the answering cannot*.
  - The trustee comparison has no source: Kong sets fiduciary duty aside (p. 4).
- **§2 Transfer, Nissenbaum 1996.** She says that without new lines of accountability it "will be lost". So "stays with the institution by default" has to be your normative claim, with Nissenbaum cited for the risk.
- **§2 Transfer, Germann Molz 2018.** Airbnb pushes liability onto hosts (pp. 244–245). Cite it as the precedent for the stakes, not as support.
- **§2 Distinctness, Golubovskaya et al. 2017.** Their hospitality is an attitude, not an obligation (pp. 1283, 1287). Use it only for how service language displaces hospitality (pp. 1296, 1299–1300).
- **§2 Distinctness, the claimed surplus.** Xiang et al. and King already say hotels are "obliged to provide care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety". Claim only the narrower point: a negotiated exception owed to *this* arrival, with a party who can be reached.
- **§3, Xiang et al. 2022.** What it documents is a management-approved *compensation*, not an exception. The testing rule was not waived.
- **§3, Di Domenico & Lynch 2007.** Hosts set the rules and guests defer (pp. 332–335). Use it as the baseline: a host who is present did not by itself let guests press claims.
- **§3, standing.** "Standing exists only while a counterparty is reachable" is your addition; Darwall and Hohfeld don't say it. Darwall's standing belongs to persons as such, which strains "standing is conferred", and he uses "second-personal competence".
- **§9, AI Act.** Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 moves the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I). Article 86 still reads "from 2 August 2026". Don't print that date unqualified.
- **§1, opening on Garcia et al.** The study's actors are managers, not desk staff. The published Management Science version hasn't been read yet.

## 3. New support worth using

- **Kong 2026.** "One who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it" (p. 10); "a smaller quantity with an address" (p. 10).
- **Kropf et al. 2026.** A care robot's answerability "is currently inconceivable" (p. 9); blame stays with humans (pp. 10, 12).
- **Wilson-Nash et al. 2026.** The definition of captivity (p. 975) and its distinction from digital exclusion (p. 976). A downgraded analogue route still counts as captivity (p. 978). Refusing the app "may carry a personal cost and loss" (p. 979). Letting people choose how to access the service is the mitigation (p. 981).
- **Batat 2024 (p. 1234).** Failures by human staff "cannot be fixed" by improving the technology; this comes from the editor's own framework. Batat 2024 (p. 1233) and Mosca et al. (pp. 427, 440–441) name "seamless" as the goal outright.
- **Gursoy 2026 (p. 160).** Algorithmic governance "shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics, raising questions about accountability". This is the field's nearest statement of the authority–answerability split; engage it in §4.
- **Shryock 2012.** Hosting as sovereignty over a space, and protecting guests (pp. S20–S30). **O'Gorman 2009** gives the commercial-history anchor. **Darwall 2013 and Hohfeld 1913** ground standing. **Slattery 2002** is the rival position, that guests are just customers.
