# The duties of the host: what the literature says and where the paper can stand

10 November 2026 · for the decision meeting planned for 22–23 November

## 1. The short version

The paper assumes the host owes the guest something and builds everything on it. Five literatures can ground that assumption. They ground a narrower and more conditional duty than the pitch claims.

1. **What switches the duty on.** Innkeeper law gives a precise answer. The duty applies to a house that holds itself out to travellers, and to a traveller "who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum … and who is in a fit state to be received" (Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 s 1(3); *Calye's Case* 1584, fol. 32b). Arrival alone does not switch it on.
2. **Who holds it.** The duty attaches to "the proprietor … and shall not attach to any other person" (HPA s 1(1)). The proprietor answers for the servants who do the work (*Calye*, fol. 33a). When several firms share a hotel, one US court also held the brand answerable, because it presented the property as its own (*Crinkley v. Holiday Inns*, 4th Cir. 1988, paras 49–52).
3. **What can be delegated.** The UK Supreme Court states the structure the paper needs: the work "may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's" (*Woodland* [2013] UKSC 66, para 7). The judgment concerns schools and hospitals, not hotels, so the paper can borrow the structure but not claim the law already applies it to hotels.
4. **Where the guest's claim comes from.** The anthropology of hospitality gives the guest duties and denies the guest the right to make demands. In Pitt-Rivers's account, a guest who "makes claims or demands … usurps the host's right" (2012, p. 515). Whatever lets a guest press a claim comes from the commercial and legal side of the relation, not from hospitality as such.
5. **What is distinctive of hospitality.** The paper's two current answers fail. "Prior to harm" fails because banks, hospitals and every UK service provider now carry duties that apply before any harm (H1 Q4; *Woodland* para 7). "Includes the exception" fails because the only exceptions the law knows run in the host's favour. Three things remain distinctive:
   - a duty to *receive*, which once bound all common callings and survived for innkeepers and carriers (Burdick 1911, p. 523);
   - owed to a stranger "away from their home communities" for the length of a stay (Sandoval-Strausz 2005, pp. 62–63);
   - fixed "by law rather than by contract" once the guest is admitted (Maese 2015, p. 501).

The recommendation that survives all five workstreams is the one Fable proposed before any of this research began. Institutional role obligation is the frame, innkeeper law is the evidence for when the duty starts and who holds it, and control of the threshold is description, not ground. One choice inside that frame is yours to make (§4).

## 2. The literatures, and what each gives the paper

### 2.1 Hospitality studies: a virtue, not a role
Hospitableness research measures the host's duty as a personal disposition. Its examples are:
- Lashley's (2015) instrument to "identify those individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable";
- Liu et al.'s "host's disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity" (2026, p. 2);
- Golubovskaya et al.'s "attitude and spirit" (2017, p. 1287).

Lashley's 2014 editorial calls "the duty of the host to welcome and protect guests" a central feature of human morality, and says those obligations have lost their former importance (abstract). H1 found no hospitableness work that assigns the duty to an organisation or a role (Consensus, 10 Nov 2026). This is the position the paper argues past. It is also why hospitality studies alone cannot answer Reviewer 2: a virtue has no holder to delegate from.

**Debt:** this line rests mostly on abstracts. Every relevant full text was closed to the session: Telfer, King 1995, Lashley 2000, Lashley 2015, and Blain and Lashley 2014.

### 2.2 Anthropology: duties on both sides, demands on neither
Pitt-Rivers sets out the rule most fully (read in full):
- **The host** "has rights and obligations in regard to his guest". He must protect the guest and show concern for the guest's "needs and wishes" (pp. 514, 516).
- **The guest** "has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host" (p. 514).
- **Breach** by either side "entitles the person slighted … to relinquish his role" (p. 517).

Shryock (read in full) adds a warning about the commercial case. He hands the modern care of strangers to "the hospitality industry … and the government bodies that control immigration and naturalization" (p. S30). He suggests, hedged with "perhaps because", that relations in both spheres are "depersonalized, formulaic, monetized" (pp. S30–S31).

This answers Reviewer 3's point about "what guests owe" (R3.10). It also tells the paper not to rest its idea of standing on hospitality's own anthropology.

### 2.3 Innkeeper law and public accommodation: when the duty starts and who holds it, but not why
The common law bound innkeepers, like common carriers, to receive all comers. That duty "now attached only to common carriers by land and water (including ferrymen) and innkeepers" (Burdick 1911, p. 523), while the shopkeeper stayed free to refuse (Wyman 1904, p. 159).

What the duty contained:
- "bed, board, and hearth" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005, p. 63);
- strict liability for the guest's goods "and not his person" (*Calye*, fol. 33b). That is the holding on the writ; Bogen (p. 82 n. 160) and Maese (p. 499, restating Beale) report liability for harm to guests, neither with a date.

Today it runs through the HPA 1956 in the UK and Title II of the Civil Rights Act 1964 in the US. Title II deems every lodging establishment to affect commerce (§ 2000a(c)(1)) and exempts the owner-occupied house of five rooms or fewer (§ 2000a(b)(1)), which gives the paper a ready boundary condition.

The legal history does not settle *why* innkeepers were bound. Bogen calls the holding-out explanation "not so much a theory as a tautology" (1996, p. 52) and treats innkeeping as a status. On that account "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty" (p. 75). Sandoval-Strausz grounds it in the traveller's vulnerability (pp. 62–63), Bogen in crime control (p. 53). The paper can cite the law for when the duty starts and who holds it. It should not claim the law explains why.

### 2.4 Role obligation and non-delegable duty: what moves and what stays
The philosophers of role obligation were mostly out of reach:
- Hart could not be reached;
- Hardimon has a metadata card only;
- Vincent is read only through Kropf et al., whose account gives role duties as "specific obligations that would not exist without that role" (2026, p. 3), weighted towards positive duties.

The usable statement came from law:
- **Woodland:** a non-delegable duty arises from "an antecedent relationship", is "a positive or affirmative duty to protect", and is "personal to the defendant" (para 7). It rests on control "over the claimant", not over the environment (para 24).
- **Morgan:** "No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be" (2015, accepted manuscript p. 7).
- **A paid provider:** for this case the court notes that the duty is already non-delegable by contract, and that contracting out is "limited by legislation" (*Woodland* para 25(5)).
- **A firm that only arranges:** one "whose own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance" is not liable (para 25(3)). That gives the multi-firm question a test.

### 2.5 Multi-actor responsibility: the host is several firms
Before any algorithm, three parties shared the welcome at a 1981 Holiday Inn desk:
- the owners;
- TRAVCO, "in actual day-to-day control";
- the franchisor (*Crinkley* para 7).

The court held all three answerable. It reached the brand by apparent agency, on reliance evidence it called "marginal" (para 52). That is one circuit applying North Carolina law, not a general rule.

Technology adds a chain of firms in which "there is … typically no one actor in overall control" (Cobbe et al. 2023, arXiv p. 7). Vendors keep control while disclaiming accountability (p. 10). In short-term rentals the host role breaks into platform-defined parts: primary host, co-host and listing admin (Bosma 2022, p. 601). Self-check-in turns "hosting into ghosting" (p. 605).

### 2.6 Frontline authority: graded, not absolute
Hotel service recovery is "carefully stage-managed and 'staircased' … with empowerment strongly related to hierarchical level" (Hewagama et al. 2019, abstract). In Amsterdam five-star hotels empowerment meant "increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice … or more voice" (Hales and Klidas 1998, abstract).

Requests do change outcomes, through escalation. In Xiang et al. (2022), a front-office employee who checked "with hotel management" secured an accommodation. A refused guest took the complaint to "the general manager and the group head office". H4 found no study that follows a guest's request up the path to whoever has authority over the rule. That is a gap the paper can name in §9.

### 2.7 When the guest needs a person at the moment of decision
Baum and Laux (2026, arXiv v2) give the test. What matters is "not elapsed time but whether intervention can occur before the token output becomes effective" (p. 6).

Data-protection law covers part of the ground:
- GDPR Art. 22(3) gives the person a right "to obtain human intervention … and to contest the decision". That is an account of an addressee, which the pitch says algorithmic governance lacks.
- Art. 22(3) states no timing, and many hotel decisions may fall under the contract exception in Art. 22(2)(a).
- The EU regulators' guidance (WP251) requires a reviewer "who has the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision" (p. 27).

## 4. The one choice you must make: imposed status or assumed undertaking

H1 and H2 ground Position C differently, and the sources pull apart:

- **Imposed status (H1).** The law places the duty on whoever takes up the calling, whatever they promised. Bogen: "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty" (p. 75), and holding out as explanation is "a tautology" (p. 52). This fits the "fixed by law rather than by contract" line (Maese p. 501). It answers Reviewer 2's "what makes it non-transferable" most directly: the holder cannot shed a status by contracting out the work.
- **Assumed undertaking (H2).** The duty arises because the host undertook to receive the guest into its charge (*Woodland* paras 11, 23). This fits the paper's language of an institution that "opened the threshold". It extends naturally to platforms and brands that hold themselves out. But *Woodland* itself says the assumption is "imputed" by law (para 11), so the two accounts are closer than they look.

A workable middle, drafted by me for you to accept or reject: the law imposes the duty on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, and holding out is how a party takes up the role. That keeps Bogen's point (the duty is not a promise) and *Woodland*'s point (the holder is whoever took charge of the person received).

## 5. Definitions that remove the circularity (R2.3)

Darwall defines a duty owed to someone through the obligee's authority "to hold the obligor personally accountable" (OSO abstract; p. 36). If standing comes from Darwall and accountability is then defined as answering someone with standing, the paper runs in a circle. Thompson defines a duty owed to someone without mentioning accountability: "X has a duty to Y to do A" (2004, p. 335).

H2 proposes this order, each term defined only from those above it:

1. **Obligation.** A directed duty the host owes *to* the guest: to receive, protect and attend. Its form comes from Thompson (p. 335); its content from innkeeper law (receive, feed, keep goods safe) and Pitt-Rivers (protect, attend to "needs and wishes").
2. **Standing, held.** The guest's position as the one owed (1). It exists whether or not it is claimed (Darwall p. 35).
3. **Standing, exercised.** The guest pressing that claim to someone able to receive it. Liau's legal "standing" is this enforcement side (pdf pp. 1, 5). "Able to receive it" is the paper's own addition.
4. **Responsibility.** The holder's retained duty to see that (1) is performed by whoever does the work (*Woodland* para 7; Morgan pp. 7–8).
5. **Authority.** The power, assigned by the organisation, to change the decision or the rule. This is the paper's own definition, supported in substance by the graded authority in §2.6.
6. **Accountability.** The arrangement through which the holder answers to the guest for (1), and through which (3) can be exercised (Bovens 2007, pp. 450, 452).

The paper's diagnosis then reads without the circle. Under algorithmic mediation the guest still *holds* standing, because the obligation persists. What fails is the *exercise*, because the accountability arrangement offers no one with authority to receive the claim.

Pitt-Rivers explains why (3) needs a commercial or legal source. Di Domenico and Lynch show a fully empowered host who was present, while the guest still withheld a complaint as "not the done thing" (2007, p. 333, one researcher's note). On H4's reading, a reachable addressee with authority is necessary for the claim to count but not sufficient. That inference is H4's, not the authors'.

## 7. Sentences in your pitch and outline that the evidence contradicts

I have changed none of these. Each one is quoted, with what the sources say. Full detail is in `research/FABLE_H_a.md` §For the authors and `research/FABLE_H_b.md` §4.

1. **"what is owed to a person simply because they walked in the door, treating the capacity to negotiate an exception as an inherent part of that obligation"** (pitch, last paragraph).
   - The law makes the trigger conditional (HPA s 1(3); *Calye* fol. 32b; a full house excuses refusal, Holt CJ dissenting in *Lane v. Cotton*, 12 Mod 485).
   - No source makes an exception something owed to the guest.
2. **"commercial hosts owe guests care, sanctuary, and welcome because they control the physical premises (Shryock 2012: S25, S30 …)"** (pitch ¶2).
   - Shryock S25 concerns Jordanian tribes and the monarchy.
   - S30 hands care to the industry *and* the state.
   - The law grounds the duty in status, not in control.
3. **"Every institutional duty generates a correlative claim"** (pitch ¶3). Too broad: only duties owed *to* someone have a claimant (Thompson p. 338).
4. **"(Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–333), since floor staff often lack the authority …"** (pitch ¶6). Those pages concern owner-hosts with full authority. Baum and Laux p. 12 or WP251 p. 21 support the "since" clause.
5. **Algorithmic governance "has nothing to say about … how a guest can exercise standing"** (pitch ¶2). GDPR Art. 22(3) says something, though narrowly.
6. **"Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1296, 1299–1300" for "repeatedly reduced hospitality to transaction management"** (pitch ¶2). The pages support displacement by service terminology in one study. "Repeatedly" and "transaction management" are not the source's words.
