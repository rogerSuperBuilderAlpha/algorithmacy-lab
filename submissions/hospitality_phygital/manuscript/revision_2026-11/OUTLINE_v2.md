## Abstract (150)

- **Phenomenon.** Hospitality firms now hand hosting work — admission, pricing, check-in, room allocation, complaints — to algorithmic systems spread across several firms.
- **Question.** When the work moves, where does the host's obligation to the guest go, and what must remain for the guest to press a claim?
- **Argument.**
  - The commercial host's obligation is a role obligation. It is switched on when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller is received, and it is held by whoever took up that role.
  - The work can be delegated. The duty and the answering cannot.
  - The guest's standing persists. Its exercise depends on a reachable party with authority to change the decision.
- **Contribution, in one sentence each.**
  - A theory of the host's obligation under delegation.
  - A non-circular account of guest standing.
  - A test, scoped by decision type, for when a phygital touchpoint keeps or removes the conditions for exercising standing.

This answers Reviewer 1's request to present the test better in the abstract.

## 2. The host's obligation (1,500)

This section answers all five of Reviewer 2's questions on obligation (R2-1, R2-2) and Reviewer 3's point that welcome is not "a single duty".

### 2.1 Two accounts of the host's obligation (350)

**Claim.** Hospitality studies treat the host's obligation as a virtue of persons. The law treats it as a duty of a role. Only the second survives delegation.

- **The virtue account.**
  - Hospitableness is "a host's disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity" (Liu et al. 2026: 2).
  - Lashley's instrument identifies "individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable" (2015, abstract; full text pending).
  - Golubovskaya et al. call it "an attitude and spirit" (2017: 1287).
  - A virtue belongs to a person. It cannot be delegated, and when the person is replaced by a system it simply disappears. That is why this account cannot answer Reviewer 2.
- **The role account.**
  - The common law bound the innkeeper, like the common carrier, to receive all comers. That duty "now attached only to common carriers by land and water … and innkeepers" (Burdick 1911: 523). The shopkeeper stayed free to refuse (Wyman 1904: 159).
  - The duty was "fixed by law rather than by contract" (Maese 2015: 501).
  - "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty" (Bogen 1996: 75).
- **Position statement.** The paper takes the role account. Hospitableness remains the quality with which the role is performed, not the source of the duty.

### 2.2 What the obligation contains (250)

**Claim.** The obligation is a bundle of duties, not one duty (R3: "single duty").

- **Receive.** "Bed, board, and hearth" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 63).
- **Keep safe.** Strict liability for the guest's goods "and not his person" (*Calye* 1584, fol. 33b). Liability for harm to the guest is reported by Bogen (1996: 82 n. 160) and by Maese, restating Beale (2015: 499).
- **Attend.** In the anthropological law of hospitality the host must protect the guest and show concern for the guest's "needs and wishes" (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 516).
- **Each duty sits in a context.** The welcome is shaped by its social and organisational setting, with conditions on both sides (R3). The guest's side is set out in §3.
- **Do not claim** that the law governed the *manner* of the welcome, or that it treated a negotiated exception as owed to the guest. No source supports either.

### 2.3 What switches the obligation on, and who holds it (400)

**Claim.** The duty switches on when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller who can pay and is fit is received. It is held by whoever took up that role, and holding out is how a party takes it up.

- **When it switches on** (R2-1, R2-2).
  - The duty applies to a house "held out by the proprietor" as offering lodging to travellers, and to a traveller "who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum … and who is in a fit state to be received" (HPA 1956 s 1(3)).
  - A neighbour "who is no traveller" has no claim (*Calye*, fol. 32b).
  - A full house excuses refusal (Holt CJ, dissenting, *Lane v Cotton*, 12 Mod 485).
  - This replaces "arrival" as the trigger, which every source contradicts.
- **Who holds it first** (R2-1, R2-2).
  - The duty attaches to "the proprietor … and shall not attach to any other person" (HPA s 1(1)).
  - The proprietor answers for "a default in him or his servants" (*Calye*, fol. 33a).
- **Several firms at once** (R2-1's "hotel company, operator, employee, platform, vendor").
  - In 1981 three parties shared one Holiday Inn: owners, an operator "in actual day-to-day control", and a franchisor. The court held all three answerable, reaching the brand because it presented the property as its own and guests relied on that (*Crinkley v Holiday Inns*, 844 F.2d 156, paras 7, 49–52). That is one circuit on reliance evidence it called "marginal", and the paper should state its scope.
  - A firm whose "own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance" is not liable (*Woodland* [2013] UKSC 66, para 25(3)). The paper borrows that line: perform versus arrange.
  - Vendors sit in chains with "typically no one actor in overall control" (Cobbe et al. 2023, arXiv p. 7; convert to proceedings pages).
- **The paper's rule** (marked as the paper's own proposal).
  - Whoever holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside whoever controls the operation.
  - A party that only arranges the service, such as a vendor or a pure intermediary, answers to the host, not to the guest.
- **Why** (do not overclaim). The legal history disputes why innkeepers were bound (Bogen 1996: 52, "not so much a theory as a tautology"). The paper explains when the duty switches on and who holds it, and grounds the rationale in the stranger's exposure "away from their home communities" (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63).

### 2.4 What can and cannot be delegated (300)

**Claim.** The work of hosting can be delegated to staff, contractors or systems. The duty, and the answering for it, stay with the role-holder.

- **The legal structure.**
  - The work "may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's" (*Woodland* para 7).
  - "No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be" (Morgan 2015, accepted manuscript p. 7).
  - Woodland concerns schools and hospitals. The paper borrows the structure and says so; it does not claim hotels are already in the non-delegable category.
- **A paid provider.** A provider paid for the service already holds non-delegable responsibilities by contract, and contracting out of them is "limited by legislation" (*Woodland* para 25(5)).
- **Against Kong** (R2-2: "more familiar problem of organizational delegation or algorithmic accountability").
  - Kong keeps answerability with the deployer through control over the act of delegating (2026: 4–5).
  - The paper's duty is first-order, owed to a particular person, and held from reception onward.
  - Kropf et al. (2026: 7, 9) support the robot-as-role-performer side.
- **What is new under algorithms.** Delegation itself is old: the innkeeper's servants did the work in 1584. What changes is that the performer can be a system no guest can address, supplied by a firm no guest can reach (→ §4).

### 2.5 What is hospitality's own (200)

**Claim.** The mechanism is general. What hospitality adds is a duty to *receive* a stranger into one's house, fixed by status.

- **Concede to R2-4.**
  - Banks, hospitals and all UK service providers carry duties that apply before any harm (Equality Act 2010 s 29(1); Payment Accounts Regulations 2015 reg 18(1); EMTALA § 1395dd(a); Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s 2(2)).
  - Inaccessible decision-makers and weak appeals occur in banking and e-commerce too.
- **What remains distinctive.**
  1. The duty to receive survived only for innkeepers and carriers (Burdick 1911: 523).
  2. What is received is the stranger's body and belongings, overnight, in another's house (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). The hospital's statutory duty, by contrast, is triggered by an emergency and ends at stabilisation.
  3. Once the guest is admitted, the duty is fixed by law rather than contract (Maese 2015: 501).
- **Therefore** the failure the paper names bites hardest where these three meet: admission and the stay. §6 turns this into boundary conditions.

## 3. Guest standing (900)

This section answers R2-3 (circularity, and held versus exercised), R3's point on "what guests owe", and R1's request for host–guest negotiation.

### 3.1 Definitions, in order (300)

**Claim.** Standing is the guest's position as the one owed the host's duty. Accountability is the later arrangement through which that standing is exercised. Defined in this order, the two do not refer to each other.

Each term below is defined only from the terms above it.

| # | Term | Definition | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Obligation | the directed duties of §2.2, owed *to* the guest | Thompson 2004: 335 ("X has a duty to Y to do A") |
| 2 | Standing, held | the guest's position as the one owed (1); it exists whether or not it is claimed | Darwall 2013: 35 |
| 3 | Standing, exercised | the guest pressing the claim to a party able to receive it | Liau's legal "standing" is this enforcement side (2023, pdf pp. 1, 5) |
| 4 | Responsibility | the role-holder's retained duty to see that (1) is performed | *Woodland* para 7 |
| 5 | Authority | the organisationally assigned power to change the decision or the rule | the paper's own definition |
| 6 | Accountability | the arrangement through which the holder answers to the guest, and through which (3) happens | Bovens 2007: 450, 452 — the actor answers and the forum questions |

- **Flag the source of the circle.** Darwall defines a duty owed to someone through the right to hold the obligor accountable (p. 36). If accountability is then defined through standing, the paper runs in a loop. Thompson's directed form avoids it.
- **Only duties owed to someone have a claimant** (Thompson 2004: 338). This replaces the pitch's "every institutional duty …"; you decide the wording.

### 3.2 What guests owe (250)

**Claim.** The guest has duties too, and a breach ends the role (R3: the p. 6 claims about what guests owe).

- **In the anthropology.**
  - The guest owes "respect" and "honour" (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 514).
  - The guest breaches the role by insulting or rivalling the host, by "making himself at home", and by making "claims or demands" (p. 515).
  - A breach "entitles the person slighted … to relinquish his role" (p. 517).
- **In commercial hospitality** the guest's "only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably" (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1286, reporting King 1995; read King first).
- **In law** the traveller must appear able to pay and fit to be received (HPA s 1(3)).
- **Hotels eject non-compliant guests** (Xiang et al. 2022; Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 334).
- **Replace the v4 passage** about "what guests owe" and about measuring standing. Keep only the measurement point, which survives: the field has no instrument for the guest's side.

### 3.3 Where the power to press a claim comes from (200)

**Claim.** Hospitality's own norms withhold the guest's demand. The commercial and legal relation supplies it, and the institution decides whether there is anyone to receive it.

- **Pitt-Rivers.** A dissatisfied guest "has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out" (2012: 513).
- **Di Domenico and Lynch.** Even with a fully empowered owner-host present, a guest withheld a complaint as "not the done thing"; "in a hotel one might complain" (2007: 333; one researcher's note).
- **What follows** (the paper's inference):
  - A reachable party with authority is necessary for exercise, but not sufficient.
  - The paying relation makes pressing a claim proper, and the traveller had an action at law (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 64).
- **Competence is not standing** (folds in the old §6). Guests learn to work systems (Eslami et al. 2016), but no skill creates an addressee (Lin 2025: 8–9, 11; Vaccaro et al. 2020).

### 3.4 Negotiation as the exercise of standing (150)

This answers R1: host–guest interaction that is more than functional and that creates value.

- **Requests change outcomes through escalation.**
  - A front-office employee who checked "with hotel management" secured an accommodation for a guest (Xiang et al. 2022).
  - A refused guest took the complaint to "the general manager and the group head office" (Xiang et al. 2022).
- **Requests change outcomes through co-production.** Regulars' requests created a bar's house cocktails (Lugosi 2009, section "Situational dimensions").
- **The bounded form of a guest's protest.** Pitt-Rivers's diplomatic guest turned his plate over (2012: 513–514).
- **What the guest can expect** is a hearing by someone with authority, not the exception itself.

## 4. The authority–answerability split (900)

This answers R3's points that authority is treated as absolute and that the paper overrelies on hotel reception, and R1's request to remind the reader what "the split" is.

**Claim.** Authority in commercial hospitality was always graded and organisational. Phygital arrangements change the path from the guest to that authority: they can remove it, or move it to firms the guest cannot reach.

- **Define the split again at the head of the section** (R1). The party that can change the rule is not a party the guest can reach.
- **Before the algorithm.**
  - Hotel service recovery is "carefully stage-managed and 'staircased' … with empowerment strongly related to hierarchical level" (Hewagama et al. 2019, abstract; full text pending).
  - Empowerment meant "increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice … or more voice" (Hales and Klidas 1998, abstract).
  - Three firms shared one Holiday Inn (*Crinkley* para 7).
  - Replace v4's "all four sit in one person". That holds only for the owner-host of a commercial home (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–335).
- **The chain and the relay** (v4 §3–4, shortened). The arrangement is the unit of analysis: guest, employee, property, brand, platform, vendor.
- **Knowledge, discretion and authority** (v4 §4).
  - Knowledge drifts to the system (Rahman 2021).
  - Discretion is contested (Pedersen and Pors 2023; Spektor et al. 2023: 628–629).
  - Authority over the rule drifts to whoever configures it, often the vendor, which keeps "rights to dictate terms of use and change, withdraw, or cancel products and services at will" (Cobbe et al., arXiv p. 10).
- **Case and rule.** Discretion acts on the single case, authority on the rule. Hales and Klidas's "choice" and "voice" map onto them, although they do not make the paper's link.
- **The employee as translator** (old §6, shortened).
  - Staff carry the guest's request into terms the system accepts (Hatherley 2025).
  - Staff fall silent when speaking looks futile (Morrison 2014; Cheng et al. 2025).
- **Across formats** (R3: other hosting contexts).
  - In short-term rentals the host role is split by the platform into primary host, co-host and listing admin (Bosma 2022: 601). Self-check-in turns "hosting into ghosting" (p. 605).
  - Hosts refuse the hotel role: "I am not a butler though nor am I a receptionist in a hotel" (Kaniadakis and Farmaki 2022: 14).
  - The platform passes the guest-facing duties to hosts (Germann Molz 2018: 243–245).

## 6. Boundary conditions (650, new)

This answers R2-5 (which decisions, which formats) and R2-4 in part.

**Claim.** Immediate interruptibility matters for decisions that take effect on the guest on site and cannot be undone in time. Other decisions need a reachable appeal, and decisions too fast for any person need host-owned rules set in advance.

- **The criterion.** What matters is "not elapsed time but whether intervention can occur before the token output becomes effective" (Baum and Laux 2026, arXiv p. 6; check against the LNCS chapter).
- **Table 3 (new): hospitality decisions by stakes, reversibility and time-criticality.**
  - Adapted from WS_H5. Mark it as the paper's typology; no source makes these placements for hotels.
  - It covers admission and refusal, the walked guest, identity verification, safety and access, pricing, room assignment, complaints, and routine requests.
- **Law as a floor, not a ceiling.**
  - GDPR Art. 22(3) gives a right "to obtain human intervention … and to contest the decision", but states no timing.
  - The reviewer must have "the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision" (WP251: 27).
- **Formats.** Where the framework explains most, and where least.
  - Most: on-site, high-stakes, irreversible decisions in hotels and short-term rentals.
  - Less: remote, reversible ones.
  - Outside the commercial case: owner-occupied houses of five rooms or fewer are outside US public-accommodation law (Civil Rights Act 1964 § 2000a(b)(1)), which is the line between household and commercial host.
- **Guests do not all want speed.** Immediate recovery suited guests with mild negative emotion, delayed recovery those with intense emotion (Tang et al. 2018, abstract).

## 7. Designing for interruptible hospitality (900)

**Claim.** Interface design can secure common understanding and predictability. Only institutional safeguards secure accountability, and they should be scoped by §6's decision classes.

- **Three interface principles** (v4 §7). Keep them, with Pigac et al. 2026: 286, Yeung 2017 and Hatherley 2025.
- **Two institutional principles.**
  - **Reachable authority to reverse.** The reviewer must have "the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision" (WP251: 27). A human who "rubberstamps without genuine judgment does not thereby become an overseer" (Baum and Laux: 12). Add Shabnam et al. 2026, Lyons et al. 2021 and Martin and Waldman 2023.
  - **A real alternative.** Wilson-Nash et al. 2026 (976, 978–981); Hirschman 1970; Kim et al. 2025.
- **Separate interrupting from reversing** (the pitch joins them).
  - Synchronous intervention applies to §6's on-site irreversible class. A later appeal applies to the rest.
  - Rules set in advance must stay revisable (Baum and Laux: 6 n. 9, 8).
- **Seamlessness and interruptibility** (v4 §7). Keep Star and Ruhleder 1996 and Chalmers and Galani 2004. Define interruptibility with a counterparty.
- **Hollow voice.** Voice offered after a decision, with the decision unchanged, still raised fairness judgments (Lind et al. 1990). The counterparty has to be real.
- **Table 4: roadmap of rising cost** (old Table 3).
  - Fix stage 4 to "Constitute a forum before which an accountable actor must answer, with authority to change outcomes". Bovens: the actor answers, the forum questions (2007: 450, 452).
