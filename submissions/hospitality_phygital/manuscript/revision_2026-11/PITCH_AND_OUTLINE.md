# Who Hosts the Guest? Algorithmic Mediation, Standing, and Responsibility in Phygital Hospitality

## Pitch

Hospitality in a phygital world delegates hosting operations to algorithmic systems. Software now sets room prices, determines admission, routes requests, and even greets guests (Garcia et al. 2026; Xu et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2026). While service management research evaluates consumer acceptance (Belanche et al. 2020; Schmidt et al. 2025) and algorithmic accountability focuses on post-hoc harm (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5), neither addresses what becomes of the host's prior obligation to the guest. When an institution delegates hosting tasks to automated processes, answerability relocates to the delegating organization rather than dissolving into the machine (Kong 2026: 5, 10; Kropf et al. 2026: 9–10). However, such automated touchpoints seem to eliminate any authority to resolve claims or negotiate exceptions—the failure of automated hospitality lies in this structural loss of someone for the guest to address.

This gap exists because hospitality theory and algorithmic governance have developed in isolation. Hospitality theory explores how commercial hosts owe guests care, sanctuary, and welcome because they control the physical premises (Shryock 2012: S25, S30; Xiang et al. 2022; O'Gorman 2009), even as industry practice has repeatedly reduced hospitality to transaction management (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1296, 1299–1300; cf. Slattery 2002: 21). Algorithmic governance delegates tasks to an automated system, thus relocating answerability to the delegating institution (Kong 2026: 5; Nissenbaum 1996; Kropf et al. 2026: 9), indexing accountability almost exclusively to technical failure and downstream harm (Nissenbaum 1996; Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021; Kong 2026: 5), and has nothing to say about duties owed upon arrival or an account of how a guest can exercise standing when a machine stands in for the host.

This paper addresses that gap by linking the mechanics of delegation to the moral structure of standing (Darwall 2013: 20, 27–28; Hohfeld 1913: 32–33). Every institutional duty generates a correlative claim, giving the guest the standing to direct demands back to the host (Hohfeld 1913: 33; Darwall 2013: 28, 31–32). Delegating frontline tasks to software transfers the execution of hosting, but the obligation to answer remains with the organization (Kong 2026: 5, 10; Kropf et al. 2026: 7, 9). Phygital systems break down when they create an authority–answerability split (Gursoy 2026: 160): automated rules enforce the terms of the greeting, while the guest is cut off from anyone with the power to explain a decision or negotiate an exception.

To test whether an automated touchpoint preserves the necessary duties, the paper derives three institutional conditions from the basic structure of a welcome: common understanding, predictability, and accountability (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009: 483). While software interfaces can deliver transparency and predictable rules, information or transparency cannot replace accountability (Ananny and Crawford 2018: 984). Instead, hospitality systems must be designed around interruptibility. Preserving guest standing requires constitutional safeguards within the organization: reachable human staff who retain the authority to reverse algorithmic decisions (Lyons et al. 2021; Shabnam et al. 2026; Martin and Waldman 2023), alongside viable non-automated alternatives that prevent digital captivity (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 976, 978–981).

This paper makes three contributions. First, it establishes where the obligation to host lies under algorithmic delegation, distinguishing affirmative hospitality duties from harm-indexed accountability (Kong 2026: 5). Second, it develops the concept of guest standing, showing that while individuals can learn to navigate automated tools (Eslami et al. 2016; Lin 2025: 8–9), only institutions can establish the addressee required to exercise a claim (Vaccaro et al. 2020; Lin 2025: 11). Third, it operationalizes these principles into a five-stage roadmap that orders organizational interventions from disclosure to authority. This framework identifies a failure mode that neither service management nor algorithmic audits capture: an automated system that denies the guest any venue to be heard.

The paper sets clear boundaries. It does not argue that machines possess moral agency; it follows Kropf et al. (2026: 7, 9) in recognizing that software can perform an operational role without bearing moral answerability. Nor does it assume guests inherently prefer human staff over automated systems, as consumer preferences vary with the stakes of the encounter (Belanche et al. 2020; Schmidt et al. 2025). Crucially, the argument does not treat the physical presence of frontline workers as a substitute for standing (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–333), since floor staff often lack the authority to alter system rules (Gursoy 2026: 160; Möhlmann et al. 2021). As a conceptual study, the paper establishes the structural and institutional conditions under which commercial hosting remains possible when code, agents, interfaces, et al. mediate operations.

The argument directly engages the framework of phygital hospitality by examining the structural costs of seamless customer journeys (Batat 2024: 1220, 1233). Batat establishes that negative experiences generated by human connectors cannot be remedied by improving digital or physical touchpoints (2024: 1234). This paper extends that non-compensability principle to institutional obligations: automated efficiency cannot substitute for an accountable addressee. By identifying where the duty of hosting settles under delegation and defining the safeguards required to preserve standing, the paper shows how organizations can adopt algorithmic systems without severing the host–guest relationship.

Framing this problem through hospitality reveals a structural breakdown that service management and algorithmic accountability overlook. Service management asks whether an encounter delivers customer satisfaction. Algorithmic accountability asks who is liable when a system causes tangible harm. Hospitality asks what is owed to a person simply because they walked in the door, treating the capacity to negotiate an exception as an inherent part of that obligation.

## Outline

The paper runs to about 6,750 words of body and 2,250 of references, within the journal's 9,000-word limit. Each section takes its premise from the one before. The parenthetical v4 references mark material carried over from the submitted draft.

### Abstract (150 words)

- **Phenomenon.** Phygital hospitality hands the work of hosting to algorithmic systems.
- **Tension.** The work moves, but the obligation of hosting does not move with it, and the guest can be left with no one to address.
- **What the paper does.** It locates the obligation under delegation, defines guest standing, and derives a test of three conditions.
- **Payoff.** Accountability cannot be traded for transparency or predictability, so the remedy is institutional as well as technical.
- **The test in one sentence.** A touchpoint keeps hospitality only if the guest can understand it, anticipate it, and reach someone obliged to answer for it.

### 1. Introduction: Who answers for the welcome? (600 words)

- **Opening case.** Algorithmic hotel pricing. Garcia et al. (2026) found recommendations deliberately set away from the algorithm's own best price, to move managers who repriced too rarely. The rule in force was not one the desk could state, and the guest who questions the rate meets an employee who cannot answer for it.
- **The gap.**
  - The literature describes phygital technology by what it does: tool, mediator, facilitator, co-creator (v4 §1).
  - Germann Molz (2026) asks what happens as platforms erase the human host.
  - No one has asked what becomes of what the host owed.
- **Positioning.** Hospitality already holds that the commercial host is obliged to the guest (Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995). The open question is where that obligation goes, and whom the guest can address, once the work is delegated.
- **The heir question, briefly.** Machines can take operational roles (Kropf et al. 2026: 7). Answering for them is another matter, and the paper sets machine personhood aside.
- **Research question.** When phygital arrangements delegate the functions of hosting to algorithmic systems, where does the obligation of hosting go, and what must an arrangement preserve for the guest to remain a guest?
- **Thesis, three contributions, one itinerary.**

### 2. The host's obligation under delegation (1,000 words)

**2.1 What the commercial host owes, and who owes it.**
- **The obligation.** The commercial host is obliged to provide "care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety" (Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995). Lashley (2000) holds that a genuine welcome differs from being welcomed as a valued customer.
- **The holder.** Hospitality "is also a test of sovereignty" (Shryock 2012: S20), and "sovereignty is manifest in the ability to act as host" (S25). The modern holder is the industry, the market in hotels and restaurants (S30). Commercial thresholds have historically guaranteed "protection, sanctuary and security" (O'Gorman 2009).
- **The rival position.** Slattery (2002: 21) holds that "the buyers are not guests, they are customers." The paper answers him with Lashley.
- **The displacement.** Golubovskaya et al. (2017) found that only 6 of 22 hotel frontline staff could define hospitality (p. 1296), and that "service management terminology overrides hospitality" (pp. 1299–1300).
- **Employee and host are not the same.** A contract can specify services but not the obligation between host and guest (v4 §1). That obligation belongs to the institution that opened the threshold.

**2.2 What delegation does to the duty to answer.**
- **Nissenbaum (1996).** Answerability "cannot be spread, thinned and offset" and is obscured when blame falls on the computer. Unless new lines of accountability are built, it "will be lost".
- **Kong (2026).** Answerability is "relocated to the prior act of delegation" (p. 5). "One who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it" (p. 10). Roles mark where answerability lands across a division of labour (p. 4).
- **Kropf et al. (2026).** A care robot can hold a narrow role without accountability (p. 7). Its "answerability … is currently inconceivable" (p. 9), and blame stays with humans (pp. 10, 12).
- **Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021).** An institution's own officers must remain able to explain its behaviour "no matter how much technology is involved in the decision" (§4.3).
- **The premise.** The role of hosting can move to a machine. The answering cannot.
- **The stakes in hospitality.** Platforms have shifted "legal and tax liabilities onto homeowners" (Germann Molz 2018: 245).

**2.3 What makes this obligation hospitality's.**
- **Prior to harm.** The delegation literature indexes answerability to foreseeable harm and malfunction. The host's obligation is owed to an arrival from the threshold onward, before anything goes wrong.
- **Includes the exception.** The obligation includes the capacity to make a negotiated exception, which service management and algorithmic accountability do not treat as owed.
- **What this answers.** Why the obligation remains, where it originates, whether it transfers, and how it differs from organizational delegation and algorithmic accountability.

**2.4 Four terms.**
- **The terms.** Obligation is what the host owes; responsibility, who carries the job of meeting it. Authority is the power to change the decision or the rule; accountability, the duty to answer someone entitled to ask (Bovens 2007) (v4 §2).
- **The baseline.** In a face-to-face welcome all four sit in one person. The rest of the paper traces how algorithmic mediation pries them apart.

### 3. Guest standing (650 words)

- **Claim.** Standing is the correlate of the host's obligation: the guest's authority to address claims to the institution that owes the welcome.
- **The tradition.**
  - Every duty owed to someone has a correlative claim (Hohfeld 1913: 32–33).
  - Bipolar obligations give the obligee "a distinctive discretionary second-personal authority … to make claims and demands of obligors" (Darwall 2013: 31–32).
  - An apology "is, by definition, addressed to someone who receives it" (Darwall 2013: 31). A claim without an addressee is like an apology found in a diary.
- **Held and exercised.** The guest holds standing because the host is obliged. Exercising it requires an occasion and an addressee, which the institution provides or withholds.
- **The neighbouring concepts** (v4 §2).
  - Standing differs from consumer rights, procedural justice, contestability and customer participation.
  - It also differs from legal standing, which turns on proof of material harm (Metcalf et al. 2023: 1455).
  - It differs, too, from answerability to the people AI affects (Coeckelbergh 2019).
- **What a welcome is** (v4 §2). Recognition, admission, responsiveness, and the capacity for a negotiated exception, held together.
- **Negotiation as the practice of standing.**
  - Hotel staff negotiate with resisting guests, and a front-office employee who checks with management can secure an accommodation (Xiang et al. 2022).
  - Presence alone is not enough. In commercial homes the balance of power rests "firmly" with hosts, and guests do not complain (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–333).
  - Hospitality can establish power relations as well as reconfigure them (Lugosi 2014).
- **Hospitality runs both ways** (v4 §2). The field measures whether guests feel welcomed but not the guest's own standing. The validated scale dropped its items on choice and independence (Pijls et al. 2017: 130–131).

### 4. The authority–answerability split in the phygital arrangement (900 words)

- **Claim.** Phygital arrangements pull the power to decide away from the duty to answer, so the party who can change the rule is not a party the guest can reach.
- **The chain** (v4 §3).
  - Traditional hospitality is a straight line between host and guest.
  - Commercial hospitality inserts the company and its employee.
  - Phygital hospitality inserts an algorithm.
  - The problem is not the length of the chain but a break in it.
- **The relay** (v4 §4).
  - Physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings become physical facts: the key card that works or does not, the price on the screen at the desk.
  - The frameworks call this fluidity (Batat 2024). It is equally a relay, which raises the question the word fluidity hides: who holds authority, and who keeps the room for judgment, at each handoff?
- **The arrangement as the unit** (v4 §4). Guest, employee, property, vendor and rule-setter, not the algorithm as a lone gadget.
- **Knowledge, discretion and authority** (v4 §4) need not travel together.
  - **Knowledge** drifts to the system (Rahman 2021).
  - **Discretion** is contested, and staff work around categories set elsewhere (Pedersen and Pors 2023). Housekeeping systems can be configured to lock task order and withhold the day's list (Spektor et al. 2023: 628–629).
  - **Authority** drifts to whoever configures the rules (Gursoy 2026: 160).
- **Case and rule** (v4 §4). Discretion acts on the single case; authority acts on the rule. A guest can meet someone with the first power and never reach anyone with the second.
- **Naming the split.** The authority–answerability split is named and defined here and used under that name throughout.
- **The platform precedent** (v4 §3). Hosts strain to satisfy rating systems they cannot see (Cheng and Foley 2019). Guests are excluded on records they cannot inspect (Edelman et al. 2017).
- **The employee caught in the split** (v4 §4). Staff are still expected to make the guest feel in control while losing the controls to do so (Möhlmann et al. 2021).
- **The language of the split** (v4 §4). Saying that "the system decided" hides the people who designed and approved it.

### 5. Three conditions and a test (850 words)

- **Claim.** A touchpoint preserves hosting only if it keeps common understanding, predictability and accountability, and accountability cannot be bought back with the other two.

**Table 1. From the welcome to the conditions**

| Element of the welcome | Condition at the touchpoint | Failure when it is absent |
|---|---|---|
| Recognition | Common understanding | Guest and employee cannot see what the system is doing |
| Negotiation | Predictability | The terms shift under the guest's feet |
| Standing | Accountability | No one is obliged to answer |

- **Where the names come from.** The names are Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009: 483). In their work accountability means who is responsible for which task. Here it keeps the sense of section 2.4, the duty to answer someone entitled to ask.
- **A pattern, not a switch** (v4 §5).
  - Each condition can be present in degrees. The clearest case of replacement is all three gone at once.
  - A guest who understands a system perfectly has still been hosted by no one if no party must answer.
  - Batat (2024: 1234) makes the parallel claim for her framework: failures by human connectors "cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors".
- **The counterintuitive result** (v4 §5).
  - An opaque algorithm can still help hospitality, if its rules are steady and an empowered employee can override it.
  - A transparent one can still replace hospitality, if no one must answer for it.
  - Transparency alone "cannot create accountable systems" (Ananny and Crawford 2018: 984).

**Table 2. Six questions for any touchpoint** (v4 Table 1)

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| What rule or actor determined this outcome? | Whether the decision can be located at all |
| Could the guest have anticipated it, and are they told when it changes? | Predictability |
| Who can see how it was made? | Common understanding |
| Who can override it? | Where discretion sits |
| Who must answer if it is disputed? | Accountability |
| What does refusing the system cost the guest? | Whether a real alternative exists |

- **Two cases.**
  - **Pricing** (Garcia et al. 2026) fails on predictability and accountability.
  - **The animated chef** (Batat 2021) passes, with staff still hosting the meal.
- **Check-in** (v4 §5). A system that confirms identity does a clerical job. A system that scores a profile decides how the guest will be received (Xu et al. 2021).
- **Guest acceptance depends on the stakes** (Schmidt et al. 2025; Belanche et al. 2020).

### 6. What guests and staff can learn, and what institutions must provide (550 words)

- **Claim.** Guests and staff learn to read and even bend systems, but no skill creates an addressee.
- **Folk theories.** People build working theories of systems through use (Eslami et al. 2016; Ytre-Arne and Moe 2021). They learn which requests land and how to phrase them (v4 §6).
- **The employee as translator** (v4 §6).
  - Staff carry the guest's request into terms the system accepts, lending the guest their competence.
  - That knowledge depreciates when rules change silently (Hatherley 2025).
  - Designing the employee out of that spot removes the translator along with the task.
- **The ceiling.**
  - Users bent non-political curation but could not move the platform's power (Lin 2025: 8–9, 11).
  - Appeals did not lift fairness judgments (Vaccaro et al. 2020).
  - A validated scale of algorithmic competency measures a worker's ability to file an appeal, not whether anyone must consider it (Zhou et al. 2025).
- **The principle.** Competence is learned; the conditions for exercising standing are provided by institutions.
- **The same holds for employees** (v4 §6).
  - Staff grow more expert about systems while losing authority over outcomes.
  - Employees fall silent when speaking looks futile (Morrison 2014), and frontline staff aware of AI in their jobs grow more silent (Cheng et al. 2025).
  - Platform workers rarely get the chance to contest decisions (Duggan et al. 2026: 1448).

### 7. Designing for interruptible hospitality (950 words)

- **Claim.** Interface design can secure common understanding and predictability. Only constitutional safeguards secure accountability, and design must keep the moments in which a claim can be made.
- **Concealment or visibility** (v4 §7). One line of research advised hiding technology in luxury settings (Mosca and La Rosa 2019). The paper takes the opposite line.
- **Three interface principles.**
  - **Explain a decision where it lands.** Guests prefer little disclosure at check-in and fuller explanation later (Pigac et al. 2026: 286).
  - **Let guests adjust the settings that matter.** A path the system alone has authored is choice architecture, not choice (Yeung 2017).
  - **Make significant rule changes visible** (Hatherley 2025).
- **Two constitutional principles.**
  - **Reachable authority to reverse.**
    - Algorithmic governance should include "escalation pathways and human oversight" (Shabnam et al. 2026).
    - The deploying entity is best placed to reverse a decision (Lyons et al. 2021).
    - Of five governance mechanisms, only appeal raised perceived legitimacy (Martin and Waldman 2023).
    - After a service failure, guests lay responsibility on the firm (Belanche et al. 2020).
  - **A real alternative.**
    - Captivity is "coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable" (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 976).
    - A downgraded analogue route still counts (p. 978), and refusal "may carry a personal cost and loss" (p. 979).
    - Voice carries weight when exit stays possible (Hirschman 1970).
    - Effort shifted onto the customer is unpaid shadow work (Kim et al. 2025).
- **The split between the two sets is itself a claim** (v4 §7). It explains why "human-centred AI" fixes disappoint (Alfrink et al. 2023). The constitutional conditions decide who must answer, and in Bovens's terms the actor answers while the forum can "block or amend" (2007: 450, 452).
- **Seamlessness re-examined** (v4 §7).
  - Phygital frameworks set fluid and seamless journeys as the goal (Batat 2024: 1220, 1233; Batat and Hammedi 2023; Mosca et al. 2026: 427, 440–441).
  - Recent work criticizes the speeding-up of hospitality (Weaver 2025), and Batat cautions that a positive experience should not itself be the aim (2024: 1238).
  - A system built never to interrupt the guest can become one the guest cannot interrupt (Star and Ruhleder 1996).
- **Interruptibility** (v4 §7). A point where the guest can stop the process, ask, redirect or be treated as an exception, with a counterparty there to answer. A visible seam is no use unless somebody stands behind it (Chalmers and Galani 2004).
- **Guests who fall outside the system's script** (v4 §7).
  - Service is tuned to assumptions about what a guest of a given profile will want, and a person on the spot used to catch the mismatch (Nguyen et al. 2025).
  - Algorithmic personalization industrializes the scripting and removes the improviser (Costanza-Chock 2020).
- **Voice** (v4 §7).
  - Voice offered after a decision, with the decision unchanged, still raised fairness judgments (Lind et al. 1990). The danger is that hollow voice works.
  - Under some conditions voice can backfire (Lind et al. 1990: 953). The counterparty therefore has to be real.

**Table 3. Five stages, in rising order of cost** (v4 Table 2, with a new column)

| Stage | What the organization does | Condition it secures | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Map touchpoints and the decisions taken inside them | Common understanding | Attention |
| 2. Specify | Establish what guests and employees can understand, adjust and challenge | Common understanding, predictability | Analysis |
| 3. Design | Build the capabilities that support interpretation, adjustment and adaptation | Predictability | Design and training |
| 4. Constitute | Create a forum obliged to answer challenges, with authority to act | Accountability | Governance and staffing |
| 5. Preserve | Maintain a meaningful alternative for those who decline the automated route | Accountability, through exit | Ongoing operational commitment |

### 8. Discussion (550 words)

- **Three contributions**, each tied to the section that demonstrates it.
  - Where the obligation to host lies under algorithmic delegation, and how affirmative hospitality duties differ from harm-indexed accountability (§2).
  - Guest standing as the correlate of the host's obligation, exercisable only where an addressee is reachable (§§3, 6).
  - The three conditions, the six questions and the five-stage roadmap (§§5, 7).
- **AI as a constitutive mediator** (v4 §8). Technology changes the relationship itself, not just its speed.
- **Human-centricity** (v4 §8) is defined by what the arrangement protects or withholds — guest agency, employee discretion, relational judgment — not by keeping a human visible.
- **What the hospitality lens adds.** Service management asks whether the encounter delivers value. Algorithmic accountability asks who answers for harm. Hospitality asks what is owed to the person admitted. It catches an arrangement that works as designed and harms no one, yet leaves the guest with no one to answer.
- **Regulation.**
  - The EU AI Act gives affected persons a right to explanation from the deployer of a high-risk system (Art. 86).
  - Its list of high-risk uses names no hospitality application, and the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moves the high-risk obligations to December 2027.
  - The address of hospitality's obligation is, for now, a matter for institutions rather than law.
- **Well-being** (v4 §8).
  - The same mediation can enchant, disenchant or re-enchant (De Vos et al. 2024).
  - The withholdings that shrink one guest's standing may, in aggregate, shape what a community can expect from its hospitable institutions (Batat 2022; Mosca et al. 2026).
- **Not a case against the technology.** Phygital arrangements bring real gains in responsiveness and reach (v4 §8). The framework helps organizations keep the gains without giving up the relationship.

### 9. Limitations and future research (300 words)

- **The paper is conceptual.** Its examples illustrate the distinctions rather than test them.
- **Boundary conditions.**
  - Luxury against budget settings (Ivanov 2019 suggests different automation paths).
  - Hotels against platforms.
  - The EU against other jurisdictions.
- **Three studies.**
  - Guests' working theories of the systems that recognize and route them.
  - The three competences of section 6 measured against the validated scale (Zhou et al. 2025).
  - Whether a reachable addressee changes guests' fairness judgments in commercial hospitality.
- **Open question.** Whether employee silence and guest silence compound one another (Duggan et al. 2026).

### 10. Conclusion (250 words)

- **Back to the pricing case.** The obligation is still owed, and the institution that delegated still owes it. What failed is the address.
- **The question for phygital hospitality.** Not whether technology can perform the host's jobs, but where, in an arrangement that has scattered them across guest, employee, vendor and platform, the obligation of hosting still has an address (v4 §9).
- **Close:** "somebody still owes the guest a welcome" (v4 §9).
