## 2. The questions in HOST_DUTIES_SYNTHESIS

### King 1995: "care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety"; the guest's "only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably"
Unanswered. The article was not reached, so both phrases remain second-hand: xiang2022covid and golubovskaya2017meaning p. 1286. Neither of the two papers read in this pass cites King.

The nearest statements in a full text read here come from Lashley. The commercial welcome holds "only so long as they could pay the price" (Lashley 2015, p. 1, citing Ashness & Lashley 1995), and commercial hospitality "would be withdrawn if the payment could not be made" (p. 5, citing Ward & Martins and Ritzer, after which Lashley reports Telfer calling the view "somewhat simplistic"). Both sentences are cited critique of the conditionality of the commercial welcome, not a statement of what the guest owes, so they are consistent with a payment condition but do not support the payment half of King's sentence about the guest's obligation. On conduct, Lashley supplies a guest-conduct norm at second hand, not in the commercial setting: guests are "expected not to overstay their welcome, misbehave, or endanger the hosts (O'Gorman 2007a)" (p. 2), in pre-modern and Shakespearean settings.

### Lashley 2000: genuine welcome against "welcomed as a valued customer"; obligation and hospitableness
Unanswered from the chapter. Neither Lashley 2015 nor Blain and Lashley 2014 contains the valued-customer contrast, so the outline sentence still rests only on Slattery's quotation of "Lashley and Morrison 2000: 13".

Lashley 2015 reports his 2000 framework at second hand. He names three domains, "'cultural', and 'domestic', as well as 'commercial'", citing Lashley 2000 (p. 3). He holds that "the actual experiences of hospitality, in whatever setting, are likely to be the outcome of the influence of each of these domains (Lashley & Morrison 2000)" (p. 3). On obligation, the 2015 paper treats it as a social duty that has faded: "Offering guests hospitality has been, in all human settings, a moral obligation until the advent of mass travel and commercialism" (p. 3). In the cultural domain "the duty to provide hospitality, to act with generosity as a host, and to protect visitors is more than a matter left to the preferences of individuals" (p. 4). Hospitableness, by contrast, is "the willingness to be hospitable for its own sake, without any expectation of recompense or reciprocity" (p. 1).

### Telfer: virtue? duties of commercial hosts?
Not read. Two secondary reports of Telfer's list of motives now sit on the card, and they are not of equal standing. Only Blain and Lashley give a Telfer page.
- Blain and Lashley quote Telfer's list of motives as including "allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable, a duty help one's friends or a duty to help those in trouble" (2014, p. 2, giving Telfer p. 42).
- Lashley's version is an unquoted bulleted paraphrase with no Telfer page. It reads "A desire to entertain one's friends or to help those in trouble", has no duty clause, and adds two items (2015, p. 5).

The only quoted report (Blain & Lashley p. 2) includes "duties to be hospitable"; Lashley 2015's unquoted paraphrase (p. 5, no Telfer page) omits it. Check p. 42.

On both reports, Telfer makes hospitableness a matter of motive. Neither report says whether she calls it a virtue. She discusses commercial settings, but as hospitable people who "offer hospitableness beyond, and in spite of, the commercial transaction" (Lashley 2015, p. 5). Neither report shows her discussing the duties of a commercial host. The FABLE guess ("Telfer treats hospitableness as a virtue") stays unconfirmed.

Unanswered beyond the abstracts. The cards' abstract quotations stand: "deliberately graduated according to rank" (Hewagama et al., abstract), and "increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice … or more voice" (Hales and Klidas, abstract). Nothing new contradicts them, and nothing new supports them.

## 3. What the two full texts add

**Blain and Lashley state a host obligation and extend it, by metaphor, to service roles.** Their introduction extends host-guest relations "beyond bars, hotel and restaurants to include tour guides, air stewards, conductors and drivers on coaches, staff in museums and at attractions" (2014, p. 1). It then states: "Most significantly, the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome. Whilst there are some obligations on guests, the chief responsibilities which concern us here lie with hosts." (p. 1).

The paper then moves the whole matter into motive. Religious obligations represent an obligation which "may be at odds with Telfer's notion of hospitableness because the host is behaving in a way that has been externally imposed" (p. 2). Commercial firms are told to recruit people with the right disposition: "Commercial organisations could benefit from employing individuals who possess and demonstrate the qualities of hospitableness" (p. 2). The hospitable air steward makes passengers "welcome and safe, because they experience this desire as a genuine emotion" (p. 2).

**Lashley 2015 makes the same move.** He opens with the duty as a social obligation (pp. 3–4) and ends with an instrument to find individuals "driven by a personal desire to be hospitable to others" (p. 1). The firm appears only as the seller of hospitality "at a price" (p. 5).

For the paper, this is sharper than "hospitality studies has no duty". The field names a duty, extends it by metaphor to whoever occupies the host role, and then discharges it through hiring people with the right feelings. Neither paper gives any account of what stays with the holder when the work is delegated; that this leaves no one answerable when the welcome is delegated to a system is the paper's inference from that silence, not something the texts say. The word "unconditionally" (Blain and Lashley p. 1) cuts against the paper's legal evidence, though, and should not be borrowed.

## 4. Changes to HOST_DUTIES_SYNTHESIS

Each item quotes the synthesis sentence, then gives the verdict and the evidence. I have not edited the synthesis.

1. §2.1 heading, **"Hospitality studies: a virtue, not a role"**. *Qualifies.* Neither full text uses "virtue". Both speak of motive, desire and disposition (Lashley 2015 pp. 1, 5; Blain and Lashley 2014 pp. 1–2). Blain and Lashley also attach an obligation to the host *role* in services (p. 1). Suggested: "a motive, not a role-holder's duty".
2. §2.1, **"Hospitableness research measures the host's duty as a personal disposition."** *Qualifies.* The research measures hospitableness, and it says externally imposed obligation "may be at odds" with hospitableness (Blain and Lashley p. 2). It measures the disposition in place of the duty, not the duty as a disposition.
3. §2.1, **Lashley's (2015) instrument to "identify those individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable"**. *Supports.* Verified in the full text, p. 1 (abstract). The full sentence ends "to others".
4. §2.1, **"Lashley's 2014 editorial calls 'the duty of the host to welcome and protect guests' a central feature of human morality …"** *Unchanged.* The editorial was not part of this pass. Lashley 2015 says the same in full text: hospitality "has been, in all human settings, a moral obligation until the advent of mass travel and commercialism" (p. 3). That can replace the abstract-level editorial for the "lost importance" half. For "central feature of human morality", Lashley 2015 p. 1 is closer: "it is one of the defining features of human morality"; the abstract (p. 1) has "the universal need to welcome and protect the stranger".
5. §2.1 and §10, **"H1 found no hospitableness work that assigns the duty to an organisation or a role."** *Contradicted in part.* Blain and Lashley do not assign the obligation to service-role holders; they extend it by metaphor. Host-guest relations "can be seen to apply" to service roles, and "Using this metaphor", "the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome" (p. 1). On p. 2 the obligation is one "accepted by hosts". The paper then discharges the obligation through individual motive (p. 2). The "organisation" half of the gap survives: neither paper names an organisation as holder. Suggested gap: "the one hospitableness paper read that states a host obligation extends it, by metaphor, to service roles (Blain and Lashley 2014, p. 1) and then proposes recruiting hospitable individuals (p. 2)."
6. §2.1, **"It is also why hospitality studies alone cannot answer Reviewer 2: a virtue has no holder to delegate from."** *Supports in part.* Blain and Lashley do name a holder, "the host", and on p. 2 illustrate with the tour guide or air steward as an individual. The paper's remedy is hiring the right feelings (Blain and Lashley p. 2; Lashley 2015 p. 1). What neither paper offers is any account of delegation or answerability; "leaves no one answerable" is an inference from that silence. Reword the sentence to: "a disposition gives no account of what stays with the holder when the work is delegated".
7. §2.1, **"Debt: … Every relevant full text was closed to the session: Telfer, King 1995, Lashley 2000, Lashley 2015, and Blain and Lashley 2014."** *Superseded in part.* Lashley 2015 and Blain and Lashley 2014 are now read in full. Telfer, King and Lashley 2000 remain closed.
8. §1 item 1, **"Arrival alone does not switch it on."** *Consistent, not supporting.* The synthesis sentence is about the legal trigger of a duty. Lashley's pp. 1 and 5 concern the conditional continuation of the commercial welcome: commercial hospitality is offered "at a price, and would be withdrawn if the payment could not be made" (Lashley 2015 p. 5, citing Ward & Martins and Ritzer, then qualified via Telfer as "somewhat simplistic"), and the welcome holds "only so long as they could pay the price" (p. 1, citing Ashness & Lashley 1995). Both are cited critique. *Counter-voice to note:* Blain and Lashley insist the welcome "has to be unconditional" (p. 1), and their abstract says "Guests are unconditionally welcomed by hosts driven by altruistic motives" (p. 1).
9. §1 item 4 and §2.2, **"The anthropology of hospitality gives the guest duties and denies the guest the right to make demands."** *Consistent, weakly.* Blain and Lashley: "there are some obligations on guests, the chief responsibilities which concern us here lie with hosts" (p. 1). They do not specify the guest's obligations. Better evidence for guest duties is Lashley 2015 p. 2: guests are "expected not to overstay their welcome, misbehave, or endanger the hosts". Neither paper addresses the guest's right to make demands.
10. §4, **"Imposed status (H1)."** *Supports, at the social level only.* Lashley: the duty to host and protect "is more than a matter left to the preferences of individuals" (2015 p. 4). That is a claim about pre-industrial social norms, not law, and the relevance is thin: it describes a duty laid on everyone ("any failure to act appropriately is treated with social condemnation", p. 4), not a status attached to whoever takes up a calling, which is what §4's "imposed status" means.
