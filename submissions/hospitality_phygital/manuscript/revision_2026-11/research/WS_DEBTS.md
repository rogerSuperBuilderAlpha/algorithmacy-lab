## 2. The questions in HOST_DUTIES_SYNTHESIS

### King 1995: "care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety"; the guest's "only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably"
Unanswered. The article was not reached, so both phrases remain second-hand: xiang2022covid and golubovskaya2017meaning p. 1286. Neither of the two papers read in this pass cites King.

The nearest statement in a full text read here comes from Lashley: the commercial welcome holds "only so long as they could pay the price" (Lashley 2015, p. 1). Commercial hospitality "would be withdrawn if the payment could not be made" (p. 5). That supports the payment half of the King sentence as a description of commercial hospitality. It does not supply "behave reasonably".

### Lashley 2000: genuine welcome against "welcomed as a valued customer"; obligation and hospitableness
Unanswered from the chapter. Neither Lashley 2015 nor Blain and Lashley 2014 contains the valued-customer contrast, so the outline sentence still rests only on Slattery's quotation of "Lashley and Morrison 2000: 13".

Lashley 2015 reports his 2000 framework at second hand. He names three domains, "'cultural', and 'domestic', as well as 'commercial'", citing Lashley 2000 (p. 3). He holds that "the actual experiences of hospitality, in whatever setting, are likely to be the outcome of the influence of each of these domains (Lashley & Morrison 2000)" (p. 3). On obligation, the 2015 paper treats it as a social duty that has faded: "Offering guests hospitality has been, in all human settings, a moral obligation until the advent of mass travel and commercialism" (p. 3). In the cultural domain "the duty to provide hospitality, to act with generosity as a host, and to protect visitors is more than a matter left to the preferences of individuals" (p. 4). Hospitableness, by contrast, is "the willingness to be hospitable for its own sake, without any expectation of recompense or reciprocity" (p. 1).

### Telfer: virtue? duties of commercial hosts?
Not read. Two secondary reports with Telfer page numbers now sit on the card, and they disagree.
- Blain and Lashley quote Telfer's list of motives as including "allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable, a duty help one's friends or a duty to help those in trouble" (2014, p. 2, giving Telfer p. 42).
- Lashley's version of the same list reads "A desire to entertain one's friends or to help those in trouble" and has no duty clause (2015, p. 5).

On both reports, Telfer makes hospitableness a matter of motive. Neither report says whether she calls it a virtue. She discusses commercial settings, but as hospitable people who "offer hospitableness beyond, and in spite of, the commercial transaction" (Lashley 2015, p. 5). Neither report shows her discussing the duties of a commercial host. The FABLE guess ("Telfer treats hospitableness as a virtue") stays unconfirmed.

### Hewagama et al. and Hales and Klidas: "authority graded by rank", "responsibility without voice"
Unanswered beyond the abstracts. The cards' abstract quotations stand: "deliberately graduated according to rank" (Hewagama et al., abstract), and "increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice … or more voice" (Hales and Klidas, abstract). Nothing new contradicts them, and nothing new supports them.

## 3. What the two full texts add

**Blain and Lashley state a host obligation in service roles.** Their introduction extends host-guest relations "beyond bars, hotel and restaurants to include tour guides, air stewards, conductors and drivers on coaches, staff in museums and at attractions" (2014, p. 1). It then states: "Most significantly, the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome. Whilst there are some obligations on guests, the chief responsibilities which concern us here lie with hosts." (p. 1).

The paper then moves the whole matter into motive. Religious obligations are "at odds with Telfer's notion of hospitableness because the host is behaving in a way that has been externally imposed" (p. 2). Commercial firms are told to recruit people with the right disposition: "Commercial organisations could benefit from employing individuals who possess and demonstrate the qualities of hospitableness" (p. 2). The hospitable air steward makes passengers "welcome and safe, because they experience this desire as a genuine emotion" (p. 2).

**Lashley 2015 makes the same move.** He opens with the duty as a social obligation (pp. 3–4) and ends with an instrument to find individuals "driven by a personal desire to be hospitable to others" (p. 1). The firm appears only as the seller of hospitality "at a price" (p. 5).

For the paper, this is sharper than "hospitality studies has no duty". The field names a duty, puts it on whoever occupies the host role, and then discharges it through hiring people with the right feelings. That move leaves no one answerable when the welcome is delegated to a system, which is the paper's point. The word "unconditionally" (Blain and Lashley p. 1) cuts against the paper's legal evidence, though, and should not be borrowed.

## 4. Changes to HOST_DUTIES_SYNTHESIS

Each item quotes the synthesis sentence, then gives the verdict and the evidence. I have not edited the synthesis.

1. §2.1 heading, **"Hospitality studies: a virtue, not a role"**. *Qualifies.* Neither full text uses "virtue". Both speak of motive, desire and disposition (Lashley 2015 pp. 1, 5; Blain and Lashley 2014 pp. 1–2). Blain and Lashley also attach an obligation to the host *role* in services (p. 1). Suggested: "a motive, not a role-holder's duty".
2. §2.1, **"Hospitableness research measures the host's duty as a personal disposition."** *Qualifies.* The research measures hospitableness, and it sets externally imposed duty *against* hospitableness (Blain and Lashley p. 2). It measures the disposition in place of the duty, not the duty as a disposition.
3. §2.1, **Lashley's (2015) instrument to "identify those individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable"**. *Supports.* Verified in the full text, p. 1 (abstract). The full sentence ends "to others".
4. §2.1, **"Lashley's 2014 editorial calls 'the duty of the host to welcome and protect guests' a central feature of human morality …"** *Unchanged.* The editorial was not part of this pass. Lashley 2015 says the same in full text: hospitality "has been, in all human settings, a moral obligation until the advent of mass travel and commercialism" (p. 3). That can replace the abstract-level editorial.
5. §2.1 and §10, **"H1 found no hospitableness work that assigns the duty to an organisation or a role."** *Contradicted in part.* Blain and Lashley assign the obligation to the host role across services: "the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome" (p. 1). The holder is whoever fills the service role, by metaphor, and the paper then discharges the obligation through individual motive (p. 2). The "organisation" half of the gap survives. Suggested gap: "I found no hospitableness work that assigns the host's duty to an organisation; the one work that assigns it to a service role (Blain and Lashley 2014, p. 1) discharges it through recruiting hospitable individuals (p. 2)."
