## Synthesis: what guests owe, and what that does to the paper's "address" thesis

**The anthropology gives guests duties, and gives the host the right to end the relation when they are broken.** Pitt-Rivers states the rule in one sentence: "while a host has rights and obligations in regard to his guest, the guest has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host" (2012, p. 514). His list of a guest's infringements is specific (p. 515): insulting or rivalling the host; usurping the host's role, which includes "making himself at home", "giving orders to the dependants of his host" and making "claims or demands"; and refusing what is offered. The host's list mirrors it with protection and attention to the guest's "needs and wishes" (p. 516). Any breach ends the roles: it "entitles the person slighted ... to relinquish his role", and the parties become "enemies, not strangers" (p. 517). Overstaying is a further abuse (p. 516). Shryock agrees in a general gloss that leads into Pitt-Rivers, not in his Bedouin evidence: "The good host is graceful; the bad guest is an ingrate" (2012, p. S22); and bad guests and hosts alike "refuse to accept the proper role of host or guest" (p. S30). Lugosi carries the same pairing into hospitality studies: hosts owe the guest's wellbeing, "while guests have obligations to respect the rules of the host and to reciprocate" (2009, section "Hospitality").

**Commercial hospitality thins the guest's duties to payment and conduct.** The field's own statement, as Golubovskaya et al. report King (1995), is that commercial hospitality "often empowers the guest, whose only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably" (2017, p. 1286), after quoting Lashley that commerce absolves the guest "of mutual obligation, and loyalty" (p. 1286; both secondary). Hotel evidence shows the claim to be received ending when conduct fails. Guests who will not comply "after repeated communications" are asked to leave and become "outsiders" (Xiang et al. 2022, Findings, "Performance pathways" and "Group boundary"); a hotel employee describes discourteous guests "eventually removed from the establishment" (Golubovskaya et al. 2017, p. 1298); a guesthouse owner says "We've thrown people out before" (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007, p. 334). So v4's p. 6 sentence, "Neither asks what, if anything, a guest owes a commercial host" (`DRAFT.md` l. 37), is wrong as a claim about the field. King, as reported, answers it. R3.10 was right to call it questionable. The legal grounds for refusing or ejecting a guest belong to H1.

**The same tradition denies the guest the standing the paper gives the guest.** In Pitt-Rivers's law a guest who "makes claims or demands" usurps the host (p. 515), open complaint "would infringe the host's prerogative" (p. 514), and the dissatisfied guest "has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out" (p. 513). A claim "asserted as a right" is lost by being asserted (p. 509). The host ordains and the guest complies (p. 513). Hospitality in its classical form protects and honours the guest; it does not license the guest to press claims on the host. The outline's §3 definition of standing, "the guest's authority to address claims" (PO l. 77), therefore cannot come from the anthropology of hospitality. Something else has to supply it.

**Di Domenico and Lynch show what supplies it, and so qualify the "address" thesis.** In their commercial homes the host was present, reachable and fully empowered to set rules, admit and eject (pp. 332, 334–335). The guest still did not complain: "in a hotel one might complain about the beds being uncomfortable, but as the guest in the home one does not, it's not the done thing" (p. 333, researcher's note). Two points follow. First, the evidence is thin: one covert researcher's note, not a measured pattern among guests. Second, what silenced the complaint was not a missing addressee but the guest role. The authors name "the social niceties and obligations inherent in the interaction" (p. 333), which is Pitt-Rivers's rule in a Scottish B&B. The researcher's note, not the authors, sets the hotel against the home ("in a hotel one might", p. 333); reading this as a licence to complain that appears with the hotel is my gloss. It also appears with payment: hosts "must meet the desires and demands of the paying guest, the 'master/mistress' of the experience" (p. 335). My inference, not theirs: a reachable party with authority is **necessary** for a guest's claim to count, but **not sufficient**. The claim also needs a norm that makes pressing it proper rather than rude, and in hospitality that norm comes from the commercial side of the relation, not from the host–guest bond. The thesis survives if it is stated as a necessary condition and paired with this second one. It does not survive as "standing is lost when the address is lost", full stop, because Di Domenico and Lynch show standing absent with the address present.

**Pitt-Rivers also gives the negotiation its proper form.** Between silence and demand he records a third move: the diplomatic guest who turned his plate over registered a protest "without showing any discourtesy to his host" (pp. 513–514). That fits the weaker claim the Fable review recommended (§5, "Decide whether the negotiated exception is owed"). What a guest may expect is a hearing by someone with authority, not the exception itself.

**Requests and complaints do change outcomes in commercial settings, but through bounded authority.** In Xiang et al., a guest without a test report was received after the employee checked "with hotel management", and the hotel paid for the test; a guest refused at the desk took his complaint to "the general manager and the group head office" next day (Findings, "Performance pathways"). In Lugosi's bar, regulars' requests produced house cocktails that vanished when the requests stopped, and managers' "allowance only created opportunities" that patrons used (2009, section "Situational dimensions"). The authority behind these outcomes was never held whole at the point of contact. Hotel service recovery is "carefully stage-managed and 'staircased' ... with empowerment strongly related to hierarchical level" (Hewagama et al. 2019, abstract). In Amsterdam five-star hotels "empowerment" meant "increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice over how work was done or more voice in organizational decisions" (Hales and Klidas 1998, abstract). One employee (P-7) reports a manager telling hotel staff that guests "cannot demand everything ... we have own standard" (Golubovskaya et al. 2017, p. 1297). Outside hospitality, policy is the commonest reason an empowered employee refuses a special request (Beatty et al. 2016, p. 167), and the customer cannot see where the boundary lies (pp. 159, 169). Above the venue, brands, portfolios and intermediaries are managed at chain level (Slattery 2002, pp. 24–25); Slattery writes about corporate priorities, and reading them as where authority over the rule sits is my inference. The outline's baseline, "In a face-to-face welcome all four sit in one person" (PO l. 73), holds only for the owner-host of a commercial home, and even there no claim was pressed. R3.9 is right.

**Gap statement.** I found no study in hotels, restaurants or bars that follows a guest's request or complaint up an escalation path to the party with authority over the rule, and records whether it was heard and what changed. ## What this means for the paper's position on the host's obligation (FABLE_REVIEW §5)

- **Position A, threshold sovereignty, is weakened.** Its best anthropological source makes the sovereign host the one who "ordains" (Pitt-Rivers p. 513) and makes the guest's claim a usurpation (p. 515). Sovereignty grounds the host's duties to protect and attend (p. 516), but it produces host power and guest silence (Di Domenico and Lynch pp. 333, 335), not guest standing. A cannot carry §3.
- **Position B, public calling, is supported on the guest side.** The commercial relation is what licenses the guest's claim: payment and reasonable conduct are the guest's duties (Golubovskaya et al. p. 1286, after King), and, on my reading of a researcher's note in Di Domenico and Lynch (p. 333), the hotel is where complaint becomes proper. That is the reciprocal structure B predicts. The legal content (duty to receive, grounds for refusal) is H1's to verify.
- **Position C, institutional role obligation, is supported on authority.** Every hospitality source on frontline authority shows it assigned and graded by the organisation (Hewagama et al.; Hales and Klidas; Golubovskaya et al. p. 1297; Slattery pp. 24–25 on my inference, since Slattery describes corporate priorities rather than frontline authority). That is C's claim that employees hold job duties derived from the organisation's role, not the role itself. It also lets the paper restate the split (PO §4) without the false "all four in one person" baseline: the phygital change is that the escalation path to graded authority ("escalation" is my gloss on Hewagama et al.'s "staircased") may no longer exist, not that a once-complete frontline authority has been removed.
- **On R2.3's circle.** Pitt-Rivers separates the first-order duties (protect, attend, honour) from any right of the guest to demand them. That supports the Fable review's repair: define standing as the guest's position as the person to whom the first-order duties are owed, and treat the means of pressing a claim as a separate, commercially and organisationally supplied arrangement.
