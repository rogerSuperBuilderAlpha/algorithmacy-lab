## 1. Scoping decision

The construct — a guest's words count as a claim addressed to someone in the position of host, able to
receive it and obliged to respond — belongs to **two** traditions, not four.

1. **Second-personal authority, in its directed (bipolar) form.** The construct is a claim *addressed to
   a particular party* who is bound to that claimant. That is Darwall's bipolar obligation, in which
   the obligee holds an individual "standing" to address demands to the obligor and hold him
   answerable (darwall2013bipolar, pp. 27, 31–32). Darwall's 2006 book is mainly about moral obligation
   "period", owed as a representative person, so the 2013 chapter fits the construct better than the
   book does. It was also the one I could read in full.
2. **Rights correlativity.** "Standing is the guest's side of the same relation" (REVISION_PLAN §3) is
   Hohfeld's claim–duty correlativity (hohfeld1913fundamental, pp. 31–33). Darwall's chapter builds
   on it explicitly (p. 28), so one Darwall citation can carry both traditions if words are short.

Not needed as separate traditions:
- **Recognition (Honneth).** v4 uses "recognition" as one element of welcome (v4 §2 ¶3), not as the
  ground of standing. Recognition theory explains identity harms from misrecognition. The paper's
  standing is narrower: whether a claim is received. Distinguish by the authors' own sentence; no card.
- **Legal standing.** This is the neighbouring usage to distinguish, not a tradition to join.
  Metcalf et al. (2023) supply it. Darwall (2013, p. 31) draws the same civil-standing analogy.

## 6. Synthesis paragraph for §3

> [Drafted by Claude, rr-2026-11 — authors' wording to replace]
> Standing, as we use it, belongs to the second-personal tradition. More exactly, it is what Darwall
> calls bipolar obligation: a duty owed by an obligor to an obligee, who holds "an assumed individual
> authority or standing … with respect to the obligor that others do not have" (Darwall 2013: 27).
> That authority is exercised by address. The obligee may insist, complain, seek apology or
> compensation, and hold the obligor "personally responsible" (Darwall 2013: 31–32). Address, in turn,
> needs an addressee: an admission of guilt that reaches no one is not an apology (Darwall 2013: 31).
> Hohfeld supplies the other half of the relation, since a right in its "limited and proper meaning" is
> a claim whose "invariable correlative" is a duty borne by a determinate other (Hohfeld 1913: 31–33).
> The guest's claim against a commercial host therefore lasts as long as the institution's duty does.
> What algorithmic mediation can remove is the addressee who receives that claim as a claim — a
> condition we add to the tradition, not one it states — and that is also what separates our usage
> from the juridical standing that lets a public "stand before the court" (Metcalf et al. 2023: 1455).

Locators, claim by claim:
1. bipolar obligation / individual standing — darwall2013bipolar p. 27 (also p. 20 for the two poles)
2. exercised by address; insist, complain, apology, compensation, "personally responsible" — pp. 31–32
3. unaddressed apology is none — p. 31
4. claim / invariable correlative / determinate other — hohfeld1913fundamental pp. 31 ("invariable correlative"), 32 ("limited and proper meaning", "claim", X against Y), 33
5. claim lasts with the duty — inference from Hohfeld's correlativity (pp. 32–33), stated as the authors' inference; consistent with E.1
6. reachability is the authors' own addition — darwall2013bipolar card, Threat 2; legal standing — metcalf2023courts p. 1455

## 7. Gap statement

I found no work that applies second-personal (Darwall) standing or Hohfeldian claim-correlativity to
hotel or hospitality guests. I also found none that defines an algorithmic decision subject's standing
as conditional on a reachable counterparty. The search covered Consensus (queries 1–3 and 6–11 above),
OpenAlex title-and-abstract phrase filters (query 12) and Crossref, on 2026-11-08. Scholar Gateway
returned ACCESS_DENIED to both queries.

Adjacent work does exist, and the gap statement should not hide it. Three papers put the decision
subject on the addressee side of a responsibility relation. Coeckelbergh (2019, abstract) grounds
explainability in "responsibility as answerability" to the "addressees or patients" of AI decisions.
Grant, Behrends and Basl (2023, abstract) argue that decision-makers owe duties of consideration to
decision-subjects. Brand (2025, abstract) argues that an obligation-bearer toward right-holders needs a
reciprocal reasons-giving relationship. All three read at abstract depth only. Their full texts are OA
but were blocked to download this session, so I cannot say whether they cite Darwall. The defensible
form for the paper: "Work on algorithmic accountability has placed the decision subject as the
addressee of answerability (Coeckelbergh 2019); none we found treats the subject's standing as lasting
only while an obliged counterparty is reachable, or applies this to the guest."

## 9. For the verifier — look hardest at

1. **Threat to the construct:** "standing exists only while a counterparty is reachable" is not stated by Darwall or Hohfeld. On Hohfeld the claim survives as long as the duty does; Darwall's obligee keeps her authority. Only the addressed act needs an addressee. The paper must own this condition as its addition (darwall2013bipolar Threat 2; hohfeld card notes).
2. **"Conferred" versus Darwall's authority held by persons as such.** The bridge is p. 28 ("various keys": conventional, legal). Also Darwall's own term "second-personal competence" collides with the paper's "competence is learned".
3. Darwall chapter edition: 2012 OUP printing (10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199653492.003.0011) versus the 2013 collection read here (pp. 20–39).
4. metcalf2023courts bib author name (Emnet) and author order.
5. The Coeckelbergh, Grant and Brand full texts, before the gap statement enters the paper.
