## For the authors

Ranked by how much the argument leans on the point. v4 is quoted from `submitted.md` by line; plan moves from REVISION_PLAN.md as the cards record them. Items 1–3 are load-bearing for §2 and §7; 4–7 affect single sentences; 8–12 are wording.

1. **v4 line 71, §7: "The effect has a limit, and it runs the other way. An invitation to speak that was never going to bear on anything lowers those judgments instead of raising them, and the same research warns that a sense of fairness can be manufactured this way."** Contradicted by Lind et al. (1990). Their postdecision condition, where the goal "would not be changed regardless of what the subject said" (p. 954), *raised* both procedural and outcome fairness, and every pairwise contrast was significant (p. 955, note 3; means 2.43→3.15, 2.11→2.72, Table 1, p. 956). The "manufactured" warning is real (p. 958) but says hollow voice raises perceived fairness, "all the more likely" where a decision maker "actively hides the ineffectiveness of input" (p. 958). A lowering effect does exist in the literature, under conditions: Lind et al. cite Folger's frustration effect, which appears when "repeated unfavorable outcomes or communications from others focus attention on possible biases" (p. 953), and Vaccaro et al. quote Lind and Tyler (1988): voice effects "can be attenuated or reversed when the procedure is weak or when there is social criticism of the procedure" (Vaccaro p. 167:16). Vaccaro's own denied appeals scored numerically below no appeal on all four measures, not significantly (Table 2, p. 167:11). A conditional sentence can stand on Folger (1977; Folger et al. 1979) or Lind and Tyler (1988); "the same research" cannot carry it. The preceding sentence (line 71, post-decision voice raised both judgments) is fully SUPPORTED by Lind alone.

2. **v4 line 73, §7: "constituting a forum that is required to answer challenges and making sure someone in that forum has real authority to act, and preserving a meaningful alternative for those who decline the automated route (Bovens 2007)."** Two of three clauses fail. In Bovens "the actor has an obligation to explain and to justify his or her conduct, the forum can pose questions and pass judgement" (p. 450), so "a forum that is required to answer" inverts the roles. "Real authority to act" is consistent with Bovens: the forum may hold "veto powers" and "can block or amend decisions made by the actor" (p. 452). v4 thus merges the actor's duty and the forum's power into one body; naming the two separately keeps both. The alternative route is nowhere in Bovens; the nearest words are footnote 50's parents who "exercise exit or voice" over school reports (p. 457). Wilson-Nash et al. carry it: "co‑existence (analogue retained but downgraded and more difficult to access)" is itself a captivity trigger (p. 978); avoidance "may enhance vulnerability by restricting access to essential services" (p. 979); the mitigator is "allowing consumers to choose modes of access (e.g., digital, analogue)" (p. 981).

3. **Plan §2 *Transfer*: ownership "moves only with the role"; "Kropf et al.'s challenge from care"; the trustee/successor comparison.** Neither source supports the role premise as a ground. Kong: "the deployer's standing to answer for what an agent does is grounded in her guidance control over the act of delegation" (p. 4); "A role matters derivatively rather than intrinsically" (p. 4). But Kong does give roles a job: they specify "the domain over which control and knowledge are exercised, and thereby of the outcomes that are hers to explain", and are "how [control-based responsibility] is distributed across a division of labour" (p. 4). A weaker premise, that roles mark where answerability lands, has support there. Kong reaches the plan's conclusion ("One who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it", p. 10) and sets fiduciary duty aside as "a different matter again" (p. 4), so he does not license the trustee comparison. Kropf et al. are not a challenge to the conclusion: "the answerability of care robots is currently inconceivable" (p. 9); thick blame "lies with the moral – and therefore also human – actors" (p. 10); holding robots to account risks "allowing humans to evade moral responsibility" (p. 12). They challenge only the premise that a role cannot pass to a machine. If §2 cites both, note that Kong holds the machine "is not a responsibility-bearer in any sense" (p. 10) while Kropf et al. grant it role responsibility and thin blame (p. 7).

4. **v4 line 30, §2: "Kropf et al. (2026) argue that a robot can hold the role responsibility of a carer without being a moral agent".** PARTLY. The role is scoped and explicitly not a carer's: "A care robot in a nursing home does not have the same role obligations as a nurse" (p. 4); "not to confuse the role of Pepper in a care facility with that of a human nurse" (p. 6); expecting it to "replace a human caregiver, does not currently correspond to R" (p. 6). "Of a care robot" or "within care" is accurate. The rest of the sentence (narrows the gap, keeps human accountability) is SUPPORTED (pp. 9, 10, 12). Line 30's "The nearest challenge comes from care" inherits item 3.

5. **Plan §7: Wilson-Nash et al. "grounds the second" constitutional condition (a real alternative).** PARTLY. The paper shows why the alternative matters (pp. 974, 978, 979, 982) and names access-mode choice as a mitigator (p. 981), but frames the stake as stress and vulnerability: DTC "produces stress through felt entrapment" (p. 975). It does not frame it as accountability or standing. "Shows what is lost without the second" is what it supports. The card recommends admitting it while its status is `rejected`; that decision is yours, and it needs a references.bib entry if taken. Cite as 2026, 43(5), 974–985.

6. **Plan §2 *Distinctness*, if Golubovskaya et al. is used for hospitality as "an obligation owed to this arrival".** NOT SUPPORTED. They conceptualise hospitality "as an essence, or spirit, and service as a process" (p. 1283), "an attitude and spirit with which service is performed" (p. 1287), and list obligation among definitions they do not adopt (p. 1284). Usable only for displacement: "only the minority (6 of 22 participants) were able to define and describe hospitality" (p. 1296); service terminology "overrides hospitality within the commercial context" (pp. 1299–1300).

7. **Plan §3 and WS_C: Di Domenico and Lynch show host and guest building "negotiated normative practices".** PARTLY. The phrase is in the abstract only (p. 321). The findings show host rule-setting and guest deference: "The balance of power in the host/guest relationship is perceived by hosts to rest firmly with them" (p. 332); "as the guest in the home one does not [complain], it's not the done thing" (p. 333); decisions "lie much more with host than guest" (p. 335). Use it as the copresent-host baseline, with the warning that copresence alone did not let guests press claims.

8. **v4 line 34, §4: "the arrangement separates the power to make or shape decisions from the duty to answer for them (Bovens 2007)."** PARTLY. Bovens's many-hands problem is about finding who "can be brought to account" (p. 457), not a separation of power from duty. His corporate accountability of "the organisation as actor" (p. 458) would support the *Holder* move.

9. **v4 Hatherley uses (lines 39, 59, 65).** All PARTLY. Nothing on forecasting the guest (line 39). The harm is unintelligible change, not unannounced change: informing users of updates "may not reduce the epistemic or safety risks of update opacity" (p. 11), so line 65's "make significant rule changes visible" cites a source that doubts the remedy. The depreciation of learned knowledge is supported: "model updates have been found to disrupt users' mental models" (p. 5).

10. **v4 lines 41, 60, 86: the Vaccaro et al. uses.** All PARTLY. The study varied neither skill nor transparency: every condition carried the same explanation (p. 167:7) and opacity was the theme of 113 responses (p. 167:13). The authors read their null as design: "most of the lack of effect should be attributed to poor appeal design" (p. 167:15), and current appeals favour "users who understand the systems better" (p. 167:17). The no-lift result stands (Table 2, p. 167:11).

11. **v4 line 68, §7: "But in these frameworks smoothness itself has stayed a goal, never a suspect."** PARTLY for Shabnam et al., who fault the field's "dominant emphasis on ... seamless channel integration and engagement optimisation" (PDF p. 2) yet treat friction as a cost ("reducing friction and restoring agency", PDF p. 10). Their recommendation that "algorithmic governance must remain contestable ... escalation pathways and human oversight" (PDF p. 14) is a prior statement of the reachable-person principle and should be cited there.
