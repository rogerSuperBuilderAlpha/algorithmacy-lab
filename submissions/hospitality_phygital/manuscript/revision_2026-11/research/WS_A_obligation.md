## 4. Synthesis: the four moves of the new §2

*Drafted by Claude for the authors to accept, rewrite or reject. Every claim carries its locator; the "not supported" notes mark what is the authors' own argument.*

**Origin.** Hospitality theorists tie the host's standing to receiving and protecting whoever comes. Shryock (2012) calls hospitality "a test of sovereignty" in which the generous host is able to "protect guests from harm" [shryock2012breaking, p. S20], and guests "depend on their hosts for protection and respect" [p. S23]. He also quotes Derrida's absolute hospitality, which requires "that I let them arrive" [p. S22, quoting Derrida 2000]. O'Gorman (2009) finds the same structure in commercial hospitality's history: "Crossing thresholds of commercial hospitality also guaranteed and provided physical protection, sanctuary and security" [ogorman2009origins, pdf p. 11].
- *Not supported:* none of the sources read says that arrival **triggers** an obligation, so the trigger formulation is the authors'. The analogy "need triggers care, trust triggers fiduciary duty, risk triggers governance duty", which REVISION_PLAN.md attributes to Santoni de Sio & Mecacci (2021), is **not in that paper**. I searched the full text for "fiduciary", "governance" and "trigger" and found no trigger typology. The analogy needs its own sources or must stand as the authors' argument. Derrida's arrival belongs to *unconditional* hospitality [Shryock p. S22], so it does not carry over directly to a commercial host.

**Holder.** The obligation sits with whoever holds sovereignty over the space: "sovereignty is manifest in the ability to act as host" [shryock2012breaking, p. S25]. Hospitality fails when host and guest "cannot agree on who controls the space of interaction, who is sovereign, who belongs, and who owes or should offer respect" [p. S30]. In modern conditions the answer to "Who does so?" is "the hospitality industry (the market in hotels, restaurants, and other sites of temporary accommodation)" [p. S30]. The responsibility-gap literature supplies the institutional version. If an organisation "has to remain accountable", its own officers, "not IT experts and private companies", must keep the capacity to explain "the behaviour of their institution … no matter how much technology is involved in the decision" [santonidesio2021gaps, §4.3 ¶1].
- *Not supported:* that the **property or brand rather than the employee** holds the obligation, and that an employment contract cannot by itself explain it. Shryock's host is a household or polity, and Santoni & Mecacci's institution is a public organisation answering to citizens. Moving either to a commercial property is the authors' step.

**Transfer.** Handing a function to a system does not hand over the duty to answer for it. Blaming the computer is a barrier to accountability "because, having found one explanation for an error or injury, the further role and responsibility of human agents tend to be underestimated" [nissenbaum1996accountability, pdf p. 10]. Answerability "cannot be spread, thinned and offset" [pdf p. 15]. Kong (2026) defends "a principle of responsibility conservation under delegation" grounded in the deployer's control over the act of delegating [kong2026delegation, abstract]. Santoni & Mecacci warn that legal personhood for artificial agents shifts attention away from responsibility [§3.2 ¶9]. Kropf et al. (2026) stand on the other side: care robots "can still hold role responsibility based on their social function" [kropf2026blame, abstract], although "without displacing human accountability" [abstract]. That last clause may make them less of an opponent than §2 assumes; read the full text first.
- *Not supported:* (a) **"ownership moves only with the role"** has no read source. Hart's role-responsibility is the obvious one, but it is unread [lead outcome]. (b) The **trustee/successor comparison** has no source. (c) **Nissenbaum says something weaker** than "ownership stays with the institution by default". She says that unless "alternate lines of accountability are worked out, accountability for these important functions will be lost", and she names the deploying organization as one of three candidates, alongside designers and the human experts [pdf p. 11]. On her account the default is loss. The authors must either make the normative claim (it *ought* to stay) and cite Nissenbaum for the risk, or find a source for the stronger claim.

**Distinctness.** Algorithmic accountability asks who answers for a system's harms. Nissenbaum's accountability is "closely akin to 'answerability'" [pdf p. 3] and is worked out on malfunctions, bugs and injuries [pdf pp. 6–15]. Santoni & Mecacci's one forward-looking form, active responsibility, concerns "the goals, values, and (legal) norms that professionals such as engineers are supposed to promote" [§2.4 ¶1], which is a duty to society, not to a particular arrival. Kong's conservation principle is likewise indexed to "harmful dispositions" [abstract]. In organization theory, accountability means "who is responsible for specific elements of the task" among interdependent workers [okhuysenbechky2009, p. 483]. None of these contains an obligation owed to *this* arrival before anything goes wrong.
- *Not supported:* the positive content, **"an obligation owed to this arrival that includes the capacity for a negotiated exception"**, is in none of the sources I read. It has to come from the held hospitality cards (Lashley 2000; Lynch et al. 2011; Derrida 2000; Lugosi) or stand as the paper's contribution. Alfrink et al. come closest: contestation "cannot be handled in a fully automated fashion, because a dialectic exchange is not possible in a meaningful sense between humans and machines" [alfrink2023contestable, §6.1.4].

Notes for the authors:
- **Non-compensability is your inference.** O&B report one substitution, between predictability and common understanding ("common understandings can replace predictability and vice versa", p. 490). Accountability only "supports" the other two (p. 490). The draft's "Accountability cannot be bought back with the other two" fits this pattern but is not stated by O&B. Their "necessary, but are not always sufficient" (p. 493) is a citable hook for why a touchpoint can pass all three conditions and still fail as a welcome.
- **The roadmap order is yours.** Alfrink's stages are the ICO lifecycle phases a *developer* goes through (§6.3), whereas the roadmap is an *operator's* sequence of rising cost. The sources ground what each stage contains, not the order the stages come in.
- Two of the draft Table 1's six questions do not map to an O&B condition. "Who can override it?" sits under accountability via Alfrink §6.1.4, and "What does refusing cost?" sits under stage 5, which has no O&B condition. Say so in the derivation preamble or fold those two questions into accountability.
