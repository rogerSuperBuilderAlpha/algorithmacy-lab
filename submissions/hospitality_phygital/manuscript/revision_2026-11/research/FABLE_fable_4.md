## For the authors

Ranked by how much the argument leans on the sentence. Each item quotes v4 as submitted, says what the source says, and gives the locator.

1. **§4, line 33 (pricing desks; the hotel evidence for discretion).** v4: "the managers who run such desks describe a running negotiation between what the system directs and what their role still demands (Jianu et al. 2025)." Not supported. Jianu et al. is "a Delphi methodology to gauge consensus about the future implications of AM" (p. 4) with 22 recruits, nine of them academics (Table I, p. 4). Pricing appears only where the authors cite others (p. 3; p. 9). No manager describes a desk. Flag `claim-contradicted` stands. Write "a hotel Delphi panel expects line managers to ..." or drop the sentence.

2. **§6, line 60 (the hinge: skill cannot conjure a counterparty).** v4: "Users who understand a platform's curation well still cannot bend it, and their resistance stays inside what the platform allows (Lin 2025)." Half right. Seventeen of 31 participants did bend non-political curation by pressing dislike or reporting (pp. 8–9); what they could not move was political curation and the platform's power structure. The second clause is supported (p. 1; p. 11). Also: do not reach for Eslami et al. (2016) to shore this up. Its p. 2380 agency finding is about *felt* agency and its remedy is "changing the design to give users a greater feeling of agency ... using seamful design", the very fix v4 line 67 calls a misdiagnosis.

3. **§7, line 68 (seamlessness as the one unquestioned design value).** Both quotations are verbatim and correctly paged (Batat and Hammedi 2023: 10; Batat 2024: 1220). Three glosses lean further than the sources: (a) "describes its purpose as": the p. 10 sentence is under the abstract's "Findings –" heading; "Purpose –" on the same page says the article "aims to provide researchers with a new comprehensive and integrative extended reality technology (ERT) framework". The body's goal sentence is p. 11 and reads "guarantee a continuum in customer value" (no "in terms of"). (b) "aims at 'fluidifying ...'": the abstract makes fluidifying the means ("This is done by", p. 1220); the goal wording is p. 1235. (c) "contains no discussion of friction at all" (Mameli et al. 2026): the word never appears, but the review discusses "the potential risks and challenges" (p. 13), "drawbacks such as technical glitches and privacy concerns" (p. 14), Table 7's "Risks, and challenges" (p. 22) and "negative aspects of engagement" (p. 26). Safer: "never names friction and treats risks only as a research gap". A reviewer close to the editor may also cite Batat (2024: 1238): the framework "should not consider a positive phygital experience to be the aim".

4. **§4, line 31 (discretion splits).** v4: "staff keep some freedom to act, but more and more of their day goes to reading decisions made elsewhere and working around categories set elsewhere (Pedersen and Pors 2023; Spektor et al. 2025)." Pedersen and Pors support the workarounds (pp. 80, 89); they measure no trend in staff time. The same sentence's "translating those decisions for the people they affect (Jianu et al. 2025)": the forecast translation runs to "frontline staff" (pp. 7, 10), not to guests, and the Delphi caveat in item 1 applies. Line 59 ("hotel managers describe carrying the system's decisions to the people they affect") has the same two limits.

5. **Revision plan §2, Transfer.** The plan's move is that ownership of the obligation "moves only with the role" and, when handed to a system, "stays with the institution by default". Germann Molz (2018) shows the opposite happening: Airbnb "exempts itself from more binding regulation" (p. 244) and investors profit "while transferring legal and tax liabilities onto homeowners and regulatory obligations onto municipalities" (p. 245). Cite it as the hospitality precedent for the stakes, not as support for the conclusion.

6. **§7, line 72 (what to keep).** v4: "keeping the moments where recognition, negotiation, and exception stay possible (Padigar et al. 2025; Phillips et al. 2024)." Padigar et al. never discuss negotiation, recognition or exceptions. Their kept frictions are payment, authentication and privacy (pp. 28, 32); contacting customer service "when there is a problem" is a have-to task whose friction "can be 'frustrating friction'" and should be reduced (p. 32). The first half of the sentence ("remove the pointless kind") is theirs (p. 21; p. 28). Argue the extension, perhaps through their own subjectivity caveat (p. 29).

7. **§7, line 65 (explain where it lands).** Pigac et al. (2026) support explanation at the touchpoint via staff for low-comfort guests (Table 4, p. 291), but on timing they point the other way: "minimal disclosure during check-in; richer, retrospective transparency in loyalty apps" (p. 286). Their cases are personalization, not adverse decisions. The paper has no contest, appeal or dispute vocabulary at all; its override is the concierge's, offered to reassure (p. 291). Theme 4 is good evidence for the fourth principle as guest preference.

8. **§7, line 67 (constitutional conditions).** Hirsbrunner et al. (2025) say contestants "must be competent to contest" (p. 3), and their designer/community contrast is argued from orders of justification, not observed (p. 11). The plan cuts the citation; the full text gives no reason to keep it.

9. **§6 (employee silence).** v4: "an inability to contest decisions at all (Duggan et al. 2026)". The source says workers "rarely" have the opportunity to contest reviews (p. 1448); the one "unable to contest" wording is a table entry about ratings (p. 1444). Drop "at all".

10. **§5, line 41 (transparent algorithm can still replace hospitality).** Ananny and Crawford (2018) carry "transparency alone cannot create accountable systems" (p. 984) and transparency "disconnected from power" (p. 978). They do not supply the "obliged to answer" half; that is Bovens. If a quotation from this source is wanted near "obliged to answer", Fox's "soft" accountability, "organizations must answer for their actions", is on p. 976.

11. **§4, line 36 (craft).** v4: "Staff are still asked to produce the guest's sense of control, while no longer holding the controls that would produce it (Fuller and Smith 1991; Möhlmann et al. 2021)." Möhlmann et al. support the second half for Uber drivers who must satisfy and be rated by passengers with "little flexibility for the driver to adapt to special client requests" (p. 2009; p. 2010). Nothing on a guest's "sense of control". The card still calls this source "cut"; v4 cites it.

12. **§8 well-being sentence.** "service ecosystems ... across economic, cognitive, emotional, and social lines (Anderson and Ostrom 2015; Batat 2022; Mosca et al. 2026b)". Batat's four levels are verbatim (p. 609; p. 618; p. 612 has "cultural") but her object is the luxury ecosystem (p. 609). Mosca et al.'s dimensions are "individual, social, and societal" (p. 439), in museums. The four lines are Batat's alone.

13. **§5, line 54 (restaurant case).** v4: "the technology adds delight to an encounter whose rules stay steady and whose staff keep their discretion (Batat 2021)." Delight (p. 10) and staff presence (pp. 7–9) are in the source. Rules, discretion and whether a diner can decline the projection are not. Mark the second half as the authors' reading.

14. **§7, line 66 (Belanche).** Supported, with two small words to fix if a reviewer is strict: respondents were "331 US participants" in hotel vignettes (p. 273), not guests, and the firm effect is a main effect (H4b not supported, p. 275) that vanishes for analytical robots (p. 282).

15. **Glosses.** §3 line 24 "the platform's idea of hospitality shaping what hosts feel they must offer (... Germann Molz 2018)": Germann Molz is a discourse analysis of marketing and secondary literature (p. 235); "what hosts feel" leans on Roelofsen and Minca. §6 line 58, Eslami et al. support theories formed through use (p. 2373), but the paper's main finding is that a seamful probe produced them fast (p. 2378).
