## The two syntheses

Both files now end with a "Fable corrections" section that lists each change. The substantive ones:

- **WS_H1.** (i) "The old law owed reasonable care" was a flat statement resting on a footnote aside (Bogen p. 82 n. 160) and an undated restatement of Beale (Maese p. 499); hedged in three places. (ii) "A delegation rule built in" limited to the custody of goods, which is all Calye fol. 33a and Sandoval-Strausz p. 65 concern. (iii) HPA s 1(3) marked as a definition. (iv) Title II (c)(1). (v) "Close to a negotiated exception" narrowed by Maese p. 499. (vi) Shryock's "beyond the realm of house, host, and guest" added.
- **WS_H2.** (i) "Threshold sovereignty (A) is weakened" outran the card: Woodland affirms control of the environment and occupation of land as the ground of its first category (paras 12, 24) and is silent on hosts. (ii) Three more places cited Woodland for statements about hosts; each is now marked as the paper's application. (iii) Table row 5 cited para 25(1) for authority over a decision; the paragraph concerns authority over the pupil. (iv) "The hotel's terms can narrow it" marked as inference, with para 25(5).
- **A conflict between the two files, left for the authors.** WS_H1 grounds Position C in a status *imposed* by law: "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty" (Bogen p. 75), and the undertaking account is "not so much a theory as a tautology" (p. 52). WS_H2 grounds the same position in an *assumption* of responsibility (Woodland para 11, where the assumption is "imputed"). The paper has to choose its word. "Imposed on a status" has the innkeeper sources behind it; "undertaking" has only the school and hospital cases.

## For the authors

Ranked by how much the argument leans on the sentence. Each entry quotes the sentence, then says what the new sources do to it.

### 1. The trigger: arrival alone (contradicted)

> Pitch l. 21: "Hospitality asks what is owed to a person simply because they walked in the door, treating the capacity to negotiate an exception as an inherent part of that obligation."
> v4 l. 59: "A welcome is triggered by one thing only: someone has arrived. … The trigger is still arrival itself, not need, trust, or risk."

Every legal source read makes the trigger two-sided and conditional. The host must have held the house out, and the arrival must be a "traveller presenting himself who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum … and who is in a fit state to be received" (HPA 1956 s 1(3)). A neighbour "who is no traveller" has no action (Calye, fol. 32b). A full house excuses refusal (Holt CJ dissenting, 12 Mod 485). The rule "was not absolute" (Sandoval-Strausz p. 63). US doctrine adds admission as a second step (Maese p. 501). v4 concedes that "every real welcome sets conditions", but in law the conditions are part of the trigger. The second half of the pitch sentence has no support: the exceptions the law knows run in the host's favour (Calye fol. 33a; Sandoval-Strausz p. 63), and nothing read makes an exception something owed to the guest.

### 2. The holder: "because they control the physical premises" and "the industry" (contradicted in part)

> Pitch l. 9: "Hospitality theory explores how commercial hosts owe guests care, sanctuary, and welcome because they control the physical premises (Shryock 2012: S25, S30; …)"
> PO §2.1: "The modern holder is the industry, the market in hotels and restaurants (S30)."

Shryock does not say this. S25 describes Jordanian tribes and the monarchy. S30 hands the care of strangers to the industry "and the government bodies that control immigration and naturalization", and the sentence before it places that space "beyond domestic space, which places it beyond the realm of house, host, and guest". The next sentence suggests, hedged, that relations there are "depersonalized, formulaic, monetized" (S30–S31). The law does not ground the duty in control of premises either. It puts the duty on "the proprietor … and shall not attach to any other person" (HPA s 1(1)), on a status (Bogen pp. 52, 90). Sandoval-Strausz grounds it in the traveller's vulnerability (pp. 62–63), Bogen in crime control (p. 53). For duties that arise from a relationship, Woodland says the defendant is liable "not because he has control but in spite of the fact that he may have none" (para 24).

### 3. Standing as correlate, and the circle (qualified; one sentence contradicted)

> Pitch l. 11: "Every institutional duty generates a correlative claim, giving the guest the standing to direct demands back to the host (Hohfeld 1913: 33; Darwall 2013: 28, 31–32)."
> PO §3: "Standing is the correlate of the host's obligation: the guest's authority to address claims to the institution that owes the welcome."
> PO §3: "Every duty owed to someone has a correlative claim (Hohfeld 1913: 32–33)."

The third sentence is right and the first is too broad. Only directed duties have a correlative claimant; "X has a duty to do A" is monadic and names no one (Thompson p. 338; Darwall p. 21). The innkeeper's own duties are the awkward case: "these were not 'rights' in any proper sense of the term" (Sandoval-Strausz p. 66), "travelers enjoyed privileges and innkeepers bore obligations, but nobody possessed automatic entitlements" (p. 68), and the duties "had to be turned into rights" (p. 54). The traveller could still sue for damages (p. 64), so the point qualifies the claim and does not defeat it. The second sentence joins two definitions. "The correlate of the host's obligation" is Thompson's relation (p. 335). "Authority to address claims" is Darwall's, and Darwall defines it through holding "the obligor personally accountable" (OSO abstract; p. 36). With PO §2.4's "accountability, the duty to answer someone entitled to ask", the second clause rebuilds R2.3's loop. Thompson p. 339 gives a located reason to keep the first clause alone: on Rawls's account "All alike are positioned to charge the promisor", and that is why it misses the one "to whom the promisor had a duty".

### 4. v4's standing exists only with a counterparty (contradicted; the outline repairs it)

> v4 l. 29: "Standing, then, is not something the guest owns by themselves. It exists only when there is a counterparty who can take their claim as a claim."
> PO §3: "The guest holds standing because the host is obliged. Exercising it requires an occasion and an addressee, which the institution provides or withholds."

Darwall: "an obligee or right holder need not actually claim her right or demand compliance with it in order for the right to exist" (p. 35). Liau separates "who has the right?" from "who can enforce a right?" (eprint p. 1). The outline's sentence is supported by both; v4's is not. Two cautions. Darwall p. 35 continues "Even so, a connection to the discretionary individual authorities … is surely part of the concepts", so it supports held-versus-exercised and nothing more. Liau's "standing" is the enforcement power (eprint p. 5), the outline's *exercised* side.

> PO §3: "It also differs from legal standing, which turns on proof of material harm (Metcalf et al. 2023: 1455)."

Too broad on Liau's Introduction. Private-law standing is "a power to hold another accountable before a court" held by "the primary right-holder" (eprint pp. 1, 5); it is not defined by proof of harm. Limit the sentence to the public-law standing Metcalf et al. discuss.

### 5. What moves and what stays (supported in structure; one word wrong)

> Pitch l. 11: "Delegating frontline tasks to software transfers the execution of hosting, but the obligation to answer remains with the organization"
> PO §2.2: "The role of hosting can move to a machine. The answering cannot."
> v4 l. 59: "The ownership of a welcome, as distinct from its performance, cannot be handed off without handing off the role itself."

The structure has a located legal form: "The work required to perform such a duty may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's" (Woodland para 7); "No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be" (Morgan AM p. 7). Three limits. First, Woodland's category is patients, children, prisoners and care-home residents (para 23(1)); the judgment never mentions inns or hotels, so the paper borrows a structure. Para 25(5) gives a closer bridge than the tort category: a paid provider's responsibilities "are already non-delegable because they are contractual". Second, the outline says the *role* moves, v4 says it cannot without the ownership moving. The law agrees with v4: the status stays with the proprietor (HPA s 1(1); Bogen p. 52) and the work moves. Third, a firm whose "own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance" is not liable (Woodland para 25(3)), so "the organization" needs naming where a brand, an operator and a platform share the job.

### 6. What makes the obligation hospitality's (contradicted)

> PO §2.3: "Prior to harm. … The host's obligation is owed to an arrival from the threshold onward, before anything goes wrong."
> PO §2.3: "Includes the exception. The obligation includes the capacity to make a negotiated exception"
> v4 l. 59: "What is peculiar to the threshold is where the role goes."
> Pitch l. 15: "distinguishing affirmative hospitality duties from harm-indexed accountability"

A "positive or affirmative duty to protect" that arises "because of an antecedent relationship" and not from the negligent act is exactly what Woodland attributes to schools and hospitals (para 7; para 23). Pre-harm, affirmative duty is not the threshold's own. WS_H1 §4 Q4 reaches the same result from the statutes. On the exception, see entry 1. What the sources do leave to hospitality is narrower: a duty to receive that survived for innkeepers and carriers (WS_H1, from Burdick), owed to a stranger "away from their home communities" (Sandoval-Strausz pp. 62–63), and fixed "by law rather than by contract" on admission (Maese p. 501).

### 7. The content of the obligation (qualified)

> PO §2.1: "The commercial host is obliged to provide 'care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety' (Xiang et al. 2022, citing King 1995)."
> v4 l. 31: "Obligation is what the host owes the guest, at a minimum, a welcome."
> v4 l. 166: "somebody still owes the guest a welcome."

The legal duty was "bed, board, and hearth" (Sandoval-Strausz p. 63): receive, feed, keep goods safe. Strict liability reached goods "and not his person" (Calye fol. 33b). Liability for harm to the guest's person is reported by Bogen (p. 82 n. 160, an uncited aside) and by Maese restating Beale (p. 499), neither with a date; modern US law states "an affirmative duty to exercise reasonable care in the protection of the guest" (Maese p. 503). So "safety" and admission have legal backing; "care, comfort, ease" and v4's "responsiveness" and "negotiated exception" have none in these sources. On Bogen's account the duty did not begin in welcome at all: it "was incidental to the innkeeper's liability for losses of his lodgers" (p. 91).

### 8. Contract and the institution that opened the threshold (supported, with one counter)

> PO §2.1: "A contract can specify services but not the obligation between host and guest (v4 §1). That obligation belongs to the institution that opened the threshold."

Supported. Innkeepers "do not deal upon contracts as others do" (Eyre J, in Bogen p. 90); the guest's and host's duties are "fixed by law rather than by contract" (Maese p. 501); the hotel is defined by what is "held out by the proprietor" (HPA s 1(3)). Two qualifications. Bogen denies that holding out explains *why* the duty exists (p. 52). And contract is not empty: a contractual duty of care "is usually interpreted as one to see that care is taken (by whoever actually provides the relevant service)" (Morgan AM p. 7).

### 9. "All four sit in one person" (qualified)

> PO §2.4: "In a face-to-face welcome all four sit in one person."
> v4 l. 31: "In an ordinary face-to-face welcome these four sit in one person."

In 1584 the innkeeper already answered for "a default in him or his servants" (Calye fol. 33a). Performer and holder were different people at the inn long before software. The paper's claim is better put as a change in whether the guest can reach the holder, which is what the outline's §4 argues.

### 10. "Accepts some responsibility" (qualified)

> v4 l. 15: "A host takes in the arriving stranger and accepts some responsibility for how that person is received (Lashley 2000)."

For the commercial host the law's verb is "imposed", not "accepts": innkeepers "were not at liberty … to pick and choose their clientele" (Sandoval-Strausz p. 63), and "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty" (Bogen p. 75). This is the same choice as the conflict between the two WS files noted above.
