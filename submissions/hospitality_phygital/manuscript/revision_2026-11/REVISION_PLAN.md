## Argument and flow

**The argument in one paragraph.** A phygital arrangement can hand the functions of hosting — recognizing the guest, setting the price, admitting or refusing — to an algorithmic system, but the obligation of hosting does not travel with them. It stays with the institution that opened its threshold, because arrival triggers the obligation and the one who arrived is owed it, and a duty of that kind changes hands only when the role itself does. Delegating the work to a system hands the role to no one. The failure phygital hospitality risks is therefore not the loss of a human face. It is the loss of an address: the guest keeps standing in principle while no party with authority over the decision is placed to answer them. From that diagnosis the paper derives a test for telling mediation that helps hosting from mediation that replaces it, a distinction between the competence guests and staff can learn and the standing only an institution can confer, and a design goal of interruptibility in place of seamlessness.

**What changes from v4.** v4 made this argument but put its foundation in the wrong place. The account of why the obligation stays — the arrival trigger, the trustee comparison, Kropf et al.'s challenge from care — sat in the middle of Section 4, after the reader had met standing, the triad and the redistribution of agency. Reviewer 2's questions, as the editor reports them, all have answers in that one paragraph: why the responsibility remains, where it comes from, whether it can be transferred, how it differs from delegation or algorithmic accountability. A reviewer reading in order forms the objection long before reaching the answer. The revision moves the theory to the front and makes every later section a consequence of it. Each section now opens by naming what it takes from the one before, which is the repair for the drift Reviewer 3 reported.

**1. Introduction.** A documented case replaces the second-person hotel arrival: the pricing study in which a hotel algorithm biased its own recommendations to move managers who repriced too rarely, leaving a rule no one at the desk could state (Garcia et al. 2026). The gap follows. The literature describes what phygital technology does for the guest, and Germann Molz (2026) describes what happens as the host is erased; no one has asked what becomes of what the host owed. Research question, thesis, three contributions and one itinerary close the section.

**2. Hosting as an obligation of the threshold.** This new section is the theoretical core and the answer to Reviewer 2. It makes four moves in order. *Origin:* hospitality theorists locate the host's obligation at the threshold (Derrida and Dufourmantelle 2000; Lashley 2000; Lynch et al. 2011), and arrival triggers it, where need triggers care, trust triggers fiduciary duty and risk triggers governance duty (Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021). *Holder:* in commercial hospitality the institution opens the threshold — the property, the brand — not the employee, which is why an employment contract cannot by itself explain the obligation. *Transfer:* a host can delegate the performance of a welcome, but its ownership moves only with the role. A trustee who resigns leaves a successor the beneficiary can name; a host who hands the answering to a system leaves none, so ownership stays with the institution by default. *Distinctness:* Bovens (2007) supplies the mechanism of answering, and algorithmic accountability asks who bears a system's harms, but neither carries hosting's content — an obligation owed to this arrival that includes the capacity for a negotiated exception. That content is what service management cannot absorb, and it meets the editor's call to show what is uniquely about hospitality. The section closes by defining obligation, responsibility, authority and accountability, and by setting their unity in the face-to-face host as the baseline that later sections watch come apart.

**3. Guest standing.** Standing is the guest's side of the same relation: the recognized position from which a claim counts as a claim, and it exists only when a counterparty obliged to receive it is within reach. The section keeps v4's separation of standing from consumer rights, procedural justice, contestability and participation. It adds what Reviewer 1 asked for, the hospitality literature on host–guest negotiation, which shows the encounter as a negotiated relation rather than a delivery. Two sentences on the measurement gap (Pijls et al. 2017) close it.

**4. How phygital arrangements separate the obligation from its address.** This section merges v4's Sections 3 and 4. The chain lengthens from dyad to commercial host to algorithmic intermediary. Across the relay between physical and digital, knowledge drifts toward the system, staff contest discretion, and authority drifts toward whoever configures the rules. The section names the result once and keeps the name: the *authority–answerability split*, in which the party able to change the rule is not a party the guest can reach. Reviewer 1's confusion over "the split" goes away because the term now carries a name and a definition where it is coined.

**5. Three conditions and a test.** Reviewers 1 and 3 both lost the thread here, so the section now opens by deriving the test from Section 2. A welcome holds recognition, negotiation and standing together. Each needs a condition at the touchpoint — common understanding, predictability, accountability — and Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) supply the names. Accountability cannot be bought back with the other two, which is why transparency alone does not make a welcome. The six questions in Table 1 put the conditions to work, and two cases show the test discriminating: the pricing study, and check-in that confirms identity against check-in that scores a profile.

**6. Competence is not standing.** The section asks whether skill could close the split and argues it cannot. Guests and staff learn to read and work a system, and employees lend that skill to guests, but no skill conjures a counterparty. Competence is learned; standing is conferred. We cut employee silence to one paragraph.

**7. Designing for interruptibility.** Five principles fall into two sets. Three interface conditions secure common understanding and predictability. Two constitutional conditions — a reachable person with authority to reverse, and a real alternative — secure accountability, and Wilson-Nash et al.'s (2026) account of digital technology captivity grounds the second of them. The section then examines seamlessness as the one design value phygital frameworks have left unquestioned, and separates interruptibility from seamful design. The roadmap in Table 2 closes it, with a new column tying each stage to the condition it secures, so the reader sees the roadmap descend from the test.

**8. Discussion.** Three contributions, each tied to the section that demonstrates it: a theory of hosting obligation under delegation (Section 2), the relational and conferred character of standing (Sections 3 and 6), and the test with its roadmap (Sections 5 and 7). Every "framework" names its referent. The section then states what the hospitality lens sees that service management and algorithmic accountability miss, with implications for managers and for regulators.

**9–10. Limitations, future research, conclusion.** The limits are those of a conceptual paper, now with boundary conditions: budget against luxury, platform against hotel. Three studies follow, with the open question of whether the two silences compound. The conclusion returns to the opening case and answers its question.
