# Structure check: DRAFT_v5 against popular *Hospitality & Society* essays

## Verdict

v5 follows the journal's essays in its front matter, its introduction and its first-person voice. It departs from them in four ways:

- it has more top-level sections than they do;
- it uses tables, which they do not;
- it has no section roadmap at the end of the introduction;
- its paragraphs are longer and more uniform.

A fifth departure, three closing sections where the exemplars have one, I have fixed in v5 by merging two of them (item 1 under "What I changed"). The others need your decision, because each one either conflicts with a standing rule in `REGISTER.md` or trades against something a reviewer or the editor asked for.

The sample is seven texts. The six most-cited conceptual papers behind the paywall are still unread (list at the end), so treat the pattern as provisional until they are in.

## What I read, and how

**Choosing the sample.** "Popular" has two public measures, and I used both:

- OpenAlex citation counts for all 282 works in the journal (source `S4210199845`);
- the journal's own "Most Cited" and "Most Read This Month" lists on its Intellect Discover home page, read on 10 Nov 2026.

From those I took every conceptual or theoretical text I could open in full. Empirical articles were left out, because v5 is a conceptual paper and their structure (method, findings) is not ours.

**Method.** For each text I extracted the headings and their levels, counted words per section, and read the opening of the introduction, its last paragraph, and the first and last paragraphs of the closing section. I also counted first-person pronouns, citation forms, tables and figures. For the two HTML texts I counted paragraphs as well; PDF extraction does not preserve paragraph breaks reliably, so the PDFs have no paragraph figures.

**Limits.**

- Three of the five articles are accepted manuscripts. Their section structure is the authors', but their heading format is not the journal's.
- The journal's typeset format rests on the two versions of record of regular length (Germann Molz 2018; Rydzik et al. 2025) and on Gill et al. (2022).
- Two of the seven are special-issue editorials, which introduce other people's articles; I use them for format and paragraph shape, not for argument structure.

Notes on the table:

- v5's figures are for the draft after the merge described below.
- "Implicit" for Lynch et al.: their introduction ends by naming the two themes that head the next two sections, without listing the sections.
- The citation row counts `(2004: 335)` forms, where the author is named in the sentence, against `(Author 2004)` forms. I ran it on the five PDFs and on v5. v4 stood at 1 : 114 on the same count.

**The pattern, in order.**

1. **Front matter.** A sentence-case title with a colon subtitle; a single-paragraph abstract of 121–212 words; six or seven keywords.
2. **Headings.** Two levels at most. The versions of record set the first level in bold small capitals and the second in bold italic. Five of seven texts leave headings unnumbered; Gill et al. (version of record) and Varley et al. (manuscript) number them.
3. **Introduction.** It runs 7–19% of the body. It opens on a scene (Germann Molz: an Airbnb advertisement set against a Barcelona protest video) or on a claim about the field (Lynch et al.: 'This new journal has been motivated by a paradox'). It states the aim in the first person ('I argue that', 'This article seeks to'). In four of the six I checked, it ends by walking the sections in order: Bulley writes 'The article begins by exploring … The second section outlines … The third section examines'.
4. **Middle.** Three to five sections with headings that name the argument: 'Hospitality as social control', 'Power and hospitality: Beyond sovereign mastery?', 'Spatial scales'. One section is much longer than the rest, at 28–50% of the body. Subsections appear only where a section walks parallel cases: Germann Molz's five spatial scales, Bulley's three aspects of the camp.
5. **Close.** One section, headed 'Conclusion' or 'Conclusions', sometimes with a subtitle. It runs 381–1,079 words. It opens by restating what the article did, draws the implications, and names future research inside the same section. None of the seven has a section headed 'Discussion', 'Limitations' or 'Contributions'.
6. **No tables.** None of the seven has one; one has a single figure.

## Where v5 follows the pattern

- **Abstract and keywords.** 155 words and six keywords, inside the exemplars' range.
- **Introduction.** 760 words and 9% of the body. It opens on the phenomenon and states the question and the argument in the first person.
- **Voice.** 'We' at 5.9 per 1,000 words, mid-range for the co-authored exemplars.
- **Middle headings.** 'The host's obligation', 'Guest standing' and 'The authority–answerability split' name the argument, as the exemplars' headings do.
- **Heading depth.** Two levels, with subsections only in sections 2 and 3.
- **Closing weight.** Discussion and Conclusion together are 1,290 words (16%), the same order as the longest exemplar closes.

## What I changed in v5

1. **One closing section.** v5 had '9. Limitations and a research agenda' and '10. Conclusion'. I merged them into '9. Conclusion', in the exemplars' order: what the paper found, its limits, the three studies, the close.
   - Paragraphs are reordered only. No sentence is changed, and a word-for-word comparison confirms it.
   - For your read-aloud: the last paragraph now repeats, in one sentence, the three studies described in the paragraph before it. You may want to trim one of the two.
   - R3 asked for limitations and the agenda to be clearer and for the conclusion to say what the paper achieved (R3.20, R3.21). R3 did not ask for a separate section, so the merge does not undo a response.
   - I repointed the reviewer letter and the editor note to the new paragraph numbers. The body is now 8,872 words.

## Departures that need your decision

2. **Section roadmap at the end of the introduction.** Four of six exemplars have one; v5 has none.
   - **Conflict:** `REGISTER.md` rule 3 says "No roadmap paragraph". That rule was measured in August on two texts, neither a regular article in this journal: Lugosi (2021), a short commentary in *Tourist Studies*, and Lynch et al. (2021), the editors' decade review.
   - **Recommendation:** add one. R3 found the structure hard to follow, and the journal's own articles do this.
   - **Draft, about 70 words, for the end of §1 ¶4 (Claude's wording, not in v5):** "The article proceeds in that order. We first set out the host's obligation and what survives its delegation, and then define guest standing from it. We next describe the authority–answerability split, derive three conditions and a test for any touchpoint, and state where the test's demands must be met at the moment of decision. We close with design principles, a roadmap for organizations, and the paper's limits."
   - **Cost:** it leaves about 58 words of headroom, which the Bulley and Riordan restorations also need.

3. **Number of sections.** v5 has nine top-level sections; the articles have five to seven.
   - Getting to seven means two folds: '6. Boundary conditions' into section 5 as a subsection, and '8. Discussion' into the Conclusion.
   - **Recommendation:** leave both for this round. The reviewers cite sections by number, 'Boundary conditions' is the visible answer to R2.5, and the Discussion holds what the editor asked for (human-centricity, well-being, industry gains).

4. **Numbered headings.** Five of seven exemplars are unnumbered, and the journal's typeset articles usually are.
   - **Recommendation:** keep the numbers through review, because the response letter locates every change by section number. The Notes for Contributors say nothing either way (`JOURNAL_SPEC.md`), and Gill et al. (2022) was published with numbers.
   - If the typesetter removes them, twelve cross-references in the body ('section 2', 'Section 6 shows') will need names instead.

5. **Tables.** v5 has four; the seven exemplars have none.
   - Each table answers a request: Table 3 answers the reviewers on boundary conditions, Table 4 is the roadmap the editor asked for, and R1 engaged with the tables in the submitted version.
   - **Recommendation:** keep Tables 2–4. Table 1 is the one to cut if you want to move toward the journal's habit; it restates in a grid what section 5's prose already says.

6. **Paragraph length.** v5's 39 paragraphs average 211 words (median 219, shortest 118). The two versions of record I could measure average 153 and 163, with medians of 155 and 156.
   - v5 is inside `REGISTER.md`'s 150–250 band but sits at its top, with little spread.
   - This is a read-aloud matter. Splitting the six or eight longest paragraphs at their natural turn would bring v5 close to the exemplars without changing a sentence.

7. **Citation form.** The exemplars name the author in the sentence about once for every two parenthetical citations. v5 does so once for every four.
   - **Conflict:** `REGISTER.md` ruling 3 makes parenthetical citation the default. It rests on the same *Tourist Studies* commentary.
   - v5 has already moved most of the way (v4 was 1 : 114). No action proposed; I am flagging that the rule's evidence was not from this journal.

8. **Title case.** Every title in the journal's top 40 is in sentence case with no serial comma ('Hospitality and organizations: Enchantment, entrenchment and reconfiguration'). Yours is in title case with a serial comma: 'Algorithmic Mediation, Standing, and Responsibility in Phygital Hospitality'. The typesetter will likely change it; you may prefer to choose the form yourselves.

## One more thing the August rules got from the wrong genre

`REGISTER.md` rule 1 says to spend the authorial first person once. Bulley writes 'I argue', 'I suggest' or the like seven times, Varley et al. write 'we argue' or 'we suggest' nine times, and v5 does so six times. v5 is in line with the journal; the rule is stricter than the journal's practice.
