## (a) Synthesis against K1 and K2

Each numbered statement has its locator on the line beneath. 'AM' and 'AV' mean the pages of an author's manuscript or author's version, not journal pages. 'As quoted in' marks words of Telfer or Derrida that I saw only in another writer's text.

### K1. Hosting is taking responsibility for the guest (customary register)

**Verdict on the texts I read: holds, narrowed.** A line of hospitality scholarship states the responsibility. 'Across the hospitality canon' is too wide, and for the commercial host the sources prescribe the responsibility or assert it; none describes it as a practice the trade observes.

**What supports K1**

1. A hospitality-management article names the responsibility in terms: 'Businesses have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests'.
   - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2 ('Business Principles'), AM p. 15. Card `morrison2008hospitality`.
2. An article on commercial hospitality makes 'safety and security' one of five dimensions of hospitality as a commercial experience and says that hospitality businesses 'must behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience'.
   - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV p. 16. Card `hemmington2007`.
3. The same article prescribes that security in hospitality 'should, therefore, focus on guest security – the security of strangers'.
   - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV p. 15.
4. The field's own history of ancient hospitality records duties to receive the guest, to protect him and to represent him in court, in Greek and Roman guest-friendship.
   - Locator: O'Gorman 2005: 144 (the *proxenos*), 145 ('There were also duties to protect guests and to represent them as patron in the courts of justice if need be'). Card `ogorman2005modern`. The Roman passage is taken from Schmitz 1875: 621, which I read: the obligations 'were to receive in his house his hospes when travelling ..., and to protect, and, in case of need, to represent him as his patron in the courts of justice'.
5. Telfer, at second hand, reports the guest's safety as the host's first responsibility by tradition: 'traditionally the most important responsibility of all was the guest's safety - hospitality was a kind of sanctuary'.
   - Locator: Telfer 2000, no page, as quoted in Hemmington 2007: AV p. 15. One carrier only. Petie et al. paraphrase to the same effect, without quotation: 'Telfer (2000) argues hospitality often includes a responsibility for guests' welfare and ensuring they come to no harm' (2025: MS p. 12; also MS p. 3).

**What narrows or refutes K1**

6. *K1's first refuter is met: canonical definitions describe hospitality with no duty of the host.* Morrison and O'Gorman's own working definition speaks of 'cordial reception, welcome and entertainment ... with kind and generous liberality' and names no duty.
   - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: AM p. 5, quoting Morrison and O'Gorman (2006, p. 3).
7. Brotherton and Wood define hospitality as 'A contemporaneous human exchange, which is voluntarily entered into', as Hemmington quotes them.
   - Locator: Brotherton and Wood 2000, no page, as quoted in Hemmington 2007: AV pp. 5-6. Their chapter is closed; I did not read it.
8. *The register is prescription, not practice, for commercial hosts.* Morrison and O'Gorman obtained their principles by reading a sixth-century monastic rule and translating its language 'into more modern business terminology'; the text presents the figure and discusses no principle.
   - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: AM p. 6 (method), AM p. 7 ('This taxonomy is presented in Fig. 2.').
9. The chapter of the Rule that the taxonomy claims to derive from, as the article prints it, has no verse on guests' health, safety or security.
   - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 1, AM p. 14, read whole (24 verses). This is my reading of Figures 1 and 2 together; the authors do not say it.
10. Hemmington reports that the trade does not treat the guest's safety as binding: it 'is often overlooked and is perhaps insufficiently recognized by the industry'.
    - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV p. 15.
11. Hemmington's 'responsibility' has the experience as its object, not the guest's safety or welfare, and its stated ground is competitive advantage.
    - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV p. 16 ('taking responsibility for the experience'), AV pp. 16-17 ('will gain competitive advantage').
12. *K1's second refuter is met in part: where the duty is stated, it is stated as tradition or ideal.* Telfer's sentence on safety begins 'traditionally'. Telfer calls hospitality an ideal that 'presents itself as joyful rather than onerous'.
    - Locator: Telfer 2000, no page, as quoted in Hemmington 2007: AV p. 15; Telfer 1996: 101, as quoted in O'Gorman 2007: AM p. 6.
13. *K1's third refuter is not met in terms, and is met in the evidence.* No source I read denies that commercial hosts bear the duty. But the duties O'Gorman documents belong to private and public guest-friendship. His Roman inns had a reputation for attracting those 'too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality' and 'a reputation for bedbugs, discomfort, violence and danger'. He extends the duty to business hospitality in a summary bullet with no text cited for the commercial case.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2005: 146 (inns), 149 ('Hospitality management, in the civic and business sense, is established as being centred on persons responsible for formal hospitality, and also for the protection of the guest/stranger and ensuring their proper conduct').
14. The classical sources disagree about how long the institution lasted. Benveniste says the archaic custom of reciprocal *hostis* relations 'had lost its force in the Roman world' in historical times; Schmitz describes Roman private hospitality as 'accurately and legally defined' and dissolved only by formal declaration. They may be describing different layers. Neither places the duty on an innkeeper.
    - Locator: Benveniste, ch. 7, para. 33 (card `benveniste1973indoeuropean`); Schmitz 1875: 621.
15. *The oldest content of the host's obligation is reciprocity, not care.* Benveniste finds in the vocabulary an 'obligation to compensate a gift or service'. O'Gorman's first ground for protection is self-protection: 'one protects the stranger in order to be protected from him'.
    - Locator: Benveniste, ch. 7, para. 31; O'Gorman 2005: 142 (quoting Muhlmann 1932, p. 463), 148 ('the protection of others in order to be protected from others'). This bears on K3 as well: these sources ground the duty in exchange, where K3 names assumption, reliance and dependence.
16. Kant's right of hospitality contains no duty to a person received.
    - Locator: Kant, *Perpetual Peace*, Third Definitive Article, trans. Campbell Smith: 137-139; AA 8:357-358. Card `kant1795perpetual`.

**Suggested wording (R1), for the authors to accept or discard.** 'A line of hospitality scholarship holds that to host is to answer for the guest's safety. Historians of ancient hospitality record the duty for guest-friends (O'Gorman 2005: 144-145), and writers on the commercial setting prescribe it for businesses (Hemmington 2007; Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2), while the management definitions of the 1980s and 1990s describe an exchange and name no duty.' The last clause rests on definitions I saw only as quoted; it needs Brotherton and Wood's own text.

### K2. The duty attaches to the role; hospitableness is how the role is performed

**Verdict on the texts I read: holds, narrowed, with one sentence failed and two parts open.** The premise holds. The gap statement fails as worded, because the literature has named a holder. The Telfer and Derrida parts stay open until their texts are read; the second-hand evidence favours K2 and narrows v6's contrast between duty and hospitableness.

**The premise: hospitality is a practice with roles and rules**

17. The earliest vocabulary names two parties to a pact with obligations, and has separate words for the one received and the one who receives.
    - Locator: Benveniste, ch. 7, para. 32 ('men bound by a pact which implies precise obligations that also devolve on their descendants'), para. 34 ('the guest (the one received) is the xénos and he who receives is the xenodókhos').
18. Hospitality scholars describe ancient hospitality as codified, with 'legal obligations on both sides', and as stratified into 'private, civic or business'.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2005: 144, 149.
19. A hospitality journal article states that hospitality in practice 'is normally governed by a set of man made rules dependent on the context: domestic, civic or commercial'.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2007: AM p. 8; restated AM pp. 21-22 ('domestic, civic or commercial, each with their own different sets of laws'). Card `ogorman2007phenomenon`.
20. Hemmington treats host and guest as a pair distinct from manager and customer and 'much more socially and culturally defined'.
    - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV p. 9; Figure 1, AV p. 10.

**Telfer: host as role, hospitable as motive (second-hand; her text is closed)**

21. Telfer's words on the commercial host are reachable verbatim through two independent carriers. She calls the paid provider 'a commercial host' and makes 'hospitable' depend on his motive and his prices: 'if a commercial host looks after his guests well out of genuine concern for their happiness and charges them reasonably, rather than extortionately, for what he does, his activities can be called hospitable.'
    - Locator: Telfer 2000: 45, as quoted in Slattery 2002: 21; and as quoted in Lynch et al. 2011 (author version, PDF p. 8), which prints 'out of a genuine concern' and stops at 'extortionately'. The two agree but for the article 'a'. See (b), row T7.
22. This is K2's distinction in Telfer's own usage: 'host' names the role whatever the motive, and 'hospitable' names the manner. The inference is mine, from one quoted sentence.
    - Locator: as statement 21. Golubovskaya et al. paraphrase Telfer to the same effect: 'being a good host and being hospitable are different phenomena' (2017: 1286; card `golubovskaya2017meaning`).
23. *Narrows v6.* On Telfer's words as quoted, duty is one of the hospitable motives. Hospitable motives 'can include ... sense of duty where there is also concern for the guests themselves'; they include 'allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable'.
    - Locator: Telfer 1996: 82, as quoted in O'Gorman 2007: AM p. 6 (and O'Gorman 2006: 51); Telfer 2000: 42, as quoted in Blain and Lashley 2014: 2. Two carriers, two different passages, one sense. Lynch et al. paraphrase p. 42 as 'ranging from pleasing others to being dutiful' (2011: author version, PDF p. 8).
24. The contrast between imposed obligation and hospitableness that v6 takes from Blain and Lashley is theirs. In their words the obligation 'may be at odds with Telfer's notion of hospitableness'; in hers, as quoted, a sense of duty joined to concern for the guest is a hospitable motive.
    - Locator: Blain and Lashley 2014: 2 (card `blain2014hospitableness`); Telfer as in statement 23.
25. K2's first refuter ('Telfer ... holding that without the motive there is no hospitality, so that a duty-bound commercial host is not a host') is not borne out by any words of hers that I could reach. It cannot be closed on quotations.
    - Locator: (b), rows T1-T16. Telfer 1995, 1996 and 2000 are all closed.

**Derrida: how the conditional laws relate to the unconditional law (second-hand; the book is closed)**

26. Derrida places rights and duties in the conditional laws: 'the laws (in the plural), those rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional'.
    - Locator: *Of Hospitality*: 77, as quoted in O'Gorman 2007: AM p. 8 (and O'Gorman 2006: 51). Westmoreland quotes another sentence from the same page with 'rights, and duties' in it (2008: 8). See (b), row D10.
27. The unconditional law breaks with hospitality as right or duty: 'absolute hospitality should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty'; 'The law of absolute hospitality commands a break with hospitality by right, with law or justice as rights'.
    - Locator: *Of Hospitality*: 25, as quoted in Westmoreland 2008: 3; and as quoted in O'Gorman 2007: AM p. 9 and, with an ellipsis, in Shryock 2012: S22. See (b), row D3.
28. The break is not a condemnation: 'Just hospitality breaks with hospitality by right; not that it condemns or is opposed to it, and it can on the contrary set and maintain it in a perpetual progressive movement; but it is as strangely heterogeneous to it as justice is heterogeneous to the law to which it is yet so close, from which in truth it is indissociable'.
    - Locator: *Of Hospitality*: 25, 27, as quoted in Shryock 2012: S22. One carrier. See (b), row D4.
29. The two are 'both contradictory, antinomic and inseparable. They both imply and exclude each other, simultaneously'.
    - Locator: *Of Hospitality*: 81, as quoted in Moysidou 2023: MS p. 4. One carrier. Westmoreland says the same in his own words (2008: 9).
30. A philosopher's guide reads the relation as a hierarchy in which each needs the other: hospitality shown 'solely out of duty or by law' is 'not absolute hospitality', such acts 'remain conditional', and 'the law of hospitality requires the laws so as to not be abstract'.
    - Locator: Westmoreland 2008: 4, 8 (citing *Of Hospitality* 79 without quotation). Card `westmoreland2008interruptions`. Bulley, in *Hospitality & Society*, puts it the same way: the conditional laws keep 'the reference point' of the unconditional (2015: author manuscript p. 4).
31. *K2's second refuter narrows to a point of register.* On the quotations, Derrida does not hold that a duty fixed by rules negates hospitality. He holds that it is conditional hospitality, which is the only hospitality there has been, and that it is heterogeneous to the unconditional law and inseparable from it. The paper's duty is therefore a duty of conditional hospitality, and the paper should say so and should not claim the unconditional law for it.
    - Locator: statements 26-30; Westmoreland 2008: 1 ('the only hospitality that we have ever encountered in the West has been conditional').

**Kant**

32. K2's check is confirmed in each part. Kant treats hospitality as a matter 'not of philanthropy, but of right'; the right is the stranger's claim 'to be treated by its owner without hostility'; it is 'not a right to be treated as a guest', which would need 'a special friendly compact'; it is 'a right of visitation' and no more than 'a permission to make an attempt at intercourse with the original inhabitants'.
    - Locator: Kant, *Perpetual Peace*, Third Definitive Article, trans. Campbell Smith: 137-139; German at AA 8:357.22-358.08 and 8:358.23-25 ('kein Gastrecht ... sondern ein Besuchsrecht').
33. The right runs against whoever holds the land the stranger arrives on; Kant's violators are coastal peoples, desert tribes and European 'commercial states'. No host who has received a guest appears, and no commerce in lodging.
    - Locator: Kant, as above: 137-139; AA 8:358.01-02 ('auf dem Boden eines andern ... von diesem').
34. Hospitality studies has overstated Kant. O'Gorman has him argue 'that individuals had a universal right to shelter in any country'. The text gives no such right.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2007: AM pp. 9-10, against Kant: 137 ('The latter may send him away again, if this can be done without causing his death').

**The gap statement**

K2 says: 'The hospitality literature states the host's duty already; what it has not supplied is a holder and a trigger for a host that is an organization.' K2's third refuter says one source that assigns the host's duty to an organization defeats it.

35. *The literature has supplied a holder.* Morrison and O'Gorman name 'Businesses' as responsible for guests' health, safety and security. This defeats the gap statement as worded.
    - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2, AM p. 15.
36. The same taxonomy describes who appears to the guest as the host: 'The person providing the service is seen by the guest as representing the business as a whole', and managers 'have to be seen by the guests as the host'. It places blame for under-equipped staff on management.
    - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2 ('Staffing Principles', 'Management Principles'), AM p. 15. This anticipates part of the paper's account of holding out, and bears on K3 and K8.
37. Hemmington addresses his prescriptions to 'hospitality businesses' and 'Hospitality organisations'.
    - Locator: Hemmington 2007: AV pp. 16-17.
38. Xiang et al. write that 'hotels, as “hosts,” are obliged to provide care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety to the guests (King, 1995)'.
    - Locator: Xiang et al. 2022, section 'A hospitality paradigm of host-guest interactions' (no pages in the PMC text; card `xiang2022covid`, written in an earlier run; I confirmed the sentence in the saved text). King 1995 is closed and the attribution is unchecked.
39. O'Gorman's history attaches responsibilities to an office: in civic and business hospitality 'particular individuals' were 'recognised as having formal and defined responsibilities for hospitality'.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2005: 149.
40. *None of these argues the point.* Morrison and O'Gorman state it in a figure. Hemmington's ground is advantage. Xiang et al. give a citation. O'Gorman gives a summary bullet.
    - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: AM p. 7; Hemmington 2007: AV pp. 16-17; Xiang et al. as above; O'Gorman 2005: 149.
41. *None argues from role as against motive.* Morrison and O'Gorman keep motive in the same list ('Personal characterises of staff must be genuinely disposed to providing service'). Hemmington secures performance by selecting staff as performers.
    - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2, AM p. 15; Hemmington 2007: AV pp. 12-13.
42. *None names a trigger for an organizational host.* The nearest things I read are triggers for a private relation: Kant's 'special friendly compact'; the Roman tie made 'by mutual presents, or by the mediation of a third person'; Benveniste's pact.
    - Locator: Kant: 137-138; Schmitz 1875: 621; Benveniste, ch. 7, paras 30, 32.
43. *None divides the duty among several firms.* Each source that names an organization knows one business.
    - Locator: Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2, read whole; Hemmington 2007, read whole.
44. A hospitality scholar has said in print that the philosophers of hospitality left the commercial context unaddressed: there is 'even less recognition given to the fact that the hospitality relationship exists in dissimilar contexts: domestic, civic or commercial, each with their own different sets of laws'.
    - Locator: O'Gorman 2007: AM pp. 21-22. This supports a narrower gap statement than K2's.

**Two closed neighbours (abstract depth only)**

45. Dogru et al. take hospitality as 'a human virtue', say 'less is understood regarding how this virtue can be practiced at the organizational level', and propose 'organization-level hospitality virtue'. The abstract assigns a virtue to organizations and no duty; it does not mention hosts, guests, duties or responsibility.
    - Locator: Dogru et al. 2025: abstract (Crossref). Card `dogru2025service`. Closed.
46. Koo and Kwon argue that 'the machine age does not eliminate hospitality's ethical and philosophical dimension but relocates ethical responsibility within socio-technical design'. The abstract does not say who then holds it.
    - Locator: Koo and Kwon 2026: abstract (Crossref). Card `koo2026three`. Closed. Bears on K9 more than K2.
47. Until both are read, the paper should not write that the organizational level of hospitality is untheorized or that no hospitality scholar has asked where responsibility goes under automation.
    - Locator: statements 45-46.

**Gap statements, in the required form.** These are statements about the ten texts I read whole, listed in (c). They are not database searches; the scout's searches are logged in `WS_P1_scout.md`, sections (a) and (e), and I ran none beyond record lookups.

- I found no text, among the ten I read whole on 11 Nov 2026, that argues a commercial host's duty from the host's role as distinct from the host's motive.
- I found no text among them that says when an organizational host's duty to a guest begins.
- I found no text among them that divides a host's duty among several firms.
- I found one text among them that names the business as the party responsible for guests' safety (Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2), and one that tells businesses to behave like hosts and take responsibility for the experience (Hemmington 2007: AV p. 16).

**Suggested wording (R1) for the gap sentence.** 'Hospitality scholars have named the business as the party responsible for the guest's safety (Morrison and O'Gorman 2008: Figure 2; Hemmington 2007). They have not argued why the duty falls on the business, said when it begins, or said which firm holds it when several firms host together.'
