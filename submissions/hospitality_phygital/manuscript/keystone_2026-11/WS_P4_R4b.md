# WS P4, reader R4b: the innkeeper's duties in United States law and in civil law

I read eight texts whole and wrote a card for each: three state supreme court decisions (*Rasnick*, *Hoyt*, *Groh*), one federal appeal (*Thomas v Pick Hotels*), Blackstone's passages on innkeepers, Digest 4.9, BGB §§ 701–704 with § 702a, and the 1962 Council of Europe convention with its annex. The scout's notes served only to find the files. I re-fetched every text from its archive on 11 Nov 2026 and each was byte-identical to the saved copy; for the convention I matched the saved PDFs' digests to the Internet Archive's index. I ran no new searches, read no Restatement, no commentary and no later case, and checked no case's later treatment.

Every statement below carries its locator on the line beneath it. Locators are reporter pages for *Rasnick*, *Hoyt* and *Thomas*, paragraphs for *Groh*, Blackstone's pages as the Avalon transcription marks them, and section numbers for the Digest, the BGB and the convention. A Restatement section is cited only as a named court quotes it.

## (a) Synthesis

### K4. Content and limits, in two tiers

**Admission.**

- Blackstone states a duty to receive that the refused traveller enforces by an action for damages and that an indictment enforces as well.
  - 3 Comm. 164 ("an action on the case will lie against him for damages, if he without good reason refuses to admit a traveler"); 4 Comm. 168 (inns "may be indicted, suppressed, and the inn-keepers fined").
- A federal appeals court applying Kansas law in 1955 called the duty to provide accommodation common law that "has never been abrogated", though it "may have been dormant or stale".
  - *Thomas*, 666.
- **Narrows.** Neither source states K4's conditions of capacity, fitness and ability to pay. Blackstone's only limits are "without good reason" and "without a very sufficient cause".
  - 3 Comm. 164; 4 Comm. 168.
- **Narrows.** Blackstone's sentence reaches "an inn-keeper, or other victualler", so he cannot be cited for confining the duty to inns.
  - 3 Comm. 164.
- **Refutes, outside the common law.** Ulpian says innkeepers are free to receive no one, and gives that freedom as a reason why their strict liability is fair.
  - D. 4.9.1.1 ("nam est in ipsorum arbitrio, ne quem recipiant").
- **Refutes, outside the common law.** The German sections on innkeepers and the convention contain no duty to admit a guest. What each obliges the hotel-keeper to receive is the guest's valuables.
  - BGB §§ 701–704, with § 702(3); Convention, Annex arts 1–7, with art. 2(2).
- K4 asks for one civil-law jurisdiction to show that the duty is not an Anglo-American accident. On these texts that is shown for goods and fails for admission. I read only the innkeeper provisions; whether German law obliges a hotel to accept guests elsewhere is unread.
  - BGB §§ 701–704 (the only German provisions read).

**Reception: goods.**

- Roman law, the German code and the convention each make the innkeeper liable for a lodged guest's things without proof of fault.
  - D. 4.9.3.1 ("etiam si sine culpa eius res periit"); BGB §§ 701(1), 702(2) no. 1; Annex arts 1(1), 4. For the BGB and the annex, liability without fault is my reading of the structure.
- Blackstone puts the same duty as a contract the law always implies with "a common inn-keeper, to secure his guest's goods in his inn".
  - 3 Comm. 164.
- The modern texts cap the liability and forbid contracting out below the cap.
  - BGB §§ 702(1), 702a(1); Annex arts 1(3), 6.

**Reception: the person.**

- The Supreme Court of Colorado held that a hotel owes its guest a duty of reasonable care, which governs how the hotel ends the stay and is not bounded by the property line.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 4, 32, 40–43.
- The Supreme Court of Iowa held that a tavern owes its patron a duty of reasonable care that reaches harm done by another patron.
  - *Hoyt*, 776–777.
- The Supreme Court of Georgia affirmed that an innkeeper must keep the premises reasonably safe, and held that a motel had no duty to investigate or check on a guest whose wife telephoned to say he might be ill. It left open whether a duty to render or summon aid exists in Georgia.
  - *Rasnick*, 567, 569–570.
- **Narrows.** K4 lays the reception duties on "every commercial host, restaurants and bars included". Iowa agrees for taverns. Colorado says its holding "reaches only hotels" and that for taverns and restaurants "the requisite special relationship is absent".
  - *Hoyt*, 777; *Groh*, n. 7.
- **Narrows.** In none of the three cases was the host held liable. The motel won on duty; the tavern and the hotel were sent to trial.
  - *Rasnick*, 570; *Hoyt*, 782; *Groh*, ¶ 51.
- The civil-law texts read are silent on the guest's person. They neither support nor refute the care limb outside the common law.
  - D. 4.9.1.6 (things and merchandise); BGB § 701(1) ("Sachen"); Convention, title and Annex art. 1(1) ("property").

**Attending as a customary and not a legal duty.**

- **Supports.** In the one case where a host declined to look in on a guest, the Georgia court separated the registers itself: "a moral or humane obligation does not compel the existence of a legal duty".
  - *Rasnick*, 569–570.
- **Narrows.** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 314A, as two courts quote it, puts an innkeeper under a duty "to give them first aid after it knows or has reason to know that they are ill or injured, and to care for them until they can be cared for by others". Colorado records that it had cited the section with approval; Georgia reserved the point.
  - As quoted in *Rasnick*, 568, and in *Groh*, ¶ 29; *Rasnick*, 570 (reserved).
- **Narrows.** Colorado's duty turns on "the guest's physical state", and the court counted letting intoxicated guests wait in the lobby among the "relatively low-cost options" open to a hotel.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 4, 36.
- K4's sentence is therefore too broad. Where these formulations are law, aid to a guest known to be ill or injured, and regard for a guest's condition when the host acts on her, are legal duties. The sentence holds if "attending" means attention beyond safety and aid.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 4, 29; *Rasnick*, 568.

**Guests who do not pay.**

- For goods, all three civil-law texts attach the liability to reception and not to payment. A guest lodged for nothing kept the Roman action. The German section and the annex define the guest by being lodged.
  - D. 4.9.6 pr., 4.9.5 pr.; BGB § 701(1) ("ein im Betrieb dieses Gewerbes aufgenommener Gast"); Annex art. 1(1). For the last two this is my reading of the words.
- In *Groh* the room was rented by the guest's sister, and the court treated the registered occupant as the guest without comment.
  - *Groh*, ¶ 5.
- None of the eight texts says who is the host of a guest placed by a public body.
  - No locator; the question does not arise in any of them.

### K5. Directedness

- **Supports, duty to receive.** The refused traveller sues in his own name in Blackstone, and in *Thomas* the refused guest pleads the duty in tort and in contract; the court calls the innkeeper's duty "relational" and its breach "redressible in tort".
  - 3 Comm. 164; *Thomas*, 665–666.
- **Narrows.** The duty to receive was public as well. Blackstone makes refusal indictable as a common nuisance, and on the page before says common nuisances are "indictable only, and not actionable". He does not reconcile that sentence with the traveller's action in book 3.
  - 4 Comm. 167–168; 3 Comm. 164.
- Blackstone's book 3 contradicts, for England in the 1760s, the view that the duty gave the traveller no right. The paper may say both routes existed; it may not say Blackstone explains how they fit.
  - 3 Comm. 163 ("they have therefore their remedy in damages"), 164.
- **Supports, goods.** The praetor gives the guest an action; the German code speaks of the claim belonging to the guest; the annex speaks of the guest being "entitled to the benefit".
  - D. 4.9.1 pr., 4.9.1.7, 4.9.6 pr.; BGB § 703; Annex art. 5.
- **Supports, care.** The injured guest and the injured patron sue in their own names, and the Iowa court quotes as a reason for the duty that the relationship "identifies a specific person to be protected".
  - *Groh*, ¶ 11; *Hoyt*, 773, 777.
- **Narrows.** The remedies are thin or unproved. In *Thomas* the tort claim was out of time and the contract claim was only allowed to be tried. The convention binds states and gives a guest nothing directly.
  - *Thomas*, 666; Convention, art. 1(1).
- The claim that the customary duty to attend gives the guest nothing to demand is consistent with *Rasnick*. None of these texts speaks to the classical code.
  - *Rasnick*, 569–570.
- I found no duty in K4 for which these texts deny the guest an action.
  - All eight texts.

### K3. Why the duty binds

**The role specifies.**

- In each tradition the duty attaches to the calling or the relationship, and not to the contract or the payment. Blackstone implies the undertaking only against one whose "common profession" it is. The Tenth Circuit treats the contract as "the means for the inducement or establishment" of a relationship to which the common law then attaches the duty. Gaius says the innkeeper is paid to let travellers stay, not for custody, and is liable for custody all the same.
  - 3 Comm. 164; *Thomas*, 665–666; D. 4.9.5 pr., 4.9.3.2; BGB § 701(1); Annex art. 1(1).

**Voluntary assumption, by holding out.**

- Blackstone rests the traveller's action on the innkeeper's own act: he "hangs out a sign and opens his house", and the law reads that as "an implied engagement" and a "universal assumpsit". He says of this class of undertakings that they were "never perhaps actually made". That is the middle position in an eighteenth-century source: holding out takes up the role, and the law supplies the duty.
  - 3 Comm. 161, 163–164.
- Ulpian defends the strict liability by the innkeeper's choice to receive.
  - D. 4.9.1.1.
- The American formulations use the language of holding open and of invitation.
  - § 314A(3) as quoted in *Rasnick*, 568 ("holds it open to the public"); § 40(b)(3) as quoted in *Hoyt*, 776 ("holds its premises open to the public"); OCGA § 51-3-1 as quoted in *Rasnick*, n. 2 ("by express or implied invitation, induces or leads others to come upon his premises").

**Reliance.**

- Ulpian's first reason is that travellers must generally trust the innkeeper's good faith and commit their things to him. That is reliance by necessity, closer to dependence than to reliance the host invited.
  - D. 4.9.1.1 ("necesse est plerumque eorum fidem sequi et res custodiae eorum committere").
- No case on my list rests the innkeeper's duty on the guest's reliance.
  - *Rasnick*, *Hoyt*, *Groh*, *Thomas* (whole opinions).

**Dependence.**

- Colorado describes the law as moving toward "a duty to aid or protect those in a dependent or mutually dependent relationship". Iowa quotes the reason that some relationships "compromise a person's ability to self-protect, while leaving the actor in a superior position to protect that person".
  - *Groh*, ¶ 29; *Hoyt*, 777 (the court quoting a comment to § 40).
- The courts' own name for the ground is the "special relationship" of innkeeper and guest.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 4, 26; *Hoyt*, 776.

**Where the texts narrow K3.**

- **Other grounds.** The texts give four grounds outside K3's list: crime control (the edict checks collusion with thieves and, in Pomponius's words as Ulpian reports them, serves "reprimendae improbitatis hoc genus hominum", the repression of dishonesty in this kind of men, my gloss); the purpose of inns ("the end of their institution"); benefit to the host; and judicial policy factors, which Colorado says may confer a duty alongside the relationship.
  - D. 4.9.1.1, 4.9.3.1; 4 Comm. 168; *Hoyt*, 777; *Groh*, ¶¶ 25, 33–37.
- **Dependence did not bind in Georgia.** A 77-year-old guest alone in his room, far from home and unable to call for help, is the paper's picture of dependence. The court found no duty to check on him, and recorded that no employee had undertaken to.
  - *Rasnick*, 565, 569–570, n. 1.
- **Not specific to hosts.** As quoted, both Restatement sections list the innkeeper beside carriers and every possessor of land open to the public. Colorado, by contrast, treats the innkeeper's relationship as one that taverns and restaurants lack.
  - *Rasnick*, 568; *Hoyt*, 776; *Groh*, n. 7.
- **Disclaimer.** K3 expects that an assumed duty can be disclaimed by notice. The Roman text allows it where passengers agree to the operator's warning. The German code and the annex void advance exclusions below the cap. Where the law closes that door, the host's consent cannot be what makes the duty bind.
  - D. 4.9.7 pr. (said of the ship's operator); BGB § 702a; Annex art. 6 (subject to the state option in Convention, art. 2(d)).
- None of the eight texts tests the hard cases. The motel in *Rasnick* was "owned and operated" by the one defendant; the defendant in *Groh* is the operating company; *Thomas* does not say who owned or ran the hotel.
  - *Rasnick*, 565; *Groh*, caption (Westin Operator, LLC); *Thomas*, 665.

### K7. The channel from the guest

**P1, duties that run for the length of the stay.**

- Colorado asks whether the risk arose "while she remained a guest", treats eviction as a process, and rejects the argument that the duty ended "at the property line". An earlier appellate opinion, withdrawn, had held that the relationship ended on eviction.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 16, 40–43.
- The goods liability runs while the guest has the accommodation and for a reasonable time before and after.
  - Annex art. 1(2); BGB § 701(2).

**P2, answering for those through whom the host acts.**

- The Roman operator answers for the staff he took on "suo periculo" (at his own risk, my gloss); the German innkeeper for "seinen Leuten"; the hotel-keeper under the annex for "any person for whose actions he is responsible". Colorado lets a claim for negligent hiring and training of the guards go to trial.
  - D. 4.9.7 pr.; BGB § 702(2) no. 1; Annex art. 4; *Groh*, ¶¶ 23, 45.

**P3, a duty to see that it is done includes the means to learn that it is not.**

- No text states P3. The Digest puts the operator's fault in whom he employed and in not looking into them first, which is the selection model that K7 lists as a defeater.
  - D. 4.9.7.4 ("explorare eum oportet, cuius fidei, cuius innocentiae sint").
- The nearest a court comes is Colorado's remark that the record lacked "determinative information on the adequacy of the Westin's training on eviction procedures".
  - *Groh*, ¶ 45.

**P4, the guest's report as the timely signal.**

- In *Groh* the hotel knew the risk because the guests told its guards they were drunk and could not drive, and the person on the spot with power over the single case refused a friend's request to wait in the lobby.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 2, 7–8.
- In *Rasnick* the guest could not report. The signal came from his wife by telephone; one operator hung up, and her last five calls met a recorded message.
  - *Rasnick*, n. 1; dissent, 572.
- For goods, the German code and the annex make the guest's prompt report to the hotel-keeper the condition of the guest's protection.
  - BGB § 703; Annex art. 5.

**What refutes or narrows the conclusion.**

- ***Rasnick* refutes C as a statement of Georgia law for a guest's illness.** The motel had a telephone, an operator and a report, and owed no duty to act on it. The court's reasons are aimed at any duty triggered by reports: "What type of inquiry would or should cause the innkeeper to inquire into the guest's state of health and from whom?", with "issues implicating the guest's privacy". It calls a requirement that an innkeeper "monitor in any manner the possible health problems of a guest" unworkable.
  - *Rasnick*, 569–570.
- The reach of *Rasnick* is limited. The risk was "internal to the guest" and so outside the premises duty; the report came from a relative; the court reserved the duty to render aid. It does not decide whether a host must be able to learn of failures in duties it does owe.
  - *Rasnick*, 568, 570, n. 1.
- **Every remedy in these texts is damages after the event.** No court or code orders a hearing or presupposes one during the stay. On these sources the tort lawyer's objection stands, and K7 rests on P3 as the paper's own step.
  - *Rasnick*, 570; *Hoyt*, 782; *Groh*, ¶ 51; *Thomas*, 666; D. 4.9.7.1; BGB § 701(1); Annex art. 1(1).
- The civil-law texts place the burden of reporting on the guest and say nothing about how the hotel-keeper is to be reached. That the provisions presuppose a reachable hotel-keeper is my inference.
  - BGB § 703; Annex art. 5.

**What supports it, narrowly.**

- The civil-law texts contain one duty that the host performs only on the guest's request, with a sanction for refusing: the hotel-keeper must take the guest's valuables into safekeeping and loses the cap if he will not.
  - BGB § 702(2) no. 2, (3); Annex art. 2(1)(b), 2(2).
- Colorado counted "allowing intoxicated guests to wait in the lobby after they call a taxi" among the options reasonable care might require. The dissent answered that the guards' conduct, though "unquestionably unprofessional", changed nothing.
  - *Groh*, ¶ 36; dissent, ¶ 59.
- **The customary footing has judicial company.** The Georgia majority grants that the facts raise "issues of morality and humanity"; its dissent asks the legislature "to close the gap"; Colorado quotes an older court on "the dictates of humanity" and another on "decent and humane treatment". These judges place the unanswered guest in the moral register, where K7's customary footing sits.
  - *Rasnick*, 569–570, 573; *Groh*, ¶ 31, n. 6 (both as quoted by the Colorado court from cases I did not read).
- **Timing.** In Colorado a host cannot end the duty by ending the stay in a dangerous way. Before reception the Roman text gives the opposite picture: an innkeeper who refuses at the door wrongs no one.
  - *Groh*, ¶¶ 40–43; D. 4.9.1.1.

### Corrections to the scout's notes

- *Rasnick* neither adopted nor rejected § 314A(2). It declined a duty "to investigate or check" and reserved the question of a duty to render or summon aid.
  - *Rasnick*, 570.
- The scout wrote that the CAP text does not show where the court's emphasis falls in § 314A. The CAP HTML italicises subsection (2).
  - sources/rasnick2011krishna/cap_ga_289_0565-01.html.
- Two courts' quotations of § 314A(1)(a) differ by a word. The *Rasnick* majority has "to protect them from unreasonable risk"; the *Rasnick* dissent and *Groh* have "against unreasonable risk".
  - *Rasnick*, 568, 571; *Groh*, ¶ 29.
- In *Hoyt* the quoted text of § 40 is on page 776; only the sentence introducing it begins on 775. The CAP text garbles the letter of the comment quoted at 777.
  - *Hoyt*, 775–777.
- Blackstone has a third innkeeper passage, on goods, beside the sign passage, and a framing sentence at 163. His page 167 says common nuisances are "indictable only, and not actionable".
  - 3 Comm. 163–164; 4 Comm. 167.
- The burdick1911origin card quotes the sign passage as "3 Bl Comm 165" with "offers his house". Avalon's first-edition text has it on page 164 with "opens his house".
  - library/cards/burdick1911origin.md, line 33; 3 Comm. 164.
- The Digest permits an agreed warning to remove the operator's liability, and gives the repression of dishonesty as a purpose of the edict. Scott mistranslates at least two phrases in the title.
  - D. 4.9.7 pr., 4.9.3.1; Scott at 4.9.1.7 ("it makes a little difference" for "parvi referre") and 4.9.3 pr. ("He also asks" for "idem ait").
