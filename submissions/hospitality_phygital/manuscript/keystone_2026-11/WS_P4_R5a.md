# WS P4, reader R5a: what stays with the duty-holder when others do the work, in English law (K7, K8)

**Scope.** Seven judgments, each read whole, and ten sections of one statute, read on 11 Nov 2026 from the copies the P4b scout saved (four of the sections I fetched myself), and carded in `library/cards/`: `barclays2020uksc`, `armes2017uksc`, `hughes2022rattan`, `ferguson1987welsh`, `naylor2004payling`, `maguire2006sefton`, `wong1995kwan`, `uk2015consumer`. I used the scout's notes to find the files and for nothing else. I ran no new searches, so this report makes no gap statement of its own. Every sentence about a case rests on the text of that case; where a judgment describes another case I have not read, the sentence says so.

**Locators.** Paragraph numbers for the five National Archives judgments; transcript page numbers for *Ferguson v Welsh* (the '- n -' marks in the BAILII text) and for *Wong Mee Wan* (the printed page numbers of the scanned transcript); section numbers for the Act. *Woodland* and the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 are cited from their existing cards and marked '(card)'; I did not re-read them.

## (a) Synthesis against K7 and K8

### Three results first

**1. 'A duty to see that the work is done with care' names two different duties in English law, and oversight belongs to the weaker one.** Lord Reed sets a duty to perform a function with care against a duty "to arrange to have performed, subject to a duty to take care in making and supervising those arrangements". Under the first, the non-delegable duty, the holder is liable for the delegate's carelessness and careful selection is no answer; the judgments give it no content of monitoring, and liability under it does not depend on what the holder knew or did. Under the second the holder must select with care and, sometimes, supervise, and is liable only for its own fault. K7's P2 is true in both senses. P3 draws oversight out of the first sense, and these courts place it in the second.
- Locators: *Armes* paras 31, 34, 37, 47-48, 75; *Hughes* paras 68, 72; *Woodland* para 24 (card).

**2. No source in this list states P3. Three limit it and six passages give it a foothold.** The House of Lords says an occupier is not ordinarily expected to supervise a contractor it reasonably believes competent, and the Court of Appeal has twice taken the same position for operators serving the public. The footholds are the second limb of the occupiers' entrustment defence, the rule that knowledge or reason to suspect ends the defence, one statutory regime of supervision, a judge's remark that an occupier who takes "no steps" may be liable, a regulator's contract that pairs answerability with checks and a complaints procedure, and the Consumer Rights Act's ordering of remedies. P3 remains the paper's own step.
- Locators: *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* paras 12, 19, 38, 50; *Maguire* paras 30, 35; *Armes* para 48; *Hughes* paras 25, 55; Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 55, 56(3).

**3. K8's contract limb is right for the services a host promises and wrong, as worded, for dangers on the premises; and two hospitality defendants have won on reasonable entrustment.** A party that undertakes to supply services answers for a sub-contractor's want of care. A party that lets a customer onto premises under contract warrants only the common duty of care, entrustment defence included. A nightclub owner and a leisure-centre operator each escaped liability to a customer for a contractor's negligence.
- Locators: *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 4, 10; *Maguire* paras 24, 31; *Naylor* paras 1, 25, 54 and the order.

### K8, limb by limb

**Employees.** The holder answers for employees and for workers whose relationship with it is akin to employment. The Supreme Court restated in 2020 that the extension stops at a person carrying on business on his own account.
- Locators: *Barclays* paras 1, 24, 27; *Armes* paras 54, 58-60.

**Independent contractors: the starting point K8 leaves out.** K8 says the rule "varies". The texts give it a fixed starting point: the employer of an independent contractor is "in general, not liable" for the contractor's torts, and the variations are exceptions. Lady Hale's examples of plain independent contractors are "the company hired to clean its windows" and "the auditors hired to audit its books". An outsourced security, cleaning or software firm with other clients is on that side of the line; the sentence applying the rule to such firms is mine, not the court's.
- Locators: *Barclays* paras 7, 28; *Naylor* paras 32, 38, 42.

**The same workers can fall on different sides under different doctrines.** Self-employed dentists who worked in the owner's premises, on his patients and under his NHS contract were not akin to employees, though the owner owed the patient a non-delegable duty for their work. Foster parents recruited, trained and supervised by a local authority were within vicarious liability, though the authority owed no non-delegable duty. K8 has to name the doctrine each time.
- Locators: *Hughes* paras 68, 72, 88-91; *Armes* paras 49, 59-62, 74.

**Reasonable entrustment: confirmed, on hospitality facts.** The occupier's defence is real and extends by analogy to the common law. A nightclub owner who used licensed door staff from a contractor was not liable to the customer they injured. A leisure-centre operator that engaged the manufacturer to maintain its machines was not liable to the paying member a machine injured.
- Locators: *Ferguson* pp. 7-8; *Naylor* paras 9, 12, 19, 25, 45, 50; *Maguire* paras 25, 27, 30-31.

**'Without that defence where the duty is non-delegable': the rule is right; its reach to guests is not shown.** A non-delegable duty cannot be discharged by careful selection. The Supreme Court calls such liabilities "exceptional" and to "be kept within reasonable limits". The Court of Appeal applied the duty to an adult who chose the business, paid at its desk and was free to go elsewhere, but did so because she was a "patient", and declined only to add a test of special vulnerability. The only ruling in this list on whether a hospitality business owes its customer a non-delegable duty is the county court judge's in *Naylor*: he held that the nightclub did not, and the Court of Appeal was not asked to review that. I read no case applying the duty to a guest.
- Locators: *Armes* paras 31-32, 49; *Hughes* paras 9-10, 71(1), 71(3); *Naylor* paras 1, 19.

**'Or contractual': refuted as worded, for the premises.** Rix LJ holds that s. 5 of the 1957 Act makes the term implied into a contract of entry "exactly the same as the duty for which section 2 provided", so that the occupier does not warrant its contractor's care, and that in this respect the position "differs from the position in contract generally". A paying customer lost in contract on that ground. Rix LJ also records that McCardie J in 1917 had stated the innkeeper's implied warranty more strictly, as premises as safe "as reasonable care and skill on the part of anyone can make them", and that s. 5 was meant to do away with such distinctions. I know *MacLenan v Segar* only from that passage; another reader in this run has carded the 1917 report itself (`maclenan1917segar`).
- Locators: *Maguire* paras 14-15, 21, 24, 31.

**The contract, for services promised: supported, with two exits.** A supplier who arranges for others to perform is not absolved and is liable if the sub-contractor performs without due care. Rix LJ states the same general rule in passing. The first exit is a contract that only arranges: such a party promises care in selection and no more, and which contract was made is a matter of construction. The second exit is an exemption clause, which the Privy Council twice treats as open to the operator. For consumer contracts in the United Kingdom the 2015 Act closes the second exit: a term excluding liability under the care-and-skill term does not bind, the bar extends to terms "preventing an obligation or duty arising or limiting its extent", and liability for death or personal injury from negligence cannot be excluded. The first exit stays open on the texts I read.
- Locators: *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 4-5, 10-11; *Maguire* para 24; Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 49(1), 57(1), 57(5), 65(1).

**The contract limb is not a warranty of safety.** The Privy Council's term is that "reasonable skill and care would be used in rendering the services", and expressly not that the customer "would be reasonably safe".
- Locator: *Wong Mee Wan* p. 11.

**Allocation among firms: the test the courts use is who undertook to provide.** The Privy Council read a brochure's "we" as showing that the tour company "undertook to provide and not merely to arrange". The Court of Appeal fixed the duty on the practice owner because the form the patient signed named him as provider, the patients were described as the practice's, and the goodwill was his; it rejected his case that the practice merely arranged treatment, and said a company in his place would owe the same. Where a statute sets the function, the statute decides whether the body performs or arranges. For vicarious liability, by contrast, how the arrangement looks to the customer no longer decides; after *Barclays* the focus is "the contractual arrangements between tortfeasor and defendant". Holding out therefore works through contract and through the non-delegable duty, and not through vicarious liability.
- Locators: *Wong Mee Wan* p. 10; *Hughes* paras 41, 60, 66, 69-70, 71(2), 86, 88; *Armes* paras 37-38, 47.

**Goods.** Nothing in this list tests the innkeeper's strict liability. *Armes* treats a bailee's responsibility for its servants, theft included, as a non-delegable duty, which is support by analogy only.
- Locator: *Armes* para 51.

**Verdict on K8 from these eight sources: holds, narrowed.** The per-duty structure is right. Three corrections are needed: state the no-liability starting point for independent contractors; confine 'non-delegable' to what the cases have held (patients, pupils, employees, bailors) and present its extension to guests as argument; and split the contract limb between services promised and the safety of the premises.

### K7, premise by premise

**P2 (the holder keeps a duty to see that the work is done with care).** Something stays with the holder under every doctrine except vicarious liability for a true independent contractor, but it is not one thing. Under the non-delegable duty and under a promise to supply services, what stays is liability for the delegate's performance. Under the occupier's duty and the general negligence rule, what stays is a duty of reasonable care in choosing the delegate and, within limits, as to the work.
- Locators: *Armes* paras 31, 34, 37; *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 4, 10; *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* paras 19, 45; *Barclays* para 27.

**P3 (a duty to see that something is done includes having the means to learn that it is not being done, and to correct it while performance is still possible).**

*What the sources do not say.* No judgment in this list derives a duty to have means of learning from a duty to see that care is taken. Where the duty is strict the courts need no such step: the holder is liable whatever it knew. The words 'monitor', 'supervise' and 'oversight' do not occur in *Barclays* or *Wong Mee Wan*; in *Hughes* 'oversight' occurs once, describing the owner's contract with the health body; in *Naylor* every occurrence of 'supervis-' is the job title 'door supervisor'.
- Locators: *Armes* paras 31, 75; *Hughes* para 55(iv); word counts run on the saved texts and, for *Wong Mee Wan*, on my OCR pass, 11 Nov 2026.

*What limits it.* Lord Keith: "It would not ordinarily be reasonable to expect an occupier of premises having engaged a contractor whom he has reasonable grounds for regarding as competent, to supervise the contractor's activities". Latham LJ: the obligation "is limited to ensuring that the independent contractor who he has employed is competent". Rix LJ: nothing required the council "to take any special measures in controlling or supervising" its contractor's inspection. All three treat the holder's duty as discharged by a reasonable choice unless something more is shown. This is the defeater K7's own list anticipates.
- Locators: *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* para 19; *Maguire* para 30.

*What gives it a foothold.*
1. The entrustment defence has a second limb, and the House of Lords reads it as reaching work in progress: an occupier may be liable "if he did not take reasonable steps to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work was being properly done". Waller LJ, as quoted in *Naylor*, reads the paragraph as laying "two obligations" on the employer of a contractor.
   - Locators: *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* para 12 (quoting *Gwilliam* para 40, which I did not read).
2. Knowledge ends the defence. Where the occupier "knows or has reason to suspect" unsafe work, action may be required; experience of an unreliable door attendant would have made reliance on the licence negligent; the leisure-centre operator escaped because it had no "reason to contemplate" a deficient inspection.
   - Locators: *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* para 50; *Maguire* para 30.
3. Where Parliament made a body an arranger, the Supreme Court described its continuing responsibility in P3's terms: approval beforehand, then inspection, supervision and removal, so that problems "are identified and addressed once they have become capable of observation". The source is a statute and regulations, the duty was one of reasonable care, and it was met although the abuse went undetected.
   - Locators: *Armes* paras 1, 8-9, 21, 38, 48.
4. An occupier that has taken "no steps" to see that a machine is safe may be liable alongside its maintainer.
   - Locator: *Maguire* para 35 (Jacob LJ).
5. A regulator's contract can pair answerability with oversight and a complaints route: the dental practice owner had to check his associates' competence, run "a practice based quality assurance system" and provide a complaints procedure, and his associates had to report complaints to him. Those terms ran to the health body and not to the patient.
   - Locators: *Hughes* paras 25-26, 31, 33, 55.
6. For consumer service contracts, statute puts correction of performance before money (see the timing boundary below).
   - Locators: Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 55(1)-(3), 56(3).

*Where the law stops short of C.* The courts make the holder's duty turn on what it knows and impose no duty to arrange to be told. What the council's architects saw on site did not count against the council, because no one "informed the council". The leisure-centre operator had contracted away its own power to open its machines, and the court drew nothing from that. A guest's report matters in law because it supplies knowledge; no source in this list requires the host to keep a way of receiving it.
- Locators: *Ferguson* p. 8; *Maguire* paras 8, 30.

*The claimant's own report.* In none of the seven cases did the claimant's report before the harm figure in the reasoning. In *Maguire* the record of the defect begins with the fall. *Hughes* records, without legal consequence, that the patient's complaint through her daughter about seeing too many dentists was "then adhered to".
- Locators: *Maguire* para 9; *Hughes* para 13.

**The timing boundary (performance still possible, or not).** All seven cases are claims for damages after the harm. The one text that orders remedies by whether performance can still be completed is the Consumer Rights Act: the consumer may require the trader "to perform the service again, to the extent necessary to complete its performance in conformity with the contract", and may have a price reduction only if that "is impossible" or is not provided in time. Damages remain alongside. This supports K7's distinction between the duty to perform and the duty to repair, for consumer service contracts.
- Locators: Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 54(3), 54(6)-(7), 55(1)-(3), 56(3).

**C (a channel from the guest to a person with power over the single case).** Nothing in this list enacts C. The ten sections of the 2015 Act that I read contain no duty on a trader to be reachable; the right to repeat performance arises "If the consumer requires such repeat performance" and presupposes that the consumer can reach the trader.
- Locators: Consumer Rights Act 2015 ss. 2, 48, 49, 54-57, 62, 65, 66 (read whole); s. 55(2).

**Verdict on K7 from these eight sources.** P2 holds, per duty, in two senses that must be kept apart. P3 is the paper's own step: the sources neither state it nor refute it as a normative claim, and three of them refute it as a statement of what English tort law requires of an occupier or employer of a contractor in the ordinary case. C is not enacted here.

### Where the sources refute or narrow a claim, stated plainly

1. **Refuted, as worded: K8's 'without that defence where the duty is ... contractual', for dangers on premises entered under contract.** *Maguire* paras 24, 31.
2. **Refuted, as a statement of law: that a holder who delegates must monitor the delegate.** In the ordinary case of an occupier or an employer of a competent contractor the duty is discharged by reasonable selection. *Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* paras 19, 38; *Maguire* para 30.
3. **Refuted for this fact pattern: that a commercial host answers for an outsourced contractor's negligence towards a customer.** *Naylor* paras 1, 25, 54 and the order; *Maguire* para 31.
4. **Narrowed: 'the holder answers'.** For a true independent contractor the general rule is no liability. *Barclays* paras 7, 27.
5. **Narrowed: non-delegable duty.** Exceptional; refused where the holder's function is to arrange; applied to an adult only as a patient. *Armes* paras 32, 37, 49; *Hughes* para 71(1).
6. **Narrowed: 'the party that promised the stay answers'.** Only if the party undertook to provide and not merely to arrange, and, outside consumer contracts, subject to an exemption clause; and the promise is of care and skill, not of safety. *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 4-5, 10-11.
7. **Narrowed: P3's 'duty to see'.** In the cases, a duty to ensure that care is taken is a liability rule with no oversight content. *Armes* paras 31, 75.
8. **Narrowed: the legal analogues.** Seven of eight sources give damages afterwards. Only the Consumer Rights Act ss. 55-56 puts correction of performance first.

### A wording the sources would bear (my suggestion for the dossier, not a finding)

P2 could read: 'What stays with a holder that performs through others depends on the duty. Where the holder undertook to provide a service, or holds a person in its care in the sense of *Woodland*, it answers for the delegate's want of care. Where it is an occupier or an employer of a contractor and no more, it must choose with reasonable care and act on what it knows.' P3 could then be put as the paper's step in these terms: 'English law makes the second kind of holder's duty turn on what it knows or has reason to suspect (*Ferguson* p. 7; *Naylor* para 50; *Maguire* para 30) and does not require it to arrange to be told. A host that removes every route by which a guest could tell it has put itself where the law's own trigger cannot operate. The paper argues that the duty should not be dischargeable in that way.' That keeps the legal claim within the cases and marks the normative step as the authors'.

### Limits of this read

- I did not read *Woodland*, *Gwilliam*, *Bottomley*, *MacLenan v Segar*, *Wilson v Best Travel*, *Wall v Silver Wing*, *Rogers v Night Riders*, *Craven*, *BXB* or *Kuoni*. Each is mentioned only as a judgment I read describes it, or from an existing card.
- Later treatment: checked only within this list (*Barclays* on *Armes*; *Hughes* on *Barclays*, *Armes* and *Woodland*). Otherwise not checked.
- The second stage of vicarious liability (the connection between the work and the tort) is outside these texts except for one sentence in *Barclays* para 1.
- The innkeeper's liability for goods, s. 5(3) of the 1957 Act and the Act's own text were not on my list.
- *Wong Mee Wan* is a Hong Kong appeal to the Privy Council; *Ferguson*'s claimant was a contractor's workman; *Armes*'s claimant was a child in compulsory care. None of the seven cases decides a hotel's liability to a guest. *Maguire* para 21 is the only passage in the list that mentions an innkeeper, and *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 7-8 describes two claims against tour operators about hotels abroad.
