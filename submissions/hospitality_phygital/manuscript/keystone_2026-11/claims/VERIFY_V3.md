## Register A3 ('The host's power and the host's obligation', S-061 to S-094)

Rows taken. Rule 1: S-065.1, S-092.1. Rule 4: S-061.2, S-064.3, S-071.2, S-076.1, S-081.2, S-087.3, S-091.2. Rule 5: S-061.1, S-078.1, S-091.1. Dispute (Hotel Proprietors Act 1956, s. 1): S-077.1, S-077.2, S-083.2, with S-078.1 and S-092.1. Found under rule 3: S-076.3.

### The Act, from its text (legislation.gov.uk, revised, saved at `hpa1956/`)

- **s. 1(1):** 'An hotel within the meaning of this Act shall, and any other establishment shall not, be deemed to be an inn; and the duties, liabilities and rights which immediately before the commencement of this Act by law attached to an innkeeper as such shall, subject to the provisions of this Act, attach to the proprietor of such an hotel and shall not attach to any other person.'
- **s. 1(3):** 'In this Act, the expression "hotel" means an establishment held out by the proprietor as offering food, drink and, if so required, sleeping accommodation, without special contract, to any traveller presenting himself who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum for the services and facilities provided and who is in a fit state to be received.'
- **Long title:** 'An Act to amend the law relating to inns and innkeepers.' **Schedule:** 'This notice does not constitute an admission either that the Act applies to this hotel or that liability thereunder attaches to the proprietor of this hotel in any particular case.' **s. 2(1):** 'Without prejudice to any other liability incurred by him'.

**Does the Act deem a hotel an inn?** It deems 'an hotel within the meaning of this Act' an inn, and every other establishment not one. The defined hotel is an establishment held out 'without special contract' to 'any traveller presenting himself'. The Act does not define 'proprietor'.

**Does naming a brand or an operating company contradict the Act?** Naming an operating company goes beyond the Act and does not contradict it: the company that holds the house out may be the proprietor, and the Act is silent on who that is. Naming a brand that neither owns nor runs the house contradicts the Act only if the brand is said to hold the innkeeper's duties 'as such' beside the proprietor, since those 'shall not attach to any other person'. For every other duty (occupier's, contractual, negligence) the Act says nothing either way. The sentence at S-092 lists alternatives with 'or'; the concurrent claim comes from the Conclusion ('held alongside whoever controls the operation').

### *Woodland v Essex County Council* [2013] UKSC 66, from its text

- **para 5:** 'The law does not in the ordinary course impose personal (as opposed to vicarious) liability for what others do or fail to do. … The expression "non-delegable duty" has become the conventional way of describing those cases in which the ordinary principle is displaced'.
- **para 7:** three characteristics, then 'The work required to perform such a duty may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's. … The contracting party will normally be taken to contract that the work will be done carefully by whomever he may get to do it'.
- **para 12:** 'It does not, however, follow from the mere existence of a positive duty that it is personal to the defendant so as to make it non-delegable. … the relevant factors are the vulnerability of the claimant, the existence of a relationship … by virtue of which the latter has a degree of protective custody over him, and the delegation of that custody to another person.'
- **para 22:** 'Non-delegable duties of care are inconsistent with the fault-based principles on which the law of negligence is based, and are therefore exceptional.'
- **para 23:** five features: (1) 'a patient or a child, or for some other reason is especially vulnerable or dependent … Other examples are likely to be prisoners and residents in care homes'; (2) a relationship 'which places the claimant in the actual custody, charge or care of the defendant', with 'an element of control over the claimant'; (3) no control by the claimant over how the defendant performs; (4) delegation of 'the defendant's custody or care of the claimant and the element of control that goes with it'; (5) negligence 'in the performance of the very function assumed'.
- **para 25(3):** 'They are liable for the negligence of independent contractors only if and so far as the latter are performing functions which the school has assumed for itself a duty to perform … In the absence of negligence of their own, for example in the selection of contractors, they will not be liable for the negligence of independent contractors where on analysis their own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance.'
- **para 25(5):** 'The responsibilities of fee-paying schools are already non-delegable because they are contractual, and the possibility of contracting out of them is limited by legislation.'

**The criteria.** The court calls the category exceptional (paras 5, 22). **The arranger** in para 25(3) is the school or education authority: the defendant that deals with the claimant and engages a contractor. The contractor is the performer. **Fee-paying schools:** their responsibilities are non-delegable because contractual, within the limits legislation sets on contracting out; a dictum, the fifth of six reasons. **An adult hotel guest** does not meet feature (1) on the court's examples, is not in the hotel's 'custody, charge or care' under (2), and so has no custody to be delegated under (4); (3) is met; (5) depends on the facts. *Hughes v Rattan* [2022] EWCA Civ 107 (two passages only) calls the five 'cumulative factors' and records the trial judge's view that an adult met the first because she was a dental patient (para 54); it does not reach customers at large.

### *Page v. Sloan*, 281 N.C. 697 (1972), read whole

- **Court and posture.** Supreme Court of North Carolina, 31 July 1972, Huskins J., on certiorari to the Court of Appeals, which had reversed summary judgment for the defendants. Affirmed. The defendants owned and operated the motel; the deceased was 'accepted as a paying guest'; a water heater repaired by a licensed plumber exploded.
- **What the court decided.** 'We hold that defendants have failed to carry the movant's burden of proof' (at 706). Liability was not decided.
- **What it said of the innkeeper's duties (at 702).** 'An innkeeper is not an insurer of the personal safety of his guests. He is required to exercise due care to keep his premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn his guests of any hidden peril. … The duties thus imposed upon an innkeeper for the protection of his guests "are nondelegable, and liability cannot be avoided on the ground that their performance was entrusted to an independent contractor." 40 Am. Jur. 2d, Hotels, Motels and Restaurants § 81. Compare the English rule'. And: 'The rule of nondelegability is grounded on the premise that an innkeeper's duty to use due care for the safety of his guests is a responsibility so important to the public that he should not be permitted to transfer it to another.'
- **On what authority.** A legal encyclopedia; *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 425, as the court quotes it: 'One who employs an independent contractor to maintain in safe condition land which he holds open to the entry of the public as his place of business … is subject to the same liability for physical harm caused by the contractor's negligent failure to maintain the land … as though he had retained its maintenance in his own hands', with its second illustration (a hotel, a plumber, a scalded guest, at 702–703); and elevator cases from New York, California, Texas and the Eighth Circuit. *Barnes v. Hotel Corp.* is cited for the standard of care only. No North Carolina decision is cited for non-delegability.
- **Its place in the reasoning (703–704).** 'depending on the evidence offered at the trial, defendants in this case could be liable on any of the following bases': (1) careless choice of contractor; '(2) Since the duties imposed upon an innkeeper for the protection of his guests are nondelegable … defendants would be subject to the same liability for an injury or death caused by the plumber's negligent failure properly to repair … as if they had made the repairs themselves' (at 704); (3) *res ipsa loquitur*. Among the open questions of fact the court lists 'Was the plumber negligent in making the repairs?' (at 708), which matters only on basis (2). The rule is therefore one of three alternative grounds on which summary judgment was held improper.
- **Limits.** Due care, not insurance. Premises kept 'in a reasonably safe condition' and warning of 'hidden peril': the physical state of the place and work done on it. The contractor must have been negligent. The rule the court quotes (§ 425) covers anyone who holds land open to the public as a place of business, so it marks shops as much as inns. Owner-operators; no brand, no operating company, no system. Nothing on crime by third parties, on services, or on decisions about a guest. Later treatment unchecked; the Fourth Circuit in 1988 described the motel's duty as any landowner's to an invitee without mentioning it.

### *Woodland* para 24, from its text

'This is undoubtedly a fundamental feature of those cases where, in the absence of a relevant antecedent relationship, the defendant has been held liable for inherently hazardous operations or dangers on the public highway. But I respectfully disagree with the view that control of the environment in which injury is caused is an essential element in the kind of case with which we are presently concerned. The defendant is not usually in control of the environment in which injury is caused by an independent contractor. That is why as a general rule he is not liable for the contractor's negligence. Where a non-delegable duty arises, the defendant is liable not because he has control but in spite of the fact that he may have none. The essential element in my view is not control of the environment in which the claimant is injured, but control over the claimant for the purpose of performing a function for which the defendant has assumed responsibility.'

**Support or undercut?** The paragraph undercuts the draft's use of it. It marks Lord Sumption's second category by control over the claimant, which para 23 glosses as custody of the vulnerable; a hotel controls premises, not an adult guest. Its three examples are defendants who escaped because they only arranged a service or had no custody. It says nothing against a hotel's ordinary duty of care, which no one disputes; it denies only that the duty is non-delegable on this ground.

**A correction to the register.** The auditor writes that 'By para 24 a defendant who controls only the environment "as a general rule… is not liable for the contractor's negligence".' The paragraph says the reverse of that gloss: the general rule of non-liability holds *because* the defendant usually lacks control of the environment, and control of the environment is 'a fundamental feature' of the hazard and highway cases, where non-delegable duties do arise. For an occupier, the rule that lets the holder escape is the statute's, not para 24's: 'the occupier is not to be treated without more as answerable for the danger if in all the circumstances he had acted reasonably in entrusting the work to an independent contractor and had taken such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought in order to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work had been properly done' (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, s. 2(4)(b)).

### Kong (2026), p. 4, from its text

'This also explains why the role-based examples above are not free-floating. A role matters derivatively rather than intrinsically: it bundles competence, control, and epistemic access, and assuming it is itself an exercise of control. The captain answers for the grounding because the role she voluntarily assumed conferred authority over navigation, the competence to exercise it, and epistemic access to the standing risks of the voyage — not because "captain" is a normatively magic word. What the role contributes is a specification of the domain over which control and knowledge are exercised, and thereby of the outcomes that are hers to explain [14]. Roles are not an alternative to control-based responsibility; they are how it is distributed across a division of labour.'

**Does the manuscript answer it?** No. The draft cites Kong at pp. 4–5 and 10, agrees with his conclusion, and says its duty 'has a different shape'. It nowhere mentions that Kong makes roles derivative of control, and its next sentence grounds its own duty in a control criterion taken from *Woodland*. Kong's sentence on what a role contributes is, as the auditor says, a source for K3's own position (the role specifies).
