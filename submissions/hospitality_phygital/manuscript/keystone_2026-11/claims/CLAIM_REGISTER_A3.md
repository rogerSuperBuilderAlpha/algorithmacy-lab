Sources, all saved, none cited in v6. Lamond v Richard and the Gordon Hotels, Limited [1897] 1 QB 541, Court of Appeal, England: the company's hotel was a common inn and its 'proprietors were bound to take in every one that came' (545). Constantine v Imperial Hotels, Limited [1944] KB 693, KBD: 'The defendants conceded that they were innkeepers' (694) and were held in breach. Orchard v Bush & Co [1898] 2 QB 284. Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 70: inns were 'gradually replaced by large, corporately owned, professionally managed hotels' to which innkeeper law applied. Hotel Proprietors Act 1963 (Ireland) s. 1(1): the proprietor is 'the person carrying on the business of the hotel'.

### S-092 · ¶11
> The holder may be the owner of a small commercial home, the proprietor of a hotel, the company that runs it day to day, or the brand under which it trades.

| Row | Proposition | Type | Source, version, locator | Passage (from the source) | Classes failed | Verdict | Severity | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S-092.1 | The holder of the host's obligation may be a commercial home's owner, a hotel's proprietor, its operating company or its brand | Own inference, stated as fact | no source in the sentence; the ledger points to Di Domenico and Lynch 2007, the 1956 Act s. 1(1) and Crinkley | Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 s. 1(1): "shall … attach to the proprietor of such an hotel and shall not attach to any other person". - **Premises.** P1 (sourced, S-078.1): in Great Britain the innkeeper's duties as such attach to the proprietor and to no other person. P2 (sourced, another batch): owners of commercial homes host in person. P3 (sourced, another batch): in Crinkley the owners, the operating company and the franchisor were all liable to guests. P4 (assumption): to be liable to a guest on any doctrine is to hold the host's obligation. C: any of the four may be the holder.
- **Is it a contradiction with s. 1(1)?** It depends on what 'holder' and 'may be' mean, and the sentence does not say.
  - *No contradiction* if the sentence means that 'proprietor' is a position that different parties fill in different hotels: an owner who runs the house, an operating company, a brand that owns and runs its own hotels. The Act does not define 'proprietor', companies have been innkeepers (S-090.1), and the Irish Act and Lord Esher both point to whoever carries on the business. On this reading the list is badly formed, since 'the proprietor of a hotel' is the position and the other three are candidates for it.
  - *Contradiction* if the sentence means that the four may hold the innkeeper's obligation side by side, or that a party who does not carry on the business may hold it. A franchisor is 'any other person'. A commercial home that takes guests by arrangement is 'any other establishment' and is not an inn at all, so the Act removes the innkeeper's duties from its owner. The Conclusion's 'held alongside' takes this second reading.
- **What each source licenses.** *The 1956 Act:* one holder of the innkeeper's duties, liabilities and rights 'as such', in Great Britain; silence on who the proprietor is; silence on every other duty. *Crinkley* (4th Cir. 1988, a federal court applying North Carolina law, majority, with a partial dissent on damages): owners and operator were negligent as occupiers; the franchisor 'was found vicariously liable on the basis of apparent agency' (para 9), which needs a representation and the guest's reliance (para 50), and the court called the reliance evidence 'marginal'. It licenses several parties liable for a guest's safety on general tort doctrines. It does not make any of them the holder of an innkeeper's duty, and it makes the brand answer for the operator's negligence, not for a duty of its own. *Lamond, Constantine:* a company can be the innkeeper.
- **Terms.** 'Holder' is the bearer of the innkeeper's status in S-078 and S-084, and anyone a guest might sue in S-092. 'Holding out' also shifts between this section and the next: here it is the profession of a public calling to all travellers (s. 1(3); Lamond); in Crinkley it is a representation that the operator is the brand's agent ('he has been held out by the other to be so', para 49).
- **Counter-model.** A British hotel owned by A, run by B under a management contract, branded by C under a franchise. On the Act the innkeeper's duties attach to one of them and 'shall not attach' to the others. S-092 says any of the three may hold them.
- **K claim.** K8 ('This allocation is a proposal with partial legal support').

- **Premises (S-093.2).** P1 (sourced): the Act defines the hotel by the proprietor's holding out. P2 (sourced, leads): the common law did the same. 'An inn is a house, the owner of which holds out that he will receive all travellers and sojourners who are willing to pay' (Best J, as quoted by Kennedy J, Orchard v Bush at 288). Whether a hotel is so held out is 'a question of fact what was the intention of those who carried on the business' (Lamond at 545). By 'putting up a sign and lodging travelers, an individual became liable' (Bogen 1996: 88, reporting a case of 1623 that I did not read). C: holding out is how the role is taken up.

Two consequences are left unstated. First, a host can decline the role by not holding out in that way (Lord Esher's London hotels; the Schedule's disclaimer; see S-083.2), so 'whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers' is narrower than whoever receives travellers. This is the weakness K3 predicts for assumption: it can be avoided by notice. Second, Bogen's objection stands against any use of holding out as the reason the duty binds (S-080.2).
- **K claim.** K3.
