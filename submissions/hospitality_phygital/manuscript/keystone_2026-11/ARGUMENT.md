# The duty of the host: the argument (draft 2)

## The answer

A commercial host owes each guest it receives, for the length of the stay, reasonable care for the guest's safety and, in the services it undertook, performance with reasonable care. A host the law treats as an innkeeper owes two further things: safe keeping of the guest's goods, and, before the stay begins, the duty to receive. Every host that serves the public in Great Britain must also not refuse a guest on a protected ground. The duty binds for reasons that do different work:

- **Holding out** settles that this party took the role up, and from when.
- **The guest's reliance on the host** settles that the duty is owed to this guest.
- **The host's control of the space the guest is in** settles that the duty is owed to this guest, and that no notice can shed it.

When others do the work, what stays with the host depends on the doctrine. Where the host undertook to provide a service, it answers for the delegate's want of care. Where it only occupies premises or employs a contractor, it must choose with care, and it must act on what it knows. Several parties may hold the care duty at once, each to its degree of control.

The paper's own step concerns the stay itself. Once the guest is inside, the guest is subject to the host's ordering of the space, and leaving has a cost. When the host performs through others and through systems, the guest's claim that the host perform can be met only if a failure in the guest's case reaches someone able to correct it while correction can still help. The guest's claim therefore justifies a further duty, owed to the guest: to keep, during the stay, some timely means of correction, including a route from the guest wherever only the guest perceives the failure. The route answers to the guest's position under the host's power. It is not the firm's quality control. This step is argued in the moral register. The law reaches part of it by another road: it forbids a host to make a consumer guest take onerous steps to exercise rights during the stay, and it requires reasonable adjustments for disabled guests. No law read requires a route to someone with power to correct.

## How to read this file

- **Registers.** Every step is labelled customary, moral or legal. No step proves a duty in one register and spends it in another. Where two registers reach the same requirement, each has its own warrant.
- **What each discipline supplies.** Hospitality scholarship supplies the roles, the setting, the protective content of the host's duty, and the host's power over the guest once inside. Moral philosophy supplies the grounds of the duty and the paper's own step. Law tests what is enforced, narrows the content and offers analogues. It is not the source of the duty's content.
- **Statuses**, as `CLAIMS_FIXED.md` defines them: holds; holds, narrowed; holds in a named jurisdiction; the paper's own step; fails; open.

## Step 1. What hosting asks of the host (customary register, and scholarly prescription; K1 and K2)

**Claim.** In the anthropology and history of hospitality, the host of a guest-friend answers for the guest's protection. For commercial hosts, a line of hospitality scholarship prescribes the same responsibility. That prescription does not describe the trade's practice. The duty attaches to the role of host; hospitableness is the manner and motive with which the role is performed. **Status: holds, narrowed.**

**Warrant.**

1. *Guest-friendship.* O'Gorman's history records duties to receive the guest, to protect him and to represent him in court (`ogorman2005modern`, pp. 144-145, resting on Schmitz 1875: 621). His article on commercial hospitality in Rome records the same duties for private hospitality only (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 45).
2. *Prescription for business.* Morrison and O'Gorman list among business principles that 'Businesses have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests' (`morrison2008hospitality`, Fig. 2). They derived the list from the Rule of St Benedict and contemporaneous monastic practice (AM p. 6). Hemmington makes 'the security of strangers' a dimension of commercial hospitality (`hemmington2007`, abstract; AV p. 15). His businesses take responsibility 'for the experience' (AV p. 16), not, in those words, for the guest. Telfer, as Hemmington quotes her, calls the guest's safety 'traditionally the most important responsibility of all' (Telfer 2000, as quoted in `hemmington2007`, AV p. 15).
3. *The classical code.* Pitt-Rivers lists among a host's infringements a failure 'to attend to his guests, to grant them the precedence which is their due, to show concern for their needs and wishes' (`pittrivers2012law`, p. 516). A guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's right to ordain (p. 515). Pitt-Rivers states this as an abstract law of hospitality whose 'detail varies of course from place to place' (pp. 513, 515). It does not fix content that a commercial duty could inherit.
4. *Role and manner.* Benveniste finds separate words for the one received and the one who receives (`benveniste1973indoeuropean`, ch. 7, para. 34). Telfer, as quoted, calls the paid provider 'a commercial host', and makes 'hospitable' depend on his motive and his prices (Telfer 2000: 45, as quoted in `slattery2002finding`, p. 21). As quoted, she counts 'sense of duty where there is also concern for the guests themselves' among hospitable motives (Telfer 1996: 82, as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 6). Duty and hospitableness are not opposed in her text. The opposition is Blain and Lashley's (`blain2014hospitableness`, p. 2).
5. *Derrida, both sentences of p. 77.* As quoted, the plural laws of hospitality are 'those rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional' (*Of Hospitality*: 77, as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 8). In marking 'limits, power, rights, and duties', the same laws consist 'in challenging and transgressing the law of hospitality, the one that would command that the "new arrival" be offered an unconditional welcome' (p. 77, as quoted in `westmoreland2008interruptions`, p. 8). Kekstaite paraphrases the second sentence: laws 'which set limits, mark powers, rights, and duties' (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM pp. 3-4).

**What this licenses.** The paper's duty belongs to the conditional laws of hospitality. Those laws mark the host's power and set limits on the welcome. The paper may not claim the unconditional law for its duty. It may use that law as the measure the conditional laws fall short of, and that is how Step 6 uses it.

**The counter-history, given its due.** Commercial lodging served those whom guest-friendship did not reach: 'the lower classes who were too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality' (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 47; `ogorman2005modern`, p. 146). Roman inns had a reputation for 'violence and danger' (`ogorman2005modern`, p. 146). Pomponius, as Ulpian reports him, explains the praetor's edict on innkeepers as showing the praetor's care 'reprimendae improbitatis hoc genus hominum', to curb the dishonesty of this kind of men (my gloss; `digest4_9`, D. 4.9.3.1). On this history the commercial host is where hospitality's protections lapsed and law stepped in. O'Gorman's Rome paper supports the first half of that history only. It names no innkeeper's duty and brings in law only to protect serving women (`ogorman2007discovering`, pp. 47-48). The 'law stepped in' half rests on the Digest.

**Narrowing.**

- The management definitions of the 1980s and 1990s describe an exchange and name no duty (Brotherton and Wood 2000, as quoted in `hemmington2007`, AV pp. 5-6).
- Hemmington reports that the trade overlooks the guest's safety (AV p. 15).
- The oldest content of the obligation is reciprocity between parties of equal standing, which 'had lost its force in the Roman world' (`benveniste1973indoeuropean`, ch. 7, paras 31, 33). The paper uses Benveniste only for the distinctness of the roles.
- Kant's right of hospitality is 'not a right to be treated as a guest' (`kant1795perpetual`, pp. 137-138).
- **Welfare.** K1 was fixed as 'safety and welfare'. The commercial sources state safety; Telfer's 'welfare' is a motive of the hospitable, not a duty of the host (as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 6). Step 6 states what follows for the guest whose welfare is at risk.

**The gap, restated against this journal's literature.** The journal's founding editorial names 'hospitality as social control' (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 3) and, after Robinson and Lynch (2007), 'micro-hosts' and 'macro-hosts' (PDF p. 8). Bulley names 'an assembled host' (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 13). Petie et al. describe a web of hosts in which 'it is the Home Office who are paying customers' and guests 'did not know where to turn for help' (`petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 4, 11). Germann Molz reports that on platforms 'responsibilities can be shifted elsewhere or even avoided' (`germannmolz2018scale`, p. 233, reporting Dredge). The literature thus names divided hosting four ways and allocates no duty in any of them. R9 found no passage, in the seven texts it read whole, that gives the guest a route, kept by the host, to someone able to correct the host's conduct during the stay (`WS_P1_R9.md`, (a), (b)). The paper's gap is that question, not the host's duty as such, which Morrison and O'Gorman already assign to businesses. Two closed neighbours must be read before the gap is stated in print: Dogru et al. (2025) and Koo and Kwon (2026) (abstracts only: `dogru2025service`, `koo2026three`).

## Step 2. What the law asks of the host (legal register; K4)

**Claim.** In England and Wales, Ireland and the United States jurisdictions named, a commercial host's legal duties fall in two tiers.

- **Admission.** An innkeeper owes a general duty to receive travellers. In Great Britain, every service-provider owes a partial duty not to refuse on a protected ground.
- **Reception.** A host that receives a guest owes reasonable care for the guest's person. A trader that supplies services to a consumer owes reasonable care and skill in performing them. An innkeeper also owes strict, capped liability for goods.

**Status: holds, narrowed, in the named jurisdictions.**

**Warrant.**

- *Admission, general.* A refused traveller had an action against the innkeeper (Blackstone, 3 Comm. 164). Birkett J: 'His right, I think, is founded on the common law. That right I found was violated' (`constantine1944imperial`, p. 708; 'a right at common law' is the headnote's phrase, p. 693). The duty now attaches to the proprietor of a hotel as the 1956 Act defines it (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1). Ireland enacts it more widely (`ie1963hotel`, s. 3(1)). In Kansas, the common-law duty 'may have been dormant or stale' but 'has never been abrogated' (`thomas1955pick`, p. 666).
- *Admission, partial.* A service-provider 'must not discriminate against a person requiring the service by not providing the person with the service' (`uk2010equality`, s. 29(1)). This reaches restaurants, bars, rental hosts and platforms, and the guest can sue (s. 114(1)(a)).
- *The person.* A hotel owes its staying guests reasonable care against third parties' crimes; the hotel admitted a general duty, and the guests lost on breach (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 187, 240; `alnajar2020cumberland`). The management of a nightclub owes its guests a duty of care (`everett2011comojo`, para 31). Colorado holds that a hotel ending a stay owes reasonable care (`groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 4, 51), and Iowa that a tavern owes it to its patrons (`hoyt2013gutterz`, pp. 776-777). The duty is not insurance (`maclenan1917segar`, p. 328). Ireland enacts it, 'independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier' (`ie1963hotel`, s. 4).
- *Services.* For a contract under which a trader supplies a service to a consumer, the trader must perform 'with reasonable care and skill', and cannot exclude that duty (`uk2015consumer`, ss. 48(1), 49, 57). The consumer may require repeat performance (s. 55). This reaches consumers dealing with traders only (s. 2(2)-(3)). The business traveller, the guest whose room another party booked, and the companion who did not contract fall outside it.
- *Goods.* Strict liability, capped, for a guest with sleeping accommodation engaged (`uk1956hotel`, s. 2). The same structure appears in Roman law, the German code and the 1962 convention (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.3.1; `bgb701`, §§ 701-702; `coe1962hotelkeepers`, Annex arts 1, 4).
- *Adjustment.* A service-provider must make reasonable adjustments for disabled people, including an auxiliary aid or service and information 'in an accessible format' (`uk2010equality`, s. 20(3)-(6); Sch. 2 para. 2).

**Narrowing.**

- *The general admission duty is not universal.* Ulpian says innkeepers are free to receive no one (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.1.1), and the German code and the convention impose no such duty (`bgb701`; `coe1962hotelkeepers`). A refreshment bar 'entirely separate' from the hotel, 'with a separate entrance', was 'a mere shop' (`rymer1877queen`, pp. 139-140). Colorado finds no special relationship for taverns and restaurants (`groh2015westin`, n. 7).
- *No modern English guest has won.* The duty may be cited; no modern English judgment holding a hotel or bar liable for a guest's injury was read.
- *Attending.* Restatement (Second) § 314A, as quoted, puts an innkeeper under a duty of first aid once it 'knows or has reason to know' a guest is ill or injured (as quoted in `rasnick2011krishna`, p. 568). *Groh* records that § 314A was replaced in 2012, and *Hoyt* applies Restatement (Third) § 40 (`groh2015westin`, n. 4; `hoyt2013gutterz`, pp. 775-776). For package holidays, the Supreme Court gave 'the service of looking after and serving holidaymakers courteously in matters relating to their holiday experience' as an example of a service inherent in every such contract (`kuoni2021uksc`, para 30). Attending survives as a customary duty only for attention beyond safety, aid and the services sold.
- *v6 is wrong on one point.* The exceptions in present law do not all run in the host's favour (`uk2010equality`, ss. 20-21; `uk1956hotel`, s. 2(3)). Flagged for the authors.

## Step 3. Why the duty binds (moral register, with the law's rationales beside it; K3)

**Claim.** The role of host specifies the content, the trigger and, in the core case, the holder of the duty. Three grounds outside the role make it bind, and they do different work:

- **Voluntary assumption.** Holding out fixes that this party took the role up, and from when.
- **Invited reliance.** The guest relies on the host, which explains why the duty is owed to this guest.
- **Control of the space the guest is in.** This explains why the duty runs to this guest and why a notice cannot exhaust it.

Control together with reliance suffices for the duty of care for the person. The other duties need the division of labour. **Status: holds, narrowed. Whether a role binds by itself stays open** (Hardimon 1994 and Simmons 1996 are closed).

**Warrant.**

- *The role specifies.* Critics of associative obligation hold that role duties rest on 'a moral principle external to it' (`dagger2021political`, §4.3, para. 8, reporting Dagger, Wellman and Simmons). Jeske argues that talk of a role's obligations may record 'merely others' expectations of persons holding such positions' (`jeske2019special`, §4, para. 10). Blackstone implies the innkeeper's undertaking only against one whose 'common profession' it is (3 Comm. 164). Dingemans J reaches the hotel's duty to the person through negligence, not innkeeper law (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 183-187).
- *Assumption.* Blackstone: an innkeeper who 'hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers' makes 'an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way' (3 Comm. 164). The 1956 Act's hotel is an establishment 'held out by the proprietor' (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(3)). In *Al-Najar*, 'the hotel invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests' (para 187).
- *Reliance.* The nightclub guest 'relies on the competence and prudence of its management' and 'is entitled to expect that there will be no violence' (`everett2011comojo`, para 31). Neither English court asked whether these guests in fact relied.
- *Control.* 'The management is in control of the premises. It can regulate who enters' (`everett2011comojo`, para 31). Colorado and Iowa describe a 'special relationship' that leaves the host 'in a superior position to protect' the guest (`groh2015westin`, ¶ 29; `hoyt2013gutterz`, p. 777, quoting Restatement (Third) § 40, comment). In occupiers' law, 'The foundation of occupier's liability is occupational control' (`wheat1966lacon`, p. 16, Lord Pearson).

**Why the division of labour.** Assumption binds a host only to what it undertook (`jeske2019special`, §4, para. 2). Reliance binds only toward those who relied, and only to the extent of what they were led to expect (`habib2022promises`, §§3.3, 5.1). Control, unlike bare dependence, does pick out the host as host. With reliance, it carries the care duty for the person whatever the host undertook. That is why the law can forbid exclusion of liability for negligent death or injury (`uk2015consumer`, s. 65) without relying on the host's consent. The law's rationales sit beside the moral grounds and do not prove them. The courts also give other rationales: crime control, the purpose of inns, benefit to the host, policy (`maclenan1917segar`, p. 329; `rymer1877queen`, pp. 140-141; `everett2011comojo`, para 31; `groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 25, 33-37).

**Objections.**

1. *Disabling, not grounding* (`dagger2021political`, §4.3, para. 9). **Reply.** The argument needs assumption only to fix the party and the moment, and an enabling condition does that as well as a ground does. The associativists' positive claim, that 'the good realized in the relationship' justifies the claims (para. 9), is compatible with the division of labour: that good is what reliance and control protect.
2. *Fair play is left out.* **Conceded.** Fair play would ground a duty owed to other hosts, not to guests (`dagger2021political`, §4.2, para. 1). It is dropped.
3. *Dependence did not bind in Georgia* (`rasnick2011krishna`, pp. 569-570). **Conceded for Georgia law on a risk internal to the guest.** Step 6 relies on that limit.

**The ground by hard case.** 'Control' carries three senses in what follows, and each is named where used:

- **(c1) control of premises and who enters** (`everett2011comojo`, para 31);
- **(c2) operational control of the business** (`crinkley1988holiday`, para 7; `billops1978magness`, pp. 197-198);
- **(c3) decisive influence over the conditions of supply.** This is a test for classifying a platform's service for market access, not for responsibility to a customer (`uber2017cjeu`, paras 39-40).

## Step 4. To whom the duty is owed (legal register, and the customary code; K5 and K6)

**Claim.** The legal duties are owed to the guest, who can sue on them. The duty to receive was also public, enforced by indictment. In the customary code the host's failures wrong the guest, while the guest may not demand. The argument does not depend on whether holding a claim includes standing to demand. **Status: K5 holds, narrowed; K6 holds.**

**Warrant.**

- *Owed to the guest.* *Constantine* (p. 708), *Al-Najar* (para 7) and *Everett* (paras 1-2) all establish it. In Kansas, the contract is 'usually treated' as establishing a relationship that creates 'a common law relational duty on the part of the innkeeper, the breach of which is redressible in tort' (`thomas1955pick`, p. 665). The 1956 Act's s. 1(2) concerns only damage to a guest's property.
- *Public as well.* Refusal was indictable (`rymer1877queen`, pp. 136, 139; Blackstone, 4 Comm. 168). In Ireland, breach of the duty to receive is an offence today, 'without prejudice to his civil liability' (`ie1963hotel`, s. 12). The two routes do not conflict. The traveller's action lay 'upon this universal assumpsit', in implied contract (3 Comm. 164). Blackstone's rule that common nuisances are 'indictable only, and not actionable' concerns suits for the nuisance itself (4 Comm. 167).
- *The customary code.* A host who fails 'to attend to his guests' or 'to show concern for their needs and wishes' infringes the law of hospitality. 'Failure to offer the best is to denigrate the guest'. The guest may not 'make claims or demands' (`pittrivers2012law`, pp. 515-516). The safe wording commits only to direction in the sense of wronging (`WS_P3_R3.md`, §3).
- *Standing.* On the standard definition, 'A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ' (`wenar2025rights`, §2.1.2). Theories divide on whether a claim includes the standing to demand it (§2.2.2; `herstein2023legal`, §4). They agree on the ordinary case of a competent adult owed an un-overridden duty (`sreenivasan2019rights`; `dekenessey2024two`, EV pp. 2, 14).

**The direction of a channel, in law.** Every channel the law creates is either the guest's own remedial right (CRA s. 55; package regs 15(4) and 17; DSA arts 12, 20, made actionable by art. 54) or a duty owed to the state. The contact point required by UK package law is outside the implied terms, and breach gives no civil action 'by reason only of the commission of an offence' (`uk2018package`, regs 7(10), 7(12), 35). Step 6 keeps this in view.

## Step 5. What stays with the holder, and several holders (legal register; K8)

**Claim.** What stays with a holder that performs through others depends on the duty and the doctrine.

| Duty | What stays with the holder |
|---|---|
| Goods (innkeeper) | Strict liability, capped |
| Services the holder undertook to provide | The holder answers for the delegate's want of care |
| The person, where the holder is an occupier or employer of a contractor and no more | The holder must choose with reasonable care, and act on what it knows or has reason to suspect |

Several parties may hold the care duty at once, each to its degree of control, with contribution between them. **Status: holds, narrowed, for England.** Ireland is open. The allocation of other duties among firms is a proposal.

**Warrant.**

- *The starting point.* The employer of an independent contractor is in general not liable for the contractor's torts (`barclays2020uksc`, paras 7, 24, 27).
- *Occupiers.* The statutory defence covers 'faulty execution of any work of construction, maintenance or repair' entrusted to a contractor, if the occupier took 'such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought' to satisfy itself of the contractor's competence and the work (`uk1957occupiers`, s. 2(4)(b)). It does not reach outsourced security, cleaning or software. At common law the employer of a competent contractor is ordinarily discharged by reasonable selection (`ferguson1987welsh`, p. 7; `naylor2004payling`, para 19; `maguire2006sefton`, para 30). Where the occupier 'knows or has reason to suspect' an unsafe system, Lord Keith said only that 'it might well be reasonable' to act, a guarded dictum that Lord Goff did not join (`ferguson1987welsh`, pp. 7, 11). The Court of Appeal makes continued reliance turn on what the holder knew or had 'reason to contemplate' (`maguire2006sefton`, para 30; `naylor2004payling`, para 50).
- *Services.* A party that 'undertook to provide and not merely to arrange' answers for the services 'even if some of the services were to be rendered by others' (`wong1995kwan`, p. 10). The organiser of a package is responsible 'irrespective of whether those services are to be performed by the organiser or by other travel service providers' (`eu2015package`, art. 13(1)). The Supreme Court held a tour operator liable to a guest for a hotel employee's assault without vicarious liability (`kuoni2021uksc`, paras 45-50; decided under Directive 90/314 and the 1992 Regulations).
- *Non-delegable duties* are 'exceptional' (`armes2017uksc`, paras 31-32). No case read applies one to an adult guest in England. In North Carolina, *Page v Sloan* stated the innkeeper's premises duties to be nondelegable, as one of three bases for trial, on review of summary judgment (`page1972sloan`, pp. 702-704, 708).
- *Several holders.* 'Two or more may be "occupiers". And whenever this happens, each is under a duty to use care towards persons coming lawfully on to the premises, dependent on his degree of control', with 'a claim to contribution from the other' (`wheat1966lacon`, p. 9, Lord Denning). In that case the guest was a paying summer guest in rooms let in a brewery-owned inn (p. 8). In *Crinkley* the jury found against the owners, the operator and the brand together (`crinkley1988holiday`, para 9). The 1956 Act attaches the innkeeper's duties to the proprietor 'and shall not attach to any other person' (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(1)), and the Irish Act defines the proprietor as 'the person carrying on the business of the hotel' (`ie1963hotel`, s. 1(1)). Who that is under a management contract is a question for a source not read.
- *Ireland.* The 1963 Act's duty 'to ensure' that the premises are 'as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them' is 'independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier' (`ie1963hotel`, s. 4). Whether that duty makes the proprietor answer for a contractor is open on the words, and no Irish judgment was read.

**Where this departs from v6.** v6 said that the work can be delegated and the duty cannot. The table replaces it.

## Step 6. The duty during the stay, when the host performs through others and through systems (moral register, with a separate legal correlate; K7)

This is the keystone. It is argued in the moral register. The legal correlate is a separate subsection with its own warrant.

### 6.1 The premises

**P1. The host owes the guest performance during the stay.** In the moral register, the host owes the guest care for the guest's person and performance of what it undertook. Assumption, reliance and control ground these duties (Step 3), and their content is the protective content of Step 1. The legal duties of Step 2 are their enforced counterpart, not their proof. **Status: holds** (on Step 3).

**P2. When others do the work, the duty stays where its ground stays.** A duty grounded in the host's undertaking stays with the host on delegation. Brandt: on delegation, 'the individual initially bound by the responsibility still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled' (`brandt2017transfer`, ms p. 3), because an arrangement with a third party cannot alter what was agreed with the party owed (ms p. 10). A duty grounded in control sits with whoever controls the guest's space, and several may share it (`wheat1966lacon`, p. 9). Brandt's own delegator is reactive: he must step in when 'it becomes clear' that the delegate is failing (ms p. 5), and he treats a mechanism for finding out as optional (ms p. 15). **Status: holds, narrowed:** the Brandt form is confined to undertaken duties.

**P3 (the paper's step). The guest's claim to performance justifies a further duty as the facts change.** Herstein reports, for legal rights, that 'the same claim-right seemingly can count in favor of different and changing duties, for instance given changes in factual circumstances', and that rights justify 'new previously unrecognized duties' (`herstein2023legal`, §8.4, paras 1-2, reporting Raz). The changed facts here are delegation and automation. Once the host performs through others and systems, whether the guest's claim is met in the guest's own case depends on the host's learning of a failure while it can still correct it. **Status: the paper's own step.** The entry files the section among features 'unique or particularly salient to legal rights' (`herstein2023legal`, preamble), though §8.4 itself speaks of 'rights' and 'claim-right' without qualification, citing Raz 1986. Its extension to the guest's moral claim rests on Raz (1986, ch. 7), which is closed in this run. The view that such 'dynamic' rights are 'only inchoate rights' is a published reply (§8.4, para. 2, reporting Kramer).

**P4 (empirical). For a failure in the guest's own case, the guest's report is the main route by which it reaches the host.** **Status: holds, narrowed, on thin evidence.**

- *For.* The Helsinki hotel 'relies mainly on customer feedback concerning service failures' (`schumacher2016case`, p. 121). At automated hotels, each of the 13 guests whose identity, reservation or kiosk failed went to find a worker, who checked them in by hand (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5035).
- *Against, first in weight.* Guests who feel low in power complain less, 'reducing an opportunity for the service provider to hear from customers' (`min2020angry`, p. 2). Negative word of mouth can come 'prior to—or in lieu of—a complaint' (`gelbrich2011metaanalysis`, p. 28). A system-wide failure needs no report: when the kiosk was down, a worker met all 51 arriving guests (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5021).
- *Gap.* R8 found no open study that measures what share of dissatisfied guests report, or whether systems detect failures sooner than guests do (`WS_P6_R8.md`, Gaps).

**P5 (hosting-specific). During the stay the guest is inside the host's controlled space, subject to its ordering, and leaving has a cost.** This premise is what makes Step 6 about hosting.

- *The journal's own theme.* Hospitality's distinctions 'need to be policed, managed and controlled, even after the threshold of the home is crossed' (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 2). 'Power and control over the space and, to an extent, over the guest are necessary conditions to the offering of hospitality' (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 4-5). The founding editorial names 'hospitality as social control' (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 3). Pitt-Rivers: 'A host is host only on the territory over which on a particular occasion he claims authority' (`pittrivers2012law`, p. 514).
- *The cost of leaving.* An au pair: 'what if I complain about something and I make the things uncomfortable? Cause I have to be here, I cannot leave' (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 7). Placed guests are housed 'on a no-choice basis' (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 2). At the limit, the host's power is the power to expel (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 10).
- *The guest's effective complaint as the guest's power.* 'Knowing how to complain, and feeling that the complaint will be acted upon, are latent forms of power' (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12). Bulley's wager for 'better' forms is a welcome kept 'open once the threshold is passed' (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 18).

**Status: holds** as a description the journal's literature gives. The use the paper makes of it is its own.

### 6.2 The conclusion

**C. The host owes the guest a further duty, justified by the guest's claim to performance: to keep, during the stay, some timely means by which a failure in the guest's case reaches someone able to correct it. Where only the guest perceives the failure, the means must include a route from the guest that the guest can use without depending on the host's goodwill.**

*How C follows.*

- **Some timely means.** Performance in the guest's case cannot be secured without some timely means of learning of failures and correcting them. Taking none is therefore incompatible with performance. Kiesewetter's transmission principle holds that 'ought' 'transmits to necessary means' (`kiesewetter2015instrumental`, p. 922). The disjunction is a necessary means even where no single means is *(my inference, R11's also)*.
- **A route from the guest.** Where only the guest perceives the failure, a route from the guest is itself necessary (P4).
- **A route usable without the host's goodwill.** This follows from P5. Where the guest is subject to the host's power and cannot easily leave, a route that depends on the host's goodwill does not secure the guest's claim. It also explains why guests who feel low in power fall silent (`min2020angry`; `moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 7) *(my inference)*.
- **Not transmission to non-necessary means.** Discussing an agent's own likely failure, Kiesewetter says the likelihood 'could' supply 'second-order reasons to adopt additional measures' (p. 937). He does not rule out a further 'ought', and C does not need one: it asks only for the necessary means.

*The duty is owed to the guest.* It is justified by the guest's claim, and claims justify correlative duties (`herstein2023legal`, §8.4, para. 1). It is not derived through the host's own reasons for taking means. This is what separates C from Hindriks's 'duty of control', which is grounded in risk 'to others' and owed to no one in particular (`hindriks2025moral`, pp. 4-5), and from a service-recovery model that treats the guest's complaint as the firm's opportunity.

*Two wrongs, not one placed by luck.* A host that keeps no means of correction during the stay breaches the further duty and wrongs its guests, whether or not a failure occurs. A host whose correctable failure then goes uncorrected also breaches the duty to perform. Whether the first breach wrongs a guest who suffers no failure is the question Oberdiek's work on imposing risk would test (closed in this run).

*The time limit.* The duty runs for the stay, because P5's subjection runs for the stay. Within the stay, what the route carries is correction. Correction covers performance that is late and repair of a lapse the same day (`WS_P3_R3.md`, statement 45). After the stay, the guest's claims run to repair through other routes: complaint, and accountability before a forum (`fox2007uncertain`, p. 665, reporting Schedler on answerability).

**Status: the paper's own conclusion, in the moral register.**

### 6.3 Who must be at the end of the route

C asks for 'someone able to correct it', meaning power to depart from what the host's arrangements produced in the guest's case. This is neutral on whether that power sits in a person or in a further process.

1. A failure in the single case is one where what the arrangements produced for this guest falls short of what the duty requires.
2. If the arrangements as configured could produce what the duty requires for this guest, retrying cures the failure. At the automated hotels, failures of face recognition were cured mostly by the guest's own retrying (`xu2024disciplined`, pp. 5027, 5030).
3. Where retrying cannot cure it, correction needs authority to depart from the output in this case. The 13 guests the kiosk could not process were checked in by hand by a worker who could override it (p. 5035).
4. **Where the failure is of a documented type**, a reachable power to override is an identifiable precaution. Kong makes a failure foreseeable when its type was: an admissible type 'names a causal or dispositional mechanism', and admissible changes include 'a monitoring requirement' (`kong2026delegation`, p. 6). Identity checks and kiosks that fail for some guests are documented types.
5. **Where the failure is of no documented type**, Kong gives no support: the genuinely novel failure is, for him, 'the faultless accident', handled by no-fault compensation (p. 7). C is therefore claimed with confidence for documented types. For novel failures, the paper offers only its own reading: the single case that the host's arrangements did not foresee is where the conditional laws meet what exceeds them (Step 1, warrant 5). That reading uses Derrida as a measure, not as a source of duty.
6. **When a natural person is required.** C requires a natural person where no configured process covers the type of failure and the stakes warrant it. Legislatures that require power to override place it in natural persons, 'as appropriate and proportionate' (`eu2024aiact`, art. 14(4), chapeau and (d)). That law excludes the paradigm hotel system: biometric verification 'the sole purpose of which is to confirm' identity is outside Annex III (`eu2024aiact`, Annex III point 1(a); recital 17 gives the 'minor impact' reason).

### 6.4 What C requires of the person reached, of the holder, and of several holders

- **The person reached owes the guest care in using the power.** The power that can correct can also be turned against the guest. In *Groh*, a guard was told that the group could not leave because 'We are drunk. We can't drive', and the group was evicted (`groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 6-8). C does not govern how the power is used; the duty of care does. The power to expel is the same power that C routes the guest to.
- **The holder owes the workers at the end of the route real authority.** In the hotel studied, reception staff's discretion was unwritten, and they did not know where its limits fell (`schumacher2016case`, pp. 121-123). In the asylum hotels, staff told of a guest's deteriorating health the night before 'lacked the knowledge needed to act', and the inquiry saw them 'as victims of decisions made by management, rather than individually responsible' (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11). A route to someone without authority is not a route to 'someone able to correct'. C therefore requires the holder to give that person authority in writing, training, and protection from becoming the object the route disciplines *(my inference)*.
- **The holder must also learn across cases.** In the same hotels, the Government evaluation found that the queries a guest lodged 'were dealt with appropriately', yet 'cumulatively they should have acted as a warning sign', and that there was 'no communication or joined up approach between services' (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 12, citing Laing 2020). News reports on the same page say 'no actions were taken to help them'. On the evaluation's account, the single-case route worked and the aggregate failed. The UN Guiding Principles state the second function of a grievance channel: 'By analysing trends and patterns in complaints, business enterprises can also identify systemic problems and adapt their practices accordingly' (`un2011guiding`, Principle 29, Commentary). Hormio puts a forward-looking duty on an organisation 'to try to fix its design' once problems emerge (`hormio2018culpable`, pp. 20-21).
- **Several holders: one party routes** *(proposal)*. Where several firms host together, each answers for its own part (`kong2026delegation`, p. 9; `pettit2007responsibility`, p. 195, on why an unincorporated collection 'is not an agent'). No text read assigns one firm a duty to route a report about another firm's part (`WS_P3_R11.md`, (d)). The paper proposes that the party in control of the guest's space owes that routing duty, because it is where the guest is. One analogue exists: in *Wheat*, the occupiers on the spot 'should have reported it to the Brewery Company' (`wheat1966lacon`, p. 11).

### 6.5 What C does not claim, and whom it leaves out

- **No duty to monitor the guest.** C concerns failures of the host's own performance. *Rasnick* refused a duty to check on a guest's health for reasons of workability and privacy (pp. 569-570), and C does not reach that case. Attentiveness without a report is part of the care duty where the law follows Restatement § 314A or Third § 40 (Step 2).
- **The guest who cannot report.** C serves guests who can perceive and report. For a guest in crisis, or one without the language, the route is not enough. In Great Britain, a disabled guest is owed reasonable adjustments, including an auxiliary service and accessible information (`uk2010equality`, s. 20(5)-(6)). Beyond that, the guest who cannot report is outside what C secures. This is a stated limit.
- **The household host.** Where the person with power over the single case is the host in their own home, any report is a confrontation (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 7, 11). The platform's reputation system makes host and guest 'monitor one another' (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 236-237). C is not claimed for household hosts. The platform owes the channel duties the DSA sets for its own service (`eu2022dsa`, arts 12, 20).
- **The placed guest.** The placing body's duty is public law (`poole2019uksc`, para 65(2); `dma2020sshd`). C reaches the operator only through its care duty, as a proposal (Step 3 table).
- **Workers as guests of nobody.** The argument's generality is conceded where its premises hold: any relation in which a party performs through others for someone confined in its space (hospitals, schools, care homes). P5 is what keeps it from reaching every firm that delegates.
- **No particular technology, and no hearing.** A small host meets C with a telephone number that the proprietor answers. The person reached must be able to correct, not to adjudicate.
- **Cost.** C asks only for necessary means. What counts as a timely means scales with what correction can still achieve.

### 6.6 The knowledge doctrines

The law treats a party that suspects a specific fact and deliberately avoids confirming it as knowing it (`globaltech2011seb`, 563 U.S. at 769; `manifest2001unipolaris`, para 116). Both doctrines exclude negligence. *The Star Sea* is nearly the host's case. The earlier losses did not give the managers the targeted suspicion that blind-eye knowledge needs (para 117, a statement about privity). Lord Scott said the findings about their lack of checks 'go to negligence, perhaps gross negligence' (paras 118-119). A host whose awareness that guests need help is general stands outside the knowledge doctrines. Its case falls under the question of what care requires, and that is where C is argued. The doctrines keep one job: a host that switches off a route because it expects complaints is in their territory. To derive C from the knowledge triggers would be circular, and the paper does not (`WS_P3_R7.md`, (iv)).

### 6.7 The legal correlate (legal register, separately)

The law reaches part of what C requires, by a different road, and stops short of the rest.

- **The guest's remedial rights need an addressee.**
  - A consumer may require repeat performance of a service not performed with care (`uk2015consumer`, s. 55).
  - A package traveller has implied terms for remedy within a reasonable period the traveller sets, and may send messages and complaints to the retailer (`uk2018package`, regs 15(4), 17).
  - A platform must keep a point of contact that 'shall not solely rely on automated tools' and a complaint system supervised by 'appropriately qualified staff' (`eu2022dsa`, arts 12(1), 20(6)).
  - From 29 March 2029 a package organiser must be 'easily contactable' and 'able to respond efficiently' about failures during the package (`eu2026package`, art. 16a(1)).
- **The law forbids obstructing the guest.** Among the factors that make a commercial practice aggressive are 'any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract' (`eu2005ucpd`, art. 9(d)). The directive applies 'during' a transaction, and exercising a contractual right is a transactional decision (arts 2(k), 3(1)). The UK successor asks 'whether the practice requires a consumer to take onerous or disproportionate action in order to exercise rights that the consumer has in relation to a product' (`uk2024dmcc`, s. 228(2)(f)). Public bodies enforce both; in the UK the aggressive-practice provisions have been in force since 6 April 2025 (`uk2024dmcc`, ss. 225-231). The UK consumer's own redress requires that the practice was 'a significant factor' in the decision to contract or to pay (s. 232(4)), and ss. 232, 234 and 235 were still prospective in every text read; until regulations come into force, redress runs under Part 4A of the 2008 Regulations (s. 252), which was not read. In Great Britain a disabled guest is owed reasonable adjustments, including an auxiliary service and accessible information, as a floor the host cannot define away (`uk2010equality`, s. 20(5)-(6); Sch. 2 para. 2).
- **The channel's legal role is allocative.** Package law lets a traveller's failure to report count against the traveller 'where such notice would have avoided or reduced the damage' (`eu2015package`, art. 13(2), recital 34; `uk2018package`, reg. 15(3)). A host that kept no route cannot fairly hold a non-report against the guest *(my inference)*.
- **Where the law stops.** No text read requires, for a guest of an accommodation provider outside packages and platform moderation, a route to a natural person with power over the single case. The UK package contact point is a duty owed to the state (`uk2018package`, regs 7(10), 7(12), 35). A missing channel adds no head of liability. Where the host undertook to provide, it is liable for the failed performance with or without a channel (`kuoni2021uksc`, paras 8, 50). In personal injury a lost chance is not compensated, so the guest must prove she would have reported, that the report would have reached someone who would have acted, and that acting would have prevented the harm (`gregg2005ukhl`, Lord Hoffmann paras 79-83, 90). Courts weigh a hotel's internal reporting as part of reasonable care (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 196-198, 205, 209, 213), not a route from guests, and the standard is that of comparable establishments (paras 196-197). The Georgia court declined to recognise a duty to check on guests that the legislature 'chose not' to impose (`rasnick2011krishna`, p. 569). In the legal register, C is a proposal for legislatures and for hosts' own practice.

### 6.8 The footing in hospitality, stated once

This is what the hospitality literature contributes to Step 6, and what it does not.

- **The question.** The journal's literature makes the host's power after the threshold hospitality's own question (`bulley2015ethics`, AM pp. 2, 10; `lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 3). It names divided hosting four ways (Step 1). It gives the guest resistance, exit, complaint to an intermediary or a rating after the stay, and no route kept by the host (`WS_P1_R9.md`, (a)).
- **The value.** It treats the guest's effective complaint as the guest's power (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12) and calls for spaces that 'enable contestation and conflict to occur' (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 9; the editors' words, not marked as Dikeç's, and Dikeç 2002 is unread; the saved copy is a submitted draft, so the wording must be checked against the version of record). Bulley's wager is a welcome kept 'open once the threshold is passed' (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 18).
- **What hospitality adds to service recovery.** In service research, complaint handling is the firm's response to a customer's complaint, studied for its effect on complainants' satisfaction and loyalty (`gelbrich2011metaanalysis`, card summary). C differs in three ways:
  - it is a duty owed to the guest, which binds where recovery does not pay;
  - its object is the guest's position under the host's power, so the route must be usable without the host's goodwill;
  - its standard is set by what correction can still achieve for the guest, not by what the firm finds useful.
- **The privilege to report.** The classical code forbids the guest to demand, and offered at most a sanctioned partial exit (`pittrivers2012law`, pp. 513-514). In the commercial relation that changes. Hemmington sets the domestic rule of 'not complaining' against the commercial relation (`hemmington2007`, AV p. 9). Package law makes reporting the traveller's duty (`eu2015package`, art. 13(2)). Reporting that something promised has failed is not a demand that the host ordain otherwise *(my inference)*.
- **What it does not supply.** No text read states a host's duty to keep a route. The duty is the paper's. Pitt-Rivers's plate is not used.

## Step 7. What an algorithm changes (legal register for admission; moral and empirical for the stay; K9)

**Claim.** Before reception, a system that decides admission can decide that the duty never arises, and can breach the duty not to refuse on a protected ground. After reception, the duties persist. What automation was shown to change is where the person with power sits, and how the guest is governed. **Status: holds, narrowed.**

- *Before reception.* The Court of Justice treats a vendor's score as an automated decision where the user 'draws strongly' on it to refuse a contract (`schufa2023cjeu`, para 73), and bound the vendor to avoid 'a lacuna in legal protection' (paras 61-63). For an innkeeper the 1956 Act settles who refused: the duty attaches to the proprietor, so an automated refusal is the proprietor's (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(1)) *(my inference)*. An automated refusal on a protected ground breaches s. 29(1) whoever wrote the system (`uk2010equality`). In the UK, solely automated significant decisions are now lawful outside special-category data, subject to safeguards (`uk2025datause`, arts 22A-22D).
- *After reception: the person moves behind the machine.* The automated hotels studied kept service workers on site 'for legal and technical reasons' (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5035, n. 1). Guests walked past the worker to the kiosk, and a guest who approached the worker was sent back to 'do it yourself' (p. 5022). Guests reached the worker by seeking them out (p. 5035). The paper may say that automation can move the person with power out of the guest's ordinary path. It may not say, on this evidence, that automation removes the person.
- *After reception: discipline and identification.* Xu and Greiffenhagen's own thesis is that guests are 'disciplined by a technology that uses voice commands to tell them what to do with their bodies' (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5016). The guest's retrying, which Step 6 counts as cure, is also compliance and labour. The kiosk's identity check automates the condition Derrida's conditional laws turn on, asking the new arrival's name (as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM pp. 8-9). What automation changes, then, includes the host's power to verify and govern the guest. That strengthens P5.
- *Workers, for comparison.* For platform work the EU legislature recorded that automated systems 'increasingly replace functions that managers usually perform', and that workers cannot 'discuss those decisions with a human contact person' (`eu2024platformwork`, recital 8). That is K9's premise for workers, not guests.
