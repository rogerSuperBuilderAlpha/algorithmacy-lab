# The duty of the host: the argument

## The answer

A commercial host owes each guest it receives, for as long as the stay lasts, three things: reasonable care for the guest's safety, the safe keeping of the guest's goods, and performance with care of the services it undertook to provide. A host the law treats as an innkeeper owes a fourth, which comes first in time: the duty to receive. The duty binds for two kinds of reason. The host took the role up by holding itself out to travellers, and that settles that it is bound and from when. The guest relies on the host and stays inside the space the host controls, and that settles that the duty is owed to this guest and cannot be shed by notice.

When others do the work, the host keeps what the governing doctrine leaves with it. Where it undertook to provide a service, it answers for the result. Where it only occupies premises or engages a contractor, it must choose with care and act on what it knows. When several firms host together, the duty to the guest's person follows control of the guest's space, and the duty to perform follows the undertaking. A brand that lends its name answers, if at all, for the operator's fault.

The paper's own step comes last. A duty performed through others and through systems can fail without the holder's knowing, and for a failure in the guest's own case the guest is usually the route by which it reaches anyone able to act. A holder that keeps no way for the guest's report to reach, while performance is still possible, someone with power to correct the failure in the single case has disabled the means of performing its own duty. It owes the guest that channel as part of the duty it already has. The channel is not a new right of the guest.

## Step 1. What hosting asks of the host (customary register; K1 and K2)

**Claim.** A line of hospitality scholarship holds that to host is to answer for the guest's safety. It locates that responsibility in the role of host, and keeps hospitableness for the manner and motive with which the role is performed. **Status: holds, narrowed.**

**Warrant.**

1. *The responsibility is stated for guest-friendship and prescribed for business.* O'Gorman's history of ancient hospitality records duties to receive the guest, to protect him and to represent him in court (`ogorman2005modern`, pp. 144–145; the Roman passage rests on Schmitz 1875: 621). Hemmington makes the security of strangers one of five dimensions of commercial hospitality and tells hospitality businesses to 'behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience' (`hemmington2007`, abstract; AV pp. 15–16). Morrison and O'Gorman list, among business principles, that 'Businesses have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests' (`morrison2008hospitality`, Fig. 2). Telfer, as Hemmington quotes her, calls the guest's safety 'traditionally the most important responsibility of all' (Telfer 2000, as quoted in `hemmington2007`, AV p. 15).
2. *The classical code fixes what is due to the guest and forbids the guest to demand it.* Pitt-Rivers lists among a host's infringements a failure 'to attend to his guests, to grant them the precedence which is their due, to show concern for their needs and wishes', and says a guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's right to ordain (`pittrivers2012law`, pp. 515–516). The guest 'has no right other than to respect' (p. 514).
3. *The role is a role.* Benveniste finds separate words for the one received and the one who receives, bound 'by a pact which implies precise obligations' (`benveniste1973indoeuropean`, ch. 7, paras 32, 34). O'Gorman describes hospitality in practice as 'governed by a set of man made rules dependent on the context: domestic, civic or commercial' (`ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 8). Telfer, as quoted, calls the paid provider 'a commercial host' and makes 'hospitable' depend on his motive and prices (Telfer 2000: 45, as quoted in `slattery2002finding`, p. 21, and in Lynch et al. 2011). The role term ('host') holds whatever the motive; the manner term ('hospitable') does not *(my inference, from one sentence with two carriers)*.
4. *Duty is not opposed to hospitableness in Telfer.* As quoted, she counts 'sense of duty where there is also concern for the guests themselves' among hospitable motives (Telfer 1996: 82, as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 6). The opposition v6 drew between imposed obligation and hospitableness is Blain and Lashley's (`blain2014hospitableness`, p. 2).
5. *Derrida places rights and duties in the conditional laws of hospitality, not against hospitality.* As quoted, the plural laws are 'those rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional' (*Of Hospitality*: 77, as quoted in `ogorman2007phenomenon`, AM p. 8). The unconditional law breaks with hospitality 'by right', yet does not condemn it and stays 'indissociable' from it (pp. 25, 27, as quoted in Shryock 2012: S22, one carrier). Westmoreland reads the relation as a hierarchy in which the law needs the laws (`westmoreland2008interruptions`, pp. 4, 8).

**Footing in the hospitality literature.** All five points come from the field's own texts or from the anthropology and philosophy it cites. The legal steps below test whether the law enforces what the canon states; they are not the source of the duty's content.

**Narrowing.**

- 'Across the hospitality canon' is too wide. The management definitions of the 1980s and 1990s describe an exchange and name no duty (Brotherton and Wood 2000, as quoted in `hemmington2007`, AV pp. 5–6; `morrison2008hospitality`, AM p. 5).
- For the commercial host the responsibility is prescribed, not observed: Hemmington reports that the trade overlooks it (AV p. 15), and Morrison and O'Gorman derive their principles from the Rule of St Benedict and contemporaneous monastic practice, whose chapter on guests, as their Figure 1 prints it, says nothing of safety (`morrison2008hospitality`, AM pp. 6, 14–15; *R1's reading*).
- The oldest content of the obligation is reciprocity, not care (`benveniste1973indoeuropean`, ch. 7, para. 31; `ogorman2005modern`, p. 142).
- Kant's right of hospitality is a right of visit against whoever holds the land, 'not a right to be treated as a guest'; it carries no duty to a person received (`kant1795perpetual`, pp. 137–139; AA 8:357–358).
- The paper's duty is a duty of conditional hospitality. It may not claim the unconditional law for it.

**The gap, restated.** Hospitality scholars have named the business as the party responsible for the guest's safety (`morrison2008hospitality`, Fig. 2; `hemmington2007`). In the ten texts R1 read whole, none argues why the duty falls on the business rather than resting on its motive, says when an organizational host's duty begins, or divides it among several firms (`WS_P1_R1.md`, gap statements). Two closed neighbours must be read before the paper says more: Dogru et al. (2025) on 'organization-level hospitality virtue' and Koo and Kwon (2026) on responsibility 'relocated' into socio-technical design (abstracts only: `dogru2025service`, `koo2026three`).

**Residual risk.** Telfer and Derrida are known here only as quoted. Telfer's chapter and Derrida's pp. 25–27 and 75–83 are on the fetch list.

## Step 2. What the law asks of the host (legal register; K4)

**Claim.** In the jurisdictions the paper names, a commercial host's legal duties fall in two tiers. Only an innkeeper owes the first, the duty to receive. Every commercial host that receives a guest owes the second: care for the guest's person, and performance of the services it undertook with reasonable care and skill. Innkeepers also owe strict, capped liability for goods. **Status: holds, narrowed, in England and Wales, Ireland, three United States jurisdictions and EU consumer law.**

**Warrant.**

- *Admission.* A refused traveller had an action against the innkeeper (Blackstone, 3 Comm. 164; `constantine1944imperial`, p. 708). The duty now attaches to the proprietor of a hotel as the 1956 Act defines it, on the conditions of room, readiness to pay and no reasonable ground of refusal (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1; `rymer1877queen`, pp. 139–140). Ireland enacts it more widely, for food and drink as well as sleeping accommodation (`ie1963hotel`, s. 3(1)). A federal appeals court called it Kansas common law that 'has never been abrogated' (`thomas1955pick`, p. 666).
- *The person.* A hotel owes its guests reasonable care, extending to third parties' crimes (`alnajar2019cumberland`, para 187; `alnajar2020cumberland`, paras 19, 34). The same holds for a bar or nightclub and its guests (`everett2011comojo`, paras 31, 36), in Colorado for a hotel ending a stay (`groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 4, 32, 40–43) and in Iowa for a tavern (`hoyt2013gutterz`, pp. 776–777). The duty is reasonable care, not insurance (`maclenan1917segar`, p. 328). Ireland enacts it in terms (`ie1963hotel`, s. 4).
- *Goods.* Strict liability, capped, for overnight guests (`uk1956hotel`, s. 2). The same structure is in Roman law, the German code and the 1962 convention (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.3.1; `bgb701`, §§ 701–702; `coe1962hotelkeepers`, Annex arts 1, 4).
- *Services.* A trader 'must perform the service with reasonable care and skill', personally or through another acting for it, and cannot exclude that liability (`uk2015consumer`, ss. 49, 57, 65).
- *Adjustment.* A service-provider must change a practice that puts disabled people at a substantial disadvantage, and the failure is discrimination against 'that person' (`uk2010equality`, ss. 20–21, 29(7)).

**Narrowing.**

- *Admission is not universal.* Ulpian says innkeepers are free to receive no one (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.1.1); the German code and the convention impose no duty to admit (`bgb701`; `coe1962hotelkeepers`). A refreshment bar 'is not an inn' (`rymer1877queen`, p. 140). Colorado finds no special relationship for taverns and restaurants (`groh2015westin`, n. 7).
- *No modern English guest has won.* Both modern English guests lost on breach (`alnajar2019cumberland`, para 240; `everett2011comojo`, paras 37–38). The paper may cite the duty, not a modern English judgment holding a hotel or bar liable for a guest's injury.
- *Attending.* That attending is customary and not legal is too broad. Where Restatement (Second) § 314A is followed, an innkeeper must give aid 'after it knows or has reason to know' a guest is ill or injured (as quoted in `rasnick2011krishna`, p. 568, and `groh2015westin`, ¶ 29). In package holidays the Supreme Court gave 'the service of looking after and serving holidaymakers courteously' as an example of services inherent in every such contract (`kuoni2021uksc`, para 30). Attending survives as a customary duty only for attention beyond safety, aid and the services sold.
- *v6 is wrong on one point.* v6 says the exceptions the law recognized 'ran in the host's favour'. The Equality Act's adjustment duty and the three conditions that lift the 1956 Act's cap run in the guest's favour (`uk2010equality`, ss. 20–21; `uk1956hotel`, s. 2(3)). This is flagged for the authors and not reworded.

## Step 3. Why the duty binds (K3)

**Claim.** The role specifies the duty: its content, its trigger and its holder. It does not bind by itself. Three grounds outside the role divide the binding work, and no one of them carries the core duty alone:

- **Voluntary assumption.** Holding out fixes that this party is bound and from when.
- **Invited reliance.** The guest's reliance on the host explains why the duty is owed to this guest.
- **Control.** The guest is inside the space the host controls, which explains why a notice cannot exhaust the duty.

**Status: holds, narrowed, as a claim about a division of labour.** Whether a role binds by itself is open in the philosophy, because the deciding texts (Hardimon 1994; Simmons 1996) are closed.

**Warrant: the role specifies.**

- In the philosophy of special obligations, critics of associative obligation hold that the duty to perform 'the (conventional) duties that constitute the role' rests on 'a moral principle external to it' (`dagger2021political`, §4.3, para. 8, reporting Dagger, Wellman and Simmons). Jeske argues the same in her own voice: talk of a role's obligations may record 'merely others' expectations' (`jeske2019special`, §4, paras 10, 18–19).
- The courts attach the innkeeper's duty to the calling. Blackstone implies the undertaking only against one whose 'common profession' it is (3 Comm. 164). The Tenth Circuit treats the contract as 'the means for the inducement or establishment' of a relationship to which the law then attaches the duty (`thomas1955pick`, pp. 665–666). Gaius says the innkeeper is paid for lodging, not custody, and answers for custody all the same (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.5 pr.).
- In both modern English cases the duty to the guest's person does not come from innkeeper law. Dingemans J notes that innkeeper law did not protect the person and reaches the duty through negligence (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 183–187). This is the structure K3 claims: the role specifies, and something outside it binds.

**Warrant: the three grounds.**

- *Assumption.* Blackstone: an innkeeper who 'hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers' makes 'an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way' (3 Comm. 164). He calls such undertakings 'never perhaps actually made' (3 Comm. 161–163). That is the 10 Nov middle position in an eighteenth-century source: holding out takes up the role, and the law supplies the duty. The statutory trigger is the same: a hotel is an establishment 'held out by the proprietor' as offering food, drink and sleeping accommodation to travellers (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(3); `ie1963hotel`, s. 1(1)). In *Al-Najar*, 'the hotel invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests' (para 187).
- *Reliance.* The nightclub guest 'relies on the competence and prudence of its management' and 'expects and is entitled to expect that there will be no violence' (`everett2011comojo`, para 31). Neither English court asked whether these guests relied; reliance is treated as general (para 31; `alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 43, 187).
- *Control and dependence.* Where K3 has dependence, the Court of Appeal has control: 'The management is in control of the premises. It can regulate who enters' (`everett2011comojo`, para 31). Colorado and Iowa describe a 'special relationship' that compromises the guest's ability to protect herself and leaves the host 'in a superior position to protect' her (`groh2015westin`, ¶ 29; `hoyt2013gutterz`, p. 777).

**Why no ground is sufficient alone** (`WS_P2_R2.md`, table of grounds):

- *Assumption* binds a host only to what it undertook (`jeske2019special`, §4, para. 2). A host that undertakes less is bound to less, so a core duty that survives a notice needs another ground.
- *Reliance* binds only toward those who relied, and only to the extent they were led to expect (`habib2022promises`, §§3.3, 5.1). A guest who expected little is still owed safety.
- *Dependence* falls on whoever can help, and does not pick out the host as host (`jeske2019special`, §3, para. 1; `dagger2021political`, §4.4, para. 5).

The division of labour answers each defect. Assumption picks the party and the moment. Reliance and control explain why the duty runs to this guest, and why the law forbids a notice to cut it below a floor. That floor is in the statutes: no exclusion below the cap in Ireland, Germany and the convention (`ie1963hotel`, s. 9; `bgb701`, § 702a; `coe1962hotelkeepers`, Annex art. 6); no exclusion of the care-and-skill term, or of liability for negligent death or injury, in UK consumer law (`uk2015consumer`, ss. 57, 65); and no waiver of the package organiser's liability (`eu2015package`, art. 23). Where the law closes the door to disclaimer, the host's consent cannot be what makes the duty bind *(R4b's point, which I adopt)*.

**Objections and replies.**

1. *Disabling, not grounding.* Associative theorists answer that freedom to leave is a condition without which the role fails, and that the good realised in the relationship is what justifies the claims (`dagger2021political`, §4.3, para. 9). **Reply.** The argument needs assumption only to fix the party and the moment. An enabling condition does that as well as a ground does, so the argument holds on either reading and does not need to settle the dispute.
2. *Combining weak grounds yields no strong theory* (`dagger2021political`, §5, para. 3, reporting Simmons). **Reply.** The grounds are not added to make one stronger ground. Each answers a different question (who is bound, to whom, and how far a notice can cut), and the law gives each a separate doctrinal form: the statutory definition, the duty of care, and the rule against exclusion.
3. *Fair play is left out.* It is one of four main contenders in the political-obligation debate (`dagger2021political`, §4.2, paras 1–2). **Reply, offered and not relied on.** A host takes custom that exists because travellers trust strangers' houses, and that trust lasts because other hosts keep the duty; a host that breaks it free-rides on their compliance *(my inference)*. This would give a ground that picks out every host trading on the practice, and it fits the oldest content of hospitality, reciprocity (`benveniste1973indoeuropean`, ch. 7, para. 31). The canonical texts on fair play were not read in this run, so the dossier names it as a possible fourth ground and does not rest on it.
4. *The courts give other grounds:* crime control, the purpose of inns, benefit to the host, policy (`maclenan1917segar`, p. 329; `rymer1877queen`, pp. 140–141; `everett2011comojo`, para 31; `groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 25, 33–37). **Concede.** K3 says what holding out triggers and which grounds bind a commercial host now. It does not offer them as the history of the doctrine.
5. *Dependence did not bind in Georgia.* A 77-year-old guest alone in his room was owed no check (`rasnick2011krishna`, pp. 569–570). **Concede for Georgia law on a risk internal to the guest.** The dependence ground explains why a duty the host does owe runs to this guest; it does not by itself create a duty to look after the guest's health. Step 6 relies on that limit.

## Step 4. To whom the duty is owed (K5 and K6)

**Claim.** The legal duties are owed to the guest, who can sue on them; the duty to receive was also public. The customary duty to attend is owed to the guest in the sense of wronging, while the guest may not demand it. The argument does not depend on whether holding a claim includes standing to demand. **Status: K5 holds, narrowed; K6 holds.**

**Warrant.**

- The refused traveller's right: 'His right, I think, is founded on the common law. That right I found was violated' (`constantine1944imperial`, p. 708). The words 'a right at common law' are the reporter's headnote (p. 693); the paper should quote the judge. Injured guests sue in their own names (`alnajar2019cumberland`, para 7; `everett2011comojo`, paras 1–2). The 1956 Act's liabilities run to 'any guest of his' (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(2)). In the United States the contract is 'usually treated' as establishing a relationship that creates 'a common law relational duty on the part of the innkeeper, the breach of which is redressible in tort' (`thomas1955pick`, p. 665).
- The duty was public as well: refusal was indictable (`rymer1877queen`, pp. 136, 139; Blackstone, 4 Comm. 168). Both routes existed, and they do not conflict: the traveller's action lay 'upon this universal assumpsit', in implied contract (3 Comm. 164), and Blackstone's rule that common nuisances are 'indictable only, and not actionable' concerns suits for the nuisance itself (4 Comm. 167; `blackstone1768commentaries`, as corrected after CV-B).
- The customary duty: in Pitt-Rivers's code, precedence is the guests' 'due'; a host who fails 'to attend to his guests' or 'to show concern for their needs and wishes' infringes the law of hospitality; 'Failure to offer the best is to denigrate the guest'; and the slighted guest may withdraw (`pittrivers2012law`, pp. 516–517). The guest may not demand it (p. 515). Whether that gives the guest a right depends on the theory of rights. The safe wording commits only to direction in the sense of wronging (`WS_P3_R3.md`, §3).
- On the standard definition, 'A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ' (`wenar2025rights`, §2.1.2). Theories divide on whether a claim includes the standing to demand it: will and demand theories say yes, interest theories no (`wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2; `herstein2023legal`, §4). They agree on the ordinary case: a competent adult to whom an un-overridden duty is owed may demand it (`sreenivasan2019rights`; `dekenessey2024two`, EV pp. 2, 14).

**What follows for Step 6.** The channel is derived from the host's duty, not from the guest's standing. A demand theorist and an interest theorist can both accept it.

## Step 5. Who holds the duty when others do the work, and when several firms host together (K8)

**Claim.** What stays with the holder depends on the duty and the doctrine.

| Duty | What stays with the holder |
|---|---|
| Goods | Strict liability stays, capped |
| Services the holder undertook to provide | The holder answers for the delegate's want of care |
| Premises and the person, where the holder is an occupier or an employer of a contractor and no more | The holder must choose with reasonable care and act on what it knows or has reason to suspect |

Among several firms, the duty of care for the person follows control, and the duty to perform follows the undertaking. **Status: holds, narrowed, in the named jurisdictions; the allocation among firms is a proposal with partial support.**

**Warrant.**

- *The starting point.* The employer of an independent contractor is in general not liable for the contractor's torts (`barclays2020uksc`, paras 7, 27). An outsourced cleaning, security or software firm with other clients is on that side of the line *(R5a's reading)*.
- *Occupiers.* The occupier is protected if it took reasonable steps to satisfy itself 'that the contractor was competent and that the work was being properly done' (`ferguson1987welsh`, p. 7, applying the 1957 Act, s. 2(4)(b)). It is 'not ordinarily' expected to supervise a competent contractor (p. 7). Where it 'knows or has reason to suspect' an unsafe system, Lord Keith said only that 'it might well be reasonable' to act, a guarded dictum, since the condition was not met; Lord Goff did not accept that knowledge 'can of itself be enough' (p. 11). The Court of Appeal makes the reasonableness of continued reliance turn on what the holder knew or had 'reason to contemplate' (`naylor2004payling`, para 50; `maguire2006sefton`, para 30). A nightclub owner and a leisure-centre operator escaped liability to customers on this footing (`naylor2004payling`, paras 19, 50; `maguire2006sefton`, paras 30–31).
- *Contract for services.* A party that 'undertook to provide and not merely to arrange' answers for the services 'even if some of the services were to be rendered by others' (`wong1995kwan`, p. 10). UK consumer law binds the trader to perform with reasonable care and skill, and defines the trader as acting 'personally or through another person acting in the trader's name or on the trader's behalf' (`uk2015consumer`, ss. 49(1), 2(2)); whether that reaches an independent sub-contractor the Act does not say in terms (`WS_P4_R5a.md`, table row 10). Package law makes the organiser answer 'irrespective of whether those services are to be performed by the organiser or by other travel service providers' (`eu2015package`, art. 13(1)), and the Supreme Court held a tour operator liable for a hotel electrician's assault on a guest without any vicarious liability (`kuoni2021uksc`, paras 45–50).
- *Premises entered under contract.* The implied term is the common duty of care, entrustment defence included; the occupier does not warrant its contractor's care (`uk1957occupiers`, s. 5(1); `maguire2006sefton`, paras 24, 31).
- *Non-delegable duties* are 'exceptional' (`armes2017uksc`, paras 31–32). No case read applies one to an adult guest in England. In North Carolina, *Page v Sloan* stated that an innkeeper's duties 'to exercise due care to keep his premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn guests of any hidden peril are nondelegable', as one of three bases on which the motel owners could be liable at trial, in a decision that only reversed summary judgment (`page1972sloan`, 281 N.C. at 702-704, 708). The court's authority was an encyclopaedia and the Restatement as quoted; later treatment is unchecked.
- *Among firms.* Control is the consistent ground: the management 'in control of the premises' (`everett2011comojo`, para 31); the operator 'in actual day-to-day control' (`crinkley1988holiday`, para 7); the platform that sets the price and selects the providers (`uber2017cjeu`, para 39; `airbnb2019cjeu`, paras 56–57). Holding out reaches a brand only vicariously, in one of three American cases (`crinkley1988holiday`, paras 49–52; against: `hayman1987ramada`, p. 279; `mobil1995bransford`, pp. 120–121). The 1956 Act attaches the innkeeper's duties to the proprietor 'and shall not attach to any other person' (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(1)).

**Where this departs from v6.** v6 said that the work can be delegated and the duty cannot. That is true of every duty only in the weak sense that careful delegation may discharge it, and in the strong sense only of *Woodland*'s exceptional category (`claims/ADJUDICATION.md`, head 1). The dossier replaces it with the table above.

**Residual risk.** The brand and platform rows rest on three American decisions from 1987–1995 and on EU market-access judgments used for an inference about responsibility. No English authority on a franchised hotel's liability was read. Nothing read addresses a hotel's system vendor.

## Step 6. What the duty asks of a host that works through others and through systems (K7)

This is the keystone. The argument runs from the host's duty, not from the guest's right.

### P1. The holder owes the guest duties that run for the length of the stay.
**Status: holds.** The care duty asks whether the risk arose 'while she remained a guest' and treats eviction as a process (`groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 42–43). The services duty runs while the services are performed (`uk2015consumer`, s. 49). The goods liability runs while the guest has the accommodation (`bgb701`, § 701(2); `coe1962hotelkeepers`, Annex art. 1(2)).

### P2. A holder that performs through others keeps a duty that the work be done with care, in the sense each doctrine gives.
**Status: holds, per duty, in two senses that must be kept apart.**

- **Liability for the result.** Where the holder undertook to provide, it answers for the delegate's performance (`wong1995kwan`, p. 10; `kuoni2021uksc`, para 50; `eu2015package`, art. 13(1)).
- **Care in choosing and acting on what it knows.** Where the holder is an occupier or an employer of a contractor and no more, it must choose with care, and what it knows or has reason to suspect may make it unreasonable to go on relying without acting (`ferguson1987welsh`, p. 7, Lord Keith's guarded dictum; `naylor2004payling`, para 50; `maguire2006sefton`, para 30).

In the moral register, Brandt states the premise: on delegation, 'the individual initially bound by the responsibility still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled' (`brandt2017transfer`, ms p. 3). He denies discharge by careful choice: 'I do not fulfil my responsibility by merely taking reasonable steps' (n. 34). He also explains why a host's outsourcing is delegation and not transfer: an arrangement with a third party cannot alter what was agreed with the party owed (ms p. 10, n. 32).

### P3. A duty that the work be done includes the means of learning that it is not being done, and of correcting it, while performance is still possible.
**Status: the paper's own step, with a published general principle behind it and partial legal analogues.**

*The argument.* Hindriks argues that an agent subject to a first-order obligation has a second-order 'duty of control': 'if an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it' (`hindriks2025moral`, p. 4). A holder that performs through others performs on information it does not itself see: the delegate's work happens where the holder is not. One position the holder must therefore be in, if it is to perform at all, is a position to learn of a failure while it can still act on it. Without that, every failure the delegate does not report goes uncorrected, and the duty's performance depends on the delegate's candour about its own faults *(my inference)*.

The argument has three supports, none of which is the step itself:

- **Philosophy.** Hindriks gives the general principle and grounds it in moral control, which he takes from Fischer and Ravizza: an agent that 'can recognize moral considerations and react to them' (their words, as Hindriks quotes them; `hindriks2025moral`, p. 3). A control gap, for Hindriks, is a deficiency in how receptive or reactive the agent is, and the duty of control requires the agent to identify it (pp. 4–5, 13). Brandt gives the correcting half: the delegator must act when 'it becomes clear' the delegate is failing (`brandt2017transfer`, ms pp. 3, 5).
- **Soft law.** The UN Guiding Principles join the two halves and name the channel. An enterprise should 'track the effectiveness of their response', drawing on feedback 'including affected stakeholders' (`un2011guiding`, Principle 20). An operational-level grievance mechanism gives 'a channel for those directly impacted ... to raise concerns when they believe they are being or will be adversely impacted', so that impacts can be 'remediated early and directly' (Principle 29, Commentary, pp. 31–32).
- **Law in neighbouring settings.**
  - *Public law.* The High Court declared a minister in breach 'in failing properly to monitor the provision of accommodation' that contractors performed for her (`dma2020sshd`, para 333(2)). The reasoning ties monitoring to being able to 'act immediately if there is a sign' that she was not acting lawfully (paras 235-239, at 236), and contrasts contract correction 'after the event' with 'securing the provision of accommodation at the time and in the individual case' (para 241). The urgency was an imminent breach of Article 3, and the paper must say so. Nineteen later judgments cite the case, four in the Court of Appeal; none was read (`CARD_CORRECTIONS.md`).
  - *Package travel.* Package law requires a contact point 'which enables the traveller to contact the organiser quickly', and a duty to remedy during the trip (`eu2015package`, arts 7(2)(d), 13(2)–(3)). From 2029 the organiser must be 'easily contactable' (`eu2026package`, art. 16a).
  - *High-risk AI.* The deployer must monitor the system and assign oversight to persons with 'the necessary competence, training and authority' (`eu2024aiact`, arts 14, 26).
  - *Corporate law.* Directors must make a good-faith effort to have a reporting system adequate to bring 'appropriate information' to their attention 'in a timely manner' (`marchand2019barnhill`, slip op. at 30, quoting *Caremark* at n. 100).
  - *The innkeeper's own statute.* The proprietor loses the cap on liability for goods where the guest wished to deposit property 'but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so' (`uk1956hotel`, s. 2(3)(c)). The innkeeper's own statute penalises a host the guest could not reach.

*The knowledge route, and why it cannot carry P3 alone.* R7 tested whether the principle 'a duty that turns on knowledge cannot be met by arranging not to know' is in print, and how far it reaches (`WS_P3_R7.md`).

- **It is in print in law, for deliberate avoidance only.** The United States Supreme Court treats as knowing a defendant who 'subjectively believe[s] that there is a high probability that a fact exists' and takes 'deliberate actions to avoid learning of that fact', and gives the doctrine a scope that 'surpasses recklessness and negligence' (`globaltech2011seb`, 563 U.S. at 769). The House of Lords requires, for blind-eye knowledge, a suspicion 'firmly grounded and targeted on specific facts' and a deliberate decision to avoid confirming it (`manifest2001unipolaris`, para 116, Lord Scott). Both exclude negligence in terms.
- ***The Star Sea* is nearly the host's case, and it goes against the knowledge route.** After two ships were lost in similar fires, the earlier masters' incompetence did not put the managers 'in a position in which they were bound to enquire into the competence of every other master' (para 117). Their lack of a system to check went to negligence, 'perhaps gross negligence', and was no basis for blind-eye knowledge (paras 118-119). A host whose awareness that guests need help is general, not targeted, stands where those managers stood.
- **The law of hosts' own triggers ask what reached the holder.** Every knowledge trigger re-read has a constructive limb ('knew or ought to have known', `ferguson1987welsh`, pp. 7-8; 'reason to contemplate', `maguire2006sefton`, para 30; 'knows or has reason to know', § 314A as quoted in `rasnick2011krishna`, p. 568). The courts measured reason to know against what reached the holder or was notorious, not against what a route the holder had not kept would have brought. In *Ferguson*, what the architects saw on site did not count because no one 'informed the council' (p. 8). In *Rasnick*, the reports did reach the motel, and the majority still found no duty to check (p. 570).
- **The objection that follows, conceded.** Below deliberate avoidance, the law calls a failure to learn negligence, and negligence is measured by what care required. To say that a host with no channel 'had reason to know' already assumes that care required a channel. That is P3 itself. So P3 cannot be derived from the knowledge triggers (`WS_P3_R7.md`, (iv)).
- **What the knowledge doctrines still do.** They close off deliberate blocking. A host that switches a channel off, or keeps it unanswered, because it expects complaints it would rather not confirm is in the doctrines' territory (`globaltech2011seb`, at 769, 771; `manifest2001unipolaris`, para 116).

*So P3 stands as a claim about what care requires, and its support is moral.* Three texts carry it:

- **Hindriks's duty of control** (above): an agent that ought to do something ought to put itself in the position to do it (`hindriks2025moral`, p. 4).
- **Culpable ignorance by tracing.** On Holly Smith's account, as Rudy-Hiller reports it, ignorance is culpable when it results from a 'benighting act', 'an action or omission the consequence of which is the lack of a true belief'. Rosen calls the duty breached a 'procedural epistemic obligation' to inform oneself (`rudyhiller2022epistemic`, §2). In the entry's terms, P3 is a duty of inquiry. Whether such duties bind without awareness at the time of the benighting act is contested between volitionists and their critics, and the entry does not settle it (§§2, 3.2, 3.4).
- **Hormio's organisational form.** She argues that 'sometimes organisational design alone can create individual ignorance', that the benighting act can be the organisation's, and that no bad will is needed (`hormio2018culpable`, pp. 1, 16-18). An organisation that runs a known risk 'without adequate safety checks' 'could not justify this way of operating by appealing to ignorance' (p. 17). Her limit, taken from Smith, is that the wrong must fall 'within the known risks' of the benighting act (p. 19). Hosting meets it: that guests will need to report a failure and be helped is a known risk of the trade *(my inference, R7's also)*.

The one legal text that makes a means of learning part of a duty to oversee is *Marchand*: directors must make a good-faith effort to have a reporting system adequate to bring information to them 'in a timely manner' (`marchand2019barnhill`, slip op. at 30, quoting *Caremark*). It runs to the corporation, needs bad faith for breach, and concerns board-level information, so it is an analogue and not authority.

*The wording of P3 for draft 2* (R7's suggestion, which I adopt with one change). A holder whose duties to the guest turn on what it knows or has reason to know may not keep those duties from arising by arranging not to know. The law holds this of deliberate avoidance. It treats ignorance that careless design produces as negligence, and it imposes on occupiers and innkeepers no general duty to arrange to be told. The paper's step goes further, with support in moral philosophy and not in law: a holder that performs through others and through systems is under a duty of inquiry, and keeping a means of learning from the guest is part of the care its duties require. A design with no such means is then itself the failure, as an organisation's own design can be the act that makes its ignorance culpable.

*Why P3 is not refuted by the law that denies a duty to supervise.* Three English courts hold that an occupier or employer is ordinarily discharged by a reasonable choice of a competent contractor (`ferguson1987welsh`, p. 7; `naylor2004payling`, para 19; `maguire2006sefton`, para 30). That is a rule about a holder whose duty is care in choosing. It does not reach a holder that undertook to provide, which answers for the delegate's want of care whatever it knew (`wong1995kwan`, p. 10; `kuoni2021uksc`, para 8). Where the law makes a holder answer for its delegates' performance, it already pairs that liability with a channel and a duty to remedy during performance, in package travel (above). For the occupier, the law's own exit is knowledge or reason to suspect. P3 says the holder may not disable that exit. **Concede:** as a statement of English tort law in the ordinary case of an occupier and a competent contractor, P3 is not the law. It is offered in the moral register.

### P4. For a failure in the guest's own case, the guest's report is the main route by which it reaches the host; and a failure that neither the system nor the guest can cure needs someone with power over the output in the single case.
**Status: holds, narrowed, on thin evidence** (two small qualitative hotel studies and one practitioner summary; `WS_P6_R8.md`). The draft-1 wording, 'often the only timely signal', is not shown in general.

- *For.* The Helsinki hotel Schumacher and Komppula studied 'relies mainly on customer feedback concerning service failures', since its staff did not ask guests how their stay was going (`schumacher2016case`, p. 121). At the automated hotels Xu and Greiffenhagen filmed, each of the 13 guests whose ID, reservation or kiosk failed went to find a worker, and a worker checked them in by hand (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5035). The guest's approach was how the failure reached a person.
- *Correction, and who did it.* Where the guest's retrying could cure the failure, no one else was needed: the face-recognition kiosk 'will simply repeat the instruction again and again, so it is the response-giver who has to modify their behaviour' (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5030; by R8's count, 86 of 610 guests needed a further attempt, p. 5027). Where it could not, a worker with power to override the machine's outcome corrected it (p. 5035). In the staffed hotel, reception staff fixed most failures with discretion over discounts and room changes; the manager took the large ones; and staff did not know where the line fell (`schumacher2016case`, pp. 121-123). Power over the single case was graded and unwritten.
- *Presupposed by law.* Package law requires the traveller to 'inform the organiser without undue delay' of a failure perceived during the trip, and lets silence count against the traveller where notice 'would have avoided or reduced the damage' (`eu2015package`, art. 13(2), recital 34). Spain, California and the platform-work directive are each built on the affected person's own request (`es2025clientela`, art. 8(2); `ca2026ab1609`, s. 22627(a); `eu2024platformwork`, art. 11(2)). The German code and the convention make the guest's prompt report a condition of protection for goods (`bgb701`, § 703; `coe1962hotelkeepers`, Annex art. 5).
- *Cases.* In *Groh* the hotel knew the risk because the guests told its guards, and the person on the spot with power over the single case refused a request to let them wait in the lobby (`groh2015westin`, ¶¶ 2, 7–8, 36). In *Rasnick* the signal came from the guest's wife by telephone; on her testimony as the court recounts it, one operator hung up, and her last calls met a recorded message (`rasnick2011krishna`, n. 1; dissent, p. 572). In *R (DMA)* a portal recorded people as having 'failed to travel', and one case was closed thirteen minutes after the claimant's charity e-mailed to ask about it (`dma2020sshd`, paras 63, 77, 79).
- *Against, stated first in weight.* Many guests do not report. In Min and Kim's restaurant experiments, customers who felt low in power complained less in public, 'reducing an opportunity for the service provider to hear from customers' (`min2020angry`, pp. 1-2; a practitioner summary with no statistics). Negative word of mouth can come 'prior to—or in lieu of—a complaint' (`gelbrich2011metaanalysis`, p. 28, following Blodgett and Anderson). A failure of the whole system needs no report: when the kiosk was down, the worker met all 51 arriving guests (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5021). The AI Act builds oversight from the deployer's own monitoring and logs, not from reports (`eu2024aiact`, art. 26(5)–(6)).
- *Gaps* (`WS_P6_R8.md`, Gaps). R8 found no openly readable hospitality study reporting from its own data what share of dissatisfied guests complain. It found none showing that staff monitoring or automated systems detect guests' service failures sooner than guests do, and none comparing recovery after a self-service failure with and without a reachable employee. The deciding studies it found are closed (Dao and Theotokis 2020; Liu et al. 2026).

*What P4 does to C.* The channel is necessary for one class of failure: a failure in the single case that the guest perceives and that neither the system nor the guest can cure. It does not replace the host's own monitoring of failures across the system. And because guests who feel powerless stay silent, a channel the guest has to hunt for will under-report. A channel serves the duty only if the guest is told of it and can use it without a confrontation *(my inference, from `min2020angry`, pp. 1-2, and package law's requirement that the contact point be stated in the contract, `eu2015package`, art. 7(2)(d))*.

### C. The holder must keep a channel from the guest to someone with power to correct performance in the single case, for as long as the duty can still be performed.
**Status: the paper's own conclusion. Partial legal analogues; enacted for one sector without its premise (package travel) and for workers with its timing (platform work).**

On this route the channel belongs to the host's duty to perform. It is not a free-standing right of the guest. The guest's claim is to performance. A host that keeps no channel, and whose performance then fails in a way a report would have let it correct, has failed in the duty of performance *(my inference)*. This keeps the conclusion inside the duty K4 established. It also matches the law's practice of weighing reporting arrangements as part of reasonable care: the High Court weighed a hotel's reporting systems, incident reviews and supervision of officers (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 205, 209, 213), and the Court of Appeal expected 'some security arrangements which can be activated as and when the need arises' (`everett2011comojo`, para 36, dictum).

### Why the channel must reach someone with power over the single case

A route that ends with someone who can only listen does not let the holder correct anything. The statutes that secure a route to a person do not give that person power (`es2025clientela`, arts 3(14), 9(2); `ca2026ab1609`, s. 22625(c)), and the statutes that create power give the affected person no route to it (`eu2024aiact`, arts 14, 26, 85, 86; art. 27(1)(f) asks some deployers to set out 'complaint mechanisms' in their impact assessment, without linking them to the overseer). The platform-work directive requires both, for persons performing platform work: a right to request review and access to a contact person (art. 11(1)-(2)), overseers with authority 'for overriding automated decisions' (art. 10(2)), a human being to take the gravest decisions (art. 10(5)) and a duty to rectify (art. 11(3)). Even there the reachable person is not the one with power: the contact person's function is 'to discuss and to clarify' (art. 11(1)). No instrument read requires, for a consumer or a guest, a channel from the person affected to a person with power over the single case, available before the effect (`WS_P4_R6b.md`, item 15; `VERIFY_CARDS_D.md`, (e)).

The argument for power over the single case, step by step *(my inference throughout)*:

1. A failure in the single case is one where what the host's arrangements produced for this guest falls short of what the duty requires.
2. Correcting it means producing what the duty requires for this guest, now.
3. If the arrangements as configured could have produced it for this guest, they would have, unless the fault was transient. For a failure that is not transient, correction requires departing from the arrangements' output in this case.
4. Departing from the output in one case requires authority to do so.
5. A further automated layer with that authority is possible, but it too acts on its configuration and fails for cases its designers did not foresee. The failures a guest reports are disproportionately those cases, since a foreseen failure would have been handled by design. The chain ends at an agent able to judge an unforeseen case. Where legislatures require power to override a system's output in the single case, they place it in natural persons (`eu2024aiact`, art. 14(4)(d): 'in any particular situation ... disregard, override or reverse the output'; `eu2024platformwork`, art. 10(5)).

Sub-step 5 is the weakest link in the chain. R8's evidence fits sub-steps 3 and 4: failures the guest could cure by retrying needed no one, and failures the machine could not process were checked in by hand by a worker able to override it (`xu2024disciplined`, pp. 5027, 5030, 5035). It does not show that guests' reports skew toward unforeseen failures; that half of sub-step 5 is unsupported. Its conceptual half may already be argued under the name 'individual justice' [PENDING P5: Binns 2022 is a lead].

### When: performance, then repair

The line falls where performance can no longer be completed. Gardner's continuity thesis gives the form. When an obligation goes unperformed, the reasons for it remain and call for the next-best conformity still available. Repair is a new, secondary obligation with the same rationale, and it never equals performance (`gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35–40, 46; paraphrase only, since the eprint asks not to be quoted; Ripstein reports the thesis in `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3). Three corrections follow:

- **The line falls lapse by lapse, not at check-out.** A duty that runs for a stay is a series of things due at times. Correcting a lapse later the same day is already repair of that lapse, while the duty stays performable for the rest of the stay (`WS_P3_R3.md`, statement 45).
- **Interruptibility belongs to performance, and accountability to repair.** This mapping is the paper's own; Gardner's thesis supplies the line between performance and what follows, not the mapping, and his secondary obligation is wider than repair (it includes belated performance; `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 34-35, 53). Answerability, 'the capacity or the right to demand answers', is one dimension of accountability and concerns what has been done (`fox2007uncertain`, pp. 665, 668). It names the after, not the channel during the stay.
- **The law orders remedies the same way in three places:**
  - package travel: remedy during the trip, then price reduction and compensation (`eu2015package`, arts 13(3)–(6), 14);
  - UK consumer law: repeat performance before price reduction (`uk2015consumer`, ss. 55–56);
  - platform work: rectify 'without delay', and compensate 'Where such rectification is not possible' (`eu2024platformwork`, art. 11(3)).

  The UN Guiding Principles separate prevention of potential impacts from remediation of actual ones and give priority to impacts 'where delayed response would make them irremediable' (`un2011guiding`, Principle 17, Commentary; Principle 24). Two leading cases make earlier intervention depend on whether later relief can be full, and treat it as the exception (`mathews1976eldridge`, pp. 331, 335, 349; `americancyanamid1975ethicon`, pp. 3, 5).

### What the conclusion does not claim

- **No duty to monitor the guest.** The channel is for reports that the host's own performance is failing. *Rasnick* refused a duty to check on a guest's health on grounds of workability and privacy (pp. 569–570); the conclusion does not reach that case.
- **No hearing, and no right to an outcome.** The person reached must be able to correct, not to adjudicate. The UN principle that an enterprise 'cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome' concerns grievance mechanisms for disputes, not correction during performance (`un2011guiding`, Principle 31, Commentary on (h)).
- **No particular technology.** A small host meets the duty with a telephone number that the proprietor answers.
- **No duty without cost limits.** The duty is part of a duty of reasonable care. Package law excuses remedy that 'entails disproportionate costs' (`eu2015package`, art. 13(3)(b)), and *Mathews* weighs cost (p. 335). The form the channel must take scales with what is at stake and with what correction can still achieve.
- **No claim that the law already requires it of hotels.** No instrument read requires, for a guest, all three of a channel, a person with power and availability during performance (`WS_P4_R6b.md`, item 15).

### The customary footing

Pitt-Rivers records 'An intermediary solution' between submitting and walking out. In diplomatic etiquette, a guest dissatisfied with his place at table could 'call attention to an error of protocol of which he was the victim' by turning his plate over, until the error 'was corrected, or at least until his protest had been registered' (`pittrivers2012law`, pp. 513–514). Even the code that forbids the guest to demand kept a channel by which a failure could reach the host while the meal was still going on. A host that cannot be told is not attending *(my inference)*.

## Step 7. What an algorithm changes (K9)

**Claim.** Before reception, a system that decides admission can decide that the duty never arises. After reception, the duties persist and what can fail is the channel Step 6 requires. **Status: the before-reception limb holds in EU law and is narrowed in UK law. The after-reception limb holds, narrowed: on the open evidence, automation moves the person with power behind the machine; it was not shown to remove them.**

- *Before reception.* The Court of Justice treats a vendor's score as an automated decision where the user 'draws strongly' on it to refuse a contract (`schufa2023cjeu`, para 73). It bound the vendor because otherwise there would be 'a lacuna in legal protection' (paras 61–63). For an innkeeper, the 1956 Act settles who refused: the duty attaches to the proprietor, so an automated refusal is the proprietor's refusal (`uk1956hotel`, s. 1(1)) *(my inference)*. In the UK, solely automated significant decisions are now lawful outside special-category data, subject to safeguards (`uk2025datause`, arts 22A–22D, in force 5 February 2026).
- *After reception.* For platform work, the EU legislature recorded that automated systems 'increasingly replace functions that managers usually perform', and that people subject to them cannot 'discuss those decisions with a human contact person' (`eu2024platformwork`, recital 8). That is K9's premise for workers, not guests. In the AI Act, oversight sits inside the deploying firm, and the person outside it receives an explanation after the event (`eu2024aiact`, arts 26(2), 86(1)).
- *Guests, on open evidence.* The one automated hotel chain read kept service workers on site 'for legal and technical reasons' although guests could register alone (`xu2024disciplined`, p. 5035, n. 1). Automation demoted them to a fall-back. Guests walked past the worker to the kiosk, and a guest who approached the worker was sent back to 'do it yourself' (p. 5022); guests whose cases the kiosk could not process reached the worker by seeking them out (p. 5035). In a survey in Macau, a remembered robot failure did not directly raise intention to switch to human-staffed service (`lee2025robot`, p. 9; a rival, on a judgmental sample). **The paper may say that automation can move the person with power over the single case out of the guest's ordinary path, so that the guest reaches them only by seeking them out. It may not say, on this evidence, that automation removes the person.** Whether arrangements without any on-site person (unstaffed hotels, short-term rentals with self check-in) remove the path is open; the studies that would decide it are closed (`WS_P6_R8.md`, (c)).
- *Hospitality neighbours.* Koo and Kwon's abstract says the machine age 'relocates ethical responsibility within socio-technical design' (`koo2026three`, abstract only). Until the article is read, the paper may not say no hospitality scholar has asked where responsibility goes [PENDING P5].

## Where the result keeps, extends and departs from the 10 Nov middle position

The middle position, as the authors chose it on 10 Nov: the law imposes the duty on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, and holding out is how a party takes the role up.

| Element | Middle position | This argument | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| How the role is taken up | Holding out | Holding out, for a single proprietor: Blackstone's 'implied engagement' and the 1956 Act's 'held out by the proprietor' | **Keeps** |
| Who the law binds | Whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers | The innkeeper's statutory duties bind the proprietor 'and shall not attach to any other person'; the care duty is general negligence and occupiers' law, owed by the party in control | **Departs**: 'whoever' overstates who holds the statutory duties |
| Why the duty binds | The law imposes it on the role | The role specifies; assumption, reliance and control divide the binding work; none suffices alone | **Extends** |
| Several firms | Holding out picks the party (implied) | Control picks the holder of the care duty; the undertaking picks the holder of the duty to perform; holding out reaches a brand only vicariously and inconsistently | **Departs** |
| Delegation | The duty cannot be delegated (v6) | What stays depends on the duty and the doctrine (Step 5 table) | **Departs** |
| The reachable person | Derived from the guest's standing (v6) | Derived from the holder's duty to perform; standing is support only | **Departs** |
| Content | Receive, sustain, protect, attend (v6) | Receive (innkeepers only); care for the person; goods; services with care; adjustment; aid where § 314A is followed. Attending is customary beyond these | **Extends and narrows** |

## What remains uncertain, ranked

1. **P3 is the paper's own step.** As a statement of English tort law for an occupier and a competent contractor, it is false (`ferguson1987welsh`, p. 7), and the law's knowledge doctrines reach only deliberate avoidance (`manifest2001unipolaris`, paras 116-119). It stands in the moral register on Hindriks's duty of control and Hormio's organisational culpable ignorance, with analogues in public law, package travel, AI regulation and corporate law. Whether a duty of inquiry binds without awareness at the time is contested in the philosophy.
2. **P4 rests on thin evidence.** Two small qualitative hotel studies show the guest's report as the main route for failures in the guest's own case; one practitioner summary shows that guests who feel powerless stay silent. No open study measures how often dissatisfied guests complain or whether systems detect failures sooner. C is therefore stated for the class of failure P4 covers, and no wider.
3. **Sub-step 5 of the 'person with power' argument** rests on an inference about which failures guests report.
4. **The allocation among firms** rests on three American decisions and two EU market-access judgments. No English franchise case was read, and nothing read addresses system vendors.
5. **Telfer, Derrida, Hardimon, Simmons, Goodin and Scanlon** are known only through others. The dossier relies on none of them for a premise. It uses Telfer and Derrida for footing and attribution only, as quoted.
