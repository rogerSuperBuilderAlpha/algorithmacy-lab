# WS P4, reader R4a: the commercial host's duties in English and Irish law

Eleven texts were assigned. I read each in full on 11 Nov 2026, wrote eight new cards and upgraded three. Every locator below is to the primary text: a paragraph number for the modern judgments, a report page for the three older cases, a section for the statutes. Citekeys name the cards in `library/cards/`. Nothing here rests on the scout's notes, which I used only to find the files.

Verdicts use the vocabulary of `CLAIMS_FIXED.md`. They cover only what these eleven texts can show. The US cases, the civil-law texts and the delegation cases belong to other readers.

## (a) Synthesis against K3, K4, K5, K7 and K8

### K4. Two tiers in the legal register: **holds, narrowed, for England and Wales; one sentence of v6 needs correcting**

**Admission.**

- English law gave a refused traveller an action against the innkeeper, and he won.
  - constantine1944imperial, pp. 693 (headnote), 708.
- The duty now attaches to the proprietor of a "hotel" as the 1956 Act defines it, and to no other person.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 1(1), s. 1(3).
- The conditions K4 states are the ones the law states: a common inn, a traveller, room available, readiness to pay, and no reasonable ground of refusal.
  - constantine1944imperial, p. 694 (conceded); rymer1877queen, pp. 139-140; uk1956hotel, s. 1(3).
- The duty stops at the inn. A refreshment bar kept by a hotel proprietor under the hotel's roof and licence "is not an inn", and there "no one has a right to insist on being served any more than in any other shop". This confirms K4's line and forbids any wider reading for bars or restaurants at common law.
  - rymer1877queen, p. 140.
- Every provider of a service to the public, hosts included, carries a separate statutory duty not to refuse on protected grounds.
  - uk2010equality, s. 29(1).
- Ireland states the admission duty by statute and more widely: "all persons who ... present themselves and require sleeping accommodation, food or drink", "unless he has reasonable grounds of refusal". Ireland is not a jurisdiction v6 names.
  - ie1963hotel, s. 3(1).

**Reception: the person.**

- A hotel owes its staying guests a duty of reasonable care for their persons, extending to the criminal acts of third parties.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 187, 240(1). Admitted in its general form: paras 8-9; alnajar2020cumberland, paras 19, 34.
- The management of a bar or nightclub owes guests a duty of care in respect of what others on the premises do, and the Court of Appeal said the same duty should govern "the managers of an hotel or night club and their guests". This is the bar case K4 asks for.
  - everett2011comojo, paras 34, 36.
- The innkeeper, as occupier and invitor, owed the guest reasonable care as early as 1917.
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 329.
- The duty is reasonable care. No source makes the host an insurer of the guest's person.
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 328 ("He does not insure the personal safety of the guest."); alnajar2019cumberland, para 229; alnajar2020cumberland, para 28.
- The safety duty is not special to hosts. Every occupier owes it to every visitor.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 2(1)-(2).
- In both modern English cases the guests lost on breach. The one guest who won, in 1917, won on a contractual warranty and on the innkeeper's personal negligence. The paper may cite the duty; from this list it may not cite a modern English judgment holding a hotel or bar liable for a guest's injury.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 229, 240; alnajar2020cumberland, paras 36-37; everett2011comojo, paras 37-38; maclenan1917segar, pp. 334-335.
- Ireland enacts the reception duty in terms: "a duty to take reasonable care of the person of the guest and to ensure that ... the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them", "independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier".
  - ie1963hotel, s. 4(1)-(2).
- I found no English restaurant case in my list. *Everett* concerns a members' bar inside a hotel and does not mention restaurants.
  - everett2011comojo, para 3; full-text search of the saved judgment, 11 Nov 2026, zero hits for "restaurant".

**Reception: goods.**

- The innkeeper is an insurer of the guest's goods, "subject to well-known statutory and common law limitations".
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 328; alnajar2019cumberland, paras 183-184.
- Today that strict liability is confined to overnight guests, excludes vehicles and animals, and is capped at £50 an article and £100 in all unless one of three conditions is met.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 2(1), s. 2(2), s. 2(3)(a)-(c), Schedule.
- Ireland: the same structure, with a £100 cap as enacted and a ban on contracting out.
  - ie1963hotel, ss. 6(1), 7(1), 9.

**Reception: the contract.**

- In 1917 the guest's contract carried an implied warranty that the premises were "as safe as reasonable care and skill on the part of any one can make them".
  - maclenan1917segar, pp. 325, 332-333.
- Statute now says that a duty resting on a term implied from the contractual right to enter "shall be the common duty of care". K4's third reception duty, "to perform the contract with care", therefore needs a present-day source that my list does not contain. The scout read the Consumer Rights Act 2015 s. 49; I did not.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 5(1).

**The refuter K4 named was found.**

- A service-provider in Great Britain owes disabled people a duty to change a "provision, criterion or practice" that puts them at a substantial disadvantage. Failure "in relation to that person" is discrimination against that person, actionable in the county court.
  - uk2010equality, ss. 20(3), 21(2), 29(7)(a), 114(1)(a); Sch. 2 para. 2(1)-(2).
- v6 says: "None of these sources makes a negotiated exception something the guest is owed, and the exceptions the law recognized ran in the host's favour" (DRAFT_v6.md, line 38). The second half is wrong for present law. The adjustment duty runs in the guest's favour. So do the three conditions that lift the innkeeper's cap. **Flagged for the authors; not reworded.**
  - uk2010equality, ss. 20(3), 21(2); uk1956hotel, s. 2(3)(a)-(c).
- K4's last sentence (attending is customary, not legal) survives in narrowed form. The adjustment duty is limited to disability, is owed by reference to "disabled persons generally", requires only reasonable steps, and stops short of altering "the nature of the service".
  - uk2010equality, Sch. 2 para. 2(2), 2(7); s. 20(3).

**Placed guests.**

- For people placed by the state, the duty to accommodate lies on the placing authority and stays there when contractors perform it: "the duty remains hers"; "the providers do not" hold it. The judgment does not say who owes the reception duties inside the building. K4's question is answered for the first duty and stays open for the rest.
  - dma2020sshd, paras 99-100, 241.

### K5. Directedness: **holds for England; the duty to receive was also public; the remedy is thin**

- To receive. The refused traveller's action is "for the violation of a right at common law"; "His right, I think, is founded on the common law. That right I found was violated."
  - constantine1944imperial, pp. 693, 708.
- This refutes, for England in 1944, the reading that the innkeeper's duty was public only with no right in the traveller.
  - constantine1944imperial, p. 708.
- The duty was also enforced by indictment, and the same judge says so. Both routes existed. K5 should say the duty was public as well as private.
  - rymer1877queen, pp. 136, 139 (indictment at quarter sessions); constantine1944imperial, p. 698.
- Even in the criminal case the judges speak of what a person "has a right to insist on".
  - rymer1877queen, p. 140.
- To take care. Injured guests sued in their own names on a duty owed to them "as guests of the hotel".
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 7, 187; everett2011comojo, paras 1-2; maclenan1917segar, pp. 325, 335.
- To keep goods. The statutory liabilities run to "any guest of his" and "any one guest".
  - uk1956hotel, ss. 1(2), 2(2), 2(3).
- To adjust for disability. The wrong is to "that person", who sues and may recover damages for injured feelings.
  - uk2010equality, ss. 21(2), 114(1)(a), 119(4).
- Ireland gives both routes in one section: an offence "without prejudice to his civil liability". The offence covers the duties to receive persons and property; it does not cover the duty of care for the person.
  - ie1963hotel, ss. 10, 12.
- I found no duty in K4 for which my texts show no guest's action.
- The limit: the vindicated right was worth five guineas, and exemplary damages were refused although the judge found "much unjustifiable humiliation and distress".
  - constantine1944imperial, p. 708.

### K3. Why the duty binds: **holds, narrowed; the courts give grounds K3 does not list, and they disagree on the test**

- Assumption by invitation has a court's words behind it: "the hotel invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests".
  - alnajar2019cumberland, para 187.
- Holding out is the statutory trigger in both Acts.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 1(3) ("held out by the proprietor"); ie1963hotel, s. 1(1) ("holds itself out").
- Reliance has a court's words too: the guest "relies on the competence and prudence of its management. He expects and is entitled to expect that there will be no violence".
  - everett2011comojo, para 31.
- Neither court asked for proof that these guests relied on anything. Reliance is treated as general. The brand's safety standard in *Al-Najar* "was not a statement advertised to guests". K3's worry that reliance needs actual reliance is not borne out by these two judgments.
  - everett2011comojo, para 31; alnajar2019cumberland, paras 43, 187.
- Where K3 puts dependence, the Court of Appeal puts control: "The management is in control of the premises. It can regulate who enters". In *Al-Najar* the guest's vulnerability when asleep is a witness's evidence, not the judge's ground.
  - everett2011comojo, para 31; alnajar2019cumberland, para 40.
- The courts add grounds K3 does not list. The economic relationship: management "wants the guest to come to spend his money". Contract for reward. Occupation and invitation.
  - everett2011comojo, paras 31, 35; maclenan1917segar, pp. 329, 330, 332-333.
- Two judgments ground the innkeeper's special duties elsewhere, as K3's refuter anticipates. The strict liability for goods is explained by the "prevalence of highway robbery and the risk of possible collusion between the thief and the innkeeper". The duty to receive exists "merely to secure that travellers shall not, while upon their journeys, be deprived of necessary food and lodging".
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 329; alnajar2019cumberland, para 183; rymer1877queen, pp. 140-141.
- The duty to the guest's person does not flow from the role of innkeeper in either modern case. Dingemans J starts from innkeeper law, notes that it did not protect the person, and reaches the duty through the general law of negligence. McCardie J applies one rule to inns, theatres and taxicabs. This fits K3's claim that the role specifies and something outside it binds.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 183-187; maclenan1917segar, p. 333.
- The grounds come apart in doctrine even in the core case. The Court of Appeal used *Caparo* proximity; the High Court preferred assumption of responsibility and recorded counsel's criticism of the earlier approach. The Court of Appeal in *Al-Najar* did not choose.
  - everett2011comojo, paras 30-34; alnajar2019cumberland, paras 186-187; alnajar2020cumberland, para 19.
- Disclaimer. The 1917 warranty applied "unless it provides to the contrary". Ireland forbids exclusion of any liability under its Act.
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 333; ie1963hotel, s. 9.

### K7. The holder's oversight requires a channel from the guest: **the paper's own step; partial legal analogues for P2 and P3; no source in this list enacts C**

**What supports P2 and P3.**

- A court has declared a duty-holder in breach for failing to monitor performance by its contractors, so that it could "act immediately if there is a sign" of failure, and has contrasted contract penalties "after the event" with "securing the provision of accommodation at the time and in the individual case". This is the nearest legal statement of P3 in my list.
  - dma2020sshd, paras 236, 238, 241, 244, 333(2).
- The occupier's contractor defence is available only if the occupier took reasonable steps "to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work had been properly done". Checking is built into the defence.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 2(4)(b).
- An innkeeper was personally negligent for failing "to take such steps and to make such inquiries as would have revealed to him" a defect, after an earlier fire.
  - maclenan1917segar, pp. 325 (jury finding 3), 334.
- The Court of Appeal's examples of what a venue's duty may require: "security arrangements which can be activated as and when the need arises"; liability if "there is no one on hand to control the outbreak". These are dicta.
  - everett2011comojo, para 36.
- Reporting systems, review of incidents and supervision of officers were weighed as parts of a hotel's reasonable care.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 205, 209, 213.

**Where the law attaches a consequence to the guest's being unable to reach someone.**

- Only for goods. The proprietor loses the cap where the guest wished to deposit property "but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so". Notice of a vehicle counts when given to a servant "authorised, or appearing to be authorised".
  - uk1956hotel, s. 2(3)(c); ie1963hotel, ss. 7(1)(c), 6(3).
- Both Acts provide for a hotel with no reception desk.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 2(3) proviso; ie1963hotel, s. 7(3).

**What cuts against K7.**

- The hotel "did not need to monitor continuously CCTV cameras" or to alarm guest doors. The content of reasonable care was set by likelihood and by what comparable hotels did.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 197, 229, 233.
- A venue's duty "may be no higher than that staff be trained to look out for any sign of trouble and to alert security staff", and a waitress who reported to her manager and not to security acted reasonably.
  - everett2011comojo, paras 36-37.
- The occupier's checking duty is limited to "construction, maintenance or repair", and the words "(if any)" allow that sometimes no step is required. This is the defeater K7 names, in part.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 2(4)(b).
- Every private-law remedy in the list came afterwards and in money: five guineas; £865 17s; nothing where breach failed. The public-law remedy was declarations and £1,000 each.
  - constantine1944imperial, p. 708; maclenan1917segar, p. 335; alnajar2019cumberland, para 240; dma2020sshd, paras 333, 341.
- One statute does go beyond later damages. A county court hearing an Equality Act claim "has power to grant any remedy which could be granted by the High Court" in tort or on judicial review, and the Act contemplates interim injunctions.
  - uk2010equality, ss. 119(2), 114(6)(a).
- No text requires a channel from the guest to a person with authority. *DMA* requires monitoring by the holder; it mentions "a complaints system for users" and does not rely on it.
  - dma2020sshd, paras 243, 296.

**P4.** The legal texts give fact patterns, not evidence. A process recorded people as having "failed to travel" on a provider's portal entry, did not usually ask the provider why, and closed one case thirteen minutes after the claimant's charity wrote to ask why she had not been collected; for those with advisers, performance "may be achieved, but through pressure to prioritise the particular case over others". A hotel guest "said that she was not given any care by the hotel when she reported" an intruder; the judge made no finding on it.
  - dma2020sshd, paras 63, 79, 148; alnajar2019cumberland, para 92.

**Reading.** P2 and P3 have real but scattered legal footing: one public-law judgment, one statutory defence, one jury finding, dicta. C does not. If the dossier keeps K7, the honest wording is that the law in places requires a holder to be able to find out, that for goods it penalises a host who cannot be reached, and that the step to a guest-facing channel is the paper's.

### K8. What stays with the holder: **goods holds with a cap; the contractor rule holds as K8 states it; the contract limb is narrowed by statute**

**Goods.**

- The strict liability stays with the proprietor "even though it was not due to any fault of the proprietor or staff of the hotel". K8's first bullet holds.
  - uk1956hotel, Schedule; ie1963hotel, s. 6(1), First Schedule.
- It is capped, and K8 does not say so. It should.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 2(3); ie1963hotel, s. 7(1).
- In Ireland it cannot be excluded by notice or agreement.
  - ie1963hotel, s. 9.

**Care for the person: employees.**

- The holder answers for an employee's negligence.
  - everett2011comojo, para 9 ("for whom the defendant would be vicariously liable").
- A hotel answered, without argument, for the conduct of security officers whom an agency employed and supplied. This is silence, not a ruling.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 63-64, 213-232.

**Care for the person: independent contractors.**

- Reasonable entrustment is the rule in occupiers' law, as K8 says, and the text adds a duty to check.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 2(4)(b).
- K8 says the defence falls away "where the duty is ... contractual". In 1917 that was right: under the guest's contract "it matters not whether the lack of care or skill be that of the defendant or his servants, or that of an independent contractor or his servants".
  - maclenan1917segar, p. 333.
- Statute then set the implied contractual term about premises at "the common duty of care", to which the entrustment rule applies. On the words of the section, K8's contract limb holds only for express terms and for implied terms of another kind. **This narrows K8.** Whether *MacLenan* survives is my inference from the text; I read no authority on it.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 5(1).
- Ireland's duty "to ensure" safe premises may keep the stricter rule there. The text does not settle it and I read no Irish case.
  - ie1963hotel, s. 4(1).
- In *Everett* the security contractor was sued, obtained summary judgment and left the case. The judgment gives no reasons, and nothing was decided about the venue's answerability for it.
  - everett2011comojo, paras 3, 7.
- A statutory duty to accommodate stays with the body that owes it when contractors perform it. This is public law.
  - dma2020sshd, paras 100, 230, 241.
- My list contains no case imposing a non-delegable duty of care for adult paying customers.

**Who the holder is.**

- "The proprietor", and "shall not attach to any other person".
  - uk1956hotel, s. 1(1).
- "the person carrying on the business of the hotel".
  - ie1963hotel, s. 1(1).
- "The management", which "is in control of the premises".
  - everett2011comojo, para 31.
- "a person occupying or having control of the premises".
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 5(1).
- "A person ... concerned with the provision of a service to the public".
  - uk2010equality, s. 29(1).
- In *Al-Najar* only the hotel's own company was sued, though it sat in a group that produced brand standards and whose head of security gave the hotel's security manager general instructions. The High Court says the defendant "operates a hotel"; the Court of Appeal calls it the "owner of the hotel". Neither judgment turns on the difference.
  - alnajar2019cumberland, paras 14, 39, 43, 53, 183; alnajar2020cumberland, para 8.
- None of these texts decides between an owner, an operator, a brand and a platform. The statutory words point to whoever runs the business and controls the premises. K8's allocation among several firms remains a proposal.
- A related point for assembled hosts: lodging the traveller at another hotel in the same ownership did not discharge the duty owed at the inn he chose. And one proprietor may be an innkeeper in one part of his premises and a shopkeeper in another.
  - constantine1944imperial, pp. 694, 697; rymer1877queen, pp. 139-140.

**Law that lets the party who held out escape.**

- The occupier's entrustment defence.
  - uk1957occupiers, s. 2(4)(b).
- The words "and shall not attach to any other person": a brand or platform that is not the proprietor is outside the innkeeper's duties altogether.
  - uk1956hotel, s. 1(1).

### Points for the authors

1. **v6, line 38**, "the exceptions the law recognized ran in the host's favour". Not so for the Equality Act 2010 ss. 20-21 or the Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 s. 2(3). Sources: uk2010equality; uk1956hotel.
2. **v6, line 38**, cites secondary accounts (Bogen, Maese) for liability for harm to the guest. Primary authority is now carded: maclenan1917segar, pp. 328-329; alnajar2019cumberland, paras 183-187.
3. **v6, line 58**, borrows from *Woodland* that paid services are non-delegable "because they are contractual". For a hotel's premises the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s. 5(1), on its words, points the other way. Source: uk1957occupiers.
4. ***Calye's Case*** is dated 1604 by Dingemans J (alnajar2019cumberland, para 183) and 1584 by McCardie J's footnote (maclenan1917segar, p. 328) and by the library card.
5. The earlier `source_basis` on uk1956hotel said "Sections 2-4 not read". The Act has three sections and a Schedule.
