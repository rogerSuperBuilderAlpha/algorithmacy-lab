# WS P4, reader R5b: how the law divides responsibility to a guest among a brand, an operator, an owner and a platform (K8; inputs to K3 and K7)

**Scope.** Four judgments and two regulations, read on 11 Nov 2026 from the copies the P4b scout saved, and carded in `library/cards/`: `hayman1987ramada`, `mobil1995bransford`, `airbnb2019cjeu`, `uber2017cjeu`, `eu2022dsa`, `eu2019p2b`. The four judgments and Regulation 2019/1150 were read whole. Of Regulation 2022/2065 I read the provisions listed in part (b) and no others. I also re-read, in the saved text, paras 1-12 and 45-69 of *Crinkley v Holiday Inns* and its three footnotes, which cover the two passages the existing card relies on for apparent agency (paras 49-52) and footnote 3. I used the scout's notes to find the files and for nothing else. I ran no literature searches, so this report makes no gap statement about the literature; where it says a thing is absent, it means absent from the texts read. Every sentence about a case rests on the text of that case; where a judgment describes another case I have not read, the sentence says so.

**Locators.** Reporter star pages for *Hayman* ('at 279') and *Mobil* ('at 121'), as the Caselaw Access Project copy marks them; paragraph numbers for the two Court of Justice judgments and for *Crinkley*; article and recital numbers for the regulations.

## (a) Synthesis against K8

### Three results first

**1. In the American cases read, holding out makes a brand answer for the operator's fault and for nothing else, and only if a court finds a representation and the guest's own reliance.** The doctrine is apparent agency. It is vicarious: the brand is treated as the operator's principal, and the operator's negligence is imputed to it. None of the three courts rests the brand's liability on a duty of its own to the guest. The three reach three results on similar signage: a jury question and a verdict against the brand (*Crinkley*), summary judgment for the brand (*Hayman*), and a complaint held insufficient in law (*Mobil*).
- Locators: *Crinkley* paras 7, 9, 49-52; *Hayman* at 275, 278-279; *Mobil* at 120-121.

**2. The brand escapes in two ways, by notice and by the guest's lack of choice, and one court accepted a notice that nobody proved was given.** In *Hayman* the licence required the operator to identify itself in advertising and on a sign at the front desk; the court treated that requirement as showing no misrepresentation, although the guest swore she saw no such sign and the dissent pointed out that no evidence showed the requirement was followed. The same court held that a guest housed by her employer had not relied. *Mobil* adds a third way out: the brand's signs, products and routine support are no representation at all.
- Locators: *Hayman* at 276, 279, 281; *Mobil* at 120-121; *Crinkley* fn 3.

**3. EU law reaches a platform by two routes, control and appearance, and neither makes the platform the guest's host.** The Court of Justice treats a platform as part of the service it arranges where it exercises decisive influence over the conditions of supply; it found that of Uber and denied it of Airbnb, in cases about licensing that began with complaints by trade associations. The Digital Services Act protects a platform from liability for what its users post and removes the protection, for consumer-law liability, where the platform would lead an average consumer to believe that it is the provider. That second rule is a holding-out test in substance and needs no proof that the particular consumer relied. It removes a shield and creates no liability.
- Locators: *Uber* paras 2, 13, 39-40; *Airbnb Ireland* paras 22, 50, 56-57, 68; Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(1)-(3), recitals 17, 23, 24.

### K8's allocation, party by party

**The party that held out (the reliance ground).** K8 says the party that held out answers on the ground of reliance. The texts support that sentence in one decided case and in one statute, and each support carries a limit.
- *Crinkley* supports it. The majority held that "a jury could reasonably conclude that the Holiday Inn-Concord was operated in such a way as to create the appearance that it was owned by Holiday Inns, Inc. and that this was one of the purposes of the franchise agreement", and that the guests' evidence of reliance sufficed. The brand's liability was vicarious: the issue was "whether TRAVCO had the power to bind Holiday Inns by virtue of its appearance as Holiday Inns' agent".
  - Locators: *Crinkley* paras 9, 49, 51, 52.
- *Hayman* denies it on facts one step away. The State's own appellate court had held a year earlier that the brand made no "false representation or concealment of material fact" where its licence told the operator to disclose itself, and that an employer-placed guest "has failed to demonstrate that she relied or acted upon any representation of defendant". *Crinkley* distinguished *Hayman* on the guests' "free choice".
  - Locators: *Hayman* at 279; *Crinkley* fn 3.
- *Mobil* denies the premise. "In today’s world, it is well understood that the mere use of franchise logos and related advertisements does not necessarily indicate that the franchisor has actual or apparent control over any substantial aspect of the franchisee’s business or employment decisions." The court receded from its earlier franchise decision so far as that suggested "that logos or other trademark symbols alone can create an apparent agency". I did not read that earlier decision; *Mobil* describes it as a case in which the franchisor "actually operated several components within the complex in question", which "“represented” to the public that the franchisor was in substantial control".
  - Locators: *Mobil* at 120-121.
- The Digital Services Act supports it for platforms, in a different form. The test is what "an average consumer" would be led to believe, to "be determined objectively"; it applies "even if that may in reality not be the case" that the platform has control. The examples are a platform that "fails to display clearly the identity of the trader", "withholds the identity or contact details of the trader until after the conclusion of the contract", or "markets the product or service in its own name". The effect is that art. 6(1) "shall not apply"; the Regulation's liability rules give no "positive basis" for liability.
  - Locators: Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(3); recitals 17, 24.

**The party that controls (the dependence ground).** Control is the ground on which these texts find a party answerable for its own fault, and it picks the operator.
- The operator in day-to-day control was the party found negligent in *Crinkley* and the party the *Hayman* and *Mobil* courts treated as responsible for the premises.
  - Locators: *Crinkley* paras 7, 9; *Hayman* at 278; *Mobil* at 121.
- A brand's standards, inspections and power to terminate are not control in North Carolina, and contractual service standards were not control for the Florida majority. Shaw J, dissenting, would have let a jury decide, because the contracts obliged the dealer to "render prompt, fair, courteous, and efficient service" and "Mobil set the standard for employee/customer relations at the Mini Mart".
  - Locators: *Hayman* at 278; *Mobil* at 120, 123.
- For platforms the Court of Justice's test is control over the conditions of supply: who selects the provider, who sets the price, whether the tool is indispensable. Uber met it. Airbnb did not, because it "does not determine, directly or indirectly, the rental price charged" and does not "select the hosts or the accommodation put up for rent on its platform". The Digital Services Act uses the same mark: a platform that "determines the price of the goods or services offered by the trader" may be one under whose "authority or control" the trader acts.
  - Locators: *Uber* para 39; *Airbnb Ireland* paras 56, 68; Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(2), recital 23.

**The owner.** K8 does not name the owner. The texts treat ownership as a proxy for control. In *Crinkley* the owners were liable with the manager. In *Mobil* the owner of the land was out because it had let the premises to someone with "actual and superseding control".
- Locators: *Crinkley* paras 7, 9; *Mobil* at 121.

**Suppliers who deal only with the host.** K8 says they answer to the host. None of the six texts addresses a supplier of systems to a hotel, so this limb gets neither support nor contradiction here. One recital bears on delegated channels: a platform may hand its complaint system "to an external service provider or other corporate structure, as long as such a provider or other corporate structure has full authority and the ability to ensure compliance". That recital protects business users, not guests.
- Locators: Regulation 2019/1150 recitals 3, 39.

**No rule of priority.** Where more than one party answers, the texts give no order among them. *Crinkley* affirmed verdicts "against all the defendants" and says nothing of contribution. Regulation 2022/2065 keeps "the due diligence obligations" of a platform "independent from the question of liability".
- Locators: *Crinkley* para 9; Regulation 2022/2065 recital 41.

### What these texts add on K7

- A legislature has required every intermediary to keep a channel that is direct, rapid and not solely automated, owed to every user: "Providers of intermediary services shall designate a single point of contact to enable recipients of the service to communicate directly and rapidly with them, by electronic means and in a user-friendly manner, including by allowing recipients of the service to choose the means of communication, which shall not solely rely on automated tools."
  - Locator: Regulation 2022/2065 art. 12(1).
- The human being is in the recital and is a matter of effort: "It should be explicitly indicated when a recipient of the service communicates with chatbots", and providers "should make all reasonable efforts to guarantee that sufficient human and financial resources are allocated". The article sets no time for an answer and requires no one with authority over a decision.
  - Locators: Regulation 2022/2065 art. 12; recital 43.
- Complaint handling comes after the decision and covers the platform's decisions about content and accounts. The six months run from "the day on which the recipient of the service is informed about the decision". The platform must reverse "without undue delay" where the complaint is well founded, and decisions on complaints must be "taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means". Out-of-court bodies "shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement".
  - Locators: Regulation 2022/2065 arts 20(1), (2), (4), (6), 21(2).
- For business users the timing is earlier: reasons "prior to or at the time of the restriction or suspension taking effect", and thirty days before termination; but the provider "might maintain in force during the duration of the complaint the decision that they have taken".
  - Locators: Regulation 2019/1150 art. 4(1), (2); recital 37.
- A limit on extending P3 to platforms: "No general obligation to monitor the information which providers of intermediary services transmit or store, nor actively to seek facts or circumstances indicating illegal activity shall be imposed on those providers."
  - Locator: Regulation 2022/2065 art. 8.
- A brand's contract with its operator may itself require the operator to hear customers. The Mobil dealer contract, as the dissent quotes it, bound the dealer to "promptly investigate all customer complaints, and make such adjustments which are reasonable and appropriate". The majority did not treat that as giving the customer anything against the brand.
  - Locator: *Mobil* at 123.

### Where the sources refute or narrow K8, stated plainly

1. **A brand that held out can escape by contract.** A licence clause requiring the operator to disclose itself defeated the representation as a matter of law, without proof of disclosure (*Hayman* at 279, 281). *Crinkley* went the other way on a contractual disclaimer and a restaurant sign (para 51, fn 3). The two decisions are a year apart under one State's law and are not reconciled by any text I read.
2. **A brand that held out escapes where the guest did not choose it.** An employer-placed guest showed no reliance (*Hayman* at 279; *Crinkley* fn 3). K4 already notes that placed guests fall outside the admission tier; here they fall outside the reliance ground as well.
3. **A State supreme court denies that branding is holding out.** Logos, branded goods and routine support are not a representation of control (*Mobil* at 120-121).
4. **The brand's liability is derivative.** It answers only if the operator is liable: "there is nothing to impute" otherwise (*Mobil* at 121). Holding out adds a defendant; it does not add a duty.
5. **An owner out of possession is out** (*Mobil* at 121).
6. **A booking platform is not the provider of accommodation where the host sets the price and the platform does not choose the hosts** (*Airbnb Ireland* paras 56-57, 68). The judgment says nothing about liability to a guest.
7. **EU law's default for platforms is exemption**, and the exception for appearances removes the exemption without creating liability, for consumers and consumer-law liability only (Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(1), (3); recital 17; art. 3(c)).
8. **Guests get nothing from the platform-to-business Regulation.** "Direct impacts ... on consumers are, however, addressed by other Union law" (Regulation 2019/1150 recital 3).

### How far the law supports 'the party that held out answers to the guest'

**It supports this much.** One federal appeals court, applying North Carolina law in 1988, let a jury hold a hotel franchisor vicariously liable to guests because it ran its system so that the motel appeared to be its own and the guests chose it for the brand (*Crinkley* paras 50-52). Florida accepts that a franchisor which has "directly or apparently participated in some substantial way in directing or managing acts of the franchisee" may enter into an agency relationship with it (*Mobil* at 120). EU legislation applying since 17 February 2024 withdraws a platform's exemption from consumer-law liability where an average consumer would be led to believe that the platform itself provides the service (Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(3)), and EU law requires platforms, other than micro and small enterprises under the 2022 Regulation, to show who the trader is (Regulation 2022/2065 arts 29(1), 30(7); Regulation 2019/1150 art. 3(5)).

**It denies this much.** In North Carolina's own Court of Appeals a franchisor that held out by name escaped on a contractual disclosure requirement and on the guest's lack of choice (*Hayman* at 279). In Florida the name and logos are not holding out at all (*Mobil* at 120-121). In every American case read the brand answers, if it answers, for the operator's negligence and not for a duty of its own (*Crinkley* para 49; *Hayman* at 275; *Mobil* at 121). In EU law an accommodation platform that leaves price and selection to hosts is a separate service (*Airbnb Ireland* para 57), exempt by default from liability for what hosts post (Regulation 2022/2065 art. 6(1)), and the rule about appearances lifts that exemption without supplying a cause of action (recital 17).

**So K8's last paragraph holds, narrowed.** 'A proposal with partial legal support' is accurate. The support for the reliance ground is one jury verdict under one State's law, contradicted within that State and in Florida, and one EU provision that removes a defence. The support for the control ground is stronger and is consistent across the three American cases, the two Court of Justice judgments and the Digital Services Act; it picks the operator or, for a platform, the party that sets the price and chooses the providers. I found nothing in these texts on a system vendor.

### A wording the sources would bear (my suggestion for the dossier, not a finding)

Among several firms the law read here places responsibility first with the party in control of the premises and the service. A party that only lends its name answers for the operator's fault where a court finds that it presented the property as its own and that the guest relied on that in choosing it; a brand has escaped where its contract required the operator to disclose itself and where the guest did not choose. EU law treats a booking platform as a separate service unless it sets the price and selects the providers, and withdraws its exemption from consumer-law liability when it presents itself to consumers as the provider.

Two points for v6's paragraph on *Crinkley*, for the authors to decide. The draft reports *Hayman* through *Crinkley*'s footnote as turning on the employer's choice of motel; *Hayman* itself gives a second and separate ground, the absence of any misrepresentation because of the licence's disclosure clause (at 279). And the draft's closing words, "no court has held this", are right for the proposed allocation as a whole; for its first limb there is one decision for (*Crinkley*) and two against (*Hayman*, *Mobil*).

### Limits of this read

- Three American decisions from 1987, 1988 and 1995, in two States. I read nothing later. *Mobil* is not a hotel case.
- The later history of *Hayman* is not established. *Crinkley* cites it as "rev. granted in part, 320 N.C. 631"; the text at that page is an order that a petition for discretionary review "as to additional issues" was "denied". I searched the case lists of North Carolina Reports vols 320-323 on static.case.law for 'Hayman' and 'Ramada' and found only that order.
- The two Court of Justice judgments classify services for market-access rules. Using them for responsibility to a customer is an inference. I did not read either Advocate General's Opinion.
- Regulation 2022/2065 was read in part (see (b)). I read no guidance, enforcement decision or case law on arts 6, 12, 20 or 21, and no consolidated text.
- Regulation 2019/1150 may have been repealed. The Publications Office metadata links a 2025 Commission proposal whose title includes "repealing Regulations (EU) 2018/1807, (EU) 2019/1150, (EU) 2022/868"; I read only that title, which is not in any saved file (it came from the Publications Office SPARQL endpoint, queried for the English expression title of the work with CELEX 52025PC0837; the response is in the session scratchpad and not in the project), and did not establish what became of the proposal.
- No English authority on a brand's liability for a franchised hotel is among these texts.
