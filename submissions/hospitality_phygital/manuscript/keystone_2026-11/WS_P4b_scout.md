# WS P4b scout: who holds the host's duties under delegation, and what law already requires a channel (K7, K8)

Scope: delegation in English law, package travel, brand–operator–owner, platforms, traders' reachability, automated decisions and a human with authority, remedies and timing, and the defeaters for K7 and K8. I wrote no cards and edited no other file.

**How to read the locators.** "Read" means I read that passage in the primary text saved under `manuscript/revision_2026-11/research/sources/<citekey>/` on 11 Nov 2026. I read the passages named and no more; a reader must still read each text whole. Every quotation below was copied from the saved text. Where I read only a search summary or an abstract, the entry says so.

**Three results a reader should see first.**

1. EU package-travel law already enacts the shape of K7. The organiser answers for services "irrespective of whether those services are to be performed by the organiser or by other travel service providers" (Directive 2015/2302, art. 13(1)); the traveller must report a failure during performance (art. 13(2)); the organiser must remedy it (art. 13(3)); and the contract must name "a contact point or … another service which enables the traveller to contact the organiser quickly and communicate with him efficiently … to complain about any lack of conformity perceived during the performance of the package" (art. 7(2)(d)). Directive (EU) 2026/1024 adds art. 16a: "The organiser shall ensure that it is easily contactable". A hotel stay sold alone is not a package (art. 3(2)), so this is an analogue and no more.
2. Three statutes now forbid a contact channel that is automated only: the Digital Services Act, art. 12(1) ("which shall not solely rely on automated tools"); Spain's Ley 10/2025, art. 8; and California's AB 1609 (Business and Professions Code §§ 22625–22628, enrolled text). None is a hospitality statute, and none ties the channel to a duty of care.
3. English law lets a hospitality occupier escape for a contractor's fault, in tort and in contract. *Naylor v Payling* [2004] EWCA Civ 560 (a nightclub, a contractor's door attendant) and *Maguire v Sefton MBC* [2006] EWCA Civ 316 (a paying leisure-centre member) both went for the defendant. K8's sentence "without that defence where the duty is non-delegable or contractual" is too wide for dangers on the premises: Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, s. 5, makes the implied contractual term the common duty of care, entrustment defence included.

## (d) Defeaters

Stated as plainly as the support. Each has a located text.

**Against K8 (the holder keeps the duty).**

1. **The general rule is no liability for an independent contractor.** *Barclays* para 7; *BXB* para 58(ii). The "akin to employment" extension stops at a contractor in business on his own account (*Barclays* para 27). An outsourced security firm, a cleaning contractor or a software vendor is that.
2. **A hospitality occupier has won on exactly these facts.** *Naylor v Payling*: nightclub, contractor's door attendant, customer injured, owner not liable (paras 1, 24–25).
3. **Contract does not rescue the guest for dangers on the premises.** OLA 1957 s. 5(1) and *Maguire* para 24: the implied term is the common duty of care, so the occupier does not warrant the contractor's care. The contractual route of *Wong Mee Wan* and *Woodland* para 25(5) holds for the services the host promised to supply. It does not hold for the state of the premises unless the contract says more. K8 should separate the two.
4. **Non-delegable duty has not reached guests.** *Woodland*'s features were applied to an adult only where the adult was a patient (*Hughes* para 71). *Armes* refused the duty where the defendant's function was to "arrange for, and then monitor" (para 47). I found no English case applying *Woodland* to a hotel guest.
5. **The promise can be drafted as a promise to arrange.** *Wong Mee Wan* pp. 4–5, 10: an agent who merely arranges owes care in selection only, and which it is turns on construction. A booking site's terms say "arrange". Package-travel law closes that door for packages (Directive art. 23(1)); nothing closes it for a stay sold alone.
6. **Brands escape on reliance and on notice.** *Hayman* at 278–79 (a sign at the desk; an employer-placed guest); *O'Banner* at 213–14 (no proof the plaintiff chose the place for the brand); *Bransford* at 120 (logos alone represent nothing "in today's world"). *Crinkley* itself called the reliance evidence "marginal" (card). Consensus abstracts report the same insulation in Australia (Terry and Huan 2019, unread). No English authority either way.
7. **Platforms are protected twice.** *Airbnb Ireland* paras 57, 68 (not the provider of the accommodation); DSA art. 6(1) and 47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1) (no liability for hosted information). The exception in DSA art. 6(3) turns on what the average consumer is led to believe, which is a holding-out test and so supports K3's reliance ground while refuting any claim that platforms are hosts by default.
8. **The organiser has defences even where the statute makes it answerable.** Directive 2015/2302 art. 14(3); SI 2018/634 reg. 16(4): fault of the traveller, of an unconnected third party, or "unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances". *Kuoni* narrows the old equivalent to events outside the "sphere of control" (CJEU para 60).

**Against K7 (oversight requires a channel while performance is possible).**

9. **Reasonable entrustment can discharge the duty with no continuing supervision.** *Ferguson v Welsh*: "It would not ordinarily be reasonable to expect an occupier … to supervise the contractor's activities". The duty to act revives on knowledge: "where the occupier knows or has reason to suspect". Read with the second limb of s. 2(4)(b), English law supports a weaker P3: a holder must take reasonable steps to satisfy itself the work "had been properly done", and must act once it knows. It does not support a standing duty to keep a channel open. The guest's report is what supplies the knowledge, which is the opening K7 can use.
10. **Reachability law does not require a person.** *Amazon EU* paras 48, 52: no duty to keep a telephone line; a form or chat may do; the trader's freedom to conduct a business weighs in the balance (para 44).
11. **The statutes that do require a person give the customer no claim, or only against large firms.** AB 1609 § 22628(d) ("does not establish a private right of action"; "good faith effort"); Ley 10/2025 art. 2(2) (size thresholds); DSA art. 12 (public enforcement; I read no provision giving the recipient a remedy for breach). These support K7's conclusion as policy. They do not make the channel something owed to the guest (K5, K6).
12. **Most of the law is satisfied by a later remedy.** *Mathews* at 339–43 (a later hearing suffices; the prior hearing of *Goldberg* is the exception); *American Cyanamid* (no interim relief where damages are adequate); DSA art. 20 (complaint within six months after the decision); GDPR art. 22(3) (no timing, per the card); UK GDPR art. 22C (safeguards for decisions already taken); Directive 2015/2302 art. 14 and CRA s. 54(7) (price reduction and damages); *Kuoni* (damages). The texts that place the human before the effect are few and specific: P2B art. 4(2); Platform Work Directive arts 10(5) and 11(1); AI Act art. 14(5); and, for performance still under way, Directive 2015/2302 art. 13(3)–(4), the new art. 16a(1), and CRA s. 55. On this evidence the remedies objection in CLAIMS_FIXED.md holds in general, and K7 has legal analogues only in those named instruments.
13. **Due process is owed by the state.** *Mathews* at 332. Neither *Mathews* nor *Goldberg* binds a private host; they supply a principle (a prior hearing is due where later relief cannot be full) and no rule.
14. **Sector and scope.** No instrument that requires a human channel is addressed to accommodation as such. The AI Act duties attach to high-risk systems, and the existing card finds no Annex III area for hospitality. The UK has moved away from the prohibition model of art. 22 (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 s. 80).
