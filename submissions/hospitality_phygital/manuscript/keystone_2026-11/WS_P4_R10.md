## (a) Synthesis

### 1. What legal footing the guest's remedial rights give a channel during the stay, and where it stops

The law read gives the channel a footing of one kind only: it forbids obstructing a consumer guest who tries to exercise contractual rights, and it gives the guest remedies, but it never requires a route to someone with power to correct. That matches T4's claim that every legal channel is the consumer's own remedial right or a duty owed to a regulator.

**EU law.** The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive applies "before, during and after a commercial transaction" (`eu2005ucpd`, art. 3(1); recital 13, "during its execution"), and a decision "to exercise a contractual right" is a transactional decision (art. 2(k)). Among the matters to be weighed in deciding whether a practice is aggressive, it lists "any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract" (art. 9(d)). That is a factor inside the test of art. 8, not a free-standing prohibition (art. 9, opening words), and it protects economic interests only (arts 1, 3(3)). Public bodies and interested persons enforce it (art. 11); since 28 May 2022 harmed consumers must have remedies, including compensation, on conditions national law sets (art. 11a; `eu2019omnibus`, art. 7(1)). The nearest EU text to a channel is a pre-contract information rule: a trader's listed means of communication must "enable the consumer to contact the trader quickly and communicate with him efficiently" (`eu2019omnibus`, Directive 2011/83 art. 6(1)(c) as replaced). It names no person with power and says nothing of the stay.

**England.** The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 keeps art. 9(d) in substance but rewords and widens it: account must be taken of "whether the practice requires a consumer to take onerous or disproportionate action in order to exercise rights that the consumer has in relation to a product" (`uk2024dmcc`, s. 228(2)(f)). "Non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader" and "rights under the contract" are gone (my comparison of the texts). The exercise of contractual rights is a transactional decision (s. 245(c)), and timing relative to supply is immaterial (s. 225(5)). Weights and measures authorities must enforce the prohibition (s. 231(1)) and an aggressive practice is an offence (s. 237(3)). The guest's own redress is narrow: the prohibited practice must be "a significant factor" in the decision to contract or to pay (s. 232(4)), the rights depend on regulations (s. 233(1)), and ss. 232, 234 and 235 were marked 'Prospective' on the latest text I could read (capture of 12 Dec 2025). A barrier met during a stay already bought gives no redress under this Chapter unless it drives a further payment (my reading of s. 232(2)(c), (4)).

**The floor T6 asked for exists for disabled guests.** The Equality Act's anticipatory duty attaches to "the provision of the service" (`uk2010equality`, addendum; Sch. 2 para. 2(4)), not to what the host undertook, and is owed to "disabled persons generally" (para. 2(2)). It includes reasonable steps to provide an auxiliary aid or "auxiliary service" (s. 20(5), (11)) and to give information "in an accessible format" (s. 20(6)). A host whose only route to help is a kiosk or an app may therefore have to give a disabled guest a reachable person (my application). The exit is a step that would "fundamentally alter" the nature of the service or trade (para. 2(7)); whether a reachable person fundamentally alters an unstaffed hotel is unanswered.

**Where it stops.** None of these texts requires a channel to a person with power over the single case (`eu2005ucpd`, 'may not say'; `uk2024dmcc`, 'may not say'; `uk2010equality`, addendum). They reach consumers dealing with traders (`eu2005ucpd`, art. 2(a)-(b); `uk2024dmcc`, s. 225(3)), so not the business traveller; where a platform host is not a trader, Union consumer rights "do not apply to the contract" (`eu2019omnibus`, art. 6a(1)(c)). The Equality Act floor reaches only disabled guests and only reasonable steps (`uk2010equality`, s. 20(3)-(5)). The legal correlate Step 6 may state is therefore negative and remedial: a host may not make a consumer guest take onerous or disproportionate steps to exercise rights during the stay, and must make reasonable adjustments for disabled guests (my inference from the texts above). C itself stays in the moral register, as T1 requires.

### 2. Whether the system vendor answers to the guest directly

In the EU, for physical harm, yes; for performance failures, no. Under Directive (EU) 2024/2853 software is a product (`eu2024product`, art. 4(1)) and recital 13 names "AI systems" among the examples. "[A]ny natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product" may claim (art. 5(1)) for death, personal injury, non-professional property or data (art. 6(1)) from the manufacturer without proof of fault (art. 8(1)(a)), jointly and severally with other liable operators (art. 12(1)) and with no contracting out (art. 15). It applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 (art. 2(1)). The defect must be a want of "the safety that a person is entitled to expect" (art. 7(1)); pure economic loss does not trigger liability (recital 24), and services as such are out (recital 17). So a smart lock that injures puts the vendor in a direct line to the guest, while a lock that wrongly refuses entry does not, unless the refusal causes damage of a listed kind (my inference). A hotel group that develops guest-facing software "for their own use" is itself a manufacturer (art. 4(10)(c)) once it puts it into service in the Union (art. 4(9)) (my inference). Separately, a vendor that runs the guest's interface "in the name of, or on behalf of" the hotel may be a 'trader' for unfair-practices law (`uk2024dmcc`, s. 225(3)(b); `eu2005ucpd`, art. 2(b)) (my inference). The UK position under the Consumer Protection Act 1987 is unread. The hard-case row on the system vendor must be confined to performance failures and loss outside art. 6(1) (`eu2024product`, 'Where it bears').

### 3. What *Poole* and *YL* do to the placed guest

Both cases move the placing body's duty into public law and leave the operator's duty in private law. *Poole* holds that "public authorities do not owe a duty of care at common law merely because they have statutory powers or duties" (`poole2019uksc`, para 65(2)); a duty to protect arises, as for private persons, where the authority created the danger or assumed responsibility (para 65(3)), as by taking a child into care (paras 47, 81). The red team attached para 81 to placement; in fact it concerns "the council’s investigating and monitoring", and the housing claim was not before the Court (paras 8, 10, 77; `poole2019uksc`, critical notes). *Poole* also points against T14's gloss on the operator: "a public body which offers a service to the public often assumes a responsibility to those using the service", "whether operated privately or under statutory powers" (para 80), and a provider can assume responsibility while acting under a third-party contract (para 42) or a statutory scheme (para 73). *YL* holds, by three to two, that a private care home housing a resident placed and largely paid for by a council was not performing "functions of a public nature" (`yl2007ukhl`, Lord Mance para 123; Lord Scott para 33; Lord Neuberger para 170). Its duties to residents are "duties governed by private law" (Lord Scott, para 29); the resident keeps her Convention rights against the council (Lord Neuberger, para 149; Lord Mance, para 118), which keeps "a continuing duty in respect of the suitability of a placement" (Lord Mance, para 114). Taken together, the placed guest has public-law and Human Rights Act claims against the placing body, and contractual and tortious claims against the operator; T14's narrowing holds for the placing body, and the operator's care duty rests on ordinary principles rather than on 'control' alone (my inference from `poole2019uksc`, paras 42, 80; `yl2007ukhl`, paras 29, 118). *YL* concerned care, not hotel accommodation, and compulsion would change it (Lord Mance, para 121; Lord Neuberger, paras 166-167).

### 4. Whether a brand that runs guest-facing components is a principal

In the US cases read, it can be, on two routes, and running components is evidence for both rather than a test in itself. In *Orlando Executive Park* the Florida Supreme Court approved a verdict against the franchisor jointly with the owner-operator on apparent agency (`orlando1983robbins`, at 492, 494), and counted towards the franchisor's representation the fact that it, "rather than OEP, operated the restaurant, lounge, and adult theater at the motel" (at 494). The holding is apparent agency, not actual agency or the franchisor's own negligence (critical notes). In *Billops* the Delaware Supreme Court held that a franchisor is the franchisee's principal where the agreement "allocates to the franchisor the right to exercise control over the daily operations of the franchise" (`billops1978magness`, at 197-198), and that a mandatory manual covering "front office procedures" and "staff procedures" raised a triable issue (at 198); it decided no liability and concerned a ballroom hire. In *J.L. v Best Western* allegations that brands set rules of operation, made employment decisions, trained staff and hosted bookings pleaded actual agency (`jl2021bestwestern`, pg 36; pg 26-27), but every claim failed because neither brand nor hotel was shown to know of this victim's trafficking (pg 19, 35, 37). A brand that mandates or runs the hotel's front-office or guest-facing systems is therefore within the agency tests these courts apply (my inference from `billops1978magness`, at 198), which refutes the brand row's 'Control: No' and its 'supplier to the holder'. English authority on brands was not in my list. On several holders generally, *Wheat* (section (c)) holds that two or more persons may be occupiers at once, each owing care "dependent on his degree of control", and each "may have a claim to contribution from the other" (Lord Denning, p. 9), which supports T10's concurrent-holder picture.

### 5. The causation problem

*Gregg v Scott* blocks any move from a missing channel to damages for the guest's injury unless causation is proved on the balance of probability. The majority refused to treat the loss of a chance of a better outcome as actionable damage in personal injury (`gregg2005ukhl`, Lord Hoffmann paras 79-80, 90; Baroness Hale paras 193, 225-226; Lord Phillips para 190). Lord Hoffmann's distinction settles where each link falls: what the claimant herself or the defendant's side would have done must be proved more likely than not, and only a third party's conduct may be valued as a chance, mostly in financial-loss cases (para 83). The guest must therefore prove that she would have reported, that the report would have reached someone who would have acted, and that acting would have prevented the harm (my application of para 83). Boyd J's dissent in *Orlando* shows the same gap in a hotel case: the plaintiff's expert would not say his proposed guards would have prevented the assault (`orlando1983robbins`, at 494-495). Remedies that do not depend on that proof, such as repeat performance or price reduction for a service not performed with care, are untouched by *Gregg* (my inference; `gregg2005ukhl`, critical notes). *J.L.* adds that what reaches staff is what the law counts as knowledge: actual knowledge failed because the plaintiff did not allege "that she alerted any staff member to her need for help" (`jl2021bestwestern`, pg 35).
