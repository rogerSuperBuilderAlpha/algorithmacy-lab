## 1. The argument in one page

**What a commercial host owes.** A commercial host owes each guest it receives, for the length of the stay:
- reasonable care for the guest's safety;
- in the services it undertook, performance with reasonable care.

A host the law treats as an innkeeper owes two things more: safe keeping of the guest's goods, and, before the stay, the duty to receive. In Great Britain every provider of a service to the public must also not refuse a guest on a protected ground.

**Why the duty binds, and to whom.** The role of host specifies the duty, meaning its content, when it arises and who holds it. Three grounds outside the role make it bind, and each does different work:
- **holding out** settles who took the role up, and when;
- **the guest's reliance** makes the duty run to this guest;
- **the host's control of the space the guest is in** keeps a notice from exhausting the duty.

Control joined to reliance carries the care duty whatever the host undertook. The duty is owed to the guest, and in the law's two tiers it was also public.

**Under delegation.** What stays with a host that performs through others depends on the duty and on the doctrine that governs it:
- A host that undertook a service answers for the delegate's want of care.
- A host that only occupies premises, or employs a contractor, must choose with care and act on what it knows.
- Several parties, owners and operators among them, may hold the care duty at once, each to its degree of control, and a brand may answer for the operator as its principal (actual or apparent agency).

**The paper's own step (moral register).** Once inside, the guest is subject to the host's ordering of the space, and leaving has a cost. When the host performs through others and through systems, the guest's claim to performance can be met only if a failure in the guest's case reaches someone able to correct it while correction can still help. Two sources supply the premises. Claims justify further duties as facts change (Herstein, reporting Raz). An 'ought' transmits to its necessary means (Kiesewetter).

The guest's claim therefore justifies a further duty: to keep, during the stay, some timely means by which a failure in the guest's case reaches someone able to correct it. The paper argues that the duty is owed to the guest; that step is its own, since Herstein reports that duties a right justifies as facts change arguably strain the correlation of rights and duties. Where only the guest perceives the failure, that means must include a route the guest can use without depending on the host's goodwill. The duty asks for correction, not adjudication (UN Guiding Principles, commentary on 31(h)).

**What an algorithm changes.** Algorithmic mediation leaves the host's duties in place. On the open evidence it can move the person able to correct a failure out of the guest's path, and it tightens the host's hold on the guest's conduct. It was not shown to remove that person.

**The law, separately.** The law reaches part of the further duty by another road:
- it gives guests remedial rights;
- it counts onerous obstacles to exercising them against a trader (UCPD art. 9(d); DMCC Act s. 228(2)(f));
- it owes disabled guests reasonable adjustments (Equality Act s. 20).

No law read requires a route to someone with power to correct. In law, the further duty is a proposal.
