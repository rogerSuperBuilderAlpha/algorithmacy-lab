## (a) Synthesis

### 1. K5 and K6: is 'B has a duty to A' equivalent to 'A has a claim against B'?

1. On the standard account the two are one relation, by definition.
   - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.1.2, displayed definition ('A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ'); §2.2.3, para. 1 ('On the standard account'). `herstein2023legal`, §3.1.1, para. 1. Existing cards: `hohfeld1913fundamental`, p. 32; `thompson2004wrong`, p. 335.
2. The definition is silent on demanding, waiving and enforcing. In Hohfeld's scheme those belong to other incidents: waiving, annulling and transferring are powers, and a privilege is the absence of a duty not to act.
   - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.1.1, displayed definition; §2.1.3, para. 4; §2.1.6, para. 4. `herstein2023legal`, §3.1, para. 3 (claims are first-order norms, powers second-order); §3.1.5, para. 1 (a creditor's claim, privilege to collect and power to forgive listed separately).
3. Claims and privileges are known to come apart in at least one recognised combination.
   - Locator: `herstein2023legal`, §3.2, para. 4 ('rights to do wrong are claim-rights without privileges', reporting Waldron and George). The privilege there is to do the protected act, not to demand an owed act; the parallel is R3's reading.
4. The doubt about correlativity that Herstein records runs from right to duty: claim-rights for which no duty has yet vested. It does not run the way K5's customary limb needs.
   - Locator: `herstein2023legal`, §3.3, paras 1–3.
5. Duties owed to no one are recognised.
   - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.3, para. 2 (the duty of the affluent to give to charity). `herstein2023legal`, §4.3, para. 1 (Feinberg's legal system of 'public duties not owed to those individuals that they are designed to benefit', as Herstein reports it).

### 2. Does any account allow a duty owed to A with no claim, or no standing, in A?

6. A duty owed to A with no right in A: Wenar and Cruft record one such view, Martin (2021), in a single clause and without discussion. Martin is closed and unread. No text read here argues for it.
   - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.3, para. 4.
7. De Kenessey does not hold it. In each of his two relations the right is 'the flip side' of the obligation.
   - Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, introduction, EV p. 2.
8. A duty owed to A where A lacks standing to demand it: the texts give three forms.
   - Holders who cannot demand (infants, the comatose). The interest theory accepts their rights; the will theory cannot; demand theories share the difficulty. Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, paras 7, 8 and 17. Sreenivasan puts the same difficulty to Gilbert: `sreenivasan2019rights`, para. beginning 'If this is right'.
   - Duties that are justifiably overridden. The person is still owed, in de Kenessey's constraint sense, and cannot fittingly demand performance. Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, §2.1, EV pp. 8–9 (premises P1, P2 and conclusion C). The person keeps 'the standing to demand an explanation and (in some cases) compensation': EV p. 3, n. 6.
   - Legal rights that cannot be enforced. Locator: `herstein2023legal`, §10.3, para. 1 ('imperfect legal rights', among them unenforceable contracts and time-barred claims; 'these are not the norm').
9. None of the eight texts describes the classical guest's position: a competent adult, owed a duty that is not overridden, whom a rule of role forbids to demand it.
   - Locator: this is a statement about the texts read (table in part (b)), not a search result. Nearest passages: `dekenessey2024two`, §3, EV p. 14 (an un-overridden constraint obligation 'typically' grounds warrant to demand; no counter-example given); `sreenivasan2019rights`, para. beginning 'My first objection, then' ('I see no reason to deny that a property owner also has standing to demand').
10. The converse is argued in two texts: people who are owed nothing can have standing to demand.
    - Locator: `sreenivasan2019rights`, paras beginning 'However things may have been' and 'I am not suggesting' (Locke's state of nature). `dekenessey2024two`, §1.2, EV p. 6 (third parties' impersonal accountability).

### 3. The host's customary duty to attend: three descriptions and what each costs

What the paper's own source says (card `pittrivers2012law`; R3 rechecked each passage in the saved PDF): a host infringes the law of hospitality 'If he fails to attend to his guests, to grant them the precedence which is their due, to show concern for their needs and wishes', and 'Failure to offer the best is to denigrate the guest' (p. 516; the second sentence is not on the existing card). The guest 'has no right other than to respect' (p. 514). A guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's right to ordain 'even where custom lays down what he should wish to ordain' (p. 515). Failure to return honour 'entitles the person slighted in this way to relinquish his role' (p. 517). Of the beggar: 'even though a diffuse obligation exists towards the beggar, he is not endowed with any corresponding right' (p. 509).

**(i) Owed to the guest, with no standing to demand.**

11. For it: the code speaks of what is the guests' 'due', says the failure denigrates the guest, and gives the response to the person slighted. That is the mark of a directed duty in the sense of wronging.
    - Locator: `pittrivers2012law`, pp. 516, 517. `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.3, para. 2 (breach of a directed duty is 'a wronging of the being to whom the duty is owed', citing Thompson).
12. Cost one: on the standard definition a duty owed to the guest is a claim of the guest, which contradicts the letter of 'no right other than to respect'. The paper would have to gloss 'right' as a right the guest may assert, or read attending as part of the respect the guest is owed. Either gloss is the paper's.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.1.2, displayed definition. `pittrivers2012law`, p. 514.
13. Cost two: the description is unavailable on two of the three main families of theory. On the will theory the guest holds no right, since the guest can neither waive (a guest who refuses what is offered infringes) nor enforce. On demand theories a right is what can be demanded 'without embarrassment or shame', and Gilbert, as reported, makes being owed equivalent to standing to demand. Only the interest theory and the bare Hohfeldian form allow it.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, para. 2 (will theory), block quotation after para. 15 (Feinberg), para. 8 (interest theory). `sreenivasan2019rights`, equivalence (3). `pittrivers2012law`, p. 515. The application to the guest is R3's reading.
14. Cost three: the paper could not cite Darwall for the customary duty's direction, since both encyclopaedia entries report Darwall as a demand theorist. It would rest on Thompson alone.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, para. 16. `herstein2023legal`, §4.3, para. 1. Existing card `darwall2013bipolar`, pp. 27, 31–32.

**(ii) Not directed: a duty of the host's office or honour.**

15. For it: Pitt-Rivers frames each failure as an infringement of 'the law of hospitality', denies the guest any right but respect, and describes an obligation 'towards' the beggar with no corresponding right. Undirected duties, and duties that benefit people without being owed to them, are recognised categories.
    - Locator: `pittrivers2012law`, pp. 509, 514, 515–516. `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.3, para. 2. `herstein2023legal`, §4.3, para. 1.
16. Cost one: K5's customary limb fails as written. The guest becomes a beneficiary, and a failure to attend is a wrong, not a wronging of the guest.
    - Locator: `CLAIMS_FIXED.md`, K5 ('failing in it is a failing towards the guest'). `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.3, para. 2 (wrong against wronging).
17. Cost two: it reads against 'their due', 'denigrate the guest' and the slighted person's entitlement to withdraw, and against v6's sentence that 'the guest has duties as well as claims'.
    - Locator: `pittrivers2012law`, pp. 516, 517. `DRAFT_v6.md`, line 46.
18. Cost three: K7's customary footing ('a host that cannot be told is not attending') survives only as a claim about what the host's office requires. It stops being something the guest is owed.
    - Locator: `CLAIMS_FIXED.md`, K7, last sentence of the first block.

**(iii) Owed to the guest, who holds the correlative claim, while the guest's own role forbids pressing it.**

19. In Hohfeld's terms: a claim that the host attend, and no privilege to demand it, because the guest is under a duty of role not to make demands. Custom fixes what the host should do, and the bar falls on the guest's act of demanding.
    - Locator: `pittrivers2012law`, p. 515 ('even where custom lays down what he should wish to ordain'). `wenar2025rights`, §2.1.1, displayed definition (a privilege is the absence of a duty not to act). The Hohfeldian description is R3's reading.
20. For it: it is the description that fits v6's own distinction between standing held and standing exercised, and three texts supply pieces of it: a right that exists unclaimed, a demand that is warranted and better not made, and claims held without privileges.
    - Locator: `DRAFT_v6.md`, line 62. Existing card `darwall2013bipolar`, p. 35 (a right exists unclaimed). `dekenessey2024two`, §1.2, EV p. 6 (a demand can be warranted when there are compelling practical reasons not to make it; his example is prudence, and the extension to a rule of role is R3's reading). `herstein2023legal`, §3.2, para. 4 (claims without privileges).
21. For it also: Pitt-Rivers records a conventional way for a guest to signal a failure without demanding. He calls it 'An intermediary solution' between complying and walking out: in diplomatic etiquette a guest could 'call attention to an error of protocol of which he was the victim' by turning his plate over, until the error 'was corrected, or at least until his protest had been registered'. Even the classical code, on this evidence, knew a channel from the guest. The inference is R3's reading.
    - Locator: `pittrivers2012law`, pp. 513–514 (rechecked in the saved PDF; the phrase 'of which he was the victim' is not on the existing card).
22. Cost one: it is a construction. No source read states it of hospitality, and the step from 'claims are defined without reference to demand' to 'a claim can be held where demand is forbidden' is an inference from definitions that neither encyclopaedia entry draws.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §§2.1.1–2.1.3 and 2.1.6 (definitions only).
23. Cost two: it holds on the interest theory and the Hohfeldian form and fails on the will and demand theories, exactly as (i) does. By Feinberg's test, as quoted, a right is something that can be demanded 'without embarrassment or shame'; the classical guest cannot.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, block quotation after para. 15. `pittrivers2012law`, p. 515.
24. Cost three: de Kenessey's taxonomy does not help. A duty to show concern for someone's needs and wishes is, in his terms, a welfare-based reason, and such reasons ground accountability obligations, whose mark is the warrant to demand.
    - Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, §3, EV p. 14 (the birthday call). The classification of the duty to attend is R3's reading.

**Which is best.** Option (iii) describes most accurately what Pitt-Rivers' code does: it fixes what is due to the guest and forbids the guest to demand it. Whether that amounts to a right the guest may not claim or to no right at all depends on the theory of rights, and the texts read do not settle it. The safest wording is therefore neutral between (i), (ii) and (iii) on the word 'right' and commits only to direction in the sense of wronging.

- Locator: statements 11–24. The judgement between the options is R3's.

- **The paper may say:** in the classical code attention is the guest's due, a host who withholds it slights the guest, and the guest may not demand it (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 515–517). It may add that on the standard definition a duty owed to someone is that person's claim (Wenar and Cruft 2025: §2.1.2), that theories of rights divide on whether a claim includes the standing to demand (§2.2.2), and that the classical guest's position is a claim on one family of theories and none on another.
- **The paper may not say:** that the classical guest holds claims, without qualification (`DRAFT_v6.md`, line 46, has 'the guest has duties as well as claims' next to 'no right other than to respect'); that any source read describes a duty owed to a competent adult who is forbidden to demand it; that Darwall supports the direction of the customary duty; that Martin (2021) or de Kenessey (2024) shows 'duties owed without rights' (Martin is unread; de Kenessey denies it, EV p. 2).
- **A small point of vocabulary.** v6 says that hospitality's norms 'tend to withhold the power to press a claim' (`DRAFT_v6.md`, line 64). In Hohfeld's scheme a power changes legal relations, and a demand, as Gilbert is reported to hold, presupposes a right and creates none. What the code withholds is a liberty. Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.1.1 and §2.1.3; `sreenivasan2019rights`, para. beginning "'Standing to demand' of someone".

### 4. K6: is standing to demand part of what a claim is?

25. The form of a claim is agreed and its function is disputed. Both encyclopaedia entries say so in terms.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2, para. 2; §2.2.2, para. 1 ('spirited dispute, literally for ages'). `herstein2023legal`, §4, para. 7 ('The debate remains unsettled').
26. Will theory, as reported: yes, in the form of control. Every right includes a power over the correlative duty, to waive, annul, enforce or transfer it. Sreenivasan places demanding inside this family as a fourth 'measure of control'.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, para. 2. `herstein2023legal`, §4.1, para. 1. `sreenivasan2019rights`, para. beginning 'Still, standing to demand performance'.
27. Demand theories, as reported (Feinberg, Darwall, Skorupski): yes. A claim-right includes the authority to demand; these theories do not require a power over the duty.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, paras 15–17. `herstein2023legal`, §4.3, para. 1.
28. Gilbert, as reported: for the class she calls demand-rights, yes by definition, and being owed a duty is equivalent to having standing to demand it.
    - Locator: `sreenivasan2019rights`, equivalences (2) and (3). `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, para. 18. Gilbert's book is closed; both locators are reports.
29. Interest theory, as reported: no. A right furthers its holder's interests; unwaivable rights and the rights of those who cannot demand are rights. Interest theorists stress the claim more than powers.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, paras 3 and 8. `herstein2023legal`, §4.2, para. 1.
30. Sreenivasan's hybrid, as reported: in part. The claim gives its holder the control that serves the holder's interests.
    - Locator: `wenar2025rights`, §2.2.2, para. 19.
31. Sreenivasan in the review: standing to demand is a real and neglected aspect of claim-rights and 'a clear mistake' as their analysis.
    - Locator: `sreenivasan2019rights`, paras beginning 'I agree with Gilbert' and 'However, I am afraid'.
32. De Kenessey: the question runs two relations together. Warrant to demand belongs to the accountability relation, which depends on what the obligor ought to do all things considered. It is no part of the constraint right.
    - Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, introduction, EV p. 2; §2.1, EV p. 8; §4, EV p. 24.

**The disagreement stated precisely.** The theories differ on what a claim-right does for its holder, and so on whether the holder's control over the duty, or authority to demand it, is constitutive of holding the claim (will and demand theories: yes; interest theory and the bare Hohfeldian form: no; hybrid: so far as it serves the holder). They differ in consequence on cases: unwaivable rights, right-holders who cannot demand, and third parties who can. They do not differ, in anything read here, on the ordinary case. A competent adult to whom an un-overridden duty is owed may ordinarily demand its performance, and in law the right of action is typically that person's alone.

- Locator for the points of difference: statements 26–32 and 8–10. Locator for the ordinary case: `sreenivasan2019rights`, para. beginning 'My first objection, then'. `dekenessey2024two`, EV p. 2 ('usually') and p. 14 ('typically'). `herstein2023legal`, §10.4, para. 2. `ripstein2026theories`, §4.4, para. 2.

**Verdict on K6: holds.** Two refinements.

33. K6 can be stated more strongly for the legal duties of a received, paying adult guest. The claim follows from the duty by definition; the remedial rights and the right of action typically follow the claim. No theory of standing to demand is needed.
    - Locator: `herstein2023legal`, §3.1.1, para. 1; §10.4, paras 1–2.
34. One narrowing. On de Kenessey's argument a right-holder's warrant to demand performance lapses where the duty-bearer is justified, all things considered, in not performing. The standing to demand an explanation remains.
    - Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, §2.1, EV pp. 8–9; EV p. 3, n. 6.

- **The paper may say:** that theories of rights disagree on whether a claim includes the standing to demand it, with one sentence naming will, interest and demand theories (Wenar and Cruft 2025: §2.2.2; Herstein 2023: §4); that the guest's legal claims follow from the host's legal duties (§2.1.2; Herstein 2023: §3.1.1); and that the argument does not depend on the dispute.
- **The paper may not say:** that 'the literature is united' either way; that Feinberg, Darwall, Hart, Raz or Gilbert holds a position on the strength of having read them (each is reported); that Sreenivasan denies a right-holder's standing to demand (he affirms it).
- **On the circle with Darwall.** Nothing read reopens it. De Kenessey gives independent support for v6's order of definition: the position of the one owed comes first, and accountability is 'a normative output'. Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, EV p. 2. `DRAFT_v6.md`, line 62.

### 5. K7: answerability and accountability

35. The cleanest sourced definition is Fox's, following Schedler: two dimensions of accountability, 'the capacity or the right to demand answers (what Schedler calls ‘answerability’)' and 'the capacity to sanction'.
    - Locator: `fox2007uncertain`, p. 665.
36. Fox places answerability inside accountability as its 'soft face'; the 'hard face' is answerability plus the possibility of sanctions. His table puts 'Sanctions, compensation and/or remediation' at the accountability end.
    - Locator: `fox2007uncertain`, p. 668, including Table 1.
37. Duff gives the philosophical counterpart with a different pair: answerability and liability, two stages of holding someone responsible. To be answerable is to be the author of an action or event and to be called on to answer for it. Liability follows where no exculpating answer is given.
    - Locator: `duff2009legal`, §2, typescript pp. 4–5.
38. To whom: in Duff's example, to the person whose car was damaged ('answer to you as the owner'); in criminal law, to the court. Fox calls both of his concepts 'inherently relational'.
    - Locator: `duff2009legal`, §2, typescript p. 4; §3, typescript pp. 6, 8. `fox2007uncertain`, pp. 663–664.
39. Neither source opposes answerability to accountability. Fox makes it a part of accountability. Duff contrasts it with liability and does not use 'accountability' as a term.
    - Locator: `fox2007uncertain`, p. 668. `duff2009legal`, §2 throughout.
40. In both sources answerability is for something already done: decisions taken, an action or event attributed. Neither supplies a concept for stopping or redirecting a process under way.
    - Locator: `fox2007uncertain`, pp. 665, 668. `duff2009legal`, §2, typescript p. 4.
41. Moral answerability does not wait for fault.
    - Locator: `duff2009legal`, §3, typescript p. 8.

- **The paper may say:** 'answerability, the capacity or right to demand answers, is one dimension of accountability; the other is the capacity to sanction (Fox 2007: 665, following Schedler)'; that answerability without sanction is accountability's 'soft face' (2007: 668); that answering is a stage prior to liability and is owed to the person affected (Duff 2009, with journal pages once the version of record is checked).
- **The paper may not say:** that Fox or Duff defines answerability 'as distinct from accountability'; that either source concerns a customer and a firm (Fox writes of states, agencies and citizens; Duff of criminal law, with tort set aside at typescript p. 1); that answerability names the channel during the stay. If the paper keeps 'interruptibility' for the channel while performance is possible, answerability belongs with what follows a lapse.

### 6. K7: the continuity thesis and the line between performing and repairing

42. Gardner's thesis, in paraphrase: when an obligation goes unperformed, the reasons that made its action obligatory remain; they can no longer be met by performance, so they call for the next-best conformity still available and justify a new, secondary obligation to provide it.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 39–40. Open restatements: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3, para. 3; Gardner's 2019 eprint 'Damages without duty', p. 12 (quoted on the card).
43. The line is whether performance is still possible. The primary obligation ends at breach; what follows is a different obligation with the same rationale.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35–36, 38–39. `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3, para. 3 ('there can be no duty to arrive at noon ... Instead, there is a new duty').
44. That matches K7's phrase 'for as long as the duty can still be performed' in form.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript p. 39; `CLAIMS_FIXED.md`, K7, conclusion C.
45. It does not match K7's mapping of 'before and during' to performing and 'after' to repairing. Gardner counts obligations by the action required and the time it was due. A duty that runs for a stay is a series of things due at times; each lapse is a breach, and putting it right later the same day is already secondary conformity for that lapse, while the duty remains performable for the rest of the stay. Repair begins during the stay.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35–36 (individuation), 46 (a primary obligation due at one time yields a secondary obligation to come as near as one now can), 53 (late delivery treated on the side of remedy). The application to a stay is R3's reading.
46. The thesis gives a reason for speed that the paper can use. Next-best conformity is measured by nearness to performance, and breach followed by correction never equals performance.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35, 40, 42 and n. 56, 46.
47. The thesis grounds reparation. It does not ground explanation or apology, which Gardner expressly places outside correction.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript p. 43, n. 57.
48. The thesis is about the duty-bearer's reasons. It does not say that the person owed must be given a way to be heard. Ripstein makes the point as a criticism. Gardner brings rights in for tort's primary obligations only, by an interest theory.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3, para. 4. `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 54–55.
49. On K7's premise P3. Before breach, Gardner holds that having an obligation gives one reason to take steps that enable its performance, and that those steps are not themselves obligatory. A footnote raises, and sets aside, the thought that a step necessary for performance may be derivatively obligatory. No text read argues for P3.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 37–38 and p. 38, n. 53.
50. For irreparable wrongs the thesis does not apply, on Gardner's own later statement, and it does not explain general damages.
    - Locator: `gardner2011what`, key fact from the 2019 eprint, p. 12, n. 31; typescript p. 56.
51. A duty to explain after a lapse has published support elsewhere, stated and not argued. De Kenessey lists 'an obligation to explain myself and to compensate for my breach' among the secondary duties owed to the person whose right was infringed, justified breach or not. Duff says the owner would expect an explanation and 'could criticise me if I refused to do so'.
    - Locator: `dekenessey2024two`, §1.1, EV p. 5; EV p. 3, n. 6. `duff2009legal`, §2, typescript p. 3.

### 7. K7: the strongest published objection

52. In the legal register the strongest objection is that no duty to repair exists before judgment. As Ripstein reports Smith, 'Until the plaintiff initiates an action and prevails in it, the defendant is not under a duty to pay damages'. Civil recourse theory adds that a remedy is something the plaintiff 'is empowered to seek' and that the correlative duty 'is owed by the state to provide an avenue of recourse'.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.4, para. 3; §4.5, para. 3. Smith (2012) was not located; Goldberg and Zipursky are closed.
53. If that is right, the law gives the guest a power after a breach and the host a liability, and K7's 'duty to repair' is not yet a legal duty of the host.
    - Locator: R3's reading of `ripstein2026theories`, §4.4, para. 3.
54. The objection is disputed, and it concerns the law of damages only. Ripstein records the reply of Steel and Stevens. Gardner's 2019 reply argues that the award fixes, with effect from the wrong, a duty the defendant already had, and points to interest running from the date of the wrong and to settlement.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.5, para. 1. Gardner, 'Damages without duty', author eprint pp. 1–2, 5–8, read whole and not carded (University of Toronto Law Journal 69(4): 412–420, doi 10.3138/utlj.2019-05-22, closed).
55. Against the use K7 makes of repair doctrine, the legal analogues in tort do point to later repair. The remedy is damages or, 'in a limited range of cases', an injunction; courts prefer money; most cases settle and most damages are paid by an employer or insurer. The law's first remedy is enforcement of the primary right where that is possible, and remedial rights come where it is not.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §2, para. 1; §4.5, para. 5. `gardner2011what`, typescript p. 53. `herstein2023legal`, §10.4, para. 2.
56. One legal theory values what K7 values. Civil recourse makes the aim of a tort remedy 'to empower plaintiffs to confront and make a demand on the persons who wronged them'. It places the duty on the state, after the wrong.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.4, para. 3.
57. In the moral register the strongest objection is Ripstein's reading of Gardner (statement 48) joined to statement 49: the continuity thesis explains what a party in breach must do next and is silent on any duty, before breach, to arrange to be told.
    - Locator: `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3, para. 4. `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 37–38 and 39–40. That the thesis is silent on such a duty is R3's reading of the whole eprint.

- **The paper may say:** that a duty which can no longer be performed leaves a duty to do the next-best thing (Gardner 2011, at the version of record; or, until then, 'as Ripstein (2026: §4.3) reports Gardner'); that repair never equals performance, so that what can be corrected while performance is still possible should be; that the channel K7 requires serves performance and near-performance, and accountability serves repair.
- **The paper may not say:** that the continuity thesis yields a duty to be reachable, to explain, or to apologise; that the line between performing and repairing falls at check-out; that tort law imposes a duty to repair before suit without noting that this is contested (Ripstein 2026: §4.4); that P3 is in the literature. P3 remains the paper's own step, and on Gardner's account it needs P4: a channel is owed only if it is necessary to performing the duty.

### 9. Gardner, 'Relations of responsibility': report without quotation

The author's eprint was read whole (typescript pp. 1–26 behind a cover page; saved at `sources/gardner2011relations/`). It carries the same request against quotation and citation as the other eprint, and nothing from it is quoted here or carded. The version of record is a chapter in Cruft, Kramer and Reiff (eds), *Crime, Punishment, and Responsibility* (Oxford University Press, 2011), pp. 87–102, Crossref 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199592814.003.0006, fetched 11 Nov 2026; it is closed.

The version of record is worth obtaining, for its opening pages. Four things in the eprint bear on K7, all in the first ten typescript pages.

- It separates a non-relational ability to give an account of oneself, owed to nobody, from a relational sense of responsibility in which some person holds a right that the account be given, and it remarks that current usage often keeps the word accountable for the second (typescript pp. 1–2). This is nearer to the paper's 'owing an answer to a particular party' than Fox or Duff (2009).
- It distinguishes two positions the addressee may hold: a right that the other explain herself, and a right to require an explanation, so that the duty arises at the addressee's option (typescript p. 3; again at p. 10). In Hohfeld's terms these are a claim and a power. K7's second route needs this distinction and v6 does not draw it.
- It holds that being able to explain oneself does not make one bound to, that when, to whom and how far one must explain needs further argument, and that demands for an account made by those with no right to one deserve suspicion (typescript pp. 3–4). This is the strongest caution found against the second route.
- It separates the thesis that responsibility is always to someone from the thesis that it is owed on the strength of some other relationship, accepts the first and doubts the second (typescript p. 9), and discusses Duff's use of the relation of host and guest between a polity and its visitors as a ground of answerability, with the guest answering to the host (typescript pp. 8–9, 17). The paper runs the relation the other way. The discussion also bears on K3, since it argues that the normal ground of authority is not a prior relationship.

The rest of the chapter concerns the authority of the criminal law and is not needed. The eprint quotes Duff's *Answering for Crime* (2007) at pp. 23–25 for the relational definition; that book is closed, and it may be the better source to obtain for the definition itself.
