# Claims fixed before the search (run rr-2026-11-k), version 2

**The question.** What does a commercial host owe a guest, on what ground, from when, held by whom, and what does that duty require of a host that performs through other firms and through systems?

**How to read this file.** Each claim states what the argument needs, what would confirm it, and what would refute or narrow it. A workstream reports against the claim numbers. A claim that fails is recorded as failed and the argument is rebuilt without it.

**Three registers, kept apart.** A host's duty can be customary (what the practice of hospitality expects), moral (what a principle of obligation requires) or legal (what a named jurisdiction enforces). The claims say which register each speaks in. The argument may not prove a duty in one register and use it in another.

**The position this starts from.** On 10 Nov the authors chose the 'middle position': the law imposes the duty on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, and holding out is how a party takes the role up. K3 keeps it and says what the role does and does not ground.

---

## K1. Content, in the customary register: hosting is taking responsibility for the guest

**Claim.** Across the hospitality canon, to host is to take responsibility for the guest's safety and welfare while the guest is under one's roof. Scholars of hospitality describe this as part of the practice.

**Confirms.** Statements of the host's duty to protect and provide in the anthropology of hospitality, in Telfer's own text, in the hospitality-management definitions, in histories of hospitality, and in this journal's founding editorial (the last already confirmed at full text on 11 Nov).

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Canonical definitions that describe hospitality with no duty of the host (as exchange, experience or product only).
- Sources that describe an ideal and do not claim that hosts are bound.
- Sources that confine the duty to private or gratuitous hospitality and deny it of commercial hosts.

**Wording rule.** K1 does not use the word 'charge', which is *Woodland*'s term for custody and would carry a legal premise into the customary register.

## K2. The duty attaches to the role, and hospitableness is how the role is performed

**Claim.** Hospitality is a practice with roles (host, guest) and rules. Hospitableness is the motive or manner with which a host's role is performed. What a host owes attaches to the role, so it can bind a host whose motive is profit and a host that is a firm. The hospitality literature states the host's duty already; what it has not supplied is a holder and a trigger for a host that is an organization.

**Confirms.** Telfer's own distinction between being a good host and being hospitable; accounts of role obligation in which the role fixes the content of the duty; Derrida on the conditional laws of hospitality.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Telfer or Lashley holding that without the motive there is no hospitality, so that a duty-bound commercial host is not a host.
- Critical hospitality theory holding that a duty fixed by rules negates hospitality. The search must find how Derrida relates the conditional laws to the unconditional law.
- Hospitableness scholars who already assign the host's duty to an organization. One such source defeats the gap statement.
- **Kant.** His 'right of hospitality' may be a right of visit against states and expressly not a guest's right. If so, it supports only the general point that hospitality has been theorized as right, and is not used for the commercial host.

## K3. Why the duty binds: the role specifies, and three external grounds bind

**Claim.** The role is the public specification that holding out invokes. It fixes the content of the duty, its trigger and its holder. It does not by itself make the duty binding. Three grounds outside the role do that, and in the core case (a staffed hotel that owns, runs and brands itself) all three pick the same party and the same duty:

1. **Voluntary assumption.** The host takes up the role by holding out to travellers.
2. **Invited reliance.** Holding out leads travellers to rely on being received and kept safe.
3. **Dependence.** A guest inside the host's space, away from home, depends on this host for shelter and safety.

Where the grounds come apart, the paper says so. The run produces a table of ground by hard case (brand, operator, platform, system vendor, placed guest, restaurant, selective rental host), and each divergence is a stated limit.

**Confirms.** A philosophical source for each ground and a legal counterpart for each (assumption of responsibility; apparent agency; the 'special relationship' of innkeeper and guest in tort). A source for the view that roles specify and external principles bind.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- If the philosophical literature holds that institutional roles bind by themselves, K3 concedes less than it needs to; report it and let the dossier choose.
- Each ground has a known weakness the search must test: assumption can be disclaimed by notice; reliance needs actual reliance (*Crinkley* called the evidence 'marginal'); dependence is not specific to hosts.
- Legal sources that ground the innkeeper's duty elsewhere (crime control, monopoly, licence). K3 says what holding out triggers and does not offer it as the explanation.

## K4. Content and limits, in the legal register, in two tiers

**Claim.** In the jurisdictions the paper names, the host's legal duties fall in two tiers.

- **Admission.** A duty to receive, which the law lays on inns and public accommodations, conditional on capacity and on the traveller being fit and able to pay.
- **Reception.** Duties to those received, which fall on every commercial host, restaurants and bars included: to keep their goods (inns), to take reasonable care for their safety, and to perform the contract with care.

Attention to the particular guest ('attending') is a customary duty of the host and not a legal one.

**Confirms.** Innkeeper law for admission; occupiers', negligence and contract law for reception, with at least one case each for a hotel and for a restaurant or bar; one civil-law jurisdiction to show the duty is not an Anglo-American accident.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- No duty to protect a guest's person in a jurisdiction the paper relies on.
- A legal duty of adjustment or exception owed to the guest (for example, reasonable adjustments under equality law). If found, it corrects v6's statement that no source makes an exception something the guest is owed.
- Placed guests, who do not pay and did not choose the host. The admission tier does not cover them; the search must find who the law treats as their host.

## K5. Directedness, per duty and per register

**Claim.** The legal duties to receive, to keep goods and to take care are owed to the particular guest, who can sue on them. The customary duty to attend is owed to the guest in the sense that failing in it is a failing towards the guest, but the classical code gives the guest no right to demand it.

**Confirms.** A refused or injured guest's own action against the host, read in the primary text; the logical form of a directed duty.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Sources holding that the innkeeper's duty was public, enforced by indictment, with no right in the traveller (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 66).
- Any duty in K4 for which no guest's action exists.

## K6. The guest holds claims; the argument does not rest on a theory of standing

**Claim.** For each duty in K5 that is owed to the guest in law, the guest holds the correlative claim. 'Standing held' names that position. Whether holding a claim includes a standing to demand is contested among theories of directed duty, and the paper's main argument (K7) does not depend on settling it.

**Confirms.** A survey of the theories of directed duty read at full text; Hohfeld on claims as distinct from powers and privileges.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- If the literature is united that a claim includes standing to demand, K6 can be stated more strongly; report it.
- v6 took its form of directed duty from Thompson to avoid a circle with Darwall. K6 must not reopen that circle.

## K7. The holder's duty of oversight requires a channel from the guest (the paper's own step)

**Claim.** The argument runs from the host's duty, not from the guest's right.

- **P1.** The holder owes the guest duties that run for the length of the stay (K4, K5).
- **P2.** A holder that performs through others keeps a duty to see that the work is done with care, for each duty and under the doctrine that applies to it (K8).
- **P3 (the paper's step).** A duty to see that something is done includes having the means to learn that it is not being done, and to correct it while performance is still possible.
- **P4 (empirical).** In hospitality the guest's own report is often the only timely signal, and correction needs a person with power over the system's output in the single case.
- **C.** The holder must keep a channel from the guest to such a person for as long as the duty can still be performed.

On this route interruptibility belongs to the duty to perform (before and during), and accountability to the duty to repair (after). The timing boundary follows from whether performance is still possible. In the customary register the same conclusion has a footing in the host's duty to attend: a host that cannot be told is not attending.

**A second route, tested separately.** That a claim-holder's standing to demand requires an addressee able to respond. This is weaker (K6) and is kept only as support.

**Fallback.** If P3 fails, reachability is presented as the authors' proposal, which the simulated second reviewer already accepted as sufficient.

**Confirms.**
- A published argument for P3, in the theory of obligation or of delegation.
- Law that enacts the conclusion: statutes that make one party answerable for performance by others and require a contact point or a human reviewer; duties on traders to be reachable.
- Evidence for P4: research on complaint handling, service recovery, and forced self-service.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Sources showing that a holder discharges the duty of oversight by choosing a competent delegate, with no continuing duty to monitor. English occupiers' law may say this for some work.
- Evidence against P4: guests' reports are not the timely signal, or systems detect failures better than guests do.
- Remedies doctrine that is satisfied by damages afterwards. If the legal analogues all point to later repair, K7 stands on P3 alone and says so.
- Published concepts that already name the phenomenon. If one states K7, the paper credits it and claims the application.

## K8. Holder: what stays with the role-holder, stated per duty

**Claim.** When work is delegated, what stays with the holder depends on the duty.

- **Goods.** The innkeeper's strict liability stays, whoever lost the goods.
- **Care for the person.** The holder answers for employees. For independent contractors the rule varies: by reasonable entrustment in occupiers' law, and without that defence where the duty is non-delegable or contractual.
- **The contract.** The party that promised the stay answers for its performance by others.

Among several firms, each ground in K3 picks out a party: the one that held out (reliance), the one that controls the guest's space and person (dependence), and suppliers who deal only with the host, who answer to the host. This allocation is a proposal with partial legal support.

**Confirms.** Each rule in its primary text; any non-delegable-duty case with adult paying customers; the organiser's liability in package travel; tests of 'decisive influence' for platforms; accounts of organizations as duty-bearers.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Law that lets the party who held out escape: franchisor and platform immunities, independent-contractor defences, reasonable entrustment. The search reports these fully.
- Hard cases where the grounds pick different parties with no rule of priority.

## K9. Mediation: what an algorithm changes, before and after reception

**Claim.** After reception, the duties and the guest's claims persist under algorithmic mediation; what can fail is the channel K7 requires. Before reception, a system that decides admission can decide that the duty never arises, which is a different failure and is named separately.

**Confirms.** Evidence that guests' problems are in fact resolved through people with authority, and that automated or self-service arrangements remove them.

**Refutes or narrows.**
- Evidence that guests in automated settings reach authority as easily as before.
- Prior work that already makes this argument for hospitality.
