Five things the caller should know before the tables.

1. **Telfer's own text is closed in all three versions** (1995 article, 1996 book chapter, 2000 chapter). What is reachable is second-hand: two texts quote her with page numbers, and four more quote or paraphrase her without them (c.2). Those quotations cut both ways for K2. Her list of hospitable motives includes 'allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable' (Telfer 2000: 42, as quoted in Blain and Lashley 2014: 2), and her remark on safety is framed as tradition: 'traditionally the most important responsibility of all was the guest's safety' (as quoted in Hemmington 2007, author version p. 15).
2. **The K2 gap statement does not survive as worded.** Morrison and O'Gorman (2008, Figure 2) list as a business principle that 'Businesses have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests'. Hemmington (2007) says hospitality businesses 'must behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience'. Xiang et al. (2022), already in the library, write that 'hotels, as "hosts," are obliged to provide care, comfort, ease, welcome, and safety to the guests'. Hospitality studies has therefore named the business as holder. I found no source that argues the point from role as against motive, names a trigger, or chooses among several firms.
3. **Kant's right is a right of visit and expressly not a guest's right.** The design critique recalled it correctly (c.5a).
4. **Derrida does say the conditional laws carry rights and duties**, and he also says absolute hospitality 'should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty'. Both sentences reached me second-hand, with pages 77 and 25 of *Of Hospitality* (c.5b). The book is closed.
5. **Bot checks closed many nominally open copies.** GlobalSpec, CORE, SOAS and Napier (Worktribe), Middlesex, Taylor & Francis, MDPI, Project MUSE, OpenEdition, OAPEN and Perseus all refused automated fetches. OpenAlex returned HTTP 429 after one call, so the citing-works half of the snowball did not run. A person with a browser can open several items listed below as 'open, not retrieved'.

**Reading for K1.** The canon splits. The management definitions of 1983–2000 (Cassee and Reuland, Tideman, Pfeifer, Jones, Brotherton, Brotherton and Wood) describe a product or a voluntary exchange and name no duty. A second line (Nailon, Hepple et al., Telfer, Hemmington, Lashley, O'Gorman) puts the guest's security inside hospitality. K1's 'across the hospitality canon' is too wide; 'a line of the canon that runs from Nailon and Hepple to Telfer and Hemmington' is what the reproductions support. Each needs its own text read before K1 can be reported as holding.

### c.5 Classical and philosophical sources: what may and may not be attributed

**(a) Kant.** Read: *Perpetual Peace*, trans. M. Campbell Smith (1903), Project Gutenberg #50922, Third Definitive Article, first paragraph. 'We are speaking here, as in the previous articles, not of philanthropy, but of right; and in this sphere hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility.' The owner 'may send him away again, if this can be done without causing his death'. 'It is not a right to be treated as a guest to which the stranger can lay claim—a special friendly compact on his behalf would be required to make him for a given time an actual inmate—but he has a right of visitation.' The plain-text file has no page numbers; the reader should add the Akademie page.
- *May be attributed*: hospitality is a matter of right; the right is one of visit and no more than 'a permission to make an attempt at intercourse'; a guest's right needs a separate compact.
- *May not be attributed*: any duty of a host to protect or provide for someone received, or anything about commercial hosts. Kyslan (2026, open; abstract) reads it the same way: 'a right of visit, not a charitable duty nor a right to settlement'. Brown (2010) and Meckstroth (2017) are the standard treatments (abstracts; the first closed, the second green at Cambridge).

**(b) Derrida.** *Of Hospitality* is closed. Second-hand, both carriers open and saved:
- Page 77, as quoted in O'Gorman (2006: 51, n. 16): 'the laws (in the plural), those rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional, as they are defined by the Greco-Roman tradition and even the Judaeo-Christian one, by all of law and all philosophy of law up to Kant and Hegel in particular, across the family, civil society, and the State'. The text extraction drops ligatures, so I checked this wording against a rendered image of the page.
- Page 77, as quoted in Westmoreland (2008: 8): "It is as though the laws (plural) of hospitality, in marking limits, power, rights, and duties, consisted in challenging and transgressing the law of hospitality, the one that would command that the 'new arrival' be offered an unconditional welcome."
- Page 25, as quoted in Westmoreland (2008: 3): 'The law of hospitality, the express law that governs the general concept of hospitality, appears as a paradoxical law, pervertible and perverting. It seems to dictate that absolute hospitality should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty'.
- Westmoreland's own gloss (2008: 8, citing p. 79): the law 'requires the laws so as to not be abstract'. Bulley (2015, carded) gives pp. 77–91 for the same relation.
- *May be attributed, second-hand until the book is read*: the conditional laws are where rights and duties sit. *May not be attributed*: that Derrida treats a duty-bound welcome as hospitality in the full sense; page 25 says the opposite.

**(c) Benveniste.** Read: *Indo-European Language and Society*, Book 1, section 2 ('Giving and Taking'), chapter 7, 'Hospitality', in the Center for Hellenic Studies online edition, from the Internet Archive capture of 7 July 2024 of archive.chs.harvard.edu/CHS/article/display/3898.7-hospitality. The page has no print pagination; 'para.' below counts the blank-line-separated blocks after the heading 'Text' in the saved extraction, verse lines included, so the reader should replace these with print pages. 'The primitive notion conveyed by hostis is that of equality by compensation: a hostis is one who repays my gift with a counter-gift' (abstract). '*hostis* will signify "he who stands in a compensatory relationship" and this is precisely the foundation of the institution of hospitality' (para. 30). Hospitality 'is founded on the idea that a man is bound to another (hostis always involves the notion of reciprocity) by the obligation to compensate a gift or service from which he has benefited' (para. 31). *Hospes* comes from *\*hosti-pet-*; 'The one who receives is not the "master" of his guest' (para. 34).
- *May be attributed*: the etymology; that *hostis* first named a partner in reciprocal obligation and not an enemy. *May not be attributed*: a host's duty to protect. His obligation is to reciprocate.

**(d) *Xenia* and *hospitium*.**
- *Hospitium*: Schmitz, 'Hospitium', in Smith's *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* (1875), pp. 620–621, Roman section (LacusCurtius; public domain). **Read.** 'The obligations which the connection of hospitality with a foreigner imposed upon a Roman were to receive in his house his hospes when travelling (Liv. XLII.1), and to protect, and, in case of need, to represent him as his patron in the courts of justice'. 'Private hospitality thus gave to the hospes the claims upon his host which the client had on his patron'. It was hereditary, and dissolved only by formal *renuntiatio*. An 1875 reference work; Nicols (2011, *Oxford Handbook of Social Relations in the Roman World*) is the modern account, closed.
- *Xenia*: Benveniste, para. 32 (**read**): '*xénos* (ξένος) indicates relations of the same type between men bound by a pact which implies precise obligations that also devolve on their descendants', 'placed under the protection of Zeus Xenios'. O'Gorman (2005, *JHTM* 12(2): 141–151; open, saved; keyword passages read) gives a hospitality-studies account of both institutions and cites Schmitz and Reece. Žbontar (2013, *Ars & Humanitas*, open) says in its English abstract that 'There were no formal laws governing xenía – it was based entirely on a moral appeal'; the body is in Slovenian. Reece (1993) is closed.
- *May be attributed*: both were institutions of reciprocal obligation; the Roman one bound the host to receive and to protect and gave the guest claims. *May not be attributed*: that either governed commercial inns. O'Gorman (2007, 'Discovering commercial hospitality in ancient Rome', open, saved) treats the inns separately.

## (d) Defeaters

### Against K1 (to host is to take responsibility for the guest)

- **D1. The management canon defines hospitality with no duty.** Brotherton and Wood's exchange is 'voluntarily entered into' (as quoted in Hemmington 2007, author version p. 6); Cassee and Reuland, Tideman, Pfeifer and Jones define a product (c.1). Slattery (2002, carded) belongs to this line.
- **D2. Where the duty is stated, it is often stated as past.** Telfer's sentence begins 'traditionally'; Lashley (2014, abstract) says the obligations 'no longer have the central importance that they once did'; Lashley (2015: 3, carded) says the same. K1's refuter 'an ideal, not a claim that hosts are bound' applies to this line until Telfer's text is read.
- **D3. In early English law the protection was the host's entitlement.** Hosts 'were entitled to protect guests' (Lambert 2016, abstract), and the compensation for a breach went to the host. The guest was protected; the right was the host's.
- **D4. King's obligation is unverified.** Two sources attribute a host's obligation of safety to King (1995). The reproductions of his definition that I could read speak of the host's understanding and generosity, and of what the guest owes.

### Against K2 (the duty attaches to the role; the literature lacks a holder for an organizational host)

- **D5. The gap statement.** Morrison and O'Gorman (2008, Figure 2), Hemmington (2007) and Xiang et al. (2022) each place responsibility for guests' safety on the business or the hotel. **This is the strongest defeater I found**, and it defeats a sentence and not the argument: none of the three argues the point, and none says when the duty starts or which firm holds it.
- **D6. Hospitality at the level of the organization already has a literature, built on virtue and culture.** Dogru et al. (2025), Line et al. (2025), Bilgihan et al. (2023), Pizam (2020). All abstracts. A reviewer from that school could say the organizational host is theorized already.
- **D7. Koo and Kwon (2026)** place 'ethical responsibility within socio-technical design' in the machine age. Abstract only.
- **D8. Telfer's hospitableness is a matter of motive**, and a good host need not be hospitable (paraphrases in Blain and Lashley 2014: 3 and Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1287). Against that, her list of motives includes allegiance to duties of hospitality (2000: 42, second-hand), so 'duty versus hospitableness' is Lashley's contrast more clearly than hers.
- **D9. Derrida, *Of Hospitality* p. 25** (second-hand): absolute hospitality 'should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty'. A critical hospitality scholar can hold that a welcome fixed by rules falls on the conditional side, which Derrida calls a transgression of the law of hospitality (p. 77, second-hand).
- **D10. Kant gives no guest's right** (read). The paper cannot cite Kant for anything owed to a person received.
- **D11. The commercial relation is not a host–guest relation.** Slattery (2002, carded); Aramberri (2001), as reported by Lynch et al. (2011); Ritzer (2007), as reported by Lashley (2015: 5); Sutton (2024, abstract), who relays the view that the restaurant guest 'is not, in fact, a guest in the traditional sense, but a paying customer'. None of the last three was read. I found no scholar who grants the host–guest relation in commerce and then denies the duty.
