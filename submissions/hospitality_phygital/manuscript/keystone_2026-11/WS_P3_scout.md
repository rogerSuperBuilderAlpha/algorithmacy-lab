## What the search found, by question

1. **Theories of directed duty.** K6's 'contested' is confirmed at full text in two open encyclopaedia entries and one open review. Wenar and Cruft write that which theory of the function of rights is better 'has been the subject of spirited dispute, literally for ages' (SEP 'Rights', §2.2.2, para. 1), and Herstein that 'The debate remains unsettled' (SEP 'Legal Rights', §4, the paragraph on extensional adequacy). The positions on standing to demand, as those two entries state them: the will theory makes the right-holder a 'small scale sovereign' with a power to waive, annul or enforce the duty (SEP 'Rights' §2.2.2; SEP 'Legal Rights' §4.1); the interest theory needs no such power and 'tend[s] to emphasis claim-rights more than powers' (SEP 'Legal Rights' §4.2); demand theories (Feinberg, Darwall, Skorupski) make the standing to claim or demand what a claim-right is (SEP 'Rights' §2.2.2; SEP 'Legal Rights' §4.3); Sreenivasan's hybrid gives the holder the control that serves her interests (SEP 'Rights' §2.2.2); Gilbert defines a separate class of 'demand-rights' by the standing to demand (same section). Sreenivasan, reviewing Gilbert, calls it 'a clear mistake to make standing to demand pivotal to the analysis of claim-rights per se' (NDPR, 1 June 2019, para. beginning 'However, I am afraid'). **One narrowing.** The dispute I read is about whether standing to demand is *constitutive* of being owed a duty, and whether it is *necessary* (infants, unwaivable rights). I found no theorist who denies that a competent adult to whom a duty is owed may ordinarily demand its performance; Sreenivasan himself sees 'no reason to deny that a property owner also has standing to demand' (same review). That is my reading at the depth stated below, not a finding.
2. **Claiming.** Feinberg (1970), Gilbert (2018), Wallace (2019) and Darwall (2006) are all closed, with no author or repository copy. Each position is reachable in an open text: Feinberg through SEP 'Legal Rights' §4.3 and Darwall (2013: 30, carded); Gilbert through her own open précis (2023) and open reply to Kamm (2025), and through Sreenivasan's review; Wallace through Kumar's NDPR review and through Vandieken (2019, 2026) and Schaab (2023), all open.
3. **Answerability.** The philosophers' triad does not give the paper its distinction. Shoemaker's 'answerability' responds to an agent's evaluative judgments and Smith's is close to attributionism (SEP 'Moral Responsibility' §3.1.3); neither is 'owing an answer to someone'. Three open texts do give it. Fox (2007: 665, 668) separates 'the capacity or the right to demand answers (what Schedler calls "answerability")' from 'the capacity to sanction', and calls answerability accountability's 'soft face'. Duff (2009, repository copy, typescript p. 3) separates answerability from liability as two stages of holding responsible. Gardner (2011, author eprint, typescript pp. 1–3) separates a non-relational 'basic responsibility', the ability to give an account, from the relational sense for which 'the word "accountable" is often reserved', and adds that someone must have 'a right that this ability be exercised'. Fox is the cleanest open source for a definition; it reports Schedler (1999, closed) at second hand.
4. **The second route to K7.** I found no published argument that holding a claim requires a reachable addressee, so that a duty-bearer who makes itself unreachable wrongs the claim-holder. The nearest supports are Duff's relational answerability (closed; quoted in Gardner's eprint), Grant, Katzenstein and Kennedy (2024, closed) on the cover-up as a second wrong, Walker (2006, closed) on victims' 'right to validation' (publisher abstract), and civil recourse theory, which puts the duty to provide an avenue on the state (SEP 'Torts' §4.4–4.5). The denials are stronger and several are open; they are listed in part (d). K7 is right to keep this route as support only.
5. **Duties after breach.** Gardner's continuity thesis is verified at the author's eprint (typescript pp. 39–40): the secondary obligation is 'a rational echo of the primary obligation', owed '[o]nce the time for performance of a primary obligation is past, so that it can no longer be performed'. That matches K7's timing boundary. The standard open map is SEP 'Theories of the Common Law of Torts' §§4.2–4.5 (Ripstein, revised 15 June 2026). The opposition is Smith (2012) and civil recourse theory: on that view no duty to pay damages exists until a court orders it. Jonker joins the two topics. He rejects the demand theory of directedness as 'either inaccurate, or too obscure' and puts in its place a 'Repair Theory': the party owed 'is the proper recipient of apology and redress' and holds the power to forgive (dissertation, ch. 2, §§11 and 14; PDF pp. 29, 32). I read those passages in his open dissertation, and none of the 2020 article.
6. **Defeaters.** Part (d).

## (d) Defeaters

**K5.**

1. **The paper's own form of directedness collides with the customary limb.** K5 says the duty to attend is owed to the guest, yet the guest has no right to demand it. On the standard account a duty owed to Y and Y's claim are one relation: 'A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ' (SEP 'Rights' §2.1.2), and Thompson's table, which v6 cites, sets 'X has a duty to Y to do A' beside 'X has a right against Y' (card `thompson2004wrong`, p. 335). Wenar and Cruft call the view that one can be wronged without being owed a duty a departure from 'the commonly accepted thesis' and cite Martin (2021) alone for directed duties that 'do not correlate with rights' (§2.2.3). The customary limb therefore needs one of three minority positions, and they are not equivalent: Martin's obligations without rights (abstract), Attie-Picker's denial that obligation entails entitlement (abstract), or de Kenessey's two relations (abstract). None is read. Martin's obligee may still blame; the guest of the classical code may not.
2. **A right of action shows directedness; its absence does not refute it.** Unenforceable 'imperfect' legal rights exist (SEP 'Legal Rights' §10.3). K5 should argue from the guest's action to the directed duty and not the other way.

**K6.**

3. **'Contested' is true of constitution and necessity, and may overstate the ordinary case.** See question 1. If the paper says only that a paying adult guest owed a legal duty may demand its performance, I found no theory that denies it. K6 could be stated more strongly for that case, on a full read of May (2015) and one primary interest theorist.
4. **'Standing' is itself unsettled.** Radzik's abstract reports that no single account fits its current use; Liau (carded) reserves it for the power to sue; Sreenivasan shows that standing to demand and being owed can come apart in both directions (review, the paragraphs on property and on Locke).
5. **The Darwall circle.** Bradley (2026) and Cornell (carded at abstract depth) reject the tie between obligation and accountability; de Kenessey separates the two relations. All three support K6's separation, and all three are unread.

**K7.**

6. **No duty to repair before a court says so.** 'Until the plaintiff initiates an action and prevails in it, the defendant is not under a duty to pay damages (Smith 2012; 2019)' (SEP 'Torts' §4.4). Civil recourse theory holds that a remedy is 'something that the plaintiff is empowered to seek from the defendant rather than something that the plaintiff is entitled to receive', and that 'the relevant duty is owed by the state to provide an avenue of recourse' (§§4.4, 4.5). On that view the law gives the guest a power and the host a liability afterwards, and no duty to give an account. Ripstein records that the claim 'has also been disputed (Steel & Stevens 2020)' (§4.5), and Gardner replies in 'Damages without duty'. K7's 'duty to repair' is safe in the moral register and contested in the legal one.
7. **Corrective duties have no agreed ground.** Fornaroli's abstract finds both leading views unsatisfactory; Cornell's book abstract (carded) says rights 'do not tell us what corrective action is appropriate after a violation'.
8. **The second route.** Six denials, in descending order of how much I read:
   - Sreenivasan: standing to demand is not what makes a duty owed (read). Jonker agrees: a demand theory is 'inaccurate' if it means that only the party owed may demand (dissertation, §14, PDF p. 32).
   - Gardner: the ability to answer is owed to nobody, and a duty to explain oneself to a given person needs 'further arguments'; he calls 'the cult of "accountability" ... a plague of our age' (eprint pp. 1, 3–4).
   - Darwall: the right exists unclaimed, and what fails without an addressee is the act (carded, pp. 31, 35). Kong: being answerable is not being held answerable (carded, p. 4). Hohfeld: correlativity says nothing about reach (carded).
   - Conversational theories make the addressee's *capacity* to understand a condition of intelligible blame (SEP 'Moral Responsibility' §3.3, on Watson, Wallace and McKenna). They state no duty to be available.
   - Macnamara denies that blame is a demand at all (same section).
   - Cruft: the powers to demand have third-party parallels (chapter abstract).
9. **P3 is not in the literature as stated.** The nearest are Hindriks' 'duty of control' and the 'duty to seek to know' in vigilance law, both at abstract depth. P3 should be reported as the paper's own step unless a reader finds it in Hindriks or Whittle.
10. **A concept may already name the phenomenon.** Knight and Goldstein's 'ambiguous actorhood' and Grant et al.'s account of thwarted accountability are candidates. Both go to P5.
