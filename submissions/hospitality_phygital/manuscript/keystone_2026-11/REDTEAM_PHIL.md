# Red team: the philosopher of obligation on ARGUMENT.md

Line numbers refer to `ARGUMENT.md` as it stood on 11 Nov 2026 (350 lines). Footing labels follow the brief: (a) text read this session, with locator; (b) the dossier against itself; (c) lead, unread.

## 1. Verdict

The dossier fixes what a commercial host owes in law, and to whom, about as far as its sources allow. It does not fix the duty it calls its own. Step 6 attaches a moral premise (P3) to legal premises (P1, P2) and then files the conclusion under the legal duty of Step 2, which breaks the file's own rule on registers. For P3 it borrows Hindriks's 'duty of control': a separate second-order duty that is owed to no one in particular. Yet the Answer says the channel is owed to the guest 'as part of the duty it already has'. The derivation also needs the channel to be a necessary means, and P4 shows only a main route. Sub-step 5 shows that someone or something must be able to override the system's output in the single case. It does not show that this must be a person. And the cost limit turns 'must keep a channel' into 'must take reasonable care', so the conclusion settles nothing that reasonable care had not already settled. Step 3's division of binding work and Step 4's customary footing are weaker than stated, but C does not rest on them. The argument is weakest where P3 meets C, on three questions: what kind of duty the channel is, whose duty it is, and to whom it is owed. A text the run already holds (Herstein §1, §8.4) offers a route that would answer all three: treat the channel as a derivative duty grounded in the guest's own claim to performance, and made necessary by the guest's dependence inside the host's controlled space. The dossier does not use that route.

## 2. Objections, ranked

### Fatal

**F1. Step 6 proves its key premise in one register and uses it in another.**
- *Step attacked.* Step 6, P1–P3 and C; 'How to read this file', first bullet.
- *Objection.* The file promises that 'No step proves a duty in one register and spends it in another' (line 15). In Step 6, P1 and P2 rest on legal authority (lines 174–180), P3 is 'offered in the moral register' (line 218), and C is then said to stay 'inside the duty K4 established' (line 235), which is a legal duty (Step 2's heading, line 50). No step establishes a first-order *moral* duty of the host to the guest. Step 1 is customary, Step 2 is legal, and Steps 3–7 carry no register label at all, which breaks the file's own rule. So C is one of two things: a legal claim that the dossier itself concedes is not law (lines 218, 270), or a moral claim whose first-order premise has not been shown in that register.
- *Footing.* (b) lines 15, 22, 50, 71, 127, 142, 169, 174–182, 218, 235, 270.
- *Severity.* Fatal as stated, and repairable.
- *What would answer it.* Re-argue Step 6. Label the register of each of Steps 3–7. State the first-order duty as a moral duty in Step 3 (assumption, reliance and control as moral grounds, with a named source such as Goodin 1985 or Scanlon 1998 ch. 7, both already on the debts list). Then run P1–C entirely in that register and use the legal material as analogue only. Drop the claim that C stays inside the K4 duty, or replace it with the narrower legal claim the cases support: courts weigh reporting arrangements as part of reasonable care (`alnajar2019cumberland`, paras 205–213).

**F2. The form of the conclusion contradicts the source used for P3, and the dossier places the wrong in two incompatible places.**
- *Step attacked.* P3 (line 184); C (lines 232–235); the Answer, third paragraph (line 11); objections table, row 3 (line 311).
- *Objection.* The dossier describes the channel as part of the existing duty: P3 says the duty 'includes' the means of learning, and the Answer says the host owes the channel 'as part of the duty it already has' and that the channel is 'not a new right'. Line 235 puts the failure in 'the duty of performance'. But Hindriks, the source chosen over Gardner, proposes a *distinct* obligation: 'I propose that the duty of control is a second-order obligation. It requires the agent to meet the prerequisites for fulfilling first-order obligations' (pp. 4–5). Gardner counts obligations by the action each requires and says that enabling steps are reasons and not obligations (typescript pp. 35–38, paraphrase per the card). On the dossier's own sources, then, the channel is either a new, second-order duty or no duty at all. There is a further problem. Line 216 says a design with no means of learning 'is then itself the failure' (Hormio's benighting act). Line 235 says the host fails only where 'performance then fails in a way a report would have let it correct'. On the first account, two hosts with the same design are both wrongdoers. On the second, whether either is a wrongdoer depends on luck.
- *Footing.* (a) `hindriks2025moral`, pp. 4–5 (saved version of record); `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35–38 (saved eprint, read for the bus-fare passage and n. 53). (b) lines 11, 184, 216, 235, 311.
- *Severity.* Fatal as stated.
- *What would answer it.* Choose one form and argue for it. (i) If the channel is a second-order duty, follow Hindriks: say so, drop 'part of the duty it already has' and 'not a new right', and supply the directedness separately (S1). (ii) If the channel is part of the first-order duty, find a source for that inclusion other than Hindriks. Either way, decide whether the wrong is the design (line 216) or the uncorrected failure (line 235).

### Serious

**S1. A means-duty derived from a directed duty is not thereby directed, so 'owes the guest' is not delivered.**
- *Step attacked.* The Answer, 'It owes the guest that channel' (line 11); Step 4, 'What follows for Step 6' (line 138); C.
- *Objection.* Nothing in P1–P4 makes the derived duty owed to the guest. Hindriks grounds the duty of control in the risk an agent poses 'to others' and never says that it is owed to anyone (pp. 4–5). Ripstein says that Gardner's account differs from Weinrib's 'because of its exclusive focus on the relation between wrongdoers and the reasons that apply to them, rather than the relation between the parties' (§4.3, para. 4). The same holds of any duty derived through the holder's own reasons for the means. On line 235's account the guest is wronged only if a correctable failure then occurs. A host that keeps no channel and suffers no such failure has wronged no guest, so 'owes the guest the channel' says more than the argument shows. Step 4's remark that demand and interest theorists 'can both accept' the channel concerns acceptance, not direction.
- *Footing.* (a) `hindriks2025moral`, pp. 4–5; `ripstein2026theories`, §4.3, para. 4 (saved text). (b) lines 11, 138, 171, 235.
- *Severity.* Serious for C; fatal for the wording of line 11.
- *What would answer it.* A new argument from a text already in the library. Herstein reports that one right can justify another when the first depends on the second for its security ('The stronger the dependency, the stronger the justification'). The entry illustrates this with *Griswold*'s 'Without those peripheral rights, the specific rights would be less secure' (§1, para. 8, citing Wellman 1995 and Nickel 2007). Herstein also reports that one claim-right can count in favour of 'different and changing duties, for instance given changes in factual circumstances', and so justify 'new previously unrecognized duties' (§8.4, para. 2; Raz 1986: 168–170 at §1, para. 7). On this route the channel is a derivative duty owed to the guest, justified by the guest's claim to performance. Delegation and automation are the changed circumstances. The route costs the sentence 'The channel is not a new right of the guest', since the channel becomes a derivative right. It does not cost the argument's independence from the theory of standing to demand. Herstein's entry concerns legal rights, so the moral use needs Raz read (lead 1).

**S2. The step from P3 to C needs the channel to be a necessary means, and P4 does not show that it is.**
- *Step attacked.* P3 to C via P4; 'What P4 does to C' (line 230).
- *Objection.* Every route the dossier's library offers from an obligation to a duty to take its means requires that the means be necessary. Gardner's footnote entertains a derived obligation only for acts 'both necessary and sufficient' (typescript p. 38, n. 53). The capacitarians Rudy-Hiller reports hold that norms of awareness bind 'because awareness of certain considerations is necessary for fulfilling moral obligations' (§3.4, para. 6). P4 shows only that the guest's report is 'the main route' (line 220). Line 230 defines the class for which the channel is 'necessary' as failures 'that the guest perceives and that neither the system nor the guest can cure'. That is not the same class as failures that *only* the guest's report can bring to the holder in time. Kiosk monitoring, staff rounds or deployer logs can bring the same failures, and the dossier's own evidence shows a worker meeting every guest when the kiosk was down (line 227). Nor is a channel sufficient: it works only together with the person and the act of correction.
- *Footing.* (a) `gardner2011what`, typescript p. 38, n. 53; `rudyhiller2022epistemic`, §3.4, para. 6. (b) lines 220–230.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* One of three repairs. (i) Narrow C to failures whose only timely route to the holder is the guest's report, then argue that this class is large in hosting. (ii) Concede that the duty is disjunctive: the host must have some timely means of learning, and a guest channel is one such means. (iii) Supply a transmission principle that holds without necessity (lead 3 is the standard defence of transmission and should be checked for whether it reaches non-necessary means).

**S3. Brandt's view of delegation is reactive, and his argument against transfer does not cover duties grounded in control.**
- *Step attacked.* P2 in the moral register (line 182); Step 5, 'the duty of care for the person follows control' (lines 9, 152).
- *Objection.* Brandt's delegator must step in when the delegate 'does not complete the task' or when 'it becomes clear' that the delegate is failing (ms pp. 3, 5). He treats 'a mechanism for finding out how their offspring are faring' as something policy might offer providers who 'so wish, but not force them to do so' (ms p. 15). He also thinks it 'unlikely that they will ever have to act on their responsibilities' (ms p. 14). His case against transfer rests on two grounds: an agreement with the party owed (the library book, ms p. 10), and having put an unconsenting person at risk (ms pp. 7, 13). Neither ground is control. Yet Step 5 lets the care duty follow control among firms. By Brandt's own definition that is a transfer, after which the first party 'no longer has any trace' of the duty (ms p. 3). So where control of a space passes to another firm, nothing remains with the first host for P3 to attach to.
- *Footing.* (a) `brandt2017transfer`, ms pp. 3, 5, 7, 10, 13–15 (saved accepted manuscript). (b) lines 9, 152, 161, 182.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* Narrow the claim. Confine the Brandt-based form of P2 to duties grounded in the host's undertaking, where his agreement argument applies. Concede that for a care duty grounded in control the residue is the occupier's care in choosing. Alternatively, supply a new argument for why a duty grounded in control cannot be shed by handing control to another firm.

**S4. Culpable ignorance presupposes the duty of inquiry that P3 needs, so it cannot ground it.**
- *Step attacked.* P3, 'So P3 stands as a claim about what care requires', bullets 2–3 (lines 211–212); the wording for draft 2 (line 216).
- *Objection.* In Rudy-Hiller's exposition of tracing, the benighting act is wrong 'since John was under a "procedural epistemic obligation" (Rosen 2004: 301) to inform himself' (§2, para. 4). Culpable ignorance is therefore a consequence of a prior duty of inquiry, not its ground. This is the same circle the dossier concedes for the knowledge triggers (line 205). Hormio asks when an organisation is *blameworthy* for ignorance (p. 1). She exempts what is 'defensibly outside the focus of the organisation' (p. 5). For ignorance that design produces without intent, she places the forward-looking duty only after 'problems emerge, or a likelihood of bad outcomes is pointed out' (pp. 20–21). Culpable ignorance also matters only where there is a first-order wrong for the ignorance to excuse. For an occupier whose duty is care in choosing, a competent contractor's failure is not the occupier's own wrong. So Hormio cannot supply the duty in the very case where P3 most needs it (line 218).
- *Footing.* (a) `rudyhiller2022epistemic`, §2, para. 4; `hormio2018culpable`, preprint pp. 1, 5, 17, 20–21. (b) lines 205, 211–212, 216, 218.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* Concede and re-argue. Keep Hormio for blame once a duty has been shown. Take the derivation instead from the capacitarian claim that norms of awareness 'are derivative' of moral obligations (Rudy-Hiller §3.4, para. 6, reporting Sher 2009 and Murray 2017). That claim needs S2's necessity premise. The procedural obligation itself requires reading lead 5.

**S5. The dossier prefers Hindriks to Gardner without argument, and Hindriks's duty of control does not reach what P3 needs.**
- *Step attacked.* P3, 'The argument' and 'Philosophy' (lines 187, 191); objections table, row 3 (line 311).
- *Objection.* The dossier says it 'sides with Hindriks' against Gardner but gives no argument for the choice. Hindriks's own support for the principle is one sentence from Goodin and the example of an addicted father (p. 4). His duty of control secures *moral* control: the ability to 'recognize moral considerations and react to them', illustrated by a drunk driver and an addicted father (p. 3). A position to learn a fact about a delegate's work is a matter of information, not moral competence, and a host that responds perfectly to reasons can still be ignorant of facts. Hindriks also bounds the duty by foreseeability. In 'an unforeseen and unprecedented pandemic' the hospital is excused (p. 10). For 'a brand-new machine that malfunctions', 'the agent was not obligated to prepare for those factors' (p. 14). Yet sub-step 5 makes unforeseen failures the channel's main target. On outsourcing, Hindriks's one case puts the blame on the hired agency: 'this agency is to blame' (p. 10, n. 24).
- *Footing.* (a) `hindriks2025moral`, pp. 3, 4, 10 and n. 24, 14. (b) lines 187, 191, 247, 311.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* A new argument. Either show that a general fall-back for unforeseen failures in the single case is itself a foreseeable precaution (foreseeability judged by type; lead 12), and that access to information falls under receptivity. Or ground P3 elsewhere: Goodin on seeing to it that something is done (lead 2), or the derivative-rights route (S1).

**S6. Sub-step 5 ends its regress by stipulation and shows a need for override power, not for a person.**
- *Step attacked.* 'Why the channel must reach someone with power over the single case', sub-step 5 (lines 241–249); C's 'someone'.
- *Objection.* Sub-steps 1–4 show that correcting a failure that is not transient requires authority to depart from what the arrangements produced. They do not show that the authority must sit with a person. Sub-step 5 stops the regress by positing 'an agent able to judge an unforeseen case'. But the person at the end also acts on training and policy and also fails in cases nobody foresaw. The dossier's own evidence says reception staff 'did not know where the line fell' and that power over the single case was 'graded and unwritten' (line 224). From 'no configured layer covers every case' it follows only that no finite arrangement covers every case, whether or not a person stands at the end of it, and a duty of reasonable care does not require complete coverage. The appeal to legislation is narrower than the dossier presents it. Art. 14(4) requires a high-risk system to be provided so that persons 'to whom human oversight is assigned are enabled, as appropriate and proportionate', to override it. The article applies to high-risk systems only, and the run's card finds that Annex III names no accommodation area.
- *Footing.* (a) Reg. (EU) 2024/1689, art. 14(4), chapeau and (d) (saved Official Journal text). (b) lines 224, 241–249; card `eu2024aiact`, Annex III note.
- *Severity.* Serious. The dossier flags sub-step 5 as the weakest link (line 249) but keeps 'someone' in C.
- *What would answer it.* Narrow C. Restate it as 'power to depart from the arrangements' output in the single case', which is neutral on automation. Require a natural person only where no configured process covers the type of failure and the stakes warrant it. Alternatively, find evidence that people correct the failures guests report better than a further automated layer does.

**S7. The mapping of interruptibility to performance and accountability to repair fails on the dossier's own lapse-by-lapse reading, and C's time limit has no principled end.**
- *Step attacked.* 'When: performance, then repair' (lines 251–262); C's 'for as long as the duty can still be performed'.
- *Objection.* The dossier adopts the lapse-by-lapse reading: correcting a lapse later the same day 'is already repair of that lapse' (line 255). Then most of what the channel carries during a stay is repair. The mapping 'Interruptibility belongs to performance, and accountability to repair' (line 256) is therefore false by the dossier's own correction. Further, Gardner's secondary obligation has 'the same rationale' as the first (line 253). Applied to that secondary obligation, P3 gives the host a duty to keep a means of learning after check-out as well, so 'for as long as the duty can still be performed' marks no principled end. The terms also shift. 'Interruptibility' occurs once and is never defined, and its natural sense, the AI Act's 'stop' button (art. 14(4)(e)), is not what the channel does. Fox's accountability is a forum's capacity to demand answers and to sanction (p. 665). That is a different thing from a wrongdoer's duty to repair.
- *Footing.* (a) `gardner2011what`, typescript pp. 35–40 (card); `fox2007uncertain`, p. 665; Reg. 2024/1689, art. 14(4)(e). (b) lines 232, 253–256.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* Drop the mapping. Name what the channel serves 'correction', covering both late performance and repair during the stay. Justify the end of the channel duty by Gardner's measure of nearness to performance, or concede a route for repair after the stay. Keep 'accountability' for the relation to a forum.

**S8. The argument proves too much or too little: nothing in it is specific to hosting, and the cost limit dissolves it into reasonable care.**
- *Step attacked.* Step 6 as a whole; 'No duty without cost limits' (line 269); the scope answer on workers (line 318).
- *Objection.* No premise of Step 6 is specific to hosting. P1 needs only a continuing directed duty, P2 only delegation, P3 is general, and P4 needs only that the person owed can perceive failures. The argument therefore requires a channel to a person with power over the single case from every employer to every worker, every landlord to every tenant, and every school and carrier to those in its care. The paper puts workers out of scope, but the argument covers them. P3 also applies to the channel itself, since a channel run through others needs its own means of learning that it has failed, and so on, until the channel ends in the holder's own hands. The dossier stops the spread only with the cost limit (line 269). But a channel whose form 'scales with what is at stake' is just a requirement of reasonable care, and then 'must keep a channel' settles nothing that reasonable care did not already settle. The features specific to hosting do work in Step 3 and none in Step 6: the guest inside the host's controlled space, the host's role that lapses at the door (`pittrivers2012law`, p. 514), and the 'special relationship' of the American cases.
- *Footing.* (a) `pittrivers2012law`, p. 514 ('A host is host only on the territory over which on a particular occasion he claims authority'). (b) lines 91, 174–235, 269, 318.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* A new premise. Tie the necessity in P4 to the guest's confinement during the stay, with little chance to leave or to help herself: Step 3's control and dependence grounds. That premise narrows C to hosts and similar custodial relations, and it supplies the dependence that S1's derivative-rights route weighs. Concede generality wherever that dependence holds outside hosting.

**S9. When several firms host together, P3 assigns no one the duty to route the guest's report.**
- *Step attacked.* C; Step 5, allocation among firms (line 152); hard-case table, brand and platform rows (lines 114, 117).
- *Objection.* C says 'the holder must keep a channel'. But in the case the paper is about there are several holders: care follows control, and performance follows the undertaking (line 152). Hindriks's bearer of the duty of control is an 'organized collective' with a collective decision procedure (p. 4; n. 3). A brand, an operator and a platform are not one such agent, so on P3's own source each firm owes a channel for its own duty only. The guest must then work out which firm each failure belongs to. That is the 'channel the guest has to hunt for' that the dossier itself says will under-report (line 230). Nothing in the argument assigns the duty to route a report to the firm with power over the part that is failing.
- *Footing.* (a) `hindriks2025moral`, p. 4 and n. 3 ('Any organized collective can bear corporate responsibility'). (b) lines 114–117, 152, 230, 232.
- *Severity.* Serious.
- *What would answer it.* A new argument that assigns the routing duty to one party: the party that presents itself to the guest as the provider (the presentation test of `eu2022dsa`, art. 6(3)), or the party in control of the guest's space. It should rest on a source on the duties of groups that are not single agents (lead 9).

**S10. Step 3's division of binding work is inconsistent with the dossier's own table and misses the objection it answers.**
- *Step attacked.* Step 3, the claim (lines 73–79), 'Why no ground is sufficient alone' (lines 93–99), and objection 1 (line 103).
- *Objection.* The step has four faults.
  - (i) Line 73 says the role specifies 'its holder'. But the hard-case table settles every divergence by control or by undertaking, and leaves holding out 'one job' (line 123).
  - (ii) Line 73 asserts that the role 'does not bind by itself', while line 79 calls that question open. The disabling-condition reply also answers only the consent half of the associativist rejoinder. The associativists' positive claim is that 'it is the good realized in the relationship that justifies participants' claims against one another' (Dagger §4.3, para. 9). Their moralized-ideal-type reply makes 'fairness, respect, care, and reliance ... part and parcel of our concepts' of a role (para. 10), which would move reliance inside the role.
  - (iii) The argument that the third ground is insufficient concerns dependence, which 'falls on whoever can help' (line 97; R2's table, row 3, 'Dependence'). But the ground the claim lists is control, which does pick out the host as host (`everett2011comojo`, para 31, as the dossier quotes it at line 91). The table also relies on control alone for the duty to the person (line 123). So 'no one of them carries the core duty alone' is not shown for control.
  - (iv) Line 99 infers what makes a duty bind from what the law forbids a host to exclude. That crosses registers. It also confuses binding with fixing content. On the dossier's own reading of Blackstone, holding out takes up the role and 'the law supplies the duty' (line 89). That reading is compatible with consent binding the host while the law fixes the floor.
- *Footing.* (a) `dagger2021political`, §4.3, paras 9–10 (saved text). (b) lines 73, 77, 79, 89, 91, 97, 99, 107, 123; `WS_P2_R2.md`, table of grounds, row 3.
- *Severity.* Serious for Step 3, which survives only narrowed or re-argued. C depends on Step 3 only through directedness.
- *What would answer it.* Label the register. In the legal register, restate Step 3 as a reconstruction of the law's rationales. In the moral register, either argue that control alone is insufficient (a new argument) or concede that control carries the duty to the person. Answer the associativists' claim about the value of the relationship, or concede that the role may bind.

**S11. Pitt-Rivers describes a sanctioned partial exit, not a channel, and the dossier never establishes the guest's privilege to report.**
- *Step attacked.* Step 6, 'The customary footing' (line 274); Step 4; objections table, the rows on 'Innkeeper law in hospitality's clothes' and 'A demanding guest is a consumer' (lines 316–317).
- *Objection.* Pitt-Rivers does not describe a channel. In his account a dissatisfied guest 'has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out'. The plate was 'once furnished in diplomatic etiquette' as a partial exit: 'In this way he retired from his role until the error was corrected, or at least until his protest had been registered'. He adds that 'To complain openly would infringe the host's prerogative' (pp. 513–514). The classical code thus offers exit and forbids the report Step 6 needs. Its success condition can be mere registration of the protest, which line 239 says 'does not let the holder correct anything'. The dossier's legal support for P4 rests on the affected person's own notice or request (line 225). Yet its answer to the critical scholar is that 'The argument does not make the guest a demander' (line 317). The argument therefore presupposes that the guest has a privilege to report, never establishes it, and Step 4's neutrality on standing to demand does not cover it.
- *Footing.* (a) `pittrivers2012law`, pp. 513–514 (saved text). (b) lines 225, 239, 274, 316–317.
- *Severity.* Serious for the customary footing and for the two answers in the table; not for C.
- *What would answer it.* Concede that the classical code keeps a sanctioned partial exit, not a channel. Then establish separately that the commercial guest holds a privilege to report; package law makes reporting a duty (`eu2015package`, art. 13(2)). Argue that reporting a failure is not a demand.

### Minor

**M1. Fair play would make the duty owed to other hosts, and the 'reciprocity' it is said to fit is a different relation.**

Hart's right, as quoted there, belongs to 'those who have submitted to these restrictions' (1955: 185). Those are other hosts, not guests. The reciprocity the reply claims to fit is different. In Benveniste it is the obligation of one who has received 'to compensate a gift or service from which he has benefited' (para. 31): an exchange between guest and host in which the guest owes. Pitt-Rivers turns host–guest reciprocity into a later reversal of roles (p. 515).

3. **AI Act, art. 14(4)(d) (line 247).** The dossier quotes it as placing override power in natural persons but omits the chapeau. The paper should attribute the term to Schedler (1999) as Fox reports it. The locator p. 665 is correct; I checked it against the PDF's running heads.
5. **Pitt-Rivers, pp. 513–514 (line 274).** The quotation is accurate but misread. The same passage says the plate is how the guest 'retired from his role', and the next sentence says that open complaint infringes the host's prerogative (S11).
