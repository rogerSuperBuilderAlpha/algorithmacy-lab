# R9: what footing the hospitality literature gives the keystone

The critical-scholar red team (`REDTEAM_CRIT.md`) says that Step 6 of the dossier draws nothing from hospitality. I re-read five *Hospitality & Society* texts whole, read two saved but uncarded texts whole, and looked for open copies of six leads the red team named. None of the six could be read: one open copy was unreachable and the rest are closed or behind bot checks. The answers below rest on the seven texts I read in full, plus a targeted check of three Derrida carriers already in the library.

Locators follow the cards. Lynch et al. (2011) is cited by the page of the saved 22-page repository file ('PDF p.'), because the copy has no journal pages. Bulley (2015) is cited by the accepted manuscript's printed page (AM p.), with the PDF page in brackets. Petie et al. (2025) and Moysidou and Stanley (2023) are cited by manuscript page. Kekstaite (2022) is cited by the repository file's page (AM p.). Germann Molz (2018) and O'Gorman (2007) are cited by journal page. Sentences marked *(my inference)* are mine.

## 1. Answers

### (a) The host's power after the threshold, and the guest's means to contest it

**The literature theorises the host's power after the threshold at length; it gives the guest resistance, exit, complaint to an intermediary or a rating after the stay, and no recognised route to correction by the host.** The contrary passages, which describe power without a route, come first.

*Power: control, filtering, expulsion, surveillance.*

- The founding editorial names "hospitality as social control", "a means of controlling the 'other' or 'stranger'", which "acts as a powerful mediating social control mechanism".
  (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 3)
- It reports Sheringham and Daruwalla (2007): "The other, while symbolically elevated, is subject to domination by the host, and to the rules of being a guest", and Robinson and Lynch (2007): in a commercial setting "the highly regulated experience may cause a loss of free will".
  (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 8; both sources reported, not read)
- Bulley holds that hospitality's distinctions "also need to be policed, managed and controlled, even after the threshold of the home is crossed", and that hospitality works by "regulating, filtering and channelling the trajectories and contacts that it allows; preventing the 'bad' trajectories, enabling the 'good', while missing and turning a blind eye to others".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM pp. 2, 5 [PDF pp. 3, 6])
- Expulsion is the limit of that power: once inside, host and guest "will perhaps seek in various ways to assert their sovereignty, but neither can successfully do so without ending the hospitable relation and casting the other out".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 10 [PDF p. 11])
- In the camp, assessment serves "the management of their behaviour once inside", and refugees were "regularly subjected to headcounts, involving being rounded up into fenced lots".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM pp. 14-15 [PDF pp. 15-16])
- Moysidou and Stanley make the host's power a condition of hosting: welcoming "denotes sovereignty over the space and a level of power over the visitor", and "power and control over the space and, to an extent, over the guest are necessary conditions to the offering of hospitality".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 4-5)
- Placed guests in asylum hotels "were only allowed to come and go from the hotel at certain times, were not allowed to stay overnight outside of the hotel, and were not allowed to 'host' visitors".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 10, citing Guma et al. 2021)
- On platforms, surveillance is mutual: the reputation system "encourages hosts and guests to monitor one another", so that they relate "through a mode of social surveillance".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 236-237)
- Kekstaite starts from the received view that hospitality is "a vertical power-relation, in which hosts impose conditions and demands upon their guests".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 1)

*The guest's means to contest.*

- **Resistance, not a route.** Bulley: management "can never achieve total control and always produce resistance and counter-conducts", such as "boycotting or diverting food rations" and "tearing down the fencing enclosures used for headcounts".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 15 [PDF p. 16])
- **Consultation, which excludes.** The one institutional route Bulley records, consultation between refugee representatives and international staff, is "rarely if ever attended by women".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 16 [PDF p. 17])
- **Exit.** "part of power is the choice of whether or not to stay".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12)
- **Complaint, as the guest's power.** "Knowing how to complain, and feeling that the complaint will be acted upon, are latent forms of power."
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12)
- **Complaint to an intermediary.** A refused couple's guest "also complained to Workaway"; the intermediary's named remedy is exit, an "Emergency Help Plan that covers accommodation if a guest faces difficult or dangerous situations with their hosts".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 10, 16)
- **Complaint to staff, unanswered.** Asylum seekers "approach staff for help with issues ranging from accommodation complaints (Neal 2022) to healthcare concerns (Jones et al. 2022), with staff largely reported as unhelpful".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 4)
- **Negotiation.** Kekstaite reports Rosello: the guest "has agency to appropriate hospitality language and negotiate hospitality relationships".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 6)
- **A rating after the stay.** "After a hospitality encounter, hosts and guests go onto the platform to rate and leave references for one another."
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, p. 236)
- **The nearest normative statement.** Bulley's wager: "'better' forms would not only seek a more open welcome to the other, but also seeks to keep that welcome open once the threshold is passed. Such a negotiation between host and guest would perhaps allow the flourishing of other forms of life ... without seeking to control every aspect of the other's behaviour."
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 18 [PDF p. 19]; he calls any account of the 'better' "tentative", same page)

I found no passage in the seven texts that gives the guest a recognised route, kept by the host, to someone able to correct the host's conduct during the stay.
(Gap statement: the seven texts in section 2, read whole, 11 Nov 2026.)

### (b) Several hosts at once

**The literature names the problem in four forms and allocates no duty in any of them.**

- **Micro-hosts and macro-hosts.** "as shown in Robinson and Lynch (2007), the hospitality relationship often involves micro-hosts such as the initial sitting passenger above whose own micro-host is, for example, the train guard and macro-hosts such as the train company". The point in context is that people are "both hosts and guests simultaneously".
  (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 8; Robinson and Lynch 2007 closed in this run)
- **The assembled host.** Agencies "perform the role of an assembled host while being themselves guests in a space to which they do not formally 'belong'"; the assembled hosts "are separated in compounds, reducing or annihilating the space where hosts and guests can meet or mix".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM pp. 13, 15 [PDF pp. 14, 16])
- **The web of hosts, with a payer who is not the guest.** Asylum seekers are guests "of the hotel, the staff, private housing contractors, and the local community. Despite being guests, in the commercial sense of hospitality, it is the Home Office who are paying customers."
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 4)
  "reports highlighted a lack of clarity around what each different agency was responsible for. This generated confusion amongst asylum seekers who did not know where to turn for help."
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11)
- **Platform and household.** Dredge, as reported: "Moral responsibility is relational, distributed and multi-lateral", and "responsibilities can be shifted elsewhere or even avoided". Airbnb's lawyers: "any penalty should be on the hosts renting out their homes, not on the company".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 233, 244)
- Hosting without a host present: "homeowners can host their guests without ever meeting them".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, p. 244)
- Private households hosting in place of the state: "we do the work of the state. We are like an invisible gigantic social service".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 12, a host's words)

The red team is right that Step 1's gap statement must meet these terms (`REDTEAM_CRIT.md`, S10). The terms describe and criticise divided hosting; none says which host answers to the guest.
(`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 8; `bulley2015ethics`, AM pp. 13-16; `petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 4, 11-12; `germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 243-245)

### (c) The guest's voice as part of hospitality, as against consumer complaint

**The critical literature treats contestation as part of hospitality, but as contestation of the host's power and order, not as a route to correction.** The contrary passages come first.

- Kekstaite treats contestation as a risk that comes with hospitality: hospitality "is not a merely benevolent practice allowing for mutual recognition, but can also create circumstances for contestations and conflicts, or possibly obtain hostile or even perverse forms (Derrida 2000; Rosello 2001; Lynch 2011)".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 9)
- On platforms the guest's voice is a mutual rating that doubles as "a marketing system".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 236-237)

*The Dikeç sentence, located.* The sentence closes a paragraph that runs from PDF p. 8 to PDF p. 9 of the saved repository copy of Lynch et al. (2011):

> "It is apparent that dynamics in spaces of hospitality are critical to understand. Dikeç (2002), whilst recognizing the importance of boundaries in hospitality, argues that hospitality is about opening those boundaries and giving space to the stranger such that mutual recognition is achieved. Hospitality then becomes concerned with provision of the social, cultural, institutional, ethical and political spaces where participants can learn to engage with and learn from one another. Indeed, hospitality space should facilitate not just recognition but also enable contestation and conflict to occur."
> (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF pp. 8-9)

The editors do not mark the last sentence as Dikeç's. Dikeç (2002) could not be read (section 3), so the paper may attribute 'enable contestation and conflict' only to Lynch et al.
(`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 9; section 3 below)

*Further footing.*

- Kekstaite: navigating hospitality "provides an opportunity for contesting them through daily mundane interaction"; host and guest relations can "speak back to and contest the state power to define what welcoming entails".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM pp. 9, 3)
- Bulley: the micro-politics of hospitality is "a mode of critique which displaced 'guests' are already conducting".
  (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 18 [PDF p. 19])
- Moysidou and Stanley treat a guest's effective complaint as the guest's power, not as data for the host.
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12)
- Petie et al. report guests consulted on menus, "improving their wellbeing".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11, citing Laing 2020)

No text frames the guest's voice as consumer complaint, and none frames it as a route the host keeps. *(My inference, from the passages above.)*

### (d) O'Gorman, 'Discovering commercial hospitality in Ancient Rome'

**The article shows neither picture.** It records duties only for private guest-friendship, names no duty of an innkeeper to a guest, and brings in law in the commercial setting only to regulate prostitution and to protect serving girls.

- Private hospitality "seems to have been more precisely and legally defined". Obligations ran to the *hospes*: receiving him "in their house", and "duties of protection and, in case of need, to represent a guest as his patron in the courts of justice. Jupiter was thought to watch over the ius hospitia (law of hospitality) in the Roman Empire". The duties sentence carries no note.
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 45)
- Across antiquity hospitality "brought expectations: a warm welcome, food, a comfortable place to sit, charming company and entertainment". These are expectations, not duties, and nothing ties them to inns.
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 44)
- Commercial lodging served those outside guest-friendship: "not all travellers required such services. Accommodation along major roads and at city gates gained a reputation for attracting the lower classes who were too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality." The rich used it to travel "to lands where they were not known ... without the need to establish a household there".
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 47)
- The only law in the commercial sections: the code of Justinian forbids a sold female slave to "prostitute herself in a tavern under the pretext of serving therein", and the code of Theodosius "protects serving girls from prosecution and affords them safety under the law".
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 48)
- The article does not mention an innkeeper's liability, the praetor's edict, *receptum*, Ulpian or the Digest.
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, searched saved text and page images, pp. 44-52)

For the red team's S3, the article fits only the first half of the counter-history: commercial lodging served those whom guest-friendship did not reach (p. 47). The 'law stepped in' half rests on the Digest (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.1.1, 4.9.3.1), not on O'Gorman. The dossier may not cite O'Gorman (2007) for a commercial host's duty.
(`ogorman2007discovering`, pp. 45-48)

### (f) Workers

**The literature treats the people who perform hospitality for an organisation as labourers whom guests see as hosts, who are under-equipped and under-protected, and on whom blame should not fall.**

- The editorial lists "labour issues such as poor working conditions", "the significance of emotional labour (Hochschild 1983)", "the nature of hosting as work (Veijola 2009) and the labour involved in home hosting", and "employee/worker voice and experience".
  (`lynch2011theorizing`, PDF pp. 9, 13)
- In the asylum hotels, "Hotel staff overnight effectively became hosts" to guests with needs that require "knowledge and skills not usually required in commercial hospitality".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 4-5, 11)
- Staff "were made aware of Badreddin's deteriorating mental health the night prior to the attack but they lacked the knowledge needed to act".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11)
- The inquiry "considered hotel staff as victims of decisions made by management, rather than individually responsible".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11)
- The platform host is "a service provider performing manual and emotional labor", owed "none of the protections afforded by employment laws".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 236, 243)
- Germann Molz takes the "hostessing society" from Veijola and Jokinen (2008: 176), in which "the whole world is also hosting".
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, p. 231; Veijola and Jokinen closed in this run)
- Household hosting is gendered. Moysidou and Stanley note that B&B hosting "tended to be seen as women's work".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 14)
- Kekstaite found "prevailingly more women than men engage in hosting activities".
  (`kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 6)
- In the one ancient source, the law protects the worker, not the guest: the code of Theodosius "protects serving girls from prosecution and affords them safety under the law".
  (`ogorman2007discovering`, p. 48)

This supports the red team's S7. In the journal's own cases the person a guest reaches is a worker, and the literature treats that worker's lack of authority and training as the organisation's failure.
(`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11; `REDTEAM_CRIT.md`, S7)

### (g) Household hosts on platforms, and placed guests: complaining and leaving

**The household guest's routes are silence, exit, or complaint to the intermediary. The placed guest's are complaint to staff who could not act, or transfer.**

*Household hosts on platforms and agencies.*

- An au pair: "What if I complain about something and I make the things uncomfortable?' Cause I have to be here, I cannot leave. ...So that's probably why I haven't complained about little things."
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 7)
- A WWOOFer: "it was sort of a situation I couldn't really say anything", and "We might have actually left a few days early because of it".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 11)
- Guests who feel indebted or infantilized are left with "a sense that they 'cannot complain'".
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 13)
- The complaint route in the data runs to the intermediary ("I also complained to Workaway"), and the intermediary's remedy is exit (the Emergency Help Plan).
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 10, 16)
- The authors' gloss credits a complaint to the agency to Alice, but the excerpt that reports one is Pauline's.
  (`moysidou2023theorizing`, pp. 8, 10, 14)
- On Airbnb the guest's voice is a mutual rating after the stay, inside a system that is "also a marketing system", run by a firm that disclaims control over hosts.
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, pp. 236-237, 244)
- I found nothing in the article on complaining or leaving during a stay.
  (`germannmolz2018scale`, read whole)

*Placed guests.*

- Housing is "provided on a no-choice basis", and people "can be moved by the Home Office at short notice without their consent".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 2)
- Complaints to staff met staff "largely reported as unhelpful", and Badreddin "called the Home Office and other support organisations more than 70 times".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 4, 9)
- Exit came only by transfer, experienced as "'freedom' and 'escape'", or by a court order that a resident "must be moved".
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 10, 14)
- **Contrary, with equal weight.** "the queries lodged by Badreddin at the Park Inn were dealt with appropriately, cumulatively they should have acted as a warning sign (Laing 2020). There was no communication or joined up approach between services, so warning signs were missed."
  (`petie2025exploring`, MS p. 12)

The red team's S5 statement that "The single-case channel worked" leaves out MS p. 11, where staff who were told the night before "lacked the knowledge needed to act". Both passages must be cited together.
(`petie2025exploring`, MS pp. 11-12)

### (h) My judgement

*R9's judgement, not a source's.* A partial footing survives F1, and it survives S1 only if Step 6 changes its derivation. Without the plate, the journal's literature supplies three things:

- It makes the host's power after the threshold hospitality's own question (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 10; `lynch2011theorizing`, PDF p. 3).
- It treats hosts whom the guest cannot meet or locate as a failure of hospitality (`bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 15; `petie2025exploring`, MS p. 11).
- It treats the guest's effective complaint and negotiation as the guest's power, part of a welcome kept "open once the threshold is passed" (`moysidou2023theorizing`, p. 12; `bulley2015ethics`, AM p. 18; `kekstaite2022beyond`, AM p. 6).

That grounds a channel as a check on the host's power over the guest. It does not ground the dossier's derivation, a channel as the host's means of performing. No text I read states a host's duty to keep one.
