## 1. Answers, contrary passages first

### (a) Is the derivative-duty structure in the open texts, and does it reach moral rights? How does dependence figure?

**Short answer.** Yes, in one text, and for legal rights only. Herstein reports two structures: a right that justifies a further right when the first depends on the second (graded by the strength of the dependence), and a claim-right that justifies new duties as the facts change. He places both among features "unique or particularly salient to legal rights". No text I read reports either structure for moral rights.

**Against.**

- The entry's own preamble assigns §§1-2 and 6-10 to features particular to legal rights, and only §§3-5 to features common to "all rights (legal, moral, or otherwise)".
  - Locator: Herstein, preamble, para. 1.
- §1 frames derivation as legal justification: "Typically, justification involves a legal norm grounding—as a matter of law—other legal norms", within "the tradition of legal positivism".
  - Locator: Herstein §1, paras 6, 9.
- The dependence sentence, read literally, has the justified right depending on the justifying one ("the successful exercise and implementation of the latter is dependent on the former"). Its *Griswold* example runs the other way: the core rights "would be less secure" without the peripheral rights they justify. The red team's reading follows the example, not the sentence.
  - Locator: Herstein §1, para. 8 and block quotation; REDTEAM_PHIL, S1.
- The justification is defeasible: "in the absence of countervailing reasons, rights justify such duties".
  - Locator: Herstein §8.4, para. 1.
- The dynamic view is disputed: Hohfeldians can reply that "dynamic rights" are "only inchoate rights" (Kramer).
  - Locator: Herstein §8.4, para. 2.
- The only cross-register justification reported runs from moral rights to legal rights, as means ("a legal right to φ may protect and further adherence to ... moral right(s) to something else altogether"), not from one moral right to another moral right or duty.
  - Locator: Herstein §9.3, para. 2.

**For.**

- Derivation by content: "Legal rights can justify other legal rights"; "courts may find that one right derives from another (Raz 1986: 168–170)".
  - Locator: Herstein §1, para. 7.
- Derivation by dependence: "Rights can also justify other rights when the successful exercise and implementation of the latter is dependent on the former (Wellman 1995: 26–27). The stronger the dependency, the stronger the justification (Nickel 2007: 87–88)."
  - Locator: Herstein §1, para. 8.
- Rights justify duties, and the same right can justify new ones: "claim-rights are reasons for duties"; "the same claim-right seemingly can count in favor of different and changing duties, for instance given changes in factual circumstances"; legal rights are "instruments of legal change, justifying new previously unrecognized duties (Raz 1970: 225–227; 1994: 269)".
  - Locator: Herstein §8.4, paras 1-2.

**How dependence figures.** It figures only in the rights-to-rights structure, and as a matter of degree: "The stronger the dependency, the stronger the justification". The entry gives no threshold and no measure. The *Griswold* measure is security ("less secure"), not necessity. The rights-to-duties structure in §8.4 does not mention dependence; its trigger is "changes in factual circumstances".
- Locator: Herstein §1, para. 8; §8.4, para. 2.

**Two routes** *(my inference)*. The red team merges two structures. Route A (§1, para. 8) makes the channel a derivative *right* of the guest and costs the dossier's sentence "The channel is not a new right of the guest". Route B (§8.4, para. 2) makes it a new *duty* justified by the guest's existing claim to performance, with delegation and automation as the changed facts. Route B keeps that sentence on the dynamic view but not on the Hohfeldian reply. Only Route A carries the dependence gradient.
- Locator: Herstein §1, para. 8; §8.4, paras 1-2; ARGUMENT.md, The answer, para. 3.

**What the paper may cite.** Herstein, for these structures as reported in legal theory, with Raz, Wellman and Nickel named as his sources. The entry does not support a moral claim. The moral version rests on Raz 1986, ch. 7, which is closed in this run.
- Locator: Herstein, preamble; §1, paras 7-8; §8.4, para. 2; section 3 below (Raz).

**The other texts.** Kiesewetter's derivation (transmission) runs from an 'ought' to an 'ought', not from a right to a duty, and his 'ought' is the all-things-considered one, which he distinguishes from moral obligation. Baum et al. derive backward-looking answerability from a prospective obligation ("retrospective responsibility depends on, and is fixed in scope by, prospective responsibility", after Duff), which is not the rights structure. Pettit has no derivation of duties.
- Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 922-923, 932; Baum et al. p. 20 and n. 13; Pettit pp. 173-175.

### (b) Does any read text hold that a duty to take means transmits only to necessary means, or also to means that make performance more secure or likely?

**Short answer.** The one text that defends transmission restricts it to necessary means, and treats measures that only make conformity more secure as matters of "second-order reasons", not of a further 'ought'. No read text holds that an obligation transmits to merely securing means. The nearest support for a wider duty comes from outside the transmission framework: Herstein's dependence gradient (rights to rights), and Baum et al.'s role obligation to guard against harm, whose discharge includes monitoring and escalation without any necessity condition.

**Against a wider transmission.**

- "'Ought', that is, transmits to necessary means"; the principle "states only that 'ought' transmits to necessary means and thus does not entail that it transmits to necessary side effects or logical implications."
  - Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 922, 925.
- A principle "not restricted to necessary means" is "open to objections that do not apply to a transmission principle restricted to necessary means" (Kiesewetter reporting Way's own concession).
  - Locator: Kiesewetter p. 922, n. 2.
- Likelihood of failing gives "second-order reasons to adopt additional measures that ensure his conformity to his obligations", and does not change what one is obliged to do (the drink-driving example).
  - Locator: Kiesewetter p. 937.
- His argument for transmission works through incompatible alternatives: "If buying is a necessary means to going to the concert, then not-buying is an incompatible alternative". Omitting a means that is not necessary is not an incompatible alternative, so the argument does not reach it *(my inference)*.
  - Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 945-946.
- A necessary means with a low chance of success is not required, and the source 'ought' falls (Kolodny's Unlucky, .1 probability).
  - Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 941-944.
- Gardner and Rudy-Hiller, per the red team, also require necessity; I did not re-read them.
  - Locator: REDTEAM_PHIL, S2 (`gardner2011what`, typescript p. 38, n. 53; `rudyhiller2022epistemic`, §3.4, para. 6).

**For a disjunctive duty.** If performance cannot be achieved without at least one of several means, then taking none is an incompatible alternative to performance, and Kiesewetter's argument yields an 'ought' to take *some* means, not a particular one *(my inference)*. This supports the red team's repair S2(ii): the host must keep some timely means of learning of a failure. It supports S2(i) for the class of failures where the guest's report is the only timely route.
- Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 924 (means include "preventing something that would prevent A's φ-ing"), 945-946; REDTEAM_PHIL, S2.

**Outside the transmission framework.**

- Herstein: justification grows with dependence, measured in *Griswold* by security.
  - Locator: Herstein §1, para. 8.
- Baum et al.: the prospective obligation is "the role-grounded obligation to guard against the harms one's activities impose on others", and "There is no further thing the principal must do to discharge the obligation, over and above specifying, monitoring, and refining the conditions under which her agent acts". The agenda includes escalation triggers to "defer to a human or hand control back". These precautions belong to the obligation's content; they are not derived as necessary means.
  - Locator: Baum et al. pp. 11, 20.
- Kong lists "a monitoring requirement" among the specification changes a diligent deployer could have made, as a test of what she must answer for, not as a duty.
  - Locator: Kong p. 6; p. 4 ("It is a claim about where the demand for an account properly lands").
- Hindriks: "if an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it", a condition of ability, not of security.
  - Locator: `hindriks2025moral`, p. 4 (card).

### (c) Does any read text say to whom a duty to take precautions, or a duty of oversight, is owed?

**Short answer.** No read text says that a duty of precaution or oversight is owed to the people at risk. Baum et al. come nearest: the *answerability* that follows the role-holder's prospective obligation is relational, owed to parties with standing, and standing goes to those "whose interests the agenda was meant to protect". The prospective obligation itself is described only as an obligation to guard against harms "to others".

- Baum et al.: answerability is a liability whose "correlative ... is a power, held by certain others, to put the answerable party under an obligation to respond"; "standing is settled by the deployment context: by whose interests the agenda was meant to protect"; in their example the under-supervised student and the collaborator each "holds the power to demand that the principal answer". The prospective obligation is "to guard against the harms one's activities impose on others".
  - Locator: Baum et al. pp. 20, 22.
- Baum et al. add that affected parties need "institutionally recognised standing and the resources to engage", which the framework does not supply.
  - Locator: Baum et al. pp. 17, 27.
- Herstein: claim-rights justify "correlative duties", that is, duties owed to the right-holder; the duties a claim-right justifies as facts change are, on the natural reading of "Relatedly", duties of the same correlative kind *(my reading)*.
  - Locator: Herstein §8.4, paras 1-2; §3.1.1, para. 1 (card).
- Pettit: blame is the stance of "a creditor: someone to whom at least an apology is owed"; outside complainants in his example "can only blame the corporate group as a whole". That concerns blame, not duties.
  - Locator: Pettit pp. 174, 198.
- Kong: answerability makes the deployer "the correct address" for a demand for an account; he does not say whose demand.
  - Locator: Kong pp. 3-4 (card; p. 4 re-read).
- Kiesewetter: silent; his 'ought' is not directed.
  - Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 923, 932.
- Hindriks: the duty of control is grounded in risk "to others" and not said to be owed to them.
  - Locator: `hindriks2025moral`, pp. 4-5 (card).

### (d) What do the texts give for allocating a duty among several firms that are not one agent?

**Short answer.** Little, and the main text cuts against treating such firms together. Pettit holds that an unincorporated collection "is not an agent" and cannot be held responsible as a group. Kong distributes answerability to each party for the foreseeable risks of its own contribution. Baum et al. make principal and provider each fully answerable, but put multi-principal and institutional cases outside their account. No text assigns to one firm a duty to route a report about another firm's part.

**Against allocating to the group.**

- "For the collection that they constitute, being unincorporated, is not an agent. It fails the first condition for fitness to be held responsible." Holding loose groupings responsible "may not be strictly appropriate", though it "may actually prompt the grouping to incorporate and organize against the condemned behavior".
  - Locator: Pettit pp. 195, 201.
- Baum et al. leave "multi-principal deployments" and principals that are "an institution rather than a person" outside their account.
  - Locator: Baum et al. pp. 25, 27.

**What the texts give.**

- Per contribution (Kong): the conservation principle "distributes answerability to each party for the foreseeable risks of its own contribution", the developer for the base model and the deployer for the objective and domain, separable because "the epistemic bases differ".
  - Locator: Kong p. 9.
- Shared in full (Baum et al.): "Answerability under (i) and (iii) is shared, not distributed: principal and provider are each fully answerable for the harm"; the deployer's and provider's obligations are "real, separately grounded, and discharged otherwise".
  - Locator: Baum et al. pp. 20, 25.
- Within one firm (Pettit): the group controls performance by arranging that "should the performers fail, there will be others to take their place as backups", and answers for "having organized things so that such harm was likely or inevitable".
  - Locator: Pettit pp. 192, 197.
- Hindriks: when a hired agency fails, "this agency is to blame".
  - Locator: `hindriks2025moral`, p. 10, n. 24 (card).

**What follows** *(my inference)*. On every text read, each firm answers for its own part. The red team's repair for S9 (a routing duty on the firm that presents itself as the provider, or that controls the guest's space) needs a ground none of these texts supplies; the DSA's presentation test (`eu2022dsa`, art. 6(3), per the red team) is a legal analogue, not a moral argument.
- Locator: Pettit p. 195; Kong p. 9; Baum et al. p. 25; REDTEAM_PHIL, S9.

### Kong's 'foreseeability at the level of types': does it make a fall-back for unforeseen single-case failures a foreseeable precaution?

**Short answer.** Only in part. Kong's criterion makes a failure foreseeable when its *type* was, and so covers the single unforeseen instance of a documented failure mechanism. It does not cover a general fall-back for failures nobody can describe in advance: a type stated only as 'some failure' names no mechanism and is inadmissible, and the genuinely unforeseeable case is, for Kong, a faultless accident handled by no-fault compensation.

- The criterion: a gap is genuine only when the harmful disposition "was not a foreseeable property of the deployment specification, foreseeability being assessed at the level of types and dispositions rather than particular acts."
  - Locator: Kong p. 5.
- The admissibility test: a type-description counts only if "(i) it names a causal or dispositional mechanism by which the harm was produced, rather than merely a harm-category", (ii) evidence of that mechanism was available at deployment, and (iii) "it identifies something the deployer could have acted upon by altering the objective, the constraints, the testing regime, or the decision to deploy at all". "'Harm to someone' fails (i), naming no mechanism, and fails (iii)". Admissible specification changes include "a monitoring requirement".
  - Locator: Kong p. 6.
- The unforeseeable case: in Case 2 a cosmic-ray bit flip "causes a single anomalous action"; "No admissible type-description is available", "no alteration of the objective or constraints would have addressed it", and it is "the faultless accident, which our practices already handle through no-fault compensation rather than through the attribution of responsibility."
  - Locator: Kong p. 7.
- The tendency: "As agents become harder to follow in the particular, they become easier to anticipate in the type."
  - Locator: Kong p. 8.
- The adequate answer includes oversight: a deployer who "tested against foreseeable failure modes, and maintained meaningful oversight is answerable — she must give an account — and her account may fully exculpate her."
  - Locator: Kong p. 10.
- Scope: the principle is "a claim about where the demand for an account properly lands"; it concerns answerability, not a duty of precaution.
  - Locator: Kong p. 4.

**Bearing** *(my inference)*. For hosting, the failures guests bring to staff often have documented mechanisms (identity checks and kiosks that fail for some guests; `xu2024disciplined`, pp. 5027, 5035, per ARGUMENT.md P4). For those, Kong's criterion makes the type foreseeable and a reachable person with override power an identifiable precaution, even when the single instance was not foreseen. For the residue that sub-step 5 targets (failures of no documented type), Kong gives no support and points the other way. Baum et al. reach further: on Kiener's account a role-holder answers, without fault, for harm within her role "even when, on the specific occasion, the harm was unforeseeable", because she had a "prior" opportunity "to take meaningful precautions against a type of harm". That is answerability, not a duty to keep the fall-back. The red team's S5 therefore stands for genuinely novel failures; it is answered for documented types.
- Locator: Kong pp. 6-7; Baum et al. p. 23; ARGUMENT.md, sub-step 5; REDTEAM_PHIL, S5.

### (e) My wording for P3 and C in the moral register (my judgement, not a finding)

> **P3.** The guest's claim that the host perform during the stay can justify new duties of the host as the facts change (Herstein §8.4, para. 2, reporting Raz). Once the host performs through others and through systems, whether that claim is met in the guest's own case depends on the host's learning of a failure while it can still correct it (P4).
>
> **C.** The host therefore owes the guest a further duty, justified by the guest's claim: to keep, during the stay, some timely means by which a failure in the guest's case reaches someone able to correct it. Where only the guest perceives the failure, the means must include a route from the guest, since there it is necessary (Kiesewetter, p. 922). Keeping no means breaches this duty; an uncorrected failure breaches the duty to perform.

(137 words.)

**How it meets the red team.**

- **F1 (registers).** P3 and C are moral throughout. The legal texts (*R (DMA)*, package law, *Marchand*) become analogues, and the claim that C keeps the conclusion "inside the duty K4 established" is dropped. The debt: the one text for the dynamic structure reports it for legal rights (Herstein, preamble; §8.4, para. 2), so the moral premise rests on Raz 1986, ch. 7, which is closed. The paper should say so or cite Raz only after reading him.
  - Locator: ARGUMENT.md, C, para. 1; Herstein, preamble; section 3 below.
- **F2 (form; where the wrong lies).** The channel is a further duty, distinct from performance, as Hindriks and Kiesewetter would have it, but directed, unlike Hindriks's. It replaces "part of the duty it already has". Two wrongs, not one placed by luck: two hosts with the same design both breach the further duty; only the one whose failure goes uncorrected also breaches the duty to perform. Whether the first breach wrongs a guest who suffers no failure is the question Oberdiek (closed) would test; on Route B it does, because the duty is the guest's correlative duty.
  - Locator: `hindriks2025moral`, pp. 4-5 (card); Kiesewetter pp. 922, 945-946; Herstein §8.4, para. 1; REDTEAM_PHIL, F2.
- **S1 (direction).** The duty is directed because a claim-right of the guest justifies it, and rights justify "correlative duties". It is not derived through the host's own reasons for means. The debt: whether a dynamically justified duty is correlative in the Hohfeldian sense is contested (Kramer).
  - Locator: Herstein §8.4, paras 1-2.
- **S2 (necessity).** C requires "some timely means" (the disjunction, which is necessary) and a route from the guest only where only the guest perceives the failure (where that route is necessary). Neither step needs transmission to non-necessary means.
  - Locator: Kiesewetter pp. 922 and n. 2, 925, 945-946 (disjunction my inference); REDTEAM_PHIL, S2.
- **Not answered here.** 'Someone able to correct it' is neutral between a person and a further automated layer, which meets S6 but drops the dossier's 'person with power over the single case'. S8 (generality) is untouched: P3 as worded holds wherever performance runs through others.
  - Locator: REDTEAM_PHIL, S6, S8.
