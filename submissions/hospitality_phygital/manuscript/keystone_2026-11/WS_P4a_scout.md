# WS P4a scout: the commercial host's legal duties (K4, K5)

Scope: the duty to receive, and the duties owed to guests once received, in hotels, restaurants and bars. All searching and reading was done on 11 Nov 2026. Every text named as "read" was fetched in this session and is saved under `manuscript/revision_2026-11/research/sources/<citekey>/` (folder names are given in the candidate table).

**Reading depth used below.** *Whole* = the full judgment or provision. *Part* = the paragraphs or pages listed. *Headnote* = headnote and statement of facts only. *Record* = a database or catalogue record, text unread. Three kinds of saved text are OCR and need checking against page images before any quotation is used: the Internet Archive Law Reports extracts, the Caselaw Access Project (CAP) HTML, and the 1777 and 1769 reports already carded.

**What the search found, in brief.**

- **K4, reception.** English and US courts state a duty on hotels, bars and nightclubs to take reasonable care for a guest's person, third parties included. The English basis is invitation and assumed responsibility (*Al-Najar* [187]) or control, reliance and the economic relationship (*Everett* [31]); the US basis is the "special relationship" of innkeeper and guest (Restatement (Second) § 314A, quoted in full in *Rasnick*; Restatement (Third) § 40, quoted in part in *Hoyt*). In the three modern English cases on hotels and bars (*Al-Najar*, *Everett*, *Lewis*) the claimant lost on breach; the guest won in *MacLenan* (1917) and in *Bhamra* (a caterer).
- **K4, admission.** The duty to receive attaches to inns and not to bars, restaurants or public houses (*R v Rymer*; *Sealey v Tandy*; *Alpaugh v Wolverton*). The Irish Hotel Proprietors Act 1963 states both tiers in one statute (ss. 3 and 4).
- **K4, civil law.** The Roman edict, BGB §§ 701–704 and the 1962 Convention cover the guest's goods only, and Ulpian says innkeepers were free to refuse anyone (D. 4.9.1.1). On these texts the goods duty is not an Anglo-American accident; the duty to receive is not shown outside the common law.
- **K4, a legal exception owed to the guest.** The Equality Act 2010, ss. 20–21 and Sch. 2 para. 2, and the ADA, 42 U.S.C. § 12182(b)(2)(A)(ii), require a provider to change its practices for a disabled guest. This corrects v6's statement that no source makes an exception something the guest is owed.
- **K5.** The refused traveller could sue in his own name (*Constantine*: "a right at common law"; *Thomas v Pick Hotels*: a "relational duty ... redressible in tort"), and the innkeeper could also be indicted (*R v Rymer*; Blackstone; *Constantine* at 698). The duty was both public and private.

### Locators for the Tier 1 candidates

Quotations are copied from the saved texts. Hyphens at OCR line breaks are mended and page furniture that falls inside a sentence is dropped (*Rymer* at 140–141; *Thomas* at 665–666); any other repair is in square brackets.

**1. *Al-Najar* (HC).**
- The question: "whether a hotel proprietor owes a duty to guests to take reasonable care to protect against injury caused by the criminal actions of third parties" ([1]).
- On *Calye*: "the law imposed stricter duties for the protection of the goods of guests than the duties for the protection of guests" ([183]). The judge dates *Calye's Case* "(1604)"; the library card dates it 1584.
- The duty and its basis: "This is because the hotel invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests." ([187]). "It is clear that the common law relating to hotel proprietors has developed since 1604." ([187]).
- Outcome: "The duty was to take reasonable care to prevent the attacks, it was not an absolute duty to prevent an attack." ([229]); no liability ([240]).

**2. *Everett v Comojo*.**
- Basis: "The management is in control of the premises. It can regulate who enters, who is refused entry and who is to be removed after entry. The guest comes to the night club to relax and enjoy himself and for that prospect relies on the competence and prudence of its management." ([31]). The court adds the economic relationship ([31]) and the analogy with the Occupiers' Liability Act ([33]).
- The duty: "there is a duty on the management of a night club in respect of the actions of third parties on the premises but I stress that the standard of care imposed or the scope of the duty must also be fair, just and reasonable." ([34]). It should "govern the relationship between the managers of an hotel or night club and their guests in relation to the actions of third parties on the premises" ([36]).
- Outcome: no breach ([37]); appeal dismissed ([38]). The judgment gives two dates for the incident (9 October 2002 at [1]; 8 November 2002 at [4]).

**3. *MacLenan v Segar*.**
- Headnote: "there is an implied warranty by the innkeeper that the inn premises are, for the purpose of personal use by the guest, as safe as reasonable care and skill on the part of any one can make them" (325).
- On the person: "He does not insure the personal safety of the guest." (328).
- On contractors: "it matters not whether the lack of care or skill be that of the defendant or his servants, or that of an independent contractor or his servants" (333).
- Outcome: judgment for the plaintiff for £865 17s (335). Dingemans J's summary at *Al-Najar* [184] quotes the occupier's duty McCardie J states at 329, not the wider warranty he goes on to find.

**4. OLA 1957, s. 5(1).** Where persons enter premises "in exercise of a right conferred by contract with a person occupying or having control of the premises, the duty he owes them in respect of dangers due to the state of the premises or to things done or omitted to be done on them, in so far as the duty depends on a term to be implied in the contract by reason of its conferring that right, shall be the common duty of care." Whether *MacLenan*'s warranty survives this section is my inference from the text and needs an authority.

**5. *Constantine*.**
- Headnote: "An action by a traveller against an innkeeper for wrongfully refusing to receive and lodge him is for the violation of a right at common law so that the action, although sounding in case, can be sustained without proof of special damage." (693).
- The defendants conceded that they were innkeepers, that the hotel was a common inn, that there was room and that the plaintiff would pay (694). A contract existed and "no claim was made in contract" (694).
- Both routes: "incidentally, an indictment may be brought, as in Reg. v. Sprague" (698; the OCR sets "Reg.v." without a space). The OCR gives two dates for *Sprague* (1891 in the text, 1899 in the footnote).
- Holding: "His right, I think, is founded on the common law. That right I found was violated. The law affords him a remedy, and the injury which he has suffered imports damage." (708). Nominal damages of five guineas; exemplary damages refused (708).

**6. *R v Rymer*.** The indictment charged that the defendant, "being an inn-keeper, and under the obligation to receive the prosecutor in his inn", refused without reasonable cause (139). Kelly CB: "A tavern is not within the definition. In such a place as this no one has a right to insist on being served any more than in any other shop." (140). Denman J: the object of the law "is merely to secure that travellers shall not, while upon their journeys, be deprived of necessary food and lodging" (140–141). Conviction quashed.

**7. Hotel Proprietors Act 1963 (Ireland).**
- s. 1(1): a hotel "provides or holds itself out as providing sleeping accommodation, food and drink for reward for all comers without special contract".
- s. 3(1): "The proprietor of a hotel is under a duty to receive at the hotel as guests all persons who, whether or not under special contract, present themselves and require sleeping accommodation, food or drink and to provide them therewith, unless he has reasonable grounds of refusal."
- s. 4(1): "Where a person is received as a guest at a hotel, whether or not under special contract, the proprietor of the hotel is under a duty to take reasonable care of the person of the guest and to ensure that, for the purpose of personal use by the guest, the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them." s. 4(2): "This duty is independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier of the premises."
- s. 9: notices or agreements excluding liability are void. s. 12: breach of s. 3 or s. 5 is an offence "without prejudice to his civil liability".
- I read the Act as enacted; later amendment or repeal was not checked.

**8. *Rasnick*.** Section 314A as the court quotes it (568): "(1) A common carrier is under a duty to its passengers to take reasonable action (a) to protect them from unreasonable risk of physical harm, and (b) to give them first aid after it knows or has reason to know that they are ill or injured, and to care for them until they can be cared for by others. (2) An innkeeper is under a similar duty to his guests. (3) A possessor of land who holds it open to the public is under a similar duty to members of the public who enter in response to his invitation. (4) One who is required by law to take or who voluntarily takes the custody of another under circumstances such as to deprive the other of his normal opportunities for protection is under a similar duty to the other." The court marks "Emphasis supplied"; the CAP text does not show where. The holding is quoted under Defeaters.

**9. *Hoyt*.** Section 40 as the court quotes it (775–776): "(a) An actor in a special relationship with another owes the other a duty of reasonable care with regard to risks that arise within the scope of the relationship. (b) Special relationships giving rise to the duty provided in Subsection (a) include: (1) a common carrier with its passengers, (2) an innkeeper with its guests, (3) a business or other possessor of land that holds its premises open to the public with those who are lawfully on the premises," (the court's quotation stops there). "Tavern owners fit squarely within the class of business owners contemplated by section 40(b)(3)." (777).

**10. *Groh*.** "Based on the special relationship that exists between an innkeeper and guest, we hold that a hotel that [evicts] a guest has a duty to exercise reasonable care under the [circumstances]." (¶ 4; the CAP OCR reads "eviets" and "cireumstances"). The court quotes clauses (a) and (b) of § 314A as applied to an innkeeper (¶ 29) and records that § 40 replaced it in 2012 (n. 4). The limit is quoted under Defeaters.

**11. *Thomas v Pick Hotels*.** The innkeeper–guest relationship "creates a common law relational duty on the part of the innkeeper, the breach of which is redressible in tort" (665–666). "While the common law duty of an innkeeper to provide accommodations may have been dormant or stale in Kansas, it has never been abrogated" (666). The tort claim was time-barred; the contract claim went forward.

**12. Blackstone.** "Also if an inn-keeper, or other victualler, hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers, it is an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way; and upon this universal assumpsit an action on the case will lie against him for damages, if he without good reason refuses to admit a traveler" (3 Comm. 164; long s modernised). "Inns, in particular, being intended for the lodging and receipt of travelers, may be indicted, suppressed, and the inn-keepers fined, if they refuse to entertain a traveler without a very sufficient cause" (4 Comm. 168 by the Avalon page markers; check the page against an image).

**13. Digest 4.9.** The edict: "nautae caupones stabularii quod cuiusque salvum fore receperint nisi restituent, in eos iudicium dabo" (4.9.1 pr.). The ground and the freedom to refuse: "necesse est plerumque eorum fidem sequi et res custodiae eorum committere … nam est in ipsorum arbitrio, ne quem recipiant" (4.9.1.1); in Scott's translation, "they have the choice of refusing to receive anyone". Liability without fault, save *vis maior* (4.9.3.1). An unpaid guest keeps the action: "Licet gratis navigaveris vel in caupona gratis deverteris, non tamen in factum actiones tibi denegabuntur" (4.9.6 pr.). On delegates: "cum alienos adhibet, explorare eum oportet, cuius fidei, cuius innocentiae sint" (4.9.7.4, of the shipowner).

**14. BGB.** "Ein Gastwirt, der gewerbsmäßig Fremde zur Beherbergung aufnimmt, hat den Schaden zu ersetzen, der durch den Verlust, die Zerstörung oder die Beschädigung von Sachen entsteht, die ein im Betrieb dieses Gewerbes aufgenommener Gast eingebracht hat." (§ 701(1)). The official translation renders this "An innkeeper who accommodates strangers commercially". The claim lapses unless the guest notifies the innkeeper without undue delay (§ 703).

**15. Convention, annex.** "A hotel-keeper shall be liable for any damage to or destruction or loss of property brought to the hotel by any guest who stays at the hotel and has sleeping accommodation put at his disposal." (art. 1(1)). "Any notice or agreement purporting to exclude or diminish the hotel-keeper's liability given or made before the damage, destruction or loss has occurred shall be null and void." (art. 6). The guest loses the benefit if he "does not inform the hotel-keeper without undue delay" (art. 5).

**16. Equality Act 2010.** The first requirement: "where a provision, criterion or practice of A's puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage … to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage" (s. 20(3)). "A discriminates against a disabled person if A fails to comply with that duty in relation to that person." (s. 21(2)). For services, the reference to a disabled person "is to disabled persons generally" (Sch. 2 para. 2(2)), and nothing requires a step that "would fundamentally alter … the nature of the service" (Sch. 2 para. 2(7)). A cannot charge the disabled person for compliance (s. 20(7)). Damages "may include compensation for injured feelings" (s. 119(4)).

**17. *DMA*.** "Regardless of the contract, the Secretary of State has her legal duty; the providers do not." ([241]). Monitoring is required "so that the Secretary of State can know whether she is acting lawfully and in accordance with her duty, and can act immediately if there is a sign that either is not the case" ([236]). "Without proper monitoring the system is without a key means by which to identify and correct failure" ([238]). Contractual KPIs "are well after the event and they are focused on the contractual position between the Secretary of State and the accommodation provider" ([301]). Declarations at [333]. A public-law case under a statutory duty; whether it was appealed was not checked.

## (d) Defeaters

Listed strongest first. Each was read in the text named.

1. **No duty to attend to a guest who may be ill (Georgia).** The Supreme Court of Georgia refused, 4–3, to adopt § 314A(2) for a motel told by a guest's wife that he might need help: "we decline to judicially engraft into the caselaw of this State, the additional duty upon innkeepers to investigate or check on their guests to determine if they are in medical need" (*Rasnick*, 569–570). The court adds that "a moral or humane obligation does not compel the existence of a legal duty" (570). This refutes any claim that US law uniformly imposes § 314A on innkeepers, and it is authority that a host's failure to respond to a report about a guest can be no legal wrong. The dissent states the opposite rule (570–573).

2. **The duty to receive is not shown outside the common law.** Ulpian justifies the innkeeper's strict liability for goods by the innkeeper's freedom to refuse: "est in ipsorum arbitrio, ne quem recipiant" (D. 4.9.1.1). BGB §§ 701–704 and the 1962 Convention impose no duty to receive and say nothing of the guest's person. K4's civil-law support therefore reaches the goods duty only. For the person, the one civil-law lead is Arcangeli (2022), abstract only.

3. **Restaurants, bars and public houses carry no common-law duty to receive.** A refreshment bar "is not an inn" and "no one has a right to insist on being served" (*Rymer*, 140). The licensee of a public house may ask any person to leave, "except in the case of a traveller at an inn" (*Sealey v Tandy*, 299). A restaurateur "is under no common-law duty to serve everyone who applies to him. In the absence of statute, he may accept some customers and reject others on purely personal grounds." (*Alpaugh*, 948). Places of amusement enjoyed "an absolute power to serve whom they pleased" (*Madden*, 253). These support K4's line between the tiers and confine the admission tier to inns. New Jersey is the exception (*Uston*, 173).

4. **The reception tier is narrower for restaurants and bars in at least one US state.** Colorado's eviction duty "reaches only hotels … it does not govern other entertainment-based businesses because the requisite special relationship is absent" (*Groh*, n. 7). Iowa goes the other way (*Hoyt*, 777).

5. **No duty to protect a guest from the guest's own obvious risk; no general assumption of responsibility (England).** Coulson J held that a pub owed no duty to a customer who slid down its banister: "a defendant does not owe a duty to regulate the claimant's activities for the claimant's own benefit" (*Geary*, the paragraph before [58]), and "The mere fact that the defendant was an occupier cannot therefore, without more, give rise to the necessary proximity." ([69]). This narrows K3's first two grounds: in English law the host's assumed responsibility covers premises and third parties, and needs "specific facts" or reliance beyond that ([58]).

6. **The duty to the person seldom produces liability.** The claimants lost on breach in *Al-Najar* (both courts), *Everett*, *Lewis* and *Shadday*. Posner J: "The hotel becomes liable for guest-on-guest crime only when it has some reason to think such crime likely." (*Shadday*, 517). Missouri holds a motel to ordinary care, not the highest (*Wilson v KAL Motel*).

7. **Statute appears to cut back the paying guest's contractual safety warranty.** *MacLenan* made the innkeeper answer for independent contractors (333). OLA 1957 s. 5(1) now sets the implied term at the common duty of care, to which s. 2(4)(b) applies. This is my reading of the section; no authority on the point was read.

8. **K5: the duty was also public.** The innkeeper could be indicted (*Rymer*; Blackstone, 4 Comm. 168; *Constantine*, 698), the New York Court of Appeals describes the duty as owed "to the general public" (*Madden*, 253), and the Irish Act makes refusal an offence (s. 12). None of these texts denies the private action; each jurisdiction read allows both. Sandoval-Strausz's "not 'rights' in any proper sense" (p. 66, carded) collides with Birkett J's "a right at common law" for England in 1944.

9. **K5: the refused guest's remedy is thin.** Birkett J awarded five guineas and refused exemplary damages (*Constantine*, 708). In 2024 the High Court noted that since the Civil Procedure Rules "it is no longer correct to say that a claimant alleging a wrong where damage is not part of the gist of the cause of action … is entitled to have his claim adjudicated as of right" (*Uzbekov*, [38]; I read only the opening of that paragraph).

10. **"Historical curiosity".** The Tenth Circuit called the duty "dormant or stale" in Kansas while holding it unabrogated (*Thomas*, 666). Hirsch (1928, abstract) writes that "the 'reason for the rule' is no longer existent". McBain (2006) argues for abolishing the strict liability for goods, by its title; the text is closed. Against these, *Al-Najar* [183]–[187] and *Uzbekov* [37] treat the innkeeper's duties as current law, and the 1956 and 1963 Acts keep them by statute. I found no judgment holding the duty to receive obsolete.

11. **Placed guests fall outside the admission tier.** People placed in a hotel by the Home Office "do not pay Somani; payment is made to Somani through the contract with CTM" and "have no choice of room" (*Epping*, [19]). The statutory duty to accommodate lies on the Secretary of State (IAA 1999, ss. 95, 98; *Epping* [52]–[53]) or the housing authority (Housing Act 1996, ss. 188, 206), and "the providers do not" hold it (*DMA*, [241]). Nothing read says who owes the reception duties to placed residents. The occupier's duty does not turn on who pays: the claimant in *Lewis* was sent by his employer and sued as a visitor ([1], [8]).

12. **A legal exception is owed to some guests.** Equality Act ss. 20–21 and ADA § 12182(b)(2)(A)(ii) require a provider to depart from its own practice for a disabled guest. v6's sentence that no source makes an exception something the guest is owed is wrong for disabled guests in Great Britain and the United States.
