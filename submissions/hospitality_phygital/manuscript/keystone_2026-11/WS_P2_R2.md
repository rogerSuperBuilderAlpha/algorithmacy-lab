## (a) Synthesis against K3 and K7 (P2, P3)

Every statement is followed by its locator. Paragraph numbers in the three encyclopaedia entries count a block quotation, displayed principle or numbered list as part of the paragraph that introduces it. Brandt's pages are those of the accepted manuscript ('ms p.').

### K3. The role specifies content, trigger and holder; three external grounds bind

**Status on what I read: open on whether a role binds by itself; narrowed on each ground.** The two texts that decide the first question, Hardimon (1994) and Simmons (1996), are closed, and I reached them only through quotation and an abstract.

1. K3's structure is in print as one side of a live dispute. Critics of associative obligation hold that the duty to perform "the (conventional) duties that constitute the role" depends on an exercise of will or on a natural duty, and that justification "bottoms out not in the fact of association but in a moral principle external to it".
   - Locator: Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.3, para. 8, reporting Dagger (2018: 79–82), Wellman (2000: 553) and Simmons (2001: 83–84; 2005: 111–115), none of which I read.

2. The same entry describes the role as what fixes content. Roles "are at least partly constituted by the various obligations their occupants owe to the other(s) in the relationship", and "Law specifies, perhaps incompletely, what counts as doing one’s share."
   - Locator: Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.3, paras 1 and 3.

3. Jeske argues the first half of K3 in her own voice. Talk of a role's obligations may record "merely others’ expectations of persons holding such positions"; Hardimon's test of reflective acceptance is "epiphenomenal with respect to the justification"; and a just group's description of a person as a member does not bind her.
   - Locator: Jeske 2019: §4, paras 10, 18 and 19.

4. The opposing view stands in the same texts. Hardimon defines a role obligation as "a moral requirement, which attaches to an institutional role, whose content is fixed by the function of the role, and whose normative force flows from the role". Horton holds that obligations constitutive of a relationship "do not stand in need of moral justification in terms of a set of basic moral principles or some comprehensive moral theory".
   - Locator: Hardimon 1994: 334, as quoted in Jeske 2019: §4, para. 16; Horton 1992: 150–51, as quoted in Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.3, para. 4.

5. Associative theorists make three replies to K3's side, and the entry leaves them unanswered: the critics "conflate considerations that disable associative obligations with considerations that ground or justify" them; the standards invoked are "internal to the relationship"; and there is "no vantage point outside" actual practices. The entry reports that of the answers given "none now commands widespread assent."
   - Locator: Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.3, paras 9, 10 and 11; preamble, para. 1.

6. The first reply bears directly on K3's first ground. If freedom to leave is only a condition without which the role's value fails, then "it is the good realized in the relationship that justifies participants’ claims against one another ... not any consent implicit in their continued participation in it". On that view voluntary assumption enables and the role grounds.
   - Locator: Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.3, para. 9.

7. What Hardimon holds about roles a person chooses, which is the host's case, I could not establish. Jeske reports him only on unchosen roles. Dowie quotes him as saying that contractual role obligations are "acquired by signing on for the roles from which they derive". The abstract of Simmons's reply says Hardimon thinks the standard view "leaves out" an "absolutely vital factor" even for contractual roles, and that Simmons defends a version of that view as "true".
   - Locator: Jeske 2019: §4, paras 16–18; Hardimon 1994: 337, as quoted in Dowie 2024: section 'Hardimon’s view of roles as contractual', para. 1; Simmons, author's abstract of the SSRN posting (Crossref 10.2139/ssrn.1344466). See (b).

8. The role-as-specification idea is stated in terms by one source already in the library, which treats roles as derivative of control. I did not re-read it in this run.
   - Locator: card `kong2026delegation` (Kong 2026: 4, as carded on 8 Nov 2026).

9. Two objections reach K3's design and not only its parts. Fair play, the obligation to bear a share of the burdens of a cooperative practice whose benefits one takes, is one of four main contenders in the political-obligation debate, and K3 omits it. And sceptics about combining principles hold that "combining a set of principles that are unsatisfactory individually will hardly produce a strong and satisfying theory".
   - Locator: Dagger and Lefkowitz 2021: §4.2, paras 1–2; §5, para. 3 (Simmons 2007, n. 17, as reported).

10. Goodin's account, on his own abstract, denies that the grounds are independent: "it is the beneficiary's vulnerability rather than any voluntary commitment as such on the part of the benefactor that generates these special responsibilities."
    - Locator: Goodin 1985, 'Vulnerabilities and Responsibilities', *APSR* 79(3): 775–787, author's abstract as deposited with Crossref (10.2307/1956843). Abstract depth; the article and the book are closed.

### K7, P2. A holder that performs through others keeps a duty to see that the work is done with care

**Status on what I read: holds in the moral register, narrowed, on one article read in full.** The legal doctrines belong to other workstreams.

12. Brandt states the premise. When a responsibility is delegated, "the individual initially bound by the responsibility still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled"; the delegator must complete the task or "find someone else to do it", and must "‘make right’ any negative consequences", "even in the absence of blame".
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: ms pp. 3–4.

13. Brandt supplies the reason why a host's outsourcing is delegation and not transfer where the duty arose from the host's agreement with the guest. An arrangement with a third party cannot shift what was agreed with the party owed: that "would amount to permitting the unilateral alteration of a promise".
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: ms p. 10; n. 32 (ms p. 18).

14. Brandt denies discharge by careful choice: "I do not fulfil my responsibility by merely taking reasonable steps to ensure that Michelle’s needs will be fulfilled. Even if I had good reason to trust Bob, I still have responsibilities towards Michelle."
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: n. 34 (ms p. 18); the general form of the rejected view at ms p. 5.

15. The view he denies is in print. O'Neill, as Brandt quotes her, holds that parents who "take reasonable steps to ensure that their children will be adequately reared ... do not breach but transfer their parental obligations". Archard, as Jeske reports him, lets a person with "an obligation to ensure that someone acts as a parent to the child" fulfil it "by finding adoptive parents who will assume parental responsibilities".
    - Locator: O'Neill 1979, as quoted in Brandt 2017: ms pp. 4–5; Archard 2010: 104–105, 127, as reported in Jeske 2019: §6.2, para. 2. Neither primary was read.

16. Brandt narrows P2 in two ways. Transfers do occur, where the body that assigns a role reassigns it or a colleague takes it over; and a delegator who had good reason to trust the delegate "is not blameworthy if things go awry".
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: ms p. 3 (the newsletter), ms p. 6 (the physician's shift), ms pp. 3–4 (blame).

17. More than one party can hold the duty, and the delegate answers to the delegator. "It is perfectly possible for multiple individuals to be fully responsible for ensuring that the same responsibility is fulfilled", and the delegate's promise gives the delegator "recourse".
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: ms pp. 12–13. This bears on K8.

18. The UN Guiding Principles grade the responsibility for other firms' conduct. An enterprise should "Seek to prevent or mitigate" impacts "directly linked to their operations, products or services by their business relationships, even if they have not contributed to those impacts"; it should use leverage; and for such impacts the responsibility "does not require that the enterprise itself provide for remediation".
    - Locator: UN 2011: Principle 13(b), p. 14; Principle 19, Commentary, p. 22; Principle 22, Commentary, pp. 24–25.

19. Hindriks holds a collective to its duty when a member fails and leaves the case of an outside firm unclear. A collective "cannot plausibly invoke the excuse of the member as an extenuating circumstance" and "is responsible for finding a replacement"; of a hired agency that does not deliver he says only that "this agency is to blame".
    - Locator: Hindriks 2025: p. 15; p. 10, n. 24.

20. In Delaware corporate law, directors who leave operations to management keep a duty "to exercise oversight". What they keep is a duty to be informed, not a duty to guarantee the work.
    - Locator: *Marchand v. Barnhill* (Del. 2019), slip op. at 4 and 30–31.

### K7, P3. A duty to see that something is done includes the means to learn that it is not being done, and to correct it while performance is still possible

**Status on what I read: the paper's own step.** No text I read states P3 as `CLAIMS_FIXED` words it. Its parts are in print separately, and one instrument states most of the conclusion for another field.

21. The major premise has a published argument. Hindriks argues that collective agents have a 'duty of control', "a second-order obligation" that "requires the agent to meet the prerequisites for fulfilling first-order obligations", on the principle that "if an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it."
    - Locator: Hindriks 2025: pp. 4–5.

22. Hindriks's two prerequisites correspond to P3's two halves. Moral control is being able to "recognize moral considerations and react to them"; a control gap is a deficiency in "how receptive or reactive" the agent is; fulfilling the duty requires the agent "to assess such risks as well as the deficiencies that give rise to them" and "to identify the deficiency from which it suffers".
    - Locator: Hindriks 2025: pp. 3, 4, 5 and 13.

23. Three things in P3 go beyond Hindriks. He has the collective learn from its own structure, culture and members' excuses, never from the people affected; he does not say the duty is owed to those people; and he does not tie it to the time in which a performance is still possible.
    - Locator: Hindriks 2025: pp. 13–15 (the two approaches); p. 4 (risk "to others"). The words 'complaint', 'customer', 'stakeholder' and 'feedback' do not occur in the article (searched the saved text).

24. Brandt gives the correcting half and is silent on the learning half. The delegator must act when the delegate "does not complete the task" or when "it becomes clear" that the delegate is failing; in his nanny case the delegate telephones. He mentions "a mechanism for finding out" once, as something policy might offer those who "so wish".
    - Locator: Brandt 2017: ms pp. 3, 5 and 15.

25. The UN Guiding Principles join the two halves and name the channel. Enterprises should "track the effectiveness of their response", drawing on "feedback from both internal and external sources, including affected stakeholders". An operational-level grievance mechanism provides "a channel for those directly impacted by the enterprise’s operations to raise concerns when they believe they are being or will be adversely impacted", and lets impacts "be remediated early and directly by the business enterprise, thereby preventing harms from compounding".
    - Locator: UN 2011: Principle 20, p. 22; Principle 29, Commentary, p. 32.

26. The Guiding Principles also state the paper's boundary between interrupting and repairing, and a timing rule. "Potential impacts should be addressed through prevention or mitigation, while actual impacts – those that have already occurred – should be a subject for remediation"; priority goes to impacts "where delayed response would make them irremediable."
    - Locator: UN 2011: Principle 17, Commentary, p. 18; Principle 24, p. 26.

27. They are soft law on human-rights impacts. "Nothing in these Guiding Principles should be read as creating new international law obligations"; the responsibility is "a global standard of expected conduct", "distinct from issues of legal liability and enforcement". A grievance need not allege a human-rights abuse to be raised, but the mechanism exists because unaddressed concerns "may over time escalate into more major disputes and human rights abuses."
    - Locator: UN 2011: General principles, p. 1; Principle 11, Commentary, p. 13; Principle 12, Commentary, p. 14; Principle 29, Commentary, p. 32.

28. They do not require what K7 requires of the person reached, and they qualify it. The nearest words are that responsibility be "assigned to the appropriate level and function" with decision-making that can "enable effective responses". And an enterprise "cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome".
    - Locator: UN 2011: Principle 19(a), pp. 20–21; Principle 31, Commentary on (h), p. 35.

29. One court states the learning half as law: "directors must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it", and the system must be "adequate to assure the board that appropriate information will come to its attention in a timely manner as a matter of ordinary operations, so that it may satisfy its responsibility."
    - Locator: *Marchand v. Barnhill*, slip op. at 30; at 29 n. 100, quoting *Caremark*, 698 A.2d at 970, which I did not read.

30. On the facts alleged in that case the signal stopped below the body charged with oversight. Information about contamination "never made its way to the board", which "was unaware of any problems until it was too late"; procedures and reports at management level did not show that the board had arranged to be informed.
    - Locator: *Marchand*, slip op. at 12, 9 and 34–36. Pleading stage: the facts are allegations (slip op. at 6 n. 13).

31. The same case narrows P3 as law. The duty is to try: the Court was "not examining the effectiveness of a board-level compliance and reporting system after the fact". It is owed to the corporation and enforced by a stockholder; the consumers harmed have no claim under it.
    - Locator: *Marchand*, slip op. at 31; at 1 (the derivative suit and the stockholders' loss).

32. So the grounds the paper can offer for P3 are four, and none is the step itself: a philosophical argument for the general principle (Hindriks), a philosophical statement of the duty to step in (Brandt), a soft-law statement of the channel and its timing for another field (UN 2011), and one jurisdiction's law on arranging to be informed (*Marchand*). The step from these to 'a channel from the guest to a person with power over the single case, for as long as performance is possible' remains the authors'.
    - Locator: statements 21–31; the summary is mine.

33. A published concept already names much of K7's conclusion: the 'operational-level grievance mechanism'. `CLAIMS_FIXED` says that in this event the paper credits the concept and claims the application. What remains to claim is the relation (host and guest), the derivation from the host's own duty, and the requirement of a person with power.
    - Locator: UN 2011: Principle 29 and Commentary, pp. 31–32; `CLAIMS_FIXED.md`, K7, 'Refutes or narrows', last bullet.

**Defeaters for K7, in the order I would weigh them**

- Discharge by reasonable hand-over is a published view (statement 15). Brandt argues against it by cases; a referee may side with O'Neill and Archard.
- The one philosophical article on delegation states no duty to monitor (statement 24).
- The one legal source requires an effort, declines to judge effectiveness, and gives the person harmed nothing (statement 31).
- The soft-law source lightens the responsibility for merely linked firms (statement 18) and is not law (statement 27).
- Hindriks's duty is not said to be owed to anyone (statement 23).
