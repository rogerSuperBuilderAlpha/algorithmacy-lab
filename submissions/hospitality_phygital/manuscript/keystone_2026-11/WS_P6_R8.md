## (b) Synthesis

| Claim | Verdict | Strongest contrary evidence |
|---|---|---|
| K7, P4, first half: the guest's own report is often the only timely signal | **Holds, narrowed** | Guests who feel powerless stay silent (min2020angry: 1-2); a system-wide outage reached staff with no report at all (xu2024disciplined: 5021) |
| K7, P4, second half: correction needs a person with power over the system's output in the single case | **Holds, narrowed** | Recognition failures were cured by the guest alone, without any person (xu2024disciplined: 5027, 5030) |
| K9, empirical side: problems are resolved through people with authority, and automated or self-service arrangements remove them | **First half holds, narrowed; second half not shown** | The one automated hotel read kept a worker on site and used the worker as fall-back (xu2024disciplined: 5022, 5035); a remembered robot failure did not raise intention to switch to human service (lee2025robot: 9) |

### K7, P4, first half

The guest's report is often not made, and that is the strongest evidence against this half. Min and Kim's restaurant experiments found that angry customers who did not feel powerful were reluctant to complain face to face, so the operator lost the chance "to hear from customers" (min2020angry: printed pp. 1-2); the report gives no sample size or statistic. Gelbrich and Roschk, following Blodgett and Anderson, note that negative word of mouth can arise "prior to—or in lieu of—a complaint" (gelbrich2011metaanalysis: 28). Lee et al. advise operators to find "silent customers with some sort of grievance" after robot failures (lee2025robot: 17), which is advice and not a finding. A failure of the system as a whole needs no report: on the two days the check-in kiosk was down, the hotel's worker met all 51 arriving guests (xu2024disciplined: 5021).

For a failure in the guest's own case, the open evidence points the other way. The Helsinki hotel that Schumacher and Komppula studied "relies mainly on customer feedback concerning service failures", because its staff did not ask guests how their stay was going (schumacher2016case: 121); the authors' own frame still allows that staff "may either spot the service failures themselves or be informed about them by customers" (120). At Xu and Greiffenhagen's automated hotels, each of the 13 guests whose ID, reservation or kiosk failed went to find a worker (xu2024disciplined: 5035), so the guest's approach was how the failure reached a person.

**Verdict: holds, narrowed.** In the hotels studied, a failure in the guest's own case reached a person mainly, and in the automated hotel only, through the guest (schumacher2016case: 121; xu2024disciplined: 5035). 'Only' is not shown in general (schumacher2016case: 120; xu2024disciplined: 5021). The signal is often withheld (min2020angry: 2). The base is thin: two small qualitative hotel studies and one practitioner summary.

### K7, P4, second half

Correction did not always need a person. When facial recognition failed, the kiosk "will simply repeat the instruction again and again, so it is the response-giver who has to modify their behaviour", and guests did so until it worked (xu2024disciplined: 5030); by my count from Figure 11, 86 of 610 guests needed a second or later attempt (5027), and the paper reports no worker's intervention in those cases beyond examples of staff coaching guests at the kiosk (5025). Gelbrich and Roschk's meta-analysis never codes who handled a complaint or what that person could decide; the remedies it studies (compensation, the employee's manner, procedures) could in principle be delivered by rule (gelbrich2011metaanalysis: 26, 36, 39).

Where the failure lay beyond the guest's own effort, correction ran through a person who could override the outcome. The 13 guests the kiosk could not process were checked in by hand by a service worker (xu2024disciplined: 5035). In the staffed hotel, reception staff corrected most failures themselves, with discretion over discounts and room changes, and the manager took large failures and large compensation (schumacher2016case: 121-122). That authority was graded and blurred. Gift vouchers and alcohol lay outside the desk's power (122), structural failures outside anyone's at the desk (123), and staff did not know where the line fell (123). Across services, recovery outcomes ran through a remedy the firm grants and through the employee's manner, and the manner weighed more in services than in goods (gelbrich2011metaanalysis: 32, 34, 36).

**Verdict: holds, narrowed.** The person with power over the single case was needed where neither the system nor the guest's retrying could cure the failure (xu2024disciplined: 5035; cf. 5027, 5030). In a staffed hotel that person may be a receptionist with limited, unwritten discretion, with larger remedies one level up (schumacher2016case: 121-123).

### K9, empirical side

The one automated hotel read did not remove the person. Xu and Greiffenhagen's chain kept service workers on site "for legal and technical reasons" although guests could register alone (xu2024disciplined: 5035). What automation did was demote the worker to fall-back: guests walked past the worker to the kiosk, and the worker sent those who asked back to it with "do it yourself" (5022). Lee et al. found that a remembered robot failure did not directly raise intention to switch to human-staffed service (lee2025robot: 9), and mean switching intention was low (Table 2, p. 10). That study did not ask whom respondents turned to during the failure, and its sample was a judgmental WeChat sample in Macau covering robots of every kind (7-8). Reinders et al. (2008), which the brief expected to show that an employee fall-back offsets forced self-service, was closed; its abstract, seen on the WUR record, cannot be used.

The first half has support in both settings read. Guests' problems that the system could not cure were resolved by a worker (xu2024disciplined: 5035), and in the staffed hotel by reception staff and, for large cases, the manager (schumacher2016case: 121-122).

**Verdict: first half holds, narrowed; second half not shown.** On open evidence, automated hotels keep people as a fall-back that guests reach by seeking them out (xu2024disciplined: 5022, 5035); they do not remove them. Whether other arrangements remove them is open: the deciding studies (Liu et al. 2026 on unmanned smart hotels; Dao and Theotokis 2020, whose third study puts employees out of reach; Collier et al. 2017) are closed. The paper may say that automation moves the person behind the machine. It may not say, on this evidence, that automation removes the person.

### Gaps

- I found no openly readable hospitality study that reports from its own data what share of dissatisfied guests complain to the hotel, in Consensus (rows 11, 15, 28), WebSearch (row 25), Crossref and Unpaywall (rows 21-23, 26) and OpenAlex (rows 18, 20), 11 Nov 2026. The figure of 58% complaining and 42% silent comes from Su and Bowen (2001) as quoted in a practitioner note (Kwok 2019: 4); I could not open Su and Bowen, and the paper should not use the figure.
- I found no open study showing that staff monitoring or automated systems detect guests' service failures sooner or more completely than guests' own reports, in Consensus (rows 11, 15, 16, 28, 29) and WebSearch (row 25), 11 Nov 2026.
- I found no open study comparing recovery after a self-service or robot failure in a hospitality setting with and without a reachable employee, in Consensus (rows 12, 16, 29) and OpenAlex (rows 14, 18), 11 Nov 2026; the closest are closed (Dao and Theotokis 2020; Liu et al. 2026).
