## 1. Verdict

For the core case, the argument settles the host's legal duty: a single proprietor running a staffed hotel in England or Ireland for a consumer who booked the room. Steps 2 to 5 get the doctrine broadly right for that case. It does not settle the duty in the cases the paper exists to decide. Two failures cause this. First, Step 6's conclusion is spent in the legal register ('has failed in the duty of performance', 'inside the duty K4 established', l. 235), yet its premise P3 is conceded to be moral only (l. 218), so the dossier breaks its own rule (l. 15). The one English statute that legislates a channel during performance by others, the 2018 package regulations, deliberately keeps that channel out of the private-law contract. Second, Step 5 states its allocation among firms as law in 'The answer' (l. 9). In fact it is a proposal, and the authorities this run saved contradict it: they make owners, operators and brands liable concurrently, they attach innkeeper duties to the proprietor and not to whoever controls the building, and they bring brands that run guest-facing components inside agency. The weakest single point is C's legal sentence at l. 235. Next come the brand and system-vendor rows of the hard-case table. The legal channel can be repaired, but only if the authors give up the 'no free-standing right' framing in the legal register. Every channel the law actually creates for consumers is either the consumer's own remedial right or a duty owed to a regulator.

## 2. Ranked objections

Line numbers ("l.") refer to `ARGUMENT.md` as it stood at 18:04 on 11 Nov 2026. Backticked citekeys point to saved texts under `manuscript/revision_2026-11/research/sources/`. Four texts were fetched this session through a fetch tool that returns extracted passages: *Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25 (caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2019/25, read to para 90) and, from EUR-Lex, Directives 2005/29/EC, (EU) 2024/2853 and (EU) 2019/2161. Recheck quotations from those four against the official texts before using them.

### T1. C is spent in the legal register on a premise conceded to be moral (fatal)
- **Step attacked.** Step 6, C, l. 235 ('A host that keeps no channel ... has failed in the duty of performance'; 'This keeps the conclusion inside the duty K4 established'), against l. 15, l. 216 and l. 218 ('It is offered in the moral register').
- **Objection.** K4 is a legal duty. Placing C inside it turns a moral P3 into a legal conclusion, which is the move l. 15 forbids. The inference that C's sentence needs is this: a measure was absent, and the measure would have let the host correct the failure, so the host is in breach. English negligence law rejects that inference. In *Al-Najar* the judge refused to require 'an alarm system to alert security staff to open guest doors' because 'There was nothing to suggest that this should be used by any reasonable hotel proprietor' (para 229). He then found that such an alarm 'more likely than not ... would have sounded before the attack' (para 236), revisited breach, and did not change his conclusion (para 237). What the law tests is the standard of 'a reasonable 4 star London hotel' (paras 196-197), and causal efficacy does not meet that test.
- **Footing.** (b) l. 15, l. 218 against l. 235; (a) `alnajar2019cumberland` paras 196-197, 229, 236-237.
- **What would answer it.** Concession and restatement. C as derived from the holder's duty must be confined to the moral register. Any legal correlate needs its own warrant, and T3 to T6 show the only warrants available.

### T2. English package law puts the channel outside the contract (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 6, P3 'Law in neighbouring settings: Package travel' (l. 195), and l. 218 ('Where the law makes a holder answer for its delegates' performance, it already pairs that liability with a channel'). Step 4 is silent on whether the channel is owed to the guest at all.
- **Objection.** The dossier cites only Directive 2015/2302. In England and Wales the contact point is required by reg. 7(2) and Sch. 5 para 4 of SI 2018/634. Reg. 7(10) leaves reg. 7(2) out of the implied conditions. Breach is an offence (reg. 7(12)), and reg. 35 provides that 'no right of action in civil proceedings in respect of any loss arises, by reason only of the commission of an offence under regulations ... 7(12)'. The remedy duty (reg. 15(4)) and the retailer route (reg. 17) are implied terms; the channel is not. In the one setting where an English legislature dealt with a channel during performance by others, it made the channel a duty owed to the public and not to the traveller. The dossier's own card says so (`uk2018package`: 'the contact point is owed to the public authorities'), and `CARD_CORRECTIONS.md` records it (CV-C). Step 6 never mentions it.
- **Footing.** (a) `uk2018package/reg07_wb20231018.txt` (reg. 7(10), (12)); `reg35_wb20250415.txt`; `reg17.txt`.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. State that English law pairs liability for others' performance with a remedy duty owed to the traveller and a channel duty owed to the state. Use reg. 17(2) ('The traveller may address messages, requests or complaints ... directly to the retailer') as the one private-law route English law gives a traveller.

### T3. Causation, and the channel does no liability work in either branch (serious)
- **Step attacked.** C, l. 235 ('whose performance then fails in a way a report would have let it correct'); l. 218.
- **Objection.** Where the holder undertook to provide, it is liable for the failed performance with or without a channel. In *Kuoni* it was 'no part of her case that there was systemic or organisational negligence ... (such as failure to supervise N ...)' (para 8), and liability followed on 'a simple rule' (para 50). Where the holder is only an occupier, the dossier concedes that P3 is not law (l. 218). In neither branch does the absence of a channel add a legal liability. Even then, C's conditional assumes that a report would have been made, would have reached someone in time, and would have been acted on. Each link must be proved 'more likely than not' (`alnajar2019cumberland` para 235). P4's own evidence that many guests do not report (l. 227) weakens the first link in any given case. In personal-injury claims, English law does not compensate a lost chance (lead: *Gregg v Scott*).
- **Footing.** (a) `kuoni2021uksc` paras 8, 50; `alnajar2019cumberland` para 235; (b) l. 227 against l. 235; (c) *Gregg v Scott*.
- **What would answer it.** Concession, plus a new argument that gives the channel the legal role the law actually assigns it, which is allocative and remedial. Package law lets the traveller's failure to inform count against the traveller (art. 13(2), recital 34; reg. 15(3)). A host that kept no route cannot fairly hold a non-report against the guest. Separately, the guest's right to require repeat performance (CRA s. 55) needs an addressee.

### T4. The 'no free-standing right of the guest' framing does not survive in law (serious)
- **Step attacked.** C, l. 235 ('It is not a free-standing right of the guest. The guest's claim is to performance'); Step 4, 'What follows for Step 6' (l. 138).
- **Objection.** Every channel or correction route in the instruments this run read takes one of two forms. Either it is the consumer's own right: CRA s. 55(1), 'a right to require the trader to perform the service again'; reg. 17, an implied term; reg. 15(4), remedy 'within a reasonable period set by the traveller'; DSA arts 12 and 20, which art. 54 makes a source of compensation to 'Recipients of the service'. Or it is a duty owed to a regulator (T2). The law has no third category: a channel that belongs to the host's performance duty, that the guest cannot claim, and whose absence makes the performance a breach. Moreover, the performance duty itself runs only to the party to a consumer contract (CRA s. 48(1)), so 'The guest's claim is to performance' is false for a guest who did not contract.
- **Footing.** (a) `uk2015consumer` ss. 48(1), 55(1); `uk2018package` regs 15, 17; `eu2022dsa` arts 12(1), 20, 54.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. Keep the framing in the moral register, where the guest does not demand and the host owes. In the legal register, state the correlate as the guest's remedial right. This costs nothing that Step 4 needs.

### T5. *Al-Najar* paras 205-213 and *Everett* para 36 do not show a guest channel as part of reasonable care (serious)
- **Step attacked.** C, l. 235 ('the law's practice of weighing reporting arrangements as part of reasonable care').
- **Objection.** Para 205 reads in full: 'There was a system of reporting by departmental meetings and weekly meetings between Mr Stanbridge and Mr Loughrey.' Paras 209 and 213 concern incident review and the security manager's oversight of his officers. All three are internal management systems. The judge reviewed them because the claimants pleaded their absence as breaches (para 198), and he rejected every allegation. None is a route from a guest. *Everett* para 36 is a dictum about nightclubs ('it will be a rare night club that does not need ...'). It concerns staff raising the alarm, and it allows that the duty 'may be no higher than that staff be trained to look out for any sign of trouble and to alert security staff'. Because the standard is the practice of comparable establishments (Al-Najar paras 196-197), an unstaffed or self-check-in class of host may set its own lower standard.
- **Footing.** (a) `alnajar2019cumberland` paras 196-198, 205, 209, 213; `everett2011comojo` para 36.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing to 'a court may weigh internal reporting'. The authors also need a new source: an English case in which the absence of a route from guest to staff was weighed (none was read). The best English text the run holds is unused: *Kuoni* para 31 adopts Longmore LJ's view that 'hotel staff must be helpful to guests when asked for assistance', but see T6.

### T6. The dossier places the channel in the tier the host can define away (serious)
- **Step attacked.** C (l. 235), read with l. 9 ('the duty to perform follows the undertaking'), l. 95 ('Assumption binds a host only to what it undertook') and the floor at l. 99.
- **Objection.** The non-excludable floor protects care and skill in performing what was undertaken. It does not fix what is undertaken, and for services the floor is money: a restriction is void only 'if it would prevent the consumer ... from recovering the price paid' (CRA s. 57(3)). *Kuoni* measures staff assistance against the standard contracted, 'the description of the hotel as a four-star hotel' (para 31). A host that sells an unstaffed, self-service stay has not undertaken assistance. So C, located in the duty to perform, does not bind the very host the paper is about. The reliance and control grounds that the dossier uses to resist notices (l. 99) support the care duty for the person, not the performance duty.
- **Footing.** (b) l. 9, l. 95, l. 99 against l. 235; (a) `uk2015consumer` s. 57(1), (3); `kuoni2021uksc` para 31.
- **What would answer it.** A new argument that places the legal correlate in a floor the host cannot define away. Two candidates exist in texts already saved or fetched. The Equality Act's anticipatory duty to provide an 'auxiliary aid' and information 'in an accessible format' (s. 20(5)-(6)) is owed by every service-provider (s. 29(7)(a)) and is actionable by the guest (s. 114(1)(a)). It reaches a disabled guest who cannot use a kiosk or app. UCPD art. 9(d) treats as a mark of an aggressive practice 'any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract'. Otherwise, concede.

### T7. The services tier and its floor reach only consumer contracts with traders (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 2, 'Services' (l. 59) and the claim that 'Every commercial host that receives a guest owes ... performance' (l. 52); Step 3 floor (l. 99).
- **Objection.** CRA Part 1 Ch. 4 'applies to a contract for a trader to supply a service to a consumer' (s. 48(1)). A consumer is 'an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside that individual's trade' (s. 2(3)). Several kinds of guest fall outside it: the business traveller, the guest whose room an employer or a placing body booked, and the companion who did not contract. Several kinds of paid host fall outside it too. Where the host is not a trader, 'the consumer rights stemming from Union consumer protection law do not apply to the contract' (Directive 2011/83 art. 6a(1)(c), inserted by Directive 2019/2161). *Kuoni* applied the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982 s. 13 (para 7). Business contracts fall under that Act, whose implied term can be varied subject to the Unfair Contract Terms Act (lead).
- **Footing.** (a) `uk2015consumer` ss. 2(3), 48(1); `kuoni2021uksc` para 7; Directive 2019/2161 art. 4 (fetched); (c) SGSA 1982 ss. 13, 16; UCTA 1977 ss. 2-3; Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 s. 1.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing to consumer guests of trader hosts. Read SGSA and UCTA for business guests, and the 1999 Act for guests who did not contract.

### T8. 'EU consumer law' is in Step 2's status with no EU source in its warrant (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 2 status, l. 52.
- **Objection.** The warrant (ll. 56-60) cites English, Irish, American, Roman and German law and the 1962 convention. No EU instrument is cited for a hotel's duty of care for the person, for a hotel's duty to perform services with care, or for admission. EU package law binds organisers of packages, not a hotel that sells a room alone, and the DSA governs intermediaries.
- **Footing.** (b) l. 52 against ll. 56-60.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing: delete EU consumer law from the status, or confine it to package organisers.

### T9. The admission tier is not innkeeper-only (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 2 claim, 'Only an innkeeper owes the first' (l. 52); the restaurant row, 'Owes the reception tier, not admission' (l. 120).
- **Objection.** Equality Act 2010 s. 29(1) provides that a service-provider 'must not discriminate against a person requiring the service by not providing the person with the service'. That reaches restaurants, bars, rental hosts and platforms, and the guest can enforce it in the county court (s. 114(1)(a)). The table applies s. 29 to the selective rental host (l. 121) but leaves it out of the tier structure. Every commercial host owes a partial duty not to refuse, and only the innkeeper owes the general duty to receive all comers. This also bears on Step 7, because a system that refuses before reception can breach s. 29(1).
- **Footing.** (a) `eqa2010s29/s29.html`, s. 29(1); `eqa2010s20/s114.txt`; (b) l. 52 against l. 121.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing: two kinds of admission duty.

### T10. 'Control picks the holder' is a proposal stated as law, and the law read multiplies holders (serious)
- **Step attacked.** 'The answer', l. 9 (unqualified); Step 5 'Among firms' (l. 161); the middle-position table, l. 298.
- **Objection.** Step 5's status calls the allocation 'a proposal with partial support' (l. 152), but l. 9 states it as the answer. Three groups of authority read in this run contradict it. In *Crinkley* the jury found against 'all the defendants', owners, operator and brand, and found 'the motel owners and TRAVCO ... negligent' (para 9, at 159). Control did not pick one holder. The 1956 Act attaches innkeeper duties 'to the proprietor ... and shall not attach to any other person' (s. 1(1)), and the Irish Act defines the proprietor as 'the person carrying on the business of the hotel' (s. 1(1)). Under a management contract that person is the owner, not the operator in day-to-day control (lead: deRoos 2010). EU law allocates between platform and supplier by disclosure, 'without prejudice to any responsibility ... under other Union or national law' (Directive 2011/83 art. 6a(1)(d)).
- **Footing.** (b) l. 9 against l. 152; (a) `crinkley1988holiday` para 9; `hpa1956/whole_act.txt` s. 1(1); `ie1963hotel` s. 1(1); Directive 2019/2161 (fetched); (c) *Wheat v E Lacon*; Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978; deRoos 2010.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. Control suffices for a care duty, several parties may hold that duty at once, and contribution law divides the loss among them. Label the allocation a proposal wherever it appears.

### T11. 'Control' changes meaning between Step 3 and Step 5 (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 3 ground (l. 77, 'the space the host controls'); platform row (l. 117, under 'Control of the guest's space'); l. 161.
- **Objection.** Step 5 joins three different relations under one word: control of premises and of who enters (*Everett* para 31), operational control (*Crinkley* para 7), and 'decisive influence over the conditions under which that service is provided' (*Uber* para 39). The third is a test for classifying a service as 'a service in the field of transport' rather than 'an information society service' (para 40). It is a test for market access and says nothing about responsibility to a passenger, let alone a guest. Treating the three as one ground gives 'control' the platform row without any argument.
- **Footing.** (b) l. 77, l. 117, l. 161; (a) `uber2017cjeu` paras 39-40.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. Name the senses apart and say which one grounds the care duty. Treat the platform's 'control of conditions of supply' as a proposal. The dossier would also need a liability authority it has not read (lead: *Wathelet*).

### T12. The brand row misreads the US cases this run saved (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Brand row (l. 114: 'Control: No'; 'in one of three cases'; 'Where it runs a guest-facing system, it is a supplier to the holder'); l. 161; l. 298 ('inconsistently').
- **Objection.** Five decisions in the run's sources bear on brands. In *Orlando Executive Park* the Florida Supreme Court approved a verdict against Howard Johnson, which 'operated the restaurant, lounge, and adult theater at the motel' (433 So. 2d at 494). In *Billops* the Delaware Supreme Court reversed summary judgment for Hilton. It held that a mandatory operating manual regulating 'front office procedures' could 'show day-to-day control', which would make the franchisee the franchisor's agent (391 A.2d at 197-198). *Mobil* concerns a petrol-station mini-mart, not a hotel, and it distinguishes a franchisor that 'actually operated several components' (648 So. 2d at 121). The *Crinkley* majority found that the brand 'retained a significant degree of control' (para 51, at 166). The tests in these cases treat a brand that runs a guest-facing component as a participant or principal, not a supplier. Beyond agency, the saved *J.L. v Best Western* order recognises a theory of 'direct liability' for hotel brands under 18 U.S.C. § 1595(a), although it dismissed the claims on their facts.
- **Footing.** (a) `orlando1983robbins` at 494; `billops1978magness` at 197-198; `mobil1995bransford` at 120-121; `crinkley1988holiday` para 51; `jl2021bestwestern` (govinfo text, §§ IV.A.1).
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing and correction. The cases are consistent, not inconsistent: liability turns on control or participation in the operation. A brand that runs a guest-facing system may be a principal.

### T13. The system-vendor proposal fails in EU law for physical harm (serious)
- **Step attacked.** System-vendor row (l. 118: 'Answers to the holder, and the holder answers to the guest').
- **Objection.** The new Product Liability Directive provides that 'software' is a product (art. 4(1); recital 13 names AI systems among the examples). Any 'natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product' is entitled to compensation (art. 5(1)), including for 'death or personal injury' (art. 6(1)(a)), and the manufacturer is liable (art. 8(1)(a)). It applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026 (art. 2(1)). For a smart lock, alarm or other guest-facing system that injures, the vendor therefore answers to the guest directly. This is not 'the law read on hosts', but it is the law that governs the case the row addresses.
- **Footing.** (a) Directive (EU) 2024/2853 arts 2(1), 4(1), 5(1), 6(1)(a), 8(1)(a), recital 13 (fetched).
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing to performance failures and loss that is not physical. Check the UK position under the Consumer Protection Act 1987 Part I (lead).

### T14. *R (DMA)* is carried into private law for placed guests (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Placed-guest row (l. 119); objections table (l. 318); Step 6 public-law bullet (l. 194).
- **Objection.** *R (DMA)* enforced a statutory duty and Human Rights Act s. 6 against a Minister by judicial review, with £1,000 as 'just satisfaction' (para 341). It is placed in a column headed 'Who the law reaches' as if it were a duty to the guest of the host's kind. The Supreme Court has held that 'public authorities do not owe a duty of care at common law merely because they have statutory powers or duties' (*Poole* para 65(2)). Placing a family in housing 'did not involve the provision of a service to them on which they ... could be expected to rely', and the council had not 'taken the claimants into its care' (para 81). Nor is the operator's duty to a placed guest made out on 'control' alone. *Al-Najar* rests the hotel's duty on invitation (para 187), and *Everett* rests it on control, reliance and 'an economic relationship' (para 31). A placed guest lacks the invitation, the reliance and the economic relationship.
- **Footing.** (a) `dma2020sshd` paras 239, 341; *Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25 paras 65(2), 81 (fetched); `alnajar2019cumberland` para 187; `everett2011comojo` para 31; (c) *YL v Birmingham CC* on whether a private provider under contract is a public authority.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. Say the placing body's duty is a public-law duty. Mark the operator's care duty to a placed guest as a proposal. The part of *R (DMA)* that does cross into private law is its Equality Act limb (para 291), because the adjustment duty binds private service-providers too (s. 29(7)(a)).

### T15. The delegation table does not hold for Ireland, nor beyond building work (serious)
- **Step attacked.** Step 5 table, row 3, and status 'in the named jurisdictions' (ll. 150-152); 'Premises entered under contract' (l. 159).
- **Objection.** The Irish Act imposes a 'duty ... to ensure that ... the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them'. That duty is 'independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier' (s. 4(1)-(2)), and no notice can cut it (s. 9). The wording repeats the warranty in *MacLenan*, under which 'it matters not whether the lack of care or skill be that of the defendant ... or that of an independent contractor' (p. 333). The English entrustment rule in row 3 is therefore not shown to hold in Ireland. In England, the statutory defence covers only 'faulty execution of any work of construction, maintenance or repair' (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s. 2(4)(b)). It does not reach outsourced security, cleaning or software.
- **Footing.** (a) `ie1963hotel` ss. 4, 9; `maclenan1917segar` p. 333; `ola1957/s2.html` s. 2(4)(b).
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing to England. For Ireland, a new source is needed: Irish case law on s. 4 (lead).

### T16. The claimed legal gap is narrower than stated (serious)
- **Step attacked.** l. 239 ('No instrument read requires, for a consumer or a guest, a channel ... to a person with power'); l. 270.
- **Objection.** Two instruments already read come close to C. From 29 March 2029, Directive 2026/1024 inserts art. 16a(1), under which the organiser must be 'easily contactable' and 'able to respond efficiently to queries ... in relation to any lack of conformity occurring during the performance of the package and the related remedies'. That is a channel, available during performance, tied to correction, for package travellers who are guests. The DSA requires a point of contact that 'shall not solely rely on automated tools' (art. 12(1)). It also requires complaint decisions 'under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means', with a duty to 'reverse its decision ... without undue delay' (art. 20(4), (6)). Those provisions reach a guest whose platform account is suspended during a stay (art. 20(1)(b)-(c)).
- **Footing.** (a) `eu2015package/dir_2026_1024_amending_oj.txt`, art. 1(10); `eu2022dsa` arts 12(1), 20(1), (4), (6).
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing. The gap that remains is a natural person with power over the single case, for a guest of an accommodation provider outside packages and outside platform moderation.

### T17. The AI Act excludes the paradigm hotel system (minor)
- **Step attacked.** Step 6 'High-risk AI' (l. 196); sub-step 5 (l. 247).
- **Objection.** Annex III point 1(a) excludes 'AI systems intended to be used for biometric verification the sole purpose of which is to confirm that a specific natural person is the person he or she claims to be'. Recital 54 extends the exclusion to systems used 'for the sole purpose of having access to a service, unlocking a device or having secure access to premises', on the ground of their 'minor impact'. Check-in kiosks and room entry therefore fall outside arts 14 and 26. On this class of system the legislature declined to require an override.
- **Footing.** (a) `eu2024aiact/aiact.txt`, Annex III 1(a); recital 54.
- **What would answer it.** Concession. Say the hotel case is excluded, not merely 'neighbouring'.

### T18. The 1956 Act's s. 2(3)(c) does not penalise an unreachable host (minor)
- **Step attacked.** l. 198.
- **Objection.** The provision lifts the cap on goods liability where the guest could not deposit property 'through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his'. Its proviso contemplates a hotel 'where there is no reception office or desk'. The Act therefore accepts deskless hotels and only reallocates the risk to goods.
- **Footing.** (a) `hpa1956/whole_act.txt` s. 2(3).
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing to a rule about who bears the risk.

### T19. *Rasnick*'s reasons reach any judge-made channel duty (minor)
- **Step attacked.** Objections table, *Rasnick* row (l. 315).
- **Objection.** The majority's reasons were not confined to health. It asked 'What type of inquiry would or should cause the innkeeper to inquire ... and from whom?' It also deferred to the legislature: the General Assembly 'could have given innkeepers the duty to "investigate" or "check on" a guest, but it chose not to do so' (289 Ga. at 569). Distinguishing health from performance does not meet the argument from legislative choice, which applies equally to a channel duty.
- **Footing.** (a) `rasnick2011krishna` at 569-570.
- **What would answer it.** A new argument for why a court rather than a legislature should recognise C, or a concession that C is a proposal for legislatures.

### T20. Step 4 understates the public side of the duty to receive (minor)
- **Step attacked.** Step 4, l. 134 ('refusal was indictable').
- **Objection.** In Ireland, breach of the duty to receive is an offence now, 'without prejudice to his civil liability' (s. 12), so the duty is both directed and public in force today. Step 4 never asks the question Step 6 needs answered, which is whether the channel duty is owed to the guest or to the public. English package law answers 'to the public' (T2).
- **Footing.** (a) `ie1963hotel` s. 12; `uk2018package` reg. 35.
- **What would answer it.** Narrowing, plus a sentence in Step 4 on the channel's direction.

### T21. § 314A has been superseded (minor)
- **Step attacked.** Step 2 'Attending' (l. 66).
- **Objection.** *Groh* notes that § 314A 'was replaced by another Restatement provision' in 2012, and *Hoyt* applies Restatement (Third) § 40 (829 N.W.2d at 775-776).
- **Footing.** (a) `groh2015westin` (footnote at the Restatement discussion); `hoyt2013gutterz` at 775-776.
- **What would answer it.** Correction.
