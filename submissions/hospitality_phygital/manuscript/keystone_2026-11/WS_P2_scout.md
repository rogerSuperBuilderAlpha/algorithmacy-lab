# WS P2 scout report: role obligations, special obligations, duties of oversight (K2, K3, K7-P3)

Date of every search and fetch below: 11 Nov 2026. Depth words used throughout: **read** (the passage cited was read in a text fetched today or saved by an earlier run), **abstract** (publisher abstract, as carried by OpenAlex or by a Consensus record), **record** (bibliographic record only), **second-hand** (quoted with a page reference in a text I read). A Consensus record is a lead, including where it carries an abstract.

Three things the caller should know before the table.

1. **A published statement of P3 exists.** Goodin, 'Responsibilities' (*Philosophical Quarterly* 1986; reprinted as ch. 5 of *Utilitarianism as a Public Philosophy*, 1995), says what 'seeing to it that X' requires: satisfying oneself that a process is at work, checking it from time to time, and altering or replacing it when it no longer seems likely to deliver. Both printings are closed. I read the passage in a third-party scan of the 1995 book whose provenance is doubtful (see c.1, question 6), so the wording needs checking against the Cambridge edition before any card quotes it.
2. **The sharpest defeater for K3 is Hardimon's own definition**, which Jeske and Simmons both quote: a role obligation is one 'whose normative force flows from the role'. Dare (2019) and Horton (1992) hold the same line in other terms. K3 sides with Simmons.
3. **Three repositories refuse automated fetches** (PhilPapers/PhilArchive, the Open University's ORO, CORE; HTTP 403), SSRN shows a Cloudflare check, and Cambridge Core and Harvard DASH rate-limited me (HTTP 429). Several open copies are therefore listed below as 'open, not retrieved'. A person with a browser can fetch them without a login.

## (c) Candidates

### c.1 What the search found, by question

**1. Role obligations.**
- Hardimon's definition, as Jeske quotes it: a role obligation is 'a moral requirement, which attaches to an institutional role, whose content is fixed by the function of the role, and whose normative force flows from the role' (Hardimon 1994: 334, in Jeske, SEP 'Special Obligations', §4). For unchosen roles he requires that the role be 'reflectively acceptable' (Hardimon 1994: 348, in Jeske §4). **Second-hand.**
- Jeske's own reply narrows this: reflective acceptance is 'epiphenomenal' and leaves the 'justice view', on which 'one has obligations in virtue of occupying a role if and only if one's filling that role and the institution in which one would be filling that role meet certain conditions of morality or justice' (§4). **Read.**
- Simmons replies that a 'related version of this "standard view" of institutional obligations is "standard" for a very good reason - namely, that it is true' (abstract of the SSRN posting, as carried by Consensus). **Abstract.**
- **Who holds K3's view in print.** Dagger and Lefkowitz report the critics of associative obligation (Dagger 2018: 79–82; Wellman 2000: 553; Simmons 2001: 83–84; 2005: 111–115) as holding that 'the moral duty to perform the (conventional) duties that constitute the role of citizen ultimately depends either on an agent's exercise of will ... or a natural duty', so that justification 'bottoms out not in the fact of association but in a moral principle external to it' (SEP 'Political Obligation', §4.3). **Read.** Kong (2026: 4), already carded, says the role contributes 'a specification of the domain' while control grounds answerability. Miller's teleological account rests institutional duties on prior moral rights: 'a police officer's institutional duty to arrest an offender is based in part on his victim's moral right to protection' (SEP 'Social Institutions', §4). **Read.**
- **Who denies it.** Hardimon (above). Dare: 'I think roles are themselves the source of moral obligation' (abstract, Consensus record of 10.4324/9781351017350-3; **abstract**). Horton: 'the obligations which are constitutive of the relationship do not stand in need of moral justification in terms of a set of basic moral principles' (Horton 1992: 150–51, in Dagger and Lefkowitz §4.3; **second-hand**). Dworkin's associative obligations (Dworkin 1986: 196–206, in Jeske §4 and in Renzo and Green §5.3; **second-hand**). Sciaraffa grounds role duties in identification, with fair play (abstract; **abstract**).
- **The debate since.** Barber and Cordell (eds), *The Ethics of Social Roles* (OUP 2023), is the current collection; its overview chapter has an open postprint that I could not retrieve. Russell (2025) sorts positions into balancing, shielding and filtering models (abstract). Herzog and Schmode (2022) ground occupational duties in 'skills, roles, and the duty of social cooperation' against a purely contractual view (abstract). Valentini (2019) grounds the force of socially constructed norms in 'agency respect' (abstract; text saved, unread).

**2. The three grounds.**
- *(a) Voluntary assumption.* The canonical statements on the leads list, Hart 1955 and Simmons 1979, are closed, and that they are the canonical statements is itself from memory. Best open treatment: Jeske, SEP §4, which sets out the voluntarist thesis and its three strategies. Standard objection: voluntarism cannot cover the special obligations common sense recognises towards family and compatriots (Jeske §4); and Goodin argues that 'it is the beneficiary's vulnerability rather than any voluntary commitment as such on the part of the benefactor that generates these special responsibilities' (abstract, 10.2307/1956843).
- *(b) Assurance and invited reliance.* Canonical: Scanlon 1998, ch. 7 (Principle F at p. 304), and Thomson 1990, ch. 12 (both closed). Best open treatment: Habib, SEP 'Promises', §3 (assurance §3.1, reliance §3.2, trust §3.3), which reproduces Principle F. Standard objections, all in §3: circularity (Kolodny and Wallace 2003); collapse into warning and advising (Raz 1972; Owens 2006); cases where the expectation is missing (Scanlon's own 'Profligate Pal', 1998: 312). Jeske adds that the ground 'appeals to the psychological states of those to whom the obligations are owed' (§7).
- *(c) Vulnerability and dependence.* Canonical: Goodin 1985 (closed; article abstract open). Care-ethics counterparts: Kittay, *Love's Labor* (10.4324/9781315108926, closed); Collins, *The Core of Care Ethics* (10.1057/9781137011459, closed). I found **no open treatment of adequate depth**; the IEP 'Care Ethics' entry does not mention Goodin. Standard objection, in an open text: on Jeske's account of natural duties, proximity and knowledge 'facilitate my being able to fulfill my (natural) duty of mutual aid, but it is not a part of the fundamental explanation of why I have such a duty in the first place' (§3). Dependence, so read, yields a natural duty that falls on whoever is placed to help and does not single out a host as such.

**4. Role-responsibility.** Hart's definition, as Vacek quotes it: 'whenever a person occupies a distinctive place or office in a social organisation, to which specific duties are attached to provide for the welfare of others or to advance in some specific way the aims or purposes of the organisation, he is properly said to be responsible for the performance of these duties, or for doing what is necessary to fulfil them. Such duties are a person's responsibilities' (Hart 1968: 212, in Vacek 2025: 849). **Second-hand**; Mildenberger (2019: 94) gives an abridged version with pp. 212–13. The clause 'or for doing what is necessary to fulfil them' bears on P3. Cane (2016), 'Role responsibility', and Vincent (2011) are closed. Gardner's author eprint of 'Hart and Feinberg on responsibility' is open but mentions role-responsibility only in passing and carries a notice asking readers not to quote the eprint.

**5. Organizations as duty-bearers.** Moriarty (SEP 'Business Ethics', §2) maps the dispute: French (1979, 1984) for corporate moral agency; List and Pettit (2011) on corporate autonomy; against, Velasquez (1983, 2003), who holds that corporations 'cannot be held responsible for their actions, because those actions are brought about by the actions of their members', and Rönnegard (2015). **Read.** De Haan (2023) draws the line at organisation: 'Organized groups may qualify as duty-bearers, whereas purposive groups cannot qualify as duty-bearers' (abstract; text saved). Collins and Lawford-Smith (2016) argue for parity between collectives' and individuals' obligations (accepted version saved; skimmed). On members' derived duties the source is Collins (2017) and *Group Duties* (2019); both have open copies that refused automated fetch. Moen (2023), 'Against corporate responsibility', is a recent denial (hybrid open access; not fetched).

**6. K7's step P3.**
- **Goodin states it.** 'Seeing to it that X' requires, minimally: that A satisfy himself that there is some process (mechanism or activity) at work whereby X will be brought about; that A check, from time to time, to make sure that that process is still at work, and is performing as expected; and that A take steps as necessary to alter or replace processes that no longer seem likely to bring about X (Goodin 1995: 83; ch. 5, 'Responsibilities', 10.1017/cbo9780511625053.006, pp. 81–87; first published *Philosophical Quarterly* 36(142): 50–56, 10.2307/2219309). He calls these 'self-supervisory responsibilities', says they attach to duties as well as to responsibilities (pp. 83–84), and names responsibilities to see that others act 'supervisory responsibilities' (p. 82). Elsewhere in the book he distinguishes a duty 'to do X' from a duty 'to see to it that X is done' (p. 73) and gives a group a 'residual supervisory function' comprising 'regular monitoring' and readiness to organise a replacement scheme (p. 34). **Depth and caveat:** I read these pages in a 372-page scan of the whole book at utilitarianism.com/utilitarian.pdf. Nothing on the file shows the author's or publisher's permission, its text layer is noisy OCR, and I transcribed the passage by correcting obvious OCR errors, so the wording above is not yet an exact quotation. I did not save the scan to the project. Both authorised printings are closed.
- **Brandt states the retained duty.** 'When a responsibility is delegated, the individual initially bound by the responsibility still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled' (accepted ms p. 3); 'the delegator still retains the obligation to ensure that the task gets completed, and/or to make up for whatever negative consequences arise due to the delegatee's failure to fulfil her responsibilities, even in the absence of blame' (ms p. 4). **Read** (ms pp. 2–5). Brandt gives the correction half of P3 and not the learning half: his parent must act 'if it becomes clear' that the delegate is failing (ms p. 5).
- **Soft law states the conclusion C for another domain.** UN Guiding Principle 29: 'To make it possible for grievances to be addressed early and remediated directly, business enterprises should establish or participate in effective operational-level grievance mechanisms for individuals and communities who may be adversely impacted.' The Commentary says such mechanisms provide 'a channel for those directly impacted by the enterprise's operations to raise concerns when they believe they are being or will be adversely impacted' and allow impacts 'to be remediated early and directly by the business enterprise, thereby preventing harms from compounding' (OHCHR 2011: 32–33). Principle 20 requires enterprises to 'track the effectiveness of their response', drawing on 'feedback from both internal and external sources, including affected stakeholders' (p. 23). **Read.** This is a published concept that names much of K7's conclusion: the 'operational-level grievance mechanism'.
- **Corporate law states the learning half.** The Delaware Supreme Court: 'directors must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it' (*Marchand v. Barnhill*, No. 533, 2018, slip op. at 30), quoting *Caremark*, 698 A.2d at 970, on a reporting system 'adequate to assure the board that appropriate information will come to its attention in a timely manner' (slip op. at 29 n. 100). **Read** in the court's own PDF. *Caremark* itself is quoted there and was not fetched. For P4b.
- **Professional norms.** The American Nurses Association: 'The delegating RN retains accountability for the patient outcomes associated with nurse delegation, provided the person to whom the task was delegated performed it as instructed' (ANA 2012: 4); the delegation steps include 'Monitor implementation of the delegated task' (p. 9). **Read.**
- **Abstract only.** Thompson (2014) proposes that 'specific individuals can be designated as overseers, who are held responsible for monitoring the structure and making changes as necessary' (abstract, 10.1177/0275074014524013; an open copy sits in Harvard DASH, which rate-limited me).
- **Its denial.** See (d), D9–D11.

## (d) Defeaters

### Against K3 (the role specifies; three external grounds bind)

- **D1. Hardimon builds the binding force into the role.** His definition has the 'normative force' of a role obligation flow 'from the role' (1994: 334, in Jeske §4). If that is right, K3 concedes more than it needs to. Two things limit the damage, both to be tested on the full text: the host's role is one a firm enters by choice, the kind Hardimon calls contractual; and on Simmons's abstract Hardimon's complaint about the standard view of contractual roles is that it 'leaves out' a factor, which suggests that he adds to 'signing on' and does not replace it.
- **D2. Dare denies that roles are derivative.** He describes K3's picture as the habit of analytic ethics ('depending upon more fundamental moral theories which are the real source of role-obligation') and rejects it (abstract).
- **D3. The constitutive view.** Horton, Dworkin and McPherson hold that obligations constitutive of a relationship need no external justification (Dagger and Lefkowitz §4.3; Renzo and Green §5.3). The associativists' reply to K3's side is on record too: critics 'conflate considerations that disable associative obligations with considerations that ground or justify' them (Horton and Windeknecht 2015: 914; Scheffler 2018: 11, in Dagger and Lefkowitz §4.3). A K3 that treats voluntary assumption as a ground must answer that it may be only an enabling condition.
- **D4. Goodin collapses the grounds.** He argues that vulnerability, and not voluntary commitment, generates the special responsibilities usually put down to self-assumed obligation (abstract, 10.2307/1956843). On his view K3's three grounds are one, and ground 1 does no independent work. The paper's table of ground by hard case presupposes that the grounds can come apart.
- **D5. If external grounds bind, the role may be idle.** Jeske presses the question whether any proposed special obligation 'is more plausibly understood as derivative from an agent-neutral reason or a natural duty and so not really special in a fundamental sense' (§6, closing paragraph), and warns that role talk may record 'merely others' expectations of persons holding such positions' (§4). K3 must show what the role adds; 'specification of content, trigger and holder' is the answer on offer, and Kong (2026: 4) is the one source found that says so in terms.
- **D6. Ground 2 needs actual expectation, and may be circular.** Habib §3 sets out the circularity objection and the Profligate Pal; Thomson's reliance view requires that the other party in fact rely (Habib §3.2). This matches the weakness CLAIMS_FIXED already records from *Crinkley*.
- **D7. Ground 3 does not pick out hosts.** On Jeske's account proximity and knowledge 'facilitate' a natural duty and are not its ground (§3). Dependence then yields a duty on whoever is placed to help: a platform, a neighbour, another guest.
- **D8. A ground K3 omits.** Fair play: obligations from accepting the benefits of a cooperative scheme (Dagger and Lefkowitz §4.2; Renzo and Green §4.2; Sciaraffa pairs it with identification). A reviewer may ask why three grounds and not four.

### Against K7's P3 (oversight includes the means to learn and to correct in time)

- **D9. Discharge by competent choice.** Brandt reports the position he rejects. He quotes O'Neill on parents who place children with others: 'Provided that they take reasonable steps to ensure that their children will be adequately reared, they do not breach but transfer their parental obligations or some part of those obligations' (O'Neill, in Brandt ms p. 5, with Brandt's emphasis removed; **second-hand**, and the O'Neill text was not traced). He states the general form as the view that transfer is unproblematic 'so long as the transferer takes due care when selecting others' (ms p. 5) and that gamete providers 'can completely discharge their responsibilities by finding suitable parents' (ms p. 4). English occupiers' law is the legal instance CLAIMS_FIXED names. **The statute is less of a defeater than K7 assumes.** Section 2(4)(b) relieves the occupier only if he acted reasonably in entrusting the work 'and had taken such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought in order to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work had been properly done' (`sources/ola1957/s2.html`). The existing card `uk1957occupiers` quotes the subsection only as far as 'independent contractor' and omits this clause. For P4b and the card's owner.
- **D10. Brandt himself stops short of P3.** His delegator is not to blame where she 'had good reason to think the delegatee would complete the assigned task' (ms p. 3), and her duty to step in arises 'if it becomes clear' that the delegate is failing (ms p. 5). He asserts no duty to arrange to find out.
- **D11. Goodin's principle is general and untimed.** It says 'from time to time' and attaches to any responsibility; it says nothing of a channel from the person owed the duty, and nothing of correcting 'while performance is still possible'. P3 as worded in CLAIMS_FIXED is stronger than Goodin on both points. UNGP 29 supplies 'early' and 'directly' but as soft law for human-rights impacts.
- **D12. Command responsibility cuts both ways** (search summaries only): some tribunals limited a commander's duty to know to cases where he already held information, and one recent article argues for an actual-knowledge standard. Unread; low priority.
- **D13. Descriptive evidence that delegation shifts blame** (Bartling and Fischbacher 2012; Hill 2015; records only). It shows what people do, and a reviewer may cite it against the claim that responsibility is commonly understood to stay.

### Against K2 (the duty attaches to the role, and so can bind a firm)

- **D14. Firms as duty-bearers are contested.** Velasquez, Rönnegard, Sepinwall and Moen deny corporate moral responsibility; Hasnas says little hangs on it (Moriarty §2).
- **D15. An unorganised set of firms is not a duty-bearer.** De Haan's cut-off admits organised groups and excludes purposive groups without decision procedures (abstract). A brand, an operator and a platform with no joint procedure cannot hold the host's duty as a group; K8 must place it in one firm or in each.
- **D16. Roles are derivative of control** (Kong 2026: 4, per the existing card). This supports K3's structure and denies K2 any reading on which the role is the ground.
- **D17. Role obligations as 'mere expectations'.** Jeske's commandant example (§4) shows that a role's internal duties are not thereby moral duties. K2's 'the duty attaches to the role' is safe only as a claim about content and holder.
