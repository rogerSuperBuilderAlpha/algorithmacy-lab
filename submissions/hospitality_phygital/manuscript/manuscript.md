## 1. Introduction

Research on hospitality's hybrid physical-digital encounters has expanded rapidly over the past
decade. The phygital customer experience framework treats the blending of physical and digital
elements as a design problem with tangible and intangible dimensions (Batat 2024). Phygital service
research covers more parties and more outcomes, placing guests, hosts, employees and communities at
the centre of experience design and directing the field towards positive impact at individual,
community and environmental levels (Batat 2026). A review of 105 studies published between 2017 and
2025 finds phygital experience at the core of this literature and characterizes the field as
consumer-centred and technology-enabled (Brochado et al. 2026). Alongside the frameworks sits a body
of work on how well the hybrid encounter can be built and how readily guests will accept it.
Automation research in tourism organizes around adoption, acceptance and the redesign of delivery
(Tussyadiah 2020). Service robots and digital service technologies appear as strategic pathways to
cost-effective service excellence (Wirtz et al. 2023), and biometric check-in is modelled as a
calculation in which the guest weighs privacy risk against benefit and decides whether to proceed
(Xu et al. 2020; Boo and Chua 2022).

Service research, for its part, no longer models the encounter as a dyad. Technology, employee and
customer have been set out as three roles (Larivière et al. 2017), and the service triad of robot,
customer and frontline employee has been tested empirically (Odekerken-Schröder et al. 2021).
Hospitality's own review of the field names an encounter triad of customer, employee and AI (Li et
al. 2021), and co-creation has been recast as a hybrid configuration of the three (Gursoy 2026).
Hospitality theory holds settled ground of its own. Hospitality businesses are persistently
misdescribed as service operations and better understood through the host-guest relationship,
generosity and the theatricality of the encounter (Hemmington 2007), and hospitality itself names a
relational and culturally situated practice of welcome, recognition, care, presence and negotiated
access, not the efficient provision of services (Lashley 2000; Lynch et al. 2011, 2021). That the
guest now meets a third party, and that hospitality exceeds provision, are both settled.

What the third party is called has held remarkably steady. It appears as an infused tool (Bitner et
al. 2000), an automated social presence (van Doorn et al. 2017), a mediator or facilitator (Li et
al. 2021), a co-creator (Xing and Zhang 2026), an algorithmic actor bearing distributed agency
(Gursoy 2026), and, in the field's own review, as technology-enabled (Brochado et al. 2026). Each of
these terms describes a capacity of the technology, and none of them assigns it an obligation. The
nearest approach within that literature asks whether
customers blame a robot after a service failure, and finds that they blame the firm (Belanche et al.
2020), which shows that responsibility travels without showing that anything is owed. Hospitality
distributes its duties unevenly among the parties present: a host owes a welcome, and no employment
relation creates or discharges that duty. When service research placed technology in the third
position, it filled the seat marked employee and left the seat marked host empty.

The vacancy matters because hospitality confers standing on the guest: she may ask, decline, explain
herself and be received as an exception. Ordinary welcome runs on self-interest as much as on ethics
(Lynch 2017), so the standing amounts to negotiating room whichever register produces it, and the
field's own measures locate its trace
in the guest's sense of being acknowledged (Pijls et al. 2017). Host authority, moreover, runs
past the threshold and is exercised continuously inside the space (Bulley 2015). Where an
algorithmic intermediary performs the recognizing, the routing, the pricing and the settling of what
may be asked, that standing loses its counterparty: the guest keeps an addressee for complaint and
compensation, and loses the party with authority over the rules. She can be admitted and still have
nobody to negotiate the terms with. The employee faces the same movement: a hotel room-assignment system has been
observed fixing the sequence of work, withholding the day's task list and offering a rejection
affordance that workers feared to use (Spektor et al. 2023b), and employees in phygital service
settings resist when the purpose of the technology is obscured (De Vos et al. 2026). The prevailing
design value compounds the exposure, because a journey made perfectly fluid (Batat 2024) removes the
occasions on which guest or employee might have said anything at all, and the field's most recent
research agenda criticizes opacity and rigidity while leaving smoothness unexamined (Shabnam et al.
2026).

How does algorithmic mediation reconfigure relationships among guests, hospitality employees and
organizations, and under what conditions does it enhance rather than diminish the human and
relational qualities of hospitality? We take the triadic structure as given and press one question
inside it: is the third party a host? The paper distinguishes augmentative hospitality, in which
technology supports human recognition, discretion and care, from substitutive hospitality, in which
algorithmic direction displaces relational judgment, and it states the difference as a condition
checkable against a real touchpoint: an encounter runs substitutive where the guest cannot discover
what was decided, where the rule moves without announcement, and where nothing answers to her.
Hospitality algorithmacy names the situated capacity of guests and employees to read, engage and
when necessary circumvent the mediation; coordinative sovereignty names the practical ability to
retain influence over how hospitality is organized. The two stay separate because the first is a
competence a person can develop while the second is a standing only institutions confer. Five
affordances of guest-centric design follow from what the mediation withholds: transparency,
adjustability, contestability, human accessibility and bypass-ability.

The paper repositions AI from a neutral service technology to a constitutive mediator of hospitality
relationships, meanings and power. Phygital scholarship has registered that this transformation
redistributes agency, as in the museum, where curatorial authority disperses across visitors,
communities and technology (Mosca et al. 2025). What redistribution leaves unsettled is the
obligation that travels with it, and hospitality, unlike museum visitorship, carries a duty of
welcome that must land on somebody. For organizations the framework yields principles that preserve
human contact, explain consequential decisions, enable employee intervention and provide meaningful
alternatives to digitally prescribed encounters, together with a division practitioners currently
lack: some affordances can be bought with design and training, and contestability cannot, because
somebody must constitute a forum. The argument is conceptual, and every affordance-to-outcome
relationship it proposes stands as a proposition for hospitality research to test.

## 2. Hospitality beyond seamless service

Hospitality exceeds commercial service provision. Lashley's (2000) three domains locate hospitality
simultaneously in commercial exchange, in private domesticity and in the social obligations
regulating how strangers are received, with the commercial domain inheriting its meanings from the
other two rather than standing apart from them. Hemmington (2007) argues that hospitality businesses
have been persistently misdescribed as service operations, and that they are better understood
through the host–guest relationship, generosity and the theatricality of the encounter. Lynch et al.
(2011), opening this journal, located hospitality simultaneously within social control, within
social and economic exchange and within metaphor, and not one of those three registers is service
quality. Reflecting upon the decade which followed, the same authors characterize hospitality as a
lens upon power, identity, belonging and exclusion quite as much as upon provision, and in the
accompanying reprise they renew a call for critical hospitality scholarship that treats the concept
as a way of examining social relations rather than an industry sector (Lynch et al. 2021a, 2021b).

Five criteria recur across this work. Welcome marks the transition by which a stranger becomes a
guest. Recognition registers that a particular person has been received, and not a category
processed. Care attends to needs the guest has not articulated. Presence holds host and guest in the
same encounter long enough for the others to occur, and the field has treated it as embodied and
interpersonal from the start. Negotiated access governs the terms upon which entry is granted and
what may be asked for once inside. Derrida's distinction between an unconditional hospitality and
the conditional forms actually practised gives the last of these its critical edge, since every real
hospitality sets conditions and the interesting question is always who sets them and whether they
remain open to discussion (Derrida and Dufourmantelle 2000). That question has proved remarkably
generative across the journal's first decade, informing debates about migration, welcome and the
ethics of the threshold, and we take it up here in a setting where the conditions have become
computational. Bulley (2015) reads hospitality as a spatial relation whose practice continues once
the guest has crossed and is exercised through tactics that seek to contain a guest who resists. The
mediation we examine does most of its work after admission.

Hospitality resists reduction to outcomes. Lugosi (2008) describes hospitable moments as emergent
and partly unscripted, arising within the interaction rather than being delivered by it, which
implies that a guest whose stay proceeded without incident has not thereby been received.
Hospitality has always specified something flowing back to the guest. Welcome, recognition, care and
negotiated access are not enhancements appended to a transaction; they are constitutive of the
practice, and an arrangement supplying none of them is doing something other than hosting.

What flows back to the guest is easy to overstate. Lynch (2017), in the most sustained recent study
of ordinary welcome, reports something a good deal more equivocal than an ethics: welcome as life
politics, a tactical accomplishment which people deploy in order to negotiate their world day by
day, and he closes rather starkly by declaring himself pessimistic about hospiety given the
self-interest informing what he observed. He does not resolve welcome into tactics; the ethical and
the tactical run together in his account, and what survives their tension is a position. The
standing hospitality confers is negotiated in practice rather than guaranteed by the guest's moral
status: she may act back, may ask, decline, explain herself and be received as an exception. Pijls
et al. (2017) measure the feeling rather than the position, resolving the experience of hospitality
into invitation, care and comfort; two of the three are constituted by the guest's sense of having
been acknowledged, which is the trace the position leaves in experience rather than the position
itself. Service research holds a rival account in which the exception belongs to the employee, a
compliance decision granted or refused against policy (Beatty et al. 2016), and the two are
compatible: the employee decides, and hospitality is what obliges somebody to decide rather than to
refuse by default. Kekstaite (2022) argues in
these pages that reciprocity is an achievement internal to hospitality rather than a background
condition of it, and what is achieved can also be designed away. The commercial literature has
theorized the host's hospitableness and the guest's satisfaction at considerable length while
leaving the guest's own obligations largely untouched, and we could locate no instrument
measuring anything flowing from guest to host. The field's measurement apparatus has never treated
the relation as two-directional.

The field has already theorized hospitality in more than two parts, and our triad is not that triad.
Munasinghe et al. (2022) propose a triadic conceptualisation of the host–guest encounter in which
the wider social and cultural context forms a third element shaping what host and guest are able to
do. Their third element is a context that conditions an encounter between two parties. The third
element we examine is a party in its own right: it acts, it decides, and it commits determinations
binding upon the other two.

Platform accommodation has already unsettled the question of who occupies the host position. Cheng
and Foley (2019) describe short-term rental hosting as algorithmically managed work, directed
through pricing and ranking systems by a platform conferring no employment relation upon the person
doing the hosting. Roelofsen and Minca (2018) show, in a rich reading of the Superhost badge, how a
platform's definition of good hosting comes to discipline the body performing it, while Liang et al.
(2017) demonstrate why hosts pursue that badge so assiduously, since it gates search visibility and
bookings. The pattern replicates outside the urban markets where most evidence has been gathered:
Leick et al. (2024) find rural hosts professionalizing and improving their performance without
acquiring any authority over the rules that govern them, which is also the trajectory Bosma (2022)
traces through the accumulation of labour and assets into a livelihood. Farmaki and Kaniadakis
(2020) draw the general lesson, examining how interdependencies among hosts, guests and the platform
reconstitute the power relations of the exchange and reshape what hosting practically consists of.
Hospitality scholarship registered a third party in this relation before information systems arrived
to announce one.

Guests meet the same apparatus from the other side. Edelman et al. (2017) found that applicants with
distinctively African-American names were substantially less likely to be accepted, and located the
harm in a design decision of the intermediary, beyond the conduct of any individual host. Cui et al.
(2020) then found that a single positive review closed the gap: once a guest account carried a
review, acceptance rates for guests with white-sounding and African-American-sounding names became
statistically indistinguishable. Read against Derrida, the finding is arresting: negotiated access,
the criterion by which hospitality scholarship judges whether a welcome is a welcome at all, is now
administered by an artefact. Whether a guest is received turns upon a record she did not author,
cannot inspect and cannot carry elsewhere.

## 3. Phygital hospitality as triadic mediation

We theorize phygital hospitality as a system of triadic coordination among three parties: the guest,
who seeks to be received; the algorithmic intermediary, comprising the systems that classify,
recommend, gate, personalize, message, price and queue; and the human or organizational host,
comprising frontline employees, managers and the organization which remains accountable for the
welcome. The three-party structure is not our discovery. Larivière et al. (2017) set out the roles
of technology, employees and customers in what they called Service Encounter 2.0; Odekerken-Schröder
et al. (2021) study the service triad of robot, customer and frontline employee empirically; Li et
al. (2021) review AI-based service encounters in this field under the name of the encounter triad;
van Doorn et al. (2023) propose the consumer–autonomous technology–worker framework and illustrate
it in hospitality settings; and Gursoy (2026) has lately reconceptualized co-creation as a hybrid
triadic configuration of customers, employees and AI systems, examining how the technology
redistributes agency and transforms interpretive labour. The structure is established. Our question
begins where that literature leaves off.

Does the third party occupy the position of host? Not in the literature's own vocabulary, where the
technology figures as an infused tool (Bitner et al. 2000), an automated social presence (van Doorn
et al. 2017), a mediator or facilitator (Li et al. 2021), a co-creator (Xing and Zhang 2026), an
algorithmic actor bearing distributed agency (Gursoy 2026). The newest hospitality-specific name
declines the position by construction, since robotic hospitableness casts the robot as a co-creator
of felt welcome rather than as its host (Liu et al. 2026). Belanche et al. (2020) come nearest,
asking whether customers attribute responsibility to a robot after a service failure, but an
attribution of blame made by a customer is not an obligation held by a host. The distinction matters
because hospitality has never distributed its duties evenly among the parties present. A host owes a
welcome. That duty is not generated by an employment relation and cannot be discharged by one. The
service-triad literature compares machine and employee constantly; what it has not examined is
whether the employee was ever the right template, because the host does not appear in its
repertoire. A party now performs recognition, routing
and admission. Hospitality would ordinarily oblige such a party. What obliges this one?

Machine ethics has begun asking questions of this type, and the paper stands on that work rather
than beside it. Care robots have been argued to hold role responsibility, a duty grounded in the
social function they occupy rather than in any consciousness they lack (Kropf et al. 2026), and the
responsibility-gap literature has catalogued which obligations fail to travel when agency is
redistributed to machines (Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021). Neither reaches the host. Care is owed
to a patient in virtue of need, and fiduciary duty to a principal in virtue of trust; welcome is
owed to a stranger in virtue of arrival, and of the three it is the only duty that constitutes the
role that carries it. Philosophy of technology, where it has taken up hospitality at all, has run
the relation the other way, asking what welcome we owe our machines (Introna 2010). The machine has
been theorized as the guest at the threshold and never as the one who keeps it.

The frameworks which give the phygital field its vocabulary are not silent about mediation. Batat
(2024) organizes phygital customer experience around driving forces, connectors and pillars, and her
later statement of phygital service research names service experience as ethically and
technologically mediated while placing guests, employees and communities at the centre of design
(Batat 2026). Shabnam et al. (2026) press further within that programme, observing that existing
frameworks do not explain how algorithmic systems redirect, constrain or reconfigure agency. We
agree, and locate the difficulty in the movement itself. Phygitalization is a dual process in which
physical encounters are digitalized and digital ones physicalized, and a guest crosses between the
two repeatedly within a single stay: she books in one realm, is recognized in another, and meets at
the desk a determination made in the first. What these frameworks describe as fluidity we would
describe as a relay. Who holds authority at each handover?

A third element has not simply appeared. Interfaces have mediated hospitality transactions for
decades without displacing anyone. The claim is that the intermediary now performs hosting
functions. It interprets what a guest needs from data she did not knowingly supply. It directs the
encounter by determining what appears and in which order. It regulates access through
identification, verification and eligibility rules. It anticipates preferences, and it allocates
organizational attention, deciding which requests will reach a person and which will be resolved
without one. Three redistributions follow. Knowledge moves towards the intermediary and away from
both other parties. Rahman's (2021) study of an opaque evaluation system shows a platform steering
conduct precisely because it could not issue instructions, holding its criteria closely while the
evaluated party laboured to satisfy them. Calo and Rosenblat (2017) argue that the asymmetry so
produced is manufactured and held rather than incidental, and that it constitutes the arrangement's
central resource.

Discretion moves away from the frontline employee. Jianu et al. (2025) examine algorithmic
management inside hotels and find frontline managers negotiating continually between system-generated
direction and the judgement their role still demands, with process standardization
intensifying work demands and obliging managers to translate algorithmic decisions to the very
people those decisions affect. Reviewing automation across housekeeping, front desk and kitchen
work, Spektor et al. (2023a) observe that harms concentrate wherever adoption has attended to
management stakeholders alone. The mechanism is visible in adjacent expert occupations, where
Christin (2017) shows algorithmic instruments arriving as would-be substitutes for professional
judgement and relocating the categories of judgement from the practitioner into a vendor's model.
What is relocated is precisely what Lipsky (1980) identified as the substance of frontline work,
namely the authority to decide what should be done in a particular case, and Möhlmann et al. (2021)
describe platforms deploying matching and control as twin dimensions of the same algorithmic
management.

The evidence is thick in one place and thin in the other. Spektor et al. (2023b, 2025) observed a
hotel room-assignment system which fixed the sequence of work, withheld the day's full task list
under common configurations, priced each room in contractually specified credits, and offered a
rejection affordance workers reported they could not use without fearing dismissal. That is
relocation of judgement, documented in a hotel, and it is documented in the back of house. At the
front desk the picture differs: Bendoly (2013) found revenue-management prescriptions operating as
guidelines rather than mandates, with agents holding sanctioned latitude to deviate and the system
working upon their judgement through the framing of the performance measure rather than by
displacing it. The latitude endures: revenue managers' deviations are persistent enough that the
recommendations themselves are now written in anticipation of them (Garcia et al. 2026).
Hospitality research has studied the guest's experience of technology closely and the
frontline worker's hardly at all, so the half of algorithmic hospitality performed in front of the
guest remains theorized and, but for scattered and mostly informal observation, unstudied. Standardization does not simply delete discretion. Pedersen
and Pors (2022) show that technologies introduced to standardize frontline decisions bring with them
rough categories and scripts for action which generate new discretionary work, as practitioners
compensate for whatever those categories fail to capture. Displacement is consequently the wrong
word for what happens to judgement in a hotel. Judgement is pushed into unrecognized compensating
work, performed by people whose formal role no longer includes it and whose employers may have
little idea it is being done at all.

Authority, finally, moves towards whoever writes the rules and away from the parties those rules
govern. Scott and Orlikowski (2012) trace this movement within the travel sector itself, showing how
online rating systems materialized a new relation of accountability in which establishments
reoriented their conduct towards a ranking while older channels of answerability thinned. Reputation
compounds the shift because it does not travel: a host's standing inside one platform correlates
only weakly with the same property's standing elsewhere, so that leaving becomes expensive by design
(Zervas et al. 2021; Teubner et al. 2019). Taken together, these three movements describe an
arrangement in which the party best placed to know, to decide and to revise the rules is also the
party least available to answer for any of it. Mediation of this kind can expand responsiveness and
inclusion, and frequently does. It can equally produce depersonalization, surveillance, categorical
exclusion and dependence upon systems no participant is able to inspect. Riordan (2024) has already
given this journal a name for the second pole, describing algorithmic hostility among platform
food-delivery workers and proposing a virtual domain alongside Lashley's three. Both outcomes are real,
and a framework issuing a verdict upon the technology will not help anyone distinguish between them.

The stakes are easiest to see in a craft the industry already possesses. A practised hospitality
team manufactures the guest's sense of control: it defers visibly while deciding invisibly, lets
the late checkout feel like her idea, and quietly heads off the choices that would spoil her own
stay. Constructing that experience is skilled work; friendliness alone does not produce it, and the
performance depends on the team holding the very decisions it appears to surrender. Algorithmic
direction disturbs the craft at exactly that hinge. When the system settles what may be offered,
the employee can still perform deference, but she no longer holds what the deference used to
conceal, and the guest's sense of control, formerly produced by people who had control to give,
must now be produced by an interface that does not. Management by customers has been a documented
control form since long before the platforms arrived to instrument it (Fuller and Smith 1991); what
is new is the instrument, and where it sits. The industry has managed demanding guests for a
century. It has not before had to manage a gap between felt control and actual control opening
inside its own front desk.

## 4. Augmentative and substitutive hospitality

We therefore distinguish augmentative hospitality, in which technology supports human recognition,
discretion, responsiveness and care, from substitutive hospitality, in which algorithmic direction
displaces relational judgement and restricts the capacity of guests and employees to shape the
encounter. Stated in that form the distinction is intuitive and unusable. It becomes usable once
tied to what coordination actually requires. Reviewing coordination research across organizational
settings, Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) identify three integrating conditions any coordinating
mechanism must produce: accountability, which establishes who answers for what; predictability,
which allows participants to anticipate what will happen; and common understanding, which gives
participants a shared picture of the situation and of one another. One translation is needed and
should be made openly: their accountability is the coordination-theoretic kind, a clarity about who
is responsible for which piece of work among colleagues, and restoring it in a guest's relation to
a firm requires the stronger sense of answerability to a forum (Bovens 2007), because the guest is
not a member of the organization whose tasks the mechanism assigns. Settled forms of coordination
supply these conditions in their own currencies, so that a hierarchy delivers accountability
through a chain of command and predictability through rules, while a market delivers predictability
through price.

Algorithmic mediation is capable of withholding all three at once. Common understanding is withheld
in both directions, since the guest can neither ask the system what it concluded about her nor make
a need legible except through the fields the system will accept. Predictability is withheld and
inverted, because the apparatus forecasts the guest with considerable precision while revising its
own rules without announcement. Accountability is withheld wherever no forum owes the guest or the
employee an answer for a decision that matters. Substitutive hospitality, on our account, is
mediation under which all three withholdings hold together; augmentative hospitality is mediation
under which they do not. A system may be opaque and still augmentative where its rules are stable
and an employee is able to overrule it, and a system may be perfectly transparent and still
substitutive where nothing whatever answers to the party it affects. Because the condition applies
to a touchpoint rather than to an organization or a technology, it can be put to work directly, and
Table 1 sets out the questions we would ask of any particular one.

**Table 1. Diagnostic questions for a phygital touchpoint.**

| Question | Augmentative | Substitutive |
|---|---|---|
| What does the algorithm decide? | A bounded decision, known to staff | An open-ended allocation nobody in the property can enumerate |
| Who sees the decision at the point of effect? | Guest and employee, in ordinary language | Neither, or only afterwards |
| Who may change or override it? | An employee with authority and time | Nobody present; the rule sits with a vendor or head office |
| What answers a guest who disputes it? | A person or process able to reverse it | A restatement of the outcome |
| What happens to a guest who refuses the digital path? | An alternative of comparable quality | Delay, surcharge, or treatment as an exception |

Three ordinary touchpoints illustrate the poles; none reports a case. Consider room assignment. A
property that assigns rooms through an optimization the front desk cannot see, and that grants the
desk no authority to move a guest, has made assignment substitutive whatever the quality of its
rooms. The same optimization becomes augmentative where the desk is able to see why a room was
chosen and may override that choice for a guest whose reasons fall outside the model. Consider
service recovery. Automated triage routing a complaint to a person who holds discretion augments the
encounter, whereas triage that exhausts the guest through successive automated stages before any
employee appears substitutes for it. Guests are broadly positive towards service robots and continue
to prefer human staff, particularly where emotional dimensions and service failure are involved (Kim
et al. 2021), and Hou et al. (2021) usefully qualify this by showing the preference to be
conditional upon the situation, shifting with social crowding rather than expressing a fixed
attachment to human contact. The design question is where a person must remain reachable.

Consider, finally, recognition at the threshold. Identification confirming a booking augments
arrival; identification that also scores the guest, and that determines what she may request without
disclosing that it has done so, substitutes a classification for a welcome. In each case the pole is
settled by the same three withholdings, and in none of them by the sophistication of the technology.
The distinction operates upon touchpoints, not upon whole properties, and the mixing is inherited
rather than discovered: management research holds that automation and augmentation cannot be
cleanly separated at the level of task allocation, being interdependent across time and levels in
any real organization (Raisch and Krakowski 2021). That conclusion argues for the relocation made
here. Human-factors research has long graded systems along levels of what automation withholds from
its operator (Parasuraman et al. 2000), but a single ordinal ladder built around the operator
inside the loop carries no dimension for rules that move silently and none for answerability, and
the guest stands outside the loop altogether. Where the designer-side distinction dissolves under
interdependence, the place to draw it is the encounter, where what this guest could discover,
anticipate and contest are answerable questions whatever the tangle beneath.

## 5. Hospitality algorithmacy and coordinative sovereignty

Guests and employees are not passive under mediation of this kind. They form working theories of the
systems that sort them and they act upon those theories, and we propose the term hospitality
algorithmacy for this capacity: the situated capacity of guests and employees to recognize,
interpret and strategically engage with algorithmic mediation in hospitality encounters. The term
specializes a broader notion of algorithmacy, the competency for coordinating with a human
counterpart through an algorithmic intermediary; the hospitality encounter is that structure met at
the threshold, and the components named here are the competency's cognitive and practical face. The
capacity is documented in a hospitality setting. Studying accommodation hosts, Jhaver et al. (2018)
found them reverse-engineering search ranking, constructing folk theories of the system, comparing
notes socially and orienting themselves towards two audiences at once, the guest and the algorithm.
The general form appears wherever people are sorted by systems they cannot see. Eslami et al. (2016)
show users constructing folk theories of opaque curation and reorganizing their conduct around them
even when nothing economic is at stake, Bishop (2019) traces how such theories circulate until they
become a semi-institutionalized practice, and Cotter (2019) describes professionals treating
visibility as a game whose rules they infer and contest, structured by the algorithm without being
determined by it. Ytre-Arne and Moe (2021) find the response general across a representative sample,
which suggests that it belongs to the form of mediation rather than to precarity, DeVito et al.
(2017) show it sharpening whenever systems change without notice, and DeVito (2021) has since made
adaptation to changing platforms the core of algorithmic literacy itself. The components named
below therefore claim no novelty of content; each answers a specific withholding of the hospitality
mediation, and together they stop where private effort stops.

Algorithmacy specifies a documented competence, and it is distinct from digital literacy. Literacy
reads the interface; algorithmacy reads how the mediation redistributes who may act. We take it to
have three components, each answering to something the mediation withholds. Interpretation
reconstructs what the system concluded from outcomes alone. Specification compresses a real need
into the signals the system will carry, which requires knowing what can afford to be lost in the
compression. Tracking notices that a rule has moved when nobody has announced its moving. On the
floor the competence is exercised for the guest as often as by her: the agent who rephrases a wish
into the fields the system will accept is lending the guest her own specification. Employees
display the same competence and encounter the same limit. Christ-Brendemühl and Schaarschmidt (2019)
find frontline staff deviating from digital processes in both constructive and destructive
directions when technology-induced role ambiguity leaves them without a workable script, and
competence of that kind can certainly rescue an encounter. It does not, however, alter who decides.
Cheng et al. (2025) offer the sharper finding: awareness of artificial intelligence in the workplace
increases frontline employees' silence, working through a perceived breach of the psychological
contract. The employees who understand the system best may be precisely those who stop speaking
about it.

That finding registers an increment upon a baseline, because hospitality's voice conditions are
impaired before any algorithm is introduced. Frontline silence in hotels follows from supervisory
abuse and from undermining by supervisors, coworkers and guests alike (Jung and Yoon 2019;
Al-Hawari et al. 2020), and in the United Kingdom's hotel sector the combination of fragmented
workplaces, insecure status and individualized employment rights has been found to make silence the
dominant response to grievance (Papadopoulos et al. 2021). Where dissatisfaction arises, hospitality
workers reach for exit ahead of collective voice, since job dissatisfaction predicts intention to
quit while failing to predict any intention to organize (Zientara et al. 2023). Algorithmic
mediation therefore compounds a suppression the sector's labour conditions have already produced,
an exacerbation the tourism literature has begun to anticipate (Rydzik and Kissoon 2021). The
compounding is an inference from converging evidence: no study has yet modelled prior voice climate
as a condition upon which algorithmic management acts, and that interaction is one of the clearest
empirical openings this framework leaves.

The list of components stops at three because the fourth condition is different in kind.
Interpretation, specification and tracking address common understanding and predictability, both of
which are states a party can be in: a guest either holds an accurate expectation of what will happen
or she does not, and private effort can move her between those states. Accountability is not a
state. It is a relation between an actor and a forum, in which the actor owes explanation and the
forum can pose questions and pass judgement (Bovens 2007), a reading that algorithmic-accountability
research has pressed home (Metcalf et al. 2023), and no amount of skill on the
part of the first party creates that obligation in anyone else. Citron and Pasquale (2014) make the
point in the language of due process, arguing that what a scoring system owes the people it scores
is procedural, and Ananny and Crawford (2018) explain why disclosure alone will not supply it, since
transparency delivers visibility while leaving the forum absent. The empirical record supports the
distinction rather starkly. Vaccaro et al. (2020) built an appeal process, offered it under three
designs and tested it against a baseline with no appeal at all, while holding the outcome constant.
It produced no improvement in perceived accountability, and their participants contested the goals
and the automation itself, well beyond the single decision. A grievance channel internal to the
party which writes the rules absorbs the contest instead of transferring standing.

We use coordinative sovereignty to name what competence cannot reach: the practical ability of
participants to retain meaningful influence over how hospitality is organized and enacted under
mediation they cannot simply remove. Hirschman's (1970) distinction between exit and voice organizes
it. Influence runs either through leaving or through being heard, and leaving becomes unavailable
once its costs are raised sufficiently, a schema whose analytic distinctions have since been
considerably sharpened (Dowding et al. 2000). Where a guest may readily choose another property,
exit disciplines the arrangement. Where mediation is effectively unavoidable, voice is the only
route remaining, and voice in the sense that matters is not a process feature: as Morrison (2014)
argues, a channel dignifying a decision already taken is a different thing altogether from
consequential input to someone able to act. Competence and standing therefore vary independently,
which is why the paper holds two constructs. A guest may become expert at reading a
property's systems and hold no more influence over them than she did before.

Precision matters here, because the mediated guest is not without resources. Guests bargain with
algorithmic counterparts, and defer to their offers more readily than to human ones, most of all
where income is lowest (Shen and Jin 2024); data-protection law grants the subject of an automated
decision the right to express her view and to contest it, a provision read as due process for the
mediated party (Bayamlıoğlu 2022); automated recovery grants compensation
and apology without any person appearing. What mediation removes is not the addressee but the
counterparty with authority over the rule. Complaint and compensation survive automation; the
relation in which a rule could be put in question does not. Nor is coordinative sovereignty
consumer sovereignty, which names an aggregate power exercised through choice and exit
(Denegri-Knott et al. 2006). A guest may be fully sovereign in that sense, free to book elsewhere,
informed, networked and able to punish by review, and still stand before no one able to answer for
the rule governing her case. Hospitality has been read as a test of sovereignty before, in the
anthropology of hosts and guests (Shryock 2012); ours is the coordinative case, and it names what
the encounter withholds rather than what the market provides.

## 6. Five affordances of guest-centric phygital design

If substitutive hospitality arises where the integrating conditions are withheld, then the design
task is to supply them, and five affordances follow. Four answer a withheld condition; the fifth
removes the mediation instead of repairing it. Transparency answers withheld common understanding.
Consequential decisions ought to be noticeable and intelligible where they take effect: why this
room was assigned, why the price moved, why the request was declined. Transparency alone
accomplishes remarkably little, and the objections come in different kinds that should not be run
together. Ananny
and Crawford (2018) argue the normative case, disclosure supplying visibility without the critical
audience able to act upon it, and Edwards
and Veale (2017) argue the legal one, an individual right to an explanation underdelivering as a
remedy and directing attention instead towards systemic and ex-ante obligations. Transparency at the point of
effect, paired with standing, is the version worth designing.

Adjustability answers common understanding from the other direction. Guests and employees should be
able to modify the parameters shaping an encounter without having to exit the relationship
altogether, and the design target is the guest whose need does not fit the fields she has been
given. An adjustable system widens what may be said to it; an unadjustable one offers a default path
authored entirely by the system, which is choice architecture and not choice (Yeung 2017). The
mechanism has empirical support. Studying personalized travel recommendations, Lv et al. (2024) find
privacy concern falling and attitudes improving where the presentation preserves a traveller's sense
of autonomy over her own information, with perceived information autonomy carrying the effect. What
guests object to, on this evidence, is not personalization as such but personalization they cannot
steer, a pattern the hotel application literature has recorded for a decade (Morosan and DeFranco
2016).

Announced change answers withheld predictability. Where the rules governing recognition, pricing or
access are revised, that revision should be legible to the people who organized their conduct around
the rule it replaces, since systems revising themselves silently convert competence into a moving
target. A guest who learned last year that a request made at check-in would be honoured has acquired
nothing if the rule has since been quietly rewritten. Contestability answers withheld
accountability, and it is where design most often fails. The Vaccaro finding sets the bar: an appeal
routing back to the party which wrote the rule does not confer standing. Later work sharpens the
bar rather than lowering it. Appeals were the one governance mechanism that conferred any
legitimacy on an adverse algorithmic decision, where oversight and audit conferred none (Martin and
Waldman 2022); contestability moved perceived procedural fairness where human oversight moved
nothing (Yurrita et al. 2023); and letting the affected party adjust the outcome improved perceived
fairness in every condition tested (Lee et al. 2019). What works reaches the decision, and what
merely observes it does not. Contestability must
therefore reach the rule and not merely the output, which means designing contestation across a
system's lifecycle instead of bolting an appeal onto its end (Almada 2019; Alfrink et al. 2023), and
it means a reviewer holding the authority to reverse.

Human accessibility also answers accountability, by keeping reachable a person who can recognize a
situation the model does not represent and answer for what follows from it. The person is not the
affordance; the authority the person carries is. Oversight without the power to reverse moves
nothing (Yurrita et al. 2023), and hotels already ration exactly this, granting complaint-handling
authority by rank (Hewagama et al. 2019). Guests already read the
arrangement in these terms. Belanche et al. (2020) find customers attributing responsibility for a
service failure to the firm rather than to the robot which produced it, while expecting rather less
improvement from the machine over time. Automation does not relocate answerability in the guest's
eyes, which makes a design removing the person who answers an organizational choice and not a
technical consequence. Bypass-ability removes the mediation instead of repairing it. A meaningful
alternative path, non-digital or differently mediated, restores the exit option, and where a real
alternative exists exit disciplines the arrangement and the remaining affordances matter less. Where
bypass is formally available but slower, costlier or socially marked, it is not an alternative at
all. Xu et al. (2020) and Boo and Chua (2022) find guests weighing privacy risk against benefit
before accepting identification at the door, and a guest who declines should not thereby become a
problem to be managed.

The derivation carries an organizational implication a checklist would obscure. Transparency,
adjustability and announced change address conditions guests and employees can partly supply for
themselves, which means training and communication will move them. Contestability, and the
authority a reachable person carries, address a condition no individual can supply, which means
they require organizational
design: a reviewer with authority, a staffing model treating human contact as a function of the job,
and a decision about who answers when the system is wrong. A property responding to a contestability
problem with guest education has misread which kind of condition it faces. We propose these
affordances as conditions plausibly shaping guest agency, employee discretion, recognition,
belonging, trust and well-being. The relationships are theorized here and they await empirical test.

## 7. Against frictionless hospitality

Seamlessness is a stated commitment of the frameworks upon which this special issue builds, and the
prevailing design value in phygital hospitality. Batat (2024) presents the
phygital customer experience as a matter of fluidifying customers' journeys from online to offline
and back again, and Shabnam et al. (2026), in the most recent programmatic statement of
transformative phygital service research, direct their considerable critical energy upon opacity and
rigidity while leaving smoothness itself unexamined. Hospitality itself supplies the first reason
for doubt. Lynch (2017) observes that welcome operates as a kind of social oil which becomes
remarkable only when it somehow fails. A hospitality engineered never to fail has thereby been
engineered never to be noticed, and contestation of the unnoticed, though it plainly occurs,
attaches to no agreed point in the encounter. The second reason comes from research upon voice.
Voice retains value even where it cannot change the outcome: Lind, Kanfer and Earley (1990) found
that voice offered after a decision had been made, and explicitly incapable of altering it, still
produced higher fairness judgements than no voice at all. The effect is conditional, and the
condition matters as much as the effect. Where the invitation to speak is read as never having
borne on anything, voice depresses fairness judgements below the no-voice baseline, in the
laboratory and in service settings alike (Folger 1977; Goodwin and Ross 1992). What frictionless
design withholds is therefore not a guaranteed benefit but the occasion: the surface at which a
guest could have spoken and been recognized as someone with standing to speak. Whether the occasion
pays depends upon whether anything is disposed to listen, and a seamless encounter forecloses the
question before it can be asked.

Infrastructure research explains why that cost is so easily missed. Star and Ruhleder (1996)
observed that infrastructure becomes effective as it sinks out of sight, embedded, standardized and
taken for granted, and seamlessness is that process pursued deliberately as a design goal. Star's
(1999) account of the master narrative encoded within a classification supplies the political edge,
since a standard carries one presumed point of view and renders every other position unnamed, doing
so silently, as the shape of the blanks and never as an instruction anyone issues. Bowker and Star
(1999) name the experience of a person whom a category does not fit as torque, the felt twisting
when a life is bent to a classification built for somebody else.

The cultural version of this problem is documented within hospitality's own literature, and it
predates automation by some margin. Nguyen et al. (2024) found hotel staff categorizing guests by
country of origin before arrival and running the encounter from a matching script, with any mismatch
between expected and actual behaviour producing service failure and demanding a new script on the
spot. Cultural background shapes what counts as good service in the first place (Mattila 1999;
Torres et al. 2014), and Walters et al. (2021) put the point beyond reasonable dispute at scale,
finding significant differences across all twelve facets of commercial hospitality they measured
among 2,248 tourists drawn from Asia-Pacific and European destinations. Algorithmic categorization
industrializes the scripting and, more consequentially, removes the moment at which a person noticed
the mismatch and improvised. Hotel managers themselves express doubt that generative systems can
carry a specific hospitality culture or respond to contingency (Wang 2024). Torque, then, is what a
guest experiences when her requirements fall outside algorithmically constructed norms. The
clearest documented case sits at travel's own threshold: a body scanner that requires its operator
to select male or female before it can scan reads any body fitting neither template as an anomaly,
whichever button is pressed, and no surface exists at which the traveller could be received as
herself (Costanza-Chock 2020). A
frictionless system offers no surface upon which to be received as an exception, because the
negotiation which would have surfaced that exception has been designed away. The classification
doing the sorting is inferred and continuously recomputed, so that no stable standard exists to
appeal to even in principle (Cheney-Lippold 2011), and the sorting carries material consequences for
access (Fourcade and Healy 2013).

Consumer research has begun to make a version of this argument in its own terms. Padigar et al.
(2024) distinguish four kinds of friction according to the desirability and value of the task the
effort serves, and warn that removing constructive friction, where effort is undesirable but
valuable, can destroy the very value it was thought to be obstructing. Negotiating an unusual
request is constructive friction in exactly that sense: the guest would rather not have to explain
herself, and the explaining is where recognition occurs. Phillips et al. (2024) arrive at a
compatible conclusion from retail service, finding the physical frictionless experience something of
a slippery slope for the memorability of an encounter, which suggests the cost of smoothness is not
confined to the guests whom a system fails to fit. Interaction design reaches the alternative from
another direction, Chalmers and Galani (2004) having argued that seams, the visible joins and
imperfections of a system, are resources for action and not defects, and that revealing them lets
people work with a system as well as through it. Hospitality has its own version of the insight. The
pause at a desk, the question that was not on the form, the request that required someone to think,
are the occasions upon which recognition happens.

The remedy for algorithmic exclusion frequently runs through the same instrument as the harm. Cui et
al. (2020), as we have seen, showed guest reviews reducing discrimination in acceptance, and the
finding is conditional upon the guest carrying any review at all. The
mechanism works, on the measure against which it was tested, and it simultaneously deepens the
mediation, extends the record and relocates the disadvantage onto the guest with no record:
the first-time traveller, or the guest who declines to build a profile. Platform research has
already named both halves of this pattern, as the cold-start problem and as reputation inflation,
the tendency of the scores themselves to drift upward until they inform less (Filippas et al.
2022). A design principle
failing to notice it will go on recommending more data as the answer to problems that data
created. One narrowing should be owned rather than implied. Padigar et al. (2024) identify
frictions customers value for their own sake, and we set those aside deliberately: what concerns us
is not effort the guest enjoys but the occasion for negotiation that engineered smoothness removes,
a loss that persists even where nobody wanted the effort.

## 8. Discussion and contributions

We make three contributions. The first is to reposition artificial intelligence in hospitality from
a neutral service technology to a constitutive mediator of hospitality relationships, meanings and
power. The move carries no claim that technology harms hospitality. It is a claim about where
analysis should sit, namely with the redistribution of knowledge, discretion and authority among
three parties, and not with adoption or efficiency alone. The second is a distinction with a
condition attached, since augmentative and substitutive hospitality are separated by whether the
three integrating conditions are withheld together, which makes the distinction checkable against a
specific touchpoint. The third is the separation of competence from standing. Hospitality
algorithmacy names what guests and employees are able to develop; coordinative sovereignty names
what they cannot develop and must instead be granted. The separation has a practical edge, since it
identifies which design problems training can address and which require an organization to decide
who answers for a decision. Throughout, the human-centric commitment of this special issue is
carried by three terms held together: guest agency, employee discretion and relational judgement,
each treated as something an arrangement can protect or withhold rather than a quality a property
either has or lacks.

Well-being enters the framework at two levels, and the evidence supports three directions of effect.
Within the encounter, the affordances condition guest agency, employee discretion and trust, and
through these they reach well-being at individual and collective scale, of which belonging is one
constituent. Transformative service research has treated indicators of both increasing and
decreasing well-being as central from its founding statement (Anderson and Ostrom 2015), and
hospitality research carries the individual and collective levels into its own settings (Galeone
and Sebastiani 2021), with community quality of life a distinct object from the guest's (Uysal et
al. 2016). The automation evidence runs in all three directions. Employees withdraw where the risk
robots pose to their work is salient (Pan et al. 2025) and report stress under algorithmic human
resource management (Nayak et al. 2025); guests evaluate the same machines favourably while
reporting frustration and sadness at their use in a human-driven industry (Christou et al. 2020);
and technologically mediated third places sustain community bonds or fragment them according to
identifiable design choices (Parkinson et al. 2022). The conditional findings are the instructive
ones, since effects concentrate at particular journey stages and under particular moderators rather
than holding uniformly, which is what a framework of affordances would predict. One absence in this
literature is worth claiming: the field holds individual-level evidence on automation and
collective-level evidence on well-being, and the two halves have not yet met. Societally, the same
register reaches labour displacement, and access and exclusion at population scale, where the
journal's own account of algorithmic hostility already points (Riordan 2024).

The gains for hospitality organizations are specific, and so are the challenges of the transition.
The diagnostic in section 4 classifies an actual touchpoint rather than adjudicating whether a
property is guest-centric in the abstract. The derivation in section 6 tells an operator which
affordances design and training can supply and which require an institutional arrangement, the
distinction transition programmes most often miss. The seamlessness argument names a goal the
industry pursues at cost to itself. Stated as a sequence, the framework becomes a roadmap, and
Table 2 sets it out.

**Table 2. A phygital hospitality transformation roadmap.**

| Stage | Work | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Classify every guest-facing touchpoint with the section 4 diagnostic | Where does the property run substitutive? |
| 2. Specify | For each substitutive touchpoint, name the withheld condition and the affordance answering it | What must this arrangement offer? |
| 3. Provide by design | Transparency at the point of effect; adjustability; announced change | What can be built and trained? |
| 4. Constitute | A reviewer with authority to reverse, access to the material, a duty to implement | Who answers when the system is wrong? |
| 5. Keep the exit | A non-digital path of comparable quality, unpunished | What does refusal cost a guest? |

The stages ascend in institutional cost, and the large-scale evidence orders itself the same way:
internal reversal of contested decisions works where a duty to reverse exists, and external fora
collapse where implementation is optional. A property that stops after stage three has automated
its hospitality without deciding who answers for it.

This is a conceptual paper. Our vignettes illustrate the framework rather than testing it, and we
make no claim about any particular property or guest population. The relationships we propose
between the five affordances and guest and employee outcomes are theorized and await measurement.
The evidence we have assembled is also uneven in an instructive way: the redistribution of authority
is considerably better documented on the host and employee side, where platform accommodation and
algorithmic management research have accumulated (Cheng and Foley 2019; Jianu et al. 2025), than on
the guest side, where much of the literature measures acceptance and leaves structure alone. That
asymmetry has shaped our argument, which builds the structural claim where the evidence is strongest
and derives the guest position from it. Three empirical directions follow, and we would prioritize
them in this order. Qualitative work could examine how guests and frontline employees actually
interpret and work around the systems mediating their encounters, extending folk-theory findings
into hospitality settings where they have barely been tested. Survey work could operationalize the
three components of hospitality algorithmacy and establish whether they cohere and whether they
separate from general self-efficacy. Design research could test the affordances directly, and
contestability is much the most valuable target, since existing evidence records a failure while the
conditions for success remain unspecified.

## 9. Conclusion

Algorithmic systems now perform functions hospitality has always treated as the host's: recognizing
the guest, regulating access, anticipating needs and determining what may be asked. That
participation will not be reversed. The question that remains is upon what terms it happens.
Guest-centric phygital hospitality does not depend upon making technology invisible. It depends upon
guests and employees retaining the agency to determine how technology participates in the
performance of hospitality, and upon organizations accepting that some part of that agency cannot be
trained into people and must instead be designed into the arrangement.
