# Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality

## Abstract

Hospitality firms now hand much of the work of hosting, from admission and pricing to check-in, room allocation and complaints, to algorithmic systems supplied by several firms at once. Each places the party that decides at a distance from the guest it affects. The article begins from hospitality studies' account of what a host owes and argues, with the law of innkeepers as evidence, that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation, imposed on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers and taken up by holding out. It then asks what becomes of that obligation when the work is handed on: the work can be delegated and the duty cannot, so the guest's standing survives, but its exercise depends on a reachable party with authority to change the decision. It offers a test of phygital touchpoints, scoped by the type of decision, and a roadmap for organizations. We argue that what algorithmic mediation removes is rarely the host's obligation and more often the guest's path to anyone with authority to answer for it.

**Keywords:** phygital hospitality; algorithmic mediation; host obligation; guest standing; accountability; interruptibility

## Introduction

Phygital hospitality hands much of the work of hosting, from admission and pricing to check-in, room allocation and complaints, to systems and firms that the guest rarely sees. Revenue-management systems recommend the price a guest is quoted (Garcia et al. 2026). Facial-recognition systems verify identity at check-in (Xu et al. 2021), and service robots greet, direct and serve guests, who hold the firm responsible when the robots fail (Belanche et al. 2020). In short-term rentals, self-check-in replaces the handover of keys (Bosma 2022: 605). Behind each of these stands a chain of vendors, none of whom the guest meets (Cobbe et al. 2023). We call software that takes or shapes a decision about a guest an algorithmic system; the guest, the staff, the property, the firms and the systems together, a phygital arrangement; and any moment at which the guest meets that arrangement, a touchpoint. The technologies and their jobs differ across hotels, restaurants and rentals, but each takes over part of what a host once did in person and places the party who decides at a distance from the guest it affects.

Hospitality scholars have long resisted a definition of hospitality as the provision of food, drink and a bed, and the founding editors of this journal set out to study it as a lens on social relations and power (Lynch et al. 2011). On that view the host owes the guest more than a service, and Shryock's good host is one able to 'protect guests from harm' (2012: S20). This journal has studied platforms since its first volume: Bialski (2012: 257) found that a hospitality network's profiles gave hosts 'the power to choose one member over the other', Germann Molz (2018: 244) showed Airbnb using its status as a platform to evade legal responsibility, and later work describes guests without hosts and algorithmic hostility (Germann Molz 2026; Riordan 2024). The phygital literature presents such systems as means to a smoother and more personal experience (Batat 2024, 2026; Brochado et al. 2026) and describes them by what they do, as tools, mediators, facilitators and co-creators (van Doorn et al. 2017; Li et al. 2021; Xing and Zhang 2026), without saying what the party that deploys them owes the guest. Algorithmic-accountability research keeps answerability with the party that delegates but ties it to harm (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5), and hospitality research measures hospitableness as a motive of individual hosts (Lashley 2015: 1), so the host's obligation is sought either in an injury or in a person's motive.

This article seeks to keep hospitality studies' account of what a host owes, and to say where that obligation goes when the work of hosting is delegated to algorithmic systems spread across several firms. Read as a role, the obligation has a content, a moment at which it comes into force and a holder, and the law of innkeepers supplies all three. Because the law attaches the duty to the role, the work of hosting can pass to staff, contractors or systems while the duty, and the answering for it, stay with the holder. The guest's standing therefore survives delegation. What can fail is its exercise, and from that distinction we derive a test, scoped by the type of decision, of whether a touchpoint keeps the conditions under which a guest can press a claim. We argue that algorithmic mediation rarely removes the host's obligation; what it removes is the path from the guest to anyone with the authority to answer for it.

The article begins by setting out the host's power and obligation as hospitality studies describes them, and argues that the obligation belongs to a role. The second section follows the obligation when its holder hands on the work, and defines the guest's standing from it. The third section examines touchpoints in hotels, short-term rentals and restaurants, each of which hands part of a host's work to a system with several firms behind it. We close with the paper's limits, three studies it calls for, and what it asks of organizations. We suggest that, by looking for the obligation in the host's motive, the debate about commercial hospitality has had no way to say where it goes once no person does the hosting. The role account brings into view the parties, such as operators, brands and vendors, that now do much of that work. Whether a machine could itself bear a host's duties we set aside: a robot can occupy a role without being a moral agent (Kropf et al. 2026: 7), and our concern is the parties who deploy such systems and the guests who meet them. The study of phygital hospitality has to ask who answers to the guest, and what an organization must keep for its guests to remain guests.

## The host's power and the host's obligation

The host's obligation is hard to see in commercial hospitality, in part because the trade has come to describe its own work as service. When Golubovskaya et al. asked twenty-two hotel frontline employees about their work, only six could define hospitality, and 'service' rather than 'hospitality' was their default perspective (2017: 1296). The authors conclude that service-management terminology 'overrides hospitality within the commercial context' (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1299–1300). Hospitality scholars, in contrast, have long held that the host owes the guest something beyond the exchange. The founding editorial of this journal finds, in the Greek, Roman and Enlightenment traditions that historians of hospitality study, a duty to the stranger that was sacred and reached beyond shelter to protection (Lynch et al. 2011). Commercial houses made a similar promise: by O'Gorman's account, a traveller who crossed their threshold was guaranteed protection and refuge (2009). Writing in this journal, Varley et al. (2018) put the point on the authority of King (1995): historically, the host was obliged to see to the guest's safety and comfort as well as to provide a place to stay. Guests, Shryock writes, 'depend on their hosts for protection and respect' (2012: S23). To host, on these accounts, is to take the guest into one's charge.

In the classical law of hospitality the host commands: 'It is always the host who ordains, the guest who complies' (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 513). Command brings duty with it: a host infringes the same law by failing 'to protect his guest' (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 516). Shryock (2012: S25) draws the same connection from the other side, holding that 'sovereignty is manifest in the ability to act as host'. Bulley (2015) adds that this power does not stop at the threshold: once the guest is inside, the host goes on policing and managing the distinctions that hospitality draws. We read these accounts together as saying that the party who admits the guest takes on the guest's safety for as long as the stay lasts.

Yet the field is divided over whether a host who charges can be hospitable at all. Lashley defines hospitableness as 'the willingness to be hospitable for its own sake, without any expectation of recompense or reciprocity' (2015: 1). Commercial hospitality, Lashley observes, is offered at a price and 'would be withdrawn if the payment could not be made', which is why some hold that it 'cannot deliver true hospitableness' (2015: 5). Slattery goes further: 'The buyers are not guests, they are customers. The relationship is not philanthropic it is economic' (2002: 21). Lynch et al. (2011) report a similar claim from Aramberri, for whom the people who meet in commercial tourism are better described as service providers and customers than as hosts and guests. The journal's editors answered that hospitality still describes what passes between strangers within the commercial realm (Lynch et al. 2011), and Telfer, as reported by Lashley, holds that hospitable people offer hospitableness in hotels and bars 'beyond, and in spite of, the commercial transaction' (Lashley 2015: 5).

The debate, on both sides, turns on the host's motive. Blain and Lashley set an obligation that is 'externally imposed' against hospitableness, with which it 'may be at odds', and look to secure the welcome by recruiting people with hospitable motives (2014: 2). The instruments that follow identify 'individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable to others' (Lashley 2015: 1), and a recent study of service robots defines hospitableness as 'a host's disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity' (Liu et al. 2026: 2). An account built on motive cannot follow the obligation under delegation, because a motive belongs to a person, cannot be handed on, and does not pass to the system that replaces the person. We keep hospitableness for the quality with which a role is performed, which the hospitality literature describes in detail, and distinguish it from the source of the duty. We argue instead that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation: it has a content, a moment at which it comes into force, and a holder, and the law of innkeepers shows each of them.

The obligation belongs to a role because the law attaches it to whoever receives travellers, whatever that party's motive. Under the common law, those who followed a common calling were bound to serve all who came. The duty narrowed over time until it attached 'only to common carriers by land and water (including ferrymen) and innkeepers' (Burdick 1911: 523), while the shopkeeper remained free to refuse (Wyman 1904: 159). The innkeeper's duties were, in Maese's words, 'fixed by law rather than by contract' (2015: 501). Bogen reads the English cases the same way, finding that it was 'English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment' that imposed them (1996: 75). On this account the obligation neither depends on the host's motive nor lapses when the motive fails; it belongs to whoever occupies the role.

The obligation also has a content, and the content is a bundle of duties rather than one. The innkeeper's historical duty was to provide 'bed, board, and hearth' (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 63): to receive the traveller, to feed and shelter them, and to keep their belongings safe. Calye's Case confined the innkeeper's strict liability to the guest's goods 'and not his person' (1584: fol. 33b), although later accounts report liability for harm to the guest as well (Bogen 1996: 82 n. 160; Maese 2015: 499). Pitt-Rivers adds a duty the law did not carry: a host must show concern for the guests' 'needs and wishes' (2012: 516). The commercial setting adds conditions of its own, since hotels work by 'standardized procedures and scripted interactions' that limit what employees may do (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1295). None of these sources makes a negotiated exception something the guest is owed, and the exceptions the law recognized ran in the host's favour, as where loss arose from the guest's own fault (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33a). The welcome is therefore a set of duties, to receive, to sustain, to protect and to attend, held by a role and carried out within an organization.

What separates hospitality from other services that may not turn a customer away is not this mechanism, which is general. A bank may not discriminate when a consumer applies for or uses a payment account (Payment Accounts Regulations 2015, reg. 18(1)). No provider of services in Great Britain may discriminate by refusing a person the service (Equality Act 2010, s. 29(1)). A hospital that takes part in Medicare must screen anyone who comes to its emergency department and asks (Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act 1986, § 1395dd(a)). Inaccessible decision-makers, weak appeals and costly exit trouble banking and online retail as much as hospitality. What remains distinctive is narrower: among the common callings, the duty to receive all comers survived only for innkeepers and carriers (Burdick 1911: 523), and what the innkeeper received was the stranger's person and belongings, overnight, in a house that was not the stranger's own (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). The innkeeper's liability, moreover, reached beyond his own servants to 'other guests or any unidentified party' (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 65). In this sense we disagree with Slattery (2002: 21), for whom it is 'inaccurate and misleading' to treat the relationship between host and guest as the critical one in the hospitality business; we grant him the corporate context he describes, and argue that it changes who holds the host's role, not whether the role exists.

The role has a beginning as well as a content. The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller presents themselves. Arrival alone has never been enough: common inns, Calye's Case held, 'are instituted for passengers and wayfaring men' (1584: fol. 32b), and in Holt's dissent in Lane v. Cotton a full house excused refusal (1701: 485). The Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 applies those duties to an establishment 'held out by the proprietor' as offering food, drink and, if required, a bed to 'any traveller presenting himself who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum … and who is in a fit state to be received' (s. 1(3)). The Act attaches those duties 'to the proprietor of such an hotel' and to no other person (s. 1(1)).

But why was the innkeeper bound at all? The legal history does not settle the question, and Bogen calls the explanation from holding out 'not so much a theory as a tautology' (1996: 52). The most persuasive account we have found rests on the traveller's exposure: people on a journey, 'away from their home communities and in unfamiliar surroundings', were held to deserve a special legal status (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). The duty, on this reading, answers to the condition of the person received rather than to the preference of the one receiving. To say that the law imposes the duty is not to say that the law explains it, or that it places today's hotels in any special legal category. What the law does give is the moment at which the obligation comes into force and the party who holds it.

Hospitality runs in two directions, and the guest has duties as well as claims. In Pitt-Rivers' classical rule the guest 'has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host' (2012: 514), and a guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's place (2012: 515). In commercial hospitality the guest's duties narrow: in King's formulation, as Golubovskaya et al. report it, the guest's 'only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably' (2017: 1286). The statutory traveller of 1956, able to pay and fit to be received, is already such a guest, and hotels ask guests to leave who will not follow the stay's rules after repeated communications (Xiang et al. 2022). The guest's side of the relation is therefore stated and conditional, and in the classical account a guest who fails it entitles the host to give up the role (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 517).

The role has one further property: a firm can hold it. Slattery observes that as economies develop, 'chains of hospitality businesses replace unaffiliated venues as the key operators in the industry' (2002: 24), and that the chains outsource services to suppliers, investors, consultants and internet companies (2002: 25). The holder may be the owner of a small commercial home, the proprietor of a hotel, the company that runs it day to day, or the brand under which it trades. Once the obligation belongs to whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, it is not confined to the innkeeper in person, and holding out is how a party takes the role up. The next question is what becomes of the obligation when its holder hands the work of hosting to others.

## Delegation and standing: Beyond the host in person?

Hospitality scholars have been right about what a host owes, and we have argued that the obligation belongs to a role. We now push beyond a second part of their account: its picture of the host as one person. The host of the classical account is a single figure who ordains and protects (Pitt-Rivers 2012: 513, 516), and the owners of commercial homes whom Di Domenico and Lynch studied perceived the balance of power to 'rest firmly with them' (2007: 332). A staffed hotel has never had a host of that kind, and a phygital arrangement places several firms behind each decision a guest meets.

The single host holds together four things that need to be kept apart. Obligation is what the host owes the guest, the duties set out in the previous section. Responsibility is the holder's retained duty to see that the obligation is performed, by whoever does the work (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 7; Morgan 2015: 7–8). Authority is the power, assigned by an organization, to change a decision or the rule behind it, a sense we stipulate because the word carries technical meanings elsewhere. Accountability is the arrangement through which the holder answers to the guest for how the obligation was performed, in Bovens' terms a relation in which an actor must explain and justify its conduct to a forum that can pose questions and pass judgement (2007: 450, 452). In a single host the four sit together, which fits the owner of a small commercial home (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–335) and not a staffed hotel.

Keeping them apart matters because commercial hospitality has long divided the host's position among several parties. This journal already has words for the result: Bulley calls the agencies that run a refugee camp, once refugees are past its formal threshold, an assembled host (2015), and Lynch et al. (2011), after Robinson and Lynch, distinguish micro-hosts from macro-hosts such as a train company. An assembled host raises a question the single host never did: which of its members answers to the guest. In Crinkley v. Holiday Inns, a single decision under North Carolina law, the owners of a franchised motel, the company that ran it day to day and the franchisor whose brand it carried were all held liable to guests assaulted in their room (1988: paras 7, 9). The franchisor was liable vicariously, through apparent agency: the jury could find that the brand had presented the property as its own and that the guests had relied on it, though the court called the evidence of reliance 'marginal' (1988: paras 9, 49–52). English law supplies a second line: a party whose 'own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance' is not liable for how it is performed (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 25(3)). Phygital arrangements multiply such arrangers, chiefly the vendors of systems that price, verify and allocate, and Cobbe et al. find that in such supply chains there is 'typically no one actor in overall control' (2023: 1192). We propose that the party which holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside the party that controls the operation, and that a party which only arranges a service, such as a technology vendor, answers to the host; no court has held this.

Between them, the law and the field say who holds the obligation when the guest is received. Their concern is with reception. But what becomes of the host's obligation once the work of hosting is handed on? The work can be delegated, and the duty cannot. The UK Supreme Court put the structure plainly in Woodland: the work required to perform certain duties 'may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's' (2013: para 7). Such a duty arises from 'an antecedent relationship' and is 'a positive or affirmative duty to protect' (para 7), and Morgan states the principle in a line: 'No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be' (2015: 7). Woodland concerns schools and hospitals, not hotels, and we borrow its structure without claiming that the law already places hotels in its category, although the court noted that the responsibilities of fee-paying schools are already non-delegable 'because they are contractual' (para 25(5)). Delegation is not new to hospitality, for the innkeeper of 1584 already answered for 'a default in him or his servants' (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33a). What algorithmic delegation changes is the performer. A servant who erred could be found and named, whereas a system that errs may be supplied by a firm that no guest can reach.

If the duty stays with the holder, the holder is the party the guest must be able to reach. Writers on algorithmic accountability already hold that deploying an autonomous system does not lessen the deployer's responsibility (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5). Kong grounds that responsibility in the deployer's control over the act of delegating and concludes that 'one who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it' (2026: 4–5, 10). We agree with the conclusion, but our duty has a different shape: it is not a responsibility for harm that a delegated system might cause but a first-order duty owed to a particular person from the moment of reception, whether or not anything goes wrong. The duty rests, in Woodland's terms, not on 'control of the environment' but on 'control over the claimant for the purpose of performing a function' that the defendant has assumed (2013: para 24). Kropf et al. reach a compatible position from the ethics of care, holding that a robot can occupy a narrow role without accountability, that its answerability 'is currently inconceivable', and that blame stays with the humans who deploy it (2026: 7, 9–10). A machine can therefore perform the role of hosting, while the duty to the guest, and the answering for how it is performed, remain with the party that took the role up.

If the obligation persists under delegation, so does the guest's standing, provided that standing is defined from the obligation and not from accountability. Darwall glosses the authority of an obligee as a 'standing to address claims and demands and hold accountable' (2013: 36), so that to take standing from his account and then define accountability as answering someone with standing would make each term depend on the other. Thompson's forms of directed duty, 'X has a duty to Y to do A' and 'X has a right against Y' (2004: 335), fix who owes what to whom without mentioning answering. We therefore define the guest's standing in two parts: first, standing held, the guest's position as the one to whom the host's directed duties are owed; and second, standing exercised, the guest's pressing of that claim, by asking, objecting or requesting an exception, on a party able to receive it. Standing in the first sense exists whether or not it is claimed, as Darwall himself concedes when he writes that a right holder 'need not actually claim her right or demand compliance with it in order for the right to exist' (2013: 35). Under algorithmic mediation, then, the guest still holds standing, because the host's obligation persists, and what can fail is its exercise.

Exercising standing has conditions, and guests do not supply them. Hospitality's own norms tend to withhold the power to press a claim: in Pitt-Rivers' account a dissatisfied guest 'has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out', and the diplomatic guest who turned his plate over registered a protest without discourtesy (2012: 513–514). The same restraint appears in the commercial home, where Di Domenico and Lynch's researcher noted that a complaint about the beds that one might make in a hotel is 'not the done thing' for a guest in a home (2007: 333); the evidence is a single note. In commercial hospitality what makes a claim proper to press is the paying relation and the law behind it, since commercial hosts 'must meet the desires and demands of the paying guest' (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 335) and a refused traveller could sue for damages (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 64). Moysidou and Stanley (2023), writing in this journal on homestays, treat a guest's confidence that a complaint will be acted on as a form of power, and show an au pair who cannot leave choosing not to complain about little things.

Competence with algorithmic systems does not supply those conditions either. Users build working theories of the systems they meet (Eslami et al. 2016), yet understanding a platform does not give them influence over it (Lin 2025), and knowing how to appeal is not the same as being answered (Vaccaro et al. 2020). Competence is learned, while the occasion and the addressee for a claim are provided, or withheld, by the institution that holds the host's role.

Where requests do change outcomes in hospitality, they reach someone with authority to grant them. In an upscale hotel in south-eastern China during the pandemic, a guest who arrived without a test report was not turned away: after a front-office employee checked with management, the hotel paid for the test and arranged his transport to it (Xiang et al. 2022). A guest refused at the desk in the same study took his complaint the next day to the general manager and the group head office. In Lugosi's study of a bar, house cocktails invented by customers and staff disappeared when the regulars who drank them stopped coming, and management's allowance gave patrons only the opportunity to make such things (2009). What a guest can expect from the host's obligation is therefore a hearing by someone with authority, not the exception itself, and the next section shows how phygital arrangements change the path to that person.

## Assembled hosts at the touchpoint

Once the host is understood as assembled and the duty as staying with the role's holder, what comes into view is the path a guest's claim must travel to reach that holder. The most familiar setting is the hotel front desk. The path can fail even there. In the Glasgow hotels that housed asylum seekers during the pandemic, Petie et al. (2025) describe hosting divided by outsourcing among agencies whose responsibilities no one had made clear: residents could not tell where to seek help, and the receptionist they often approached first was not equipped for complex problems. Germann Molz (2018: 243–244) has shown the other end of the path, where a company that owns no lodging and employs no hosts appeals to its status as a platform to avoid legal responsibility. To keep with the theme of this special issue, we concentrate on phygital touchpoints in hotels, short-term rentals and restaurants. We follow a claim from the guest to whoever could grant it and ask what an arrangement must keep, using cases that illustrate the distinctions rather than test them.

### The authority–answerability split

By the authority–answerability split we mean an arrangement in which the party that can change a decision, or the rule behind it, is not a party the guest can reach. Under such an arrangement a guest's claim cannot travel from the person who receives it to the person who could grant it. Accounts of phygital hospitality describe the movement between physical and digital touchpoints as fluid and make smoothness a goal of design (Batat 2024: 1220, 1233). On these accounts good technology removes queues, repetition, uncertainty and wasted effort so that the guest moves through the journey without friction (Batat and Hammedi 2023). The movement can as accurately be called a relay: physical moments become digital records, and digital rulings come back as physical facts, a key card that opens or does not, a price on the screen at the desk. A relay raises a question that fluidity hides, namely who holds authority, and who keeps room for judgement, at each handoff. The split also takes different shapes across hospitality formats, so that a theory built on the hotel desk alone would not hold.

Where the host's work sits differs between hotels, short-term rentals and restaurants. In short-term rentals the platform divides the host's role among primary hosts, co-hosts and listing administrators (Bosma 2022: 601), and self-check-in can turn hosting into what Bosma calls ghosting, with no one present at arrival (2022: 605). Hosts on such platforms resist the hotel's role, insisting 'I am not a butler though nor am I a receptionist in a hotel' (Kaniadakis and Farmaki 2022: 14), while the platform passes the guest-facing duties of hosting to them (Germann Molz 2018: 243–245). In restaurants, by contrast, technology can be added to an encounter while staff go on hosting it (Batat 2021). Across these formats the question is the same: whether a guest's claim can still reach someone with authority over the rule that produced the outcome.

Authority in commercial hospitality has long been graded and organizational. The split did not begin with algorithms. In the upscale hotels Golubovskaya et al. studied, a manager told staff that guests 'are here as your guests, but they cannot demand everything', because the hotel has its 'own standard' (2017: 1297). The owner of a small commercial home sets the rules, admits and ejects in person (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–335), while the motel in Crinkley was shared, decades before any algorithm, among owners, an operating company and a franchisor (1988: para 7). What phygital arrangements change is therefore not a complete frontline authority that once existed but the path from the guest to graded authority, which an algorithmic system can lengthen, obscure or remove.

The chain along which a claim now travels runs from guest to employee to system to whoever configured the system. Three things that need not travel together come apart along it. Knowledge drifts towards the system, which can hold a guest's history, preferences, spending and loyalty status alongside the property's inventory and constraints (Rahman 2021; Calo and Rosenblat 2017). Discretion splits, because staff keep some freedom to act while more of their day goes to reading decisions made elsewhere and working around categories set elsewhere (Pedersen and Pors 2023; Spektor et al. 2025). Authority over the rule drifts towards whoever designs, configures or controls it (Scott and Orlikowski 2012), often a vendor that keeps the right to 'change, withdraw, or cancel products and services at will' (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1195). Discretion acts on the single case and authority on the rule, so a guest can meet someone with the first and never reach anyone with the second. The problem is not the length of the chain but a break in it, at which the point where a decision is made comes apart from any point where the guest can be answered.

### Three conditions and six questions

To examine a touchpoint, then, we need to know what the guest's standing requires of it. The test we propose tells whether the conditions under which a guest can exercise standing survive the delegation of hosting work at a given touchpoint, and it measures neither satisfaction nor harm. Researchers can use it to analyse an arrangement, and managers to audit one before or after deployment. Its conditions take their names from the study of coordination, which identifies 'three integrating conditions for coordination: accountability, predictability, and common understanding' (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009: 483). Their content here comes from the host's duties: common understanding from the duty to attend to this particular guest, predictability from terms held steady enough to be negotiated, and accountability, in the sense defined above, from the duty to answer through someone with authority. Each condition can be present or absent, and present in degrees, and the other two cannot make up for a missing accountability, since a guest who understands a system and can predict it has still been hosted by no one if no party is obliged to answer. Batat makes the parallel claim within the phygital framework, that failures by human connectors 'cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors' (2024: 1234), and Ananny and Crawford show that transparency alone cannot produce accountable systems (2018: 984).

The test asks six questions, in the order in which a claim would travel.

1. What rule or actor determined the outcome?
2. Could the guest have anticipated it, and is the guest told when it changes?
3. Who can see how it was made?
4. Who can override it?
5. Who must answer if it is disputed?
6. What does refusing the system cost the guest?

The first establishes whether the decision can be located at all, and the second and third examine predictability and common understanding. The fourth and fifth separate the power over the single case from the duty to answer for the rule, and the fifth, which examines accountability, is the question on which the others depend. The sixth asks what refusing the system costs the guest, because a guest who cannot decline the automated route without paying in money, effort or embarrassment has no real alternative, and an alternative is what gives a complaint its weight (Hirschman 1970).

Four cases, chosen across technologies and formats, show the questions at work. In hotel pricing, Garcia et al. found algorithmic recommendations pitched away from the price the algorithm itself judged best, in order to move managers who repriced too rarely (2026). Neither guest nor staff could anticipate the operative rule and no one at the desk could explain the price, so the touchpoint fails on predictability and on accountability, although its legal weight is moderate unless the price bars a guest from the service (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 22). At check-in, a facial-recognition system that confirms a guest's identity does a clerical job, while one that weighs the guest's history to decide how they will be received is deciding admission (Xu et al. 2021). The six questions bear hard on the second and barely on the first. In a short-term rental with self-check-in the app may answer the first three questions well, yet when a code fails at night no one with authority may be reachable (Bosma 2022: 605). When a restaurant projects an animated chef onto the table while staff go on hosting the meal, the technology adds to an encounter whose rules stay steady and whose staff keep their discretion (Batat 2021), and the touchpoint passes. Together the cases show that a touchpoint can satisfy guests and harm no one while failing the test.

An employee often stands between the guest and the system, and does work there that is easy to miss. Skilled front-desk employees manufacture a guest's sense of control through small acts of discretion, appearing to defer while steering the options (Bendoly 2013). Under algorithmic direction that work becomes translation in both directions, as managers carry the system's decisions to the people they affect (Jianu et al. 2025) and the employee at the desk carries the guest's request into terms the system will accept. The translation is fragile, because when a system quietly revises its rules the knowledge it rests on loses its value overnight and no one tells the employee or the guest (Hatherley 2025). Staff are asked to produce a sense of control without holding the controls (Fuller and Smith 1991; Möhlmann et al. 2021), employees fall silent when speaking up looks futile (Morrison 2014), and frontline hospitality employees aware of artificial intelligence in their jobs report more silence (Cheng et al. 2025). It is useful, therefore, to ask which decisions need a person with authority at the moment they are taken, and which do not.

### When a person must be reachable

Not every touchpoint needs a person with authority standing by. Baum and Laux distinguish forms of human involvement in automated systems by whether intervention can come before a decision takes effect, and what matters, they write, is 'not elapsed time but whether intervention can occur before the token output becomes effective' (2026: 120). Synchronous intervention can change the decision a person is subject to, asynchronous review shapes future decisions, and for decisions too fast for any person the only human control is a limit set in advance and kept open to revision (Baum and Laux 2026: 120–122). Applied to hospitality, the distinction gives a boundary that follows the host's obligation. Where a decision takes effect on the guest's person, access or contract during the stay and cannot be undone before the harm lands, the guest needs a reachable party with authority before it takes effect. Where a decision can be deferred or undone, a reachable appeal is enough, and where it is too fast for anyone, the host answers for the rule it set in advance.

Table 1 places common hospitality decisions against this boundary.

**Table 1. Hospitality decisions by stakes, reversibility and the recourse they need**

| Decision | Stakes | Reversible in time? | Recourse it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Admission or refusal at the door or at check-in; a booking not honoured | High: shelter for the night | No | A reachable person with authority before refusal takes effect |
| Safety and access on the premises (lock-out, alarm, removal) | Highest | Often not; some too fast for any person | A reachable person where one can act in time; otherwise limits set in advance by the host and kept open to revision |
| Identity verification | Low as verification; high if failure blocks entry | Yes, if a staffed route exists | A staffed fallback; a reachable person if failure would refuse entry |
| Room assignment | Low to moderate; high where a guest's need makes a room unusable | Mostly | A later appeal by default; a reachable person where the room cannot be used |
| Pricing | Usually moderate; significant if a price bars access | Yes | A later appeal, and rules the host can explain |
| Complaints and service recovery | Varies with the failure | Partly | A reachable person with authority, at a time that suits the guest |
| Routine requests and information | Low | Yes | Automation, under rules set in advance |

Admission and refusal, and safety and access on the premises, head the table, because shelter for the night is at stake and some of these decisions cannot be undone in time; the regulators give the cancellation of a contract as an example of a legal effect, which would cover a booking (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 21). Routine requests and information sit at the foot and can be automated under rules set in advance. Identity checks, room allocation and pricing need a later appeal by default, and a reachable person where failure would refuse entry or leave a room unusable. The placements are our own reading of the sources cited, and no study makes them for hospitality. The order follows the duties to receive and to protect, which is why admission and safety come first.

The law sets a floor beneath this boundary rather than a ceiling. Under the General Data Protection Regulation, a person subject to a solely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects has 'at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision' (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 22(3)). The guidance on that article requires the reviewer to be someone 'who has the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision' (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). The right states no timing, and many hotel decisions may fall within the exception for decisions necessary to a contract (art. 22(2)(a)). The law therefore secures a later review, not a person at the threshold. The framework explains most where the guest is on site and the decision is high in stakes and hard to undo. It explains less where decisions are remote and reversible. It reaches its edge at the line between commercial and household hosting, where United States public accommodation law exempts an establishment with 'not more than five rooms for rent or hire' that is 'actually occupied by the proprietor of such establishment as his residence' (Civil Rights Act 1964, § 2000a(b)(1)). A short-term rental in a host's own home can fall on the household side of that line. There the role obligation we describe is at its weakest.

### Designing for interruptible hospitality

Researchers disagree about whether guests should see the technology at all. One study of luxury production found customers so uneasy about technology that it advised keeping its use hidden to protect a sense of authenticity (Mosca and La Rosa 2019), while more recent work backs transparency and human oversight for high-stakes systems (Mosca 2026). Concealment makes the system harder to see and leaves the unease where it was. Some of the conditions set out above can be delivered at the interface, and some only by the institution. Three principles belong to the interface: explain a decision where it lands, since guests prefer little disclosure at check-in and fuller explanation later in the stay (Pigac et al. 2026: 286); let guests adjust the settings that matter, because a path the system alone has chosen is not the guest's choice (Yeung 2017); and make significant rule changes visible (Hatherley 2025). These three improve the interaction, and none of them decides who is answerable within it.

The other two principles are institutional, and they secure accountability. The first is reachable authority to reverse: someone who can reverse or revise the decision must stay reachable, because a human who 'rubber-stamps without genuine judgment does not thereby become an overseer' (Baum and Laux 2026: 126). The deploying organization is best placed to reverse a decision (Lyons et al. 2021), algorithmic governance in services calls for routes of escalation and human oversight (Shabnam et al. 2026), and of five governance mechanisms only appeal raised the perceived legitimacy of an adverse algorithmic decision (Martin and Waldman 2023). What guests need is not a human presence as such but someone with the power to change the answer. The second is a real alternative: digital captivity is 'coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable' (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 976), an analogue route that is kept but downgraded is one of its triggers rather than a remedy (2026: 978), and letting consumers 'choose modes of access' is one way to prevent it (2026: 981). A guest who avoids such a system pays for doing so, since withdrawing from a service 'may carry a personal cost and loss' (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 979), and research on mandatory self-service treats the work such systems move onto the customer as labour (Kim et al. 2025).

The two institutional principles also show where interruptibility is owed. Smoothness has become a goal of phygital design (Batat 2024), and this journal has already traced the push for speed in hospitality technology to large corporations (Weaver 2026). A system built never to interrupt the guest can become one the guest cannot interrupt, since infrastructure that works sinks out of sight (Star and Ruhleder 1996), and frictionless design can remove the occasion for a claim along with the annoyance. By interruptibility we mean a point at which the guest can stop the process, ask, redirect or be treated as an exception, with a counterparty there to receive the interruption. Interaction designers have long argued for revealing a system's seams (Chalmers and Galani 2004), but a visible seam is of no use to a guest unless someone with authority stands behind it. Synchronous interruption is owed where a decision takes effect on the guest on site and cannot be undone in time, and a later appeal with authority to reverse serves the rest. Voice offered without such authority can mislead, since voice offered after a decision, with the decision unchanged, still raised judgements of fairness (Lind et al. 1990).

For organizations, the five principles line up as a roadmap of rising cost, which Table 2 sets out. A property can begin by auditing its touchpoints with the six questions and naming the decisions taken in them, then specify what guests and employees can understand, adjust and challenge, and then design the capabilities that support those things. The fourth stage constitutes accountability in Bovens' sense: a forum before which an identifiable actor with authority to change outcomes must explain and justify the organization's decisions, and which can question that actor and pass judgement (2007: 450, 452). In a hotel the forum is the place where a guest's claim can be lodged, and the actor is the manager, operator or brand that holds the host's role. The fifth stage keeps a real alternative for guests who decline the automated route, and Table 1 tells an organization where the fourth must work in real time and where a later appeal will do. A property that stops after better interfaces and better-trained staff may have automated its hospitality without deciding who answers for it.

**Table 2. Five stages from interface fixes to institutional answerability, in rising order of cost**

| Stage | What the organization does | Condition it secures | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Maps its touchpoints with the six questions and names the decisions taken in them | Common understanding | Attention |
| 2. Specify | Establishes what guests and employees can understand, adjust and challenge | Common understanding, predictability | Analysis |
| 3. Design | Builds the capabilities that support interpretation and adjustment | Predictability | Design and training |
| 4. Constitute | Establishes a forum before which an accountable actor with authority to change outcomes must answer, in real time where Table 1 requires it | Accountability | Governance and staffing |
| 5. Preserve | Keeps a real alternative for guests who decline the automated route | Accountability, through exit | Ongoing operational commitment |

## Conclusion

Hospitality studies' account of what a host owes remains the right place to begin an account of phygital hospitality. That account, however, pictures a single host who holds the obligation in person, and commercial hospitality has long divided the host's position among owners, operators and brands and now divides it among vendors as well. The obligation therefore has to be followed to its holder when the work of hosting is spread across systems and firms.

In this article we have argued that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation, imposed by law on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, taken up by holding out, and held alongside whoever controls the operation. Its work can be delegated and its duty cannot, so that algorithmic mediation leaves the obligation in place and takes away, where it takes anything, the guest's path to someone with authority to answer for it. The argument catches an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one, yet leaves the guest with no one to address. Service management asks whether an encounter delivers value to the customer, and algorithmic accountability asks who answers when a delegated system causes harm, finding that answerability stays with the deployer (Kong 2026: 5, 10) and that in chains of providers responsibility is partial and unequally distributed (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1193). The role account asks a third question, what the holder of a public calling owes the person it has received. The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act requires that the overseers of high-risk systems can stop them 'through a "stop" button' (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, art. 14(4)(e)), but that requirement is owed to overseers, not guests, and the Act lists no hospitality use as high-risk. The paper offers three things: first, an account of the host's obligation under delegation; second, an account of standing that separates holding it from exercising it; and third, a test, a typology of decisions and five stages for organizations. Human-centricity, on this reading, concerns what an arrangement protects or withholds, namely guest agency, employee discretion and relational judgement, and keeping humans visible in the encounter is neither necessary nor sufficient for any of the three.

This is a conceptual paper, and its cases illustrate its distinctions without testing them; four further limits follow from how it was built. First, its legal sources are English, United States federal and European Union law, with one decision of a United States federal appeals court, so the account of when the obligation comes into force and who holds it may not travel unchanged to other jurisdictions. Second, Table 1 is our own reading of sources that do not address hospitality directly, and it needs testing against the decisions properties take. Third, we have borrowed the structure of non-delegable duty from a judgment about schools and hospitals (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013), and whether courts would place commercial hosts in that category is a question for legal scholarship. Fourth, every case the argument uses comes from published studies and judgments, none of them gathered to answer our question.

The framework points to three studies, each starting from a gap we documented. The first would follow a guest's request or complaint from the person who receives it to the party with authority over the rule that produced it, and record whether it was heard and what changed. We found no such study in hotels, restaurants or bars, and the nearest, by Xiang et al. (2022), reports two cases. The second would examine unstaffed and app-only hotels and short-term rentals with self-check-in, asking whom a guest can reach when a decision goes against them; the studies of unstaffed hotels we found measure acceptance and satisfaction, and none asks who owes the guest anything. The third would map how management agreements, franchise agreements and platform terms divide duties to guests among owner, brand, operator, platform and vendor, testing our proposal that the party which holds itself out answers alongside the party in control.

Transformative service research asks how service systems bear on the well-being of individuals and of communities (Anderson and Ostrom 2015; Batat 2022). For the individual guest, a touchpoint that withholds any reachable authority turns a claim into a private cost, borne alone whatever the quality of the service around it. For communities the evidence suggests a range rather than a verdict, since the same digital mediation can enchant, disenchant or re-enchant a service encounter depending on the arrangements around it (De Vos et al. 2024), and whether the path from the guest to authority survives is one of those arrangements. Phygital arrangements bring real gains, in responsiveness and in how much a small team can offer many guests, and the framework is meant to help organizations keep those gains without giving up the relation that makes them worth having. The question for phygital hospitality is therefore not whether technology can do the jobs a host used to do, but where, in an arrangement that has scattered those jobs across guest, employee, brand, vendor and platform, the obligation of hosting still has an address, and whether the guest can reach it.
