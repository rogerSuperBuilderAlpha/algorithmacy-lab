## Title

**Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality**

## Abstract (100–200 words)

*175 words by whitespace count; verify against Word's counter before upload. Copied from DRAFT_v6.md.*

Hospitality firms now hand much of the work of hosting, from admission and pricing to check-in, room allocation and complaints, to algorithmic systems supplied by several firms at once. Each places the party that decides at a distance from the guest it affects. The article begins from hospitality studies' account of what a host owes and argues, with the law of innkeepers as evidence, that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation, imposed on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers and taken up by holding out. It then asks what becomes of that obligation when the work is handed on: the work can be delegated and the duty cannot, so the guest's standing survives, but its exercise depends on a reachable party with authority to change the decision. It offers a test of phygital touchpoints, scoped by the type of decision, and a roadmap for organizations. We argue that what algorithmic mediation removes is rarely the host's obligation and more often the guest's path to anyone with authority to answer for it.

## Statement of Contribution (100–150 words, anonymised; sent to reviewers)

*147 words of answers, counted. It names no author, institution or prior work of the contributors.*

**What is the contribution to hospitality knowledge, theory, policy or practice?** The paper develops a theory of the commercial host's obligation under algorithmic delegation. Drawing on hospitality studies, the law of innkeepers and the philosophy of directed duties, it argues that the obligation is a role obligation, taken up by holding out to travellers. The work it requires can be delegated; the duty cannot. The paper separates a guest's holding standing from exercising it, and shows that algorithmic mediation rarely removes the host's obligation; what it removes is the path from the guest to anyone with authority to answer for it. For practice, it offers a test of phygital touchpoints, scoped by type of decision, and a five-stage roadmap from interface fixes to institutional answerability.

**How does the paper offer a multidisciplinary social science perspective?** It reads hospitality studies alongside anthropology, the law of innkeepers, moral philosophy, and research on accountability, algorithmic accountability and algorithmic management, asking of each what a host owes the guest it has received.
