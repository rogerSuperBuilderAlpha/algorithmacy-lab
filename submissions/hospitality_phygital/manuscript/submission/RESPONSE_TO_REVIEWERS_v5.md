# Response to reviewers

**Who Hosts the Guest? Algorithmic Mediation, Standing, and Responsibility in Phygital Hospitality**

We thank the three reviewers for careful and demanding reports. Reviewer 2's request for a theory of role obligation, and Reviewer 3's challenge to our assumptions about hosting and authority, changed the paper's foundation rather than its surface. We have rebuilt the argument from that foundation. The revised manuscript opens with a new account of the host's obligation (section 2), defines guest standing without circularity (section 3), adds a section on boundary conditions (section 6) and states the contributions, limitations and research agenda afresh (sections 8 to 10). Below, each comment is quoted, followed by our response and the location of the change.

## Summary of the main changes

1. **A theory of the host's obligation (new section 2).** The obligation is treated as a role obligation, which the law imposes on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers and which a party takes up by holding itself out. The section sets out what the obligation contains, when it comes into force, who holds it when several firms share the operation, what can and cannot be delegated, and what is distinctive about hospitality. It draws on the law of innkeepers and public accommodation, the anthropology of hospitality and the philosophy of directed duties.
2. **Standing defined without circularity (section 3.1).** Standing is now defined from the host's directed duties, and accountability is defined last. We separate holding standing from exercising it.
3. **Hosting beyond the reception desk (sections 1, 4 and 5).** We now treat several technologies and formats, including short-term rentals and restaurants, and show that frontline authority in hotels was graded and organizational before any algorithm arrived.
4. **Boundary conditions (new section 6 and Table 3).** We set out which hospitality decisions require a reachable party with authority at the moment of decision, and which can wait for a later appeal.
5. **Style.** We removed the opening anecdote and every passage Reviewer 3 identified. We fixed the vocabulary, stated the research question once and answered it in the conclusion.

## Reviewer 2

**R2.1** — *"I believe the paper needs to explain more clearly where the host's obligation comes from. This is key to the argument, especially in Figure 1. The manuscript argues that hosting functions can move to algorithms while the obligation to the guest remains with the host, but it does not fully explain why that obligation remains, who actually holds it, or what makes it non-transferable. In commercial hospitality, this becomes especially important because responsibility may sit with the hotel company, operator, employee, platform, vendor, or several actors at once."*

We agree that this was the paper's central weakness. The new section 2 answers each of these questions in turn.

- **Where the obligation comes from (section 2.1).** Hospitality studies treat the obligation as fulfilled through hosts' motives. The law treats it as a duty of a role, "'fixed by law rather than by contract' (2015: 501)" in Maese's phrase. We adopt the role account.
- **When it comes into force and who holds it (section 2.3).** "The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller who can pay and is fit to be received presents themselves." The duty is held by the proprietor, who answers for those who do the work.
- **Several actors (section 2.3).** Drawing on the law's treatment of franchised hotels and of parties that only arrange a service, we propose: "The party that holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside the party that controls the operation, while a party that only arranges a service, such as a technology vendor or a pure intermediary, answers to the host rather than to the guest." We state that this allocation is our proposal, not a holding of any court.
- **Why it remains and what makes it non-transferable (section 2.4).** "The work of hosting can be delegated to staff, contractors or systems, but the duty, and the answering for it, stay with the holder."

On Figure 1: [AUTHORS DECIDE: Figure 1 does not appear in our markdown copy of the submitted manuscript. Check the submitted Word file. If it contained a figure of the chain, either revise it to show the allocation in section 2.3 (holds out / controls / arranges) or remove it, and say which here.]

**R2.2** — *"I suggest developing a clearer theory of role obligation in commercial hospitality, concerning what activates the obligation, who initially bears it, what can and cannot be delegated, and how responsibility is shared when multiple actors control the guest experience. Without this foundation, the argument risks looking like a more familiar problem of organizational delegation or algorithmic accountability rather than a specific hospitality theory."*

Thank you for this suggestion, which we have followed point for point.

- **What activates the obligation, and who first bears it:** section 2.3.
- **What can and cannot be delegated:** section 2.4, which borrows the structure of non-delegable duty. "we borrow its structure rather than claiming that the law already places hotels in its category."
- **How responsibility is shared:** section 2.3.

On the relation to organizational delegation and algorithmic accountability, section 2.4 now distinguishes our account from the closest argument in the literature. *Changed in section 2.4, second paragraph:* "We agree with the conclusion, but our duty has a different shape. It is not a responsibility for harm that the delegated system might cause; it is a first-order duty owed to a particular person, the guest, from the moment of reception onwards, whether or not anything goes wrong."

**R2.3** — *"At the moment, standing seems to mean that someone is obligated to answer the guest, while accountability also means that someone must answer a guest who has standing. This creates some circularity. It would also help to distinguish between a guest having standing and being able to exercise it."*

We agree on both points, and we traced the circularity to its source. It arises when standing is taken from Darwall's account of directed obligation, which builds accountability into the obligation, and accountability is then defined as answering someone with standing. Section 3.1 now defines standing from the host's directed duties, following Thompson (2004: 335), and defines the remaining terms in order, with accountability last.

*Changed in section 3.1:* "We therefore define the guest's standing as the guest's position as the one to whom the host's directed duties are owed. Standing in this sense is held whether or not it is claimed." And: "Under algorithmic mediation, the guest still holds standing, because the host's obligation persists. What fails is its exercise, because the accountability arrangement offers no one with the authority to receive the guest's claim."

**R2.4** — *"I urge the authors to show more clearly why hospitality itself matters to the mechanism. Many of the problems discussed here … also appear in banking, healthcare, e-commerce, and other service settings."*

We accept the premise. The mechanism is general, and the revised paper says so; we then identify what is distinctive about hospitality. [AUTHORS DECIDE: this concedes the mechanism. Confirm you are content to concede it.]

*Changed in section 2.5:* "The mechanism we have described is general, and hospitality cannot claim it as its own." The section sets the host's duties beside comparable duties of banks, service providers and hospitals, and identifies three distinctive features: the duty to receive all comers survived among the common callings only for innkeepers and carriers; what is received is a stranger's person and belongings, overnight, in another's house; and once the guest is received, the duty is fixed by status rather than contract. "The failure we describe below therefore bites hardest where these three meet, at admission and during the stay".

**R2.5** — *"The paper should also clarify the boundary conditions of the framework. In particular, which types of hospitality decisions require immediate interruptibility and answerability, and in which situations are these less important? It would also help to specify which hospitality formats … the framework explains best"*

We have added a section on boundary conditions, with a table that places common hospitality decisions by stakes, reversibility and timing.

*Changed in section 6 (new) and Table 3:* "Where a decision takes effect on the guest's person, access or contract during the stay, and cannot be undone before the harm lands, the guest needs a reachable party with authority before the decision takes effect. Where a decision can be deferred or undone, a reachable appeal is enough." On formats: "The framework therefore explains most where the guest is on site and the decision is high in stakes and hard to reverse, and less where decisions are remote and reversible." The section also identifies where the framework reaches its edge, at the statutory line between commercial and household hosting.
