# Response to reviewers

**Who hosts the guest? Algorithmic mediation, standing and responsibility in phygital hospitality**

We thank the three reviewers for careful and demanding reports. Reviewer 2's request for a theory of role obligation, and Reviewer 3's challenge to our assumptions about hosting and authority, changed the paper's foundation rather than its surface, and Reviewer 3's comments on style and structure led us to rebuild the paper on the plan of the conceptual articles this journal publishes.

## Summary of the main changes

1. **The argument now starts inside hospitality studies.** The first main section, 'The host's power and the host's obligation', sets out what hospitality scholars and this journal have said about the host's power over the guest and the host's duty to protect, and the debate about whether a commercial host is a host at all. It then argues that the obligation is a role obligation, with the law of innkeepers as evidence for its content, the moment at which it comes into force and its holder.
2. **A theory of delegation and standing.** The second section, 'Delegation and standing: Beyond the host in person?', names the several-firm host in this journal's terms (the assembled host; micro-hosts and macro-hosts), proposes which of its members answers to the guest, explains why the duty cannot be delegated although the work can, and defines standing without circularity, separating holding it from exercising it.
3. **One application section, across formats.** 'Assembled hosts at the touchpoint' defines the authority–answerability split, shows it in hotels, short-term rentals and restaurants, gives the test with its purpose, sets out which decisions need a reachable person at the moment of decision (Table 1), and closes on design principles and the roadmap (Table 2).
4. **One conclusion.** It states what we have argued, where the argument sits among neighbouring fields, four limits, three studies, and what follows for well-being and for organizations.
5. **Style.** The opening anecdote and every passage Reviewer 3 identified are gone; the vocabulary is fixed and defined at first use; the research question is stated in the introduction and answered in the conclusion. Five sections replace ten, and two tables replace four.

**R2.1** — *"I believe the paper needs to explain more clearly where the host's obligation comes from. We agree that this was the paper's central weakness, and the first two main sections now answer each question.

- **Where the obligation comes from.** Hospitality scholars locate the obligation in the host's motive; the law attaches it to a role. *Changed in 'The host's power and the host's obligation', fourth and fifth paragraphs:* "We argue instead that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation: it has a content, a moment at which it comes into force, and a holder, and the law of innkeepers shows each of them." - **When it comes into force, and who first holds it.** *Changed in the same section, eighth paragraph:* "The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller presents themselves." The statute attaches the duties "'to the proprietor of such an hotel' and to no other person (s. 1(1))".
- **Several actors.** *Changed in 'Delegation and standing', third paragraph:* "We propose that the party which holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside the party that controls the operation, and that a party which only arranges a service, such as a technology vendor, answers to the host; no court has held this."
- **Why it remains, and what makes it non-transferable.** *Changed in 'Delegation and standing', fourth paragraph:* "The work can be delegated, and the duty cannot." The paragraph sets out the structure of non-delegable duty in English law and its limits for our purpose.

**R2.3** — *"At the moment, standing seems to mean that someone is obligated to answer the guest, while accountability also means that someone must answer a guest who has standing. And: "Under algorithmic mediation, then, the guest still holds standing, because the host's obligation persists, and what can fail is its exercise."

**R2.5** — *"The paper should also clarify the boundary conditions of the framework. *Changed in 'When a person must be reachable', first paragraph, and Table 1:* "Where a decision takes effect on the guest's person, access or contract during the stay and cannot be undone before the harm lands, the guest needs a reachable party with authority before it takes effect." On formats, *final paragraph:* "The framework explains most where the guest is on site and the decision is high in stakes and hard to undo. It explains less where decisions are remote and reversible." The same paragraph locates the framework's edge at the line between commercial and household hosting.
