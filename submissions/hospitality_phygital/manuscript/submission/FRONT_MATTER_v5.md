## Abstract (100–200 words)

*155 words by whitespace count; verify against Word's counter before upload. Copied from DRAFT_v5.md.*

Hospitality firms increasingly hand the work of hosting, from admission and pricing to check-in, room allocation and complaints, to algorithmic systems supplied and configured by several firms at once. This paper asks where the host's obligation to the guest goes when that work moves. Drawing on hospitality studies, the law of innkeepers and the philosophy of directed duties, we argue that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation: the law imposes it on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, and holding out to the public is how a party takes that role up. The work of hosting can be delegated; the duty, and the answering for it, cannot. The guest's standing therefore survives delegation, but its exercise depends on a reachable party with authority to change the decision. We offer a test, scoped by type of decision, for when a phygital touchpoint preserves or removes that condition, and a roadmap for organizations.

## Statement of Contribution (100–150 words, anonymised; sent to reviewers)

*145 words of answers. It names no author, institution or prior work of the contributors, and uses only constructs that v5 itself names.*

**What is the contribution to hospitality knowledge, theory, policy or practice?** The paper develops a theory of the commercial host's obligation under algorithmic delegation. Drawing on the law of innkeepers and the philosophy of directed duties, it argues that the obligation is a role obligation, taken up by holding out to travellers. The work it requires can be delegated; the duty cannot. The paper separates a guest's holding standing from exercising it. It shows that algorithmic mediation rarely removes the host's obligation; what it removes is the path from the guest to anyone with authority to answer for it. For practice, it offers a test of phygital touchpoints, scoped by type of decision, and a five-stage roadmap from interface fixes to institutional answerability.

**How does the paper offer a multidisciplinary social science perspective?** It reads hospitality studies alongside anthropology, the law of innkeepers, moral philosophy, and research on accountability, algorithmic accountability and algorithmic management, asking of each what a host owes the guest it has received.
