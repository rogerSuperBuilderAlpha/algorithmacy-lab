# Who Hosts the Guest? Algorithmic Mediation, Standing, and Responsibility in Phygital Hospitality

## Abstract

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Hospitality firms increasingly hand the work of hosting, from admission and pricing to check-in, room allocation and complaints, to algorithmic systems supplied and configured by several firms at once. This paper asks where the host's obligation to the guest goes when that work moves. Drawing on hospitality studies, the law of innkeepers and the philosophy of directed duties, we argue that the commercial host's obligation is a role obligation: the law imposes it on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers, and holding out to the public is how a party takes that role up. The work of hosting can be delegated; the duty, and the answering for it, cannot. The guest's standing therefore survives delegation, but its exercise depends on a reachable party with authority to change the decision. We offer a test, scoped by type of decision, for when a phygital touchpoint preserves or removes that condition, and a roadmap for organizations.

**Keywords:** phygital hospitality; algorithmic mediation; host obligation; guest standing; accountability; interruptibility.

## 1. Introduction

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Phygital hospitality now distributes the work of hosting across systems and firms that the guest rarely sees. Revenue-management systems recommend the price a guest is quoted, and in hotel data those recommendations have been found to be pitched away from the system's own best estimate in order to move managers who reprice too rarely (Garcia et al. 2026). Facial-recognition systems verify identity at check-in (Xu et al. 2021). Service robots greet, direct and serve, and when they fail, guests lay the responsibility on the firm (Belanche et al. 2020). In short-term rentals, platforms split the host's role into primary hosts, co-hosts and listing administrators, and self-check-in replaces the handover of keys (Bosma 2022: 601, 605). Behind each of these sits a chain of vendors, in which there is 'typically no one actor in overall control' (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1192). These are different technologies, doing different jobs in hotels, restaurants and rentals, and we do not treat them as one thing. What they share is that each takes over some part of what a host used to do in person, and each places the party who decides at some distance from the guest who is affected.

<!-- v4, edited: first two sentences reworded to drop the "we talk" framing and the Bitner et al. citation (R3); "AI" replaced by "algorithmic systems" (fixed vocabulary); final sentences rewritten to state the gap. -->
Most writing on phygital hospitality presents these systems as tools for a smoother, more personal, more guest-centred experience (Batat 2024, 2026; Brochado et al. 2026). The literature describes them by what they do: as tools, mediators, facilitators and co-creators of the guest's experience (van Doorn et al. 2017; Li et al. 2021; Xing and Zhang 2026). None of these descriptions says what, if anything, is owed to the guest by the party that deploys it. Algorithmic-accountability research supplies part of that answer, because it holds that answerability for a delegated system stays with the party that delegated (Nissenbaum 1996; Kong 2026: 5). It indexes that answerability, however, to harm and malfunction. Hospitality research, for its part, has developed a rich account of hospitableness, but it measures hospitableness as a motive or disposition of individual hosts (Lashley 2015: 1; Liu et al. 2026: 2). Hospitality scholars have also asked what happens when platforms push the human host out of view, and the most recent of these studies describes 'guests without hosts' (Germann Molz 2026). Between these literatures lies an unasked question: where the commercial host's obligation to the guest goes when the work of hosting is delegated to algorithmic systems spread across several firms.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The question needs a vocabulary held steady, because the literature moves freely between artificial intelligence, technology, automation and algorithms. By an algorithmic system we mean software that takes or shapes a decision about a guest. By a phygital arrangement we mean the guest, the staff, the property, the firms and the systems together, considered as a single set-up in which decisions, information and power are distributed. By a touchpoint we mean any moment at which the guest meets that arrangement. Whether a machine could itself carry a host's duties is a question we set aside. A robot can occupy a role without being a moral agent (Kropf et al. 2026: 7), but our question concerns the parties who deploy such systems and the guests who meet them. Our research question is therefore where the obligation of hosting goes when phygital arrangements delegate the functions of hosting to algorithmic systems, and what an arrangement must preserve for the guest to remain a guest.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Our answer has three parts. First, we develop a theory of the commercial host's obligation under delegation. The obligation is a role obligation, imposed by law on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers; it comes into force when a house holds itself out and a traveller is received; its work can be delegated, but the duty and the answering for it stay with the holder. Second, we define guest standing in a way that does not depend on accountability, which allows us to separate a guest's holding standing from being able to exercise it. Third, we derive a test, scoped by the type of decision, for whether a touchpoint preserves the conditions under which standing can be exercised, and a roadmap that orders what organizations must do to keep them. We argue that algorithmic mediation rarely removes the host's obligation; what it removes is the path from the guest to anyone with the authority to answer for it.

## 2. The host's obligation

### 2.1 Two accounts of the host's obligation

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Hospitality studies know the host's obligation, but they locate its fulfilment in the motives of persons. Lashley observes that hospitality has been 'in all human settings, a moral obligation until the advent of mass travel and commercialism' (2015: 3). Blain and Lashley, extending a hosting metaphor to service relations, hold that 'the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome' (2014: 1). They then contrast an obligation that is 'externally imposed' with hospitableness proper, which it 'may be at odds' with, and they propose to secure the welcome by recruiting people with hospitable motives (Blain and Lashley 2014: 2). The instruments that follow measure 'individuals who are driven by a personal desire to be hospitable to others' (Lashley 2015: 1), and recent work defines hospitableness as 'a host's disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity' (Liu et al. 2026: 2). We found no hospitableness study that assigns the host's duty to an organization. A motive belongs to a person. It cannot be delegated, and when a system replaces the person, it does not travel to the system or to anyone else. An account of the host's obligation as motive cannot therefore say where the obligation goes when hosting is delegated, which is the question this paper must answer.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The law offers a different account, in which the obligation attaches to a role. Under the common law, those who exercised a common calling were bound to serve all who came, and the duty to receive all comers came to attach 'only to common carriers by land and water (including ferrymen) and innkeepers' (Burdick 1911: 523); the shopkeeper, by contrast, remained free to refuse (Wyman 1904: 159). The innkeeper's duties were, as Maese puts it, 'fixed by law rather than by contract' (2015: 501), and in Bogen's reading of the English cases it was 'English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment' that imposed them (1996: 75). On this account the obligation does not depend upon the host's motive, and it does not lapse when the host's motive fails. It belongs to whoever occupies the role. We adopt the role account, and we keep hospitableness for what the hospitality literature describes so well: the quality with which a role is performed, which is not the same thing as the source of the duty.

### 2.2 What the obligation contains

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The host's obligation is a bundle of duties rather than a single one, and each sits in a social and organizational setting that conditions it. The historical duty of the innkeeper was to provide 'bed, board, and hearth' (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 63): to receive the traveller, to feed and shelter them, and to keep their belongings safe. The innkeeper's strict liability extended to the guest's goods 'and not his person' (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33b), although later accounts report liability for harm to the guest as well (Bogen 1996: 82 n. 160; Maese 2015: 499). The anthropology of hospitality adds a duty of attention. In Pitt-Rivers' account, the host infringes the law of hospitality if he fails to protect his guest or to show concern for the guest's 'needs and wishes' (2012: 516). Hospitality studies, finally, add the conditions of the commercial setting, in which hospitality is offered at a price and within the rules, standards and scripts of an organization (Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1295). The law of innkeepers governed reception, sustenance and safety, not the manner of the welcome, and none of these sources makes a negotiated exception something owed to the guest; the exceptions the law recognized run in the host's favour (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33a). The welcome is therefore not a single act. It is a set of duties to receive, to sustain, to protect and to attend, held by a role and exercised within an organization.

### 2.3 When the obligation comes into force, and who holds it

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The obligation comes into force when a house holds itself out to travellers and a traveller who can pay and is fit to be received presents themselves. The Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 defines the hotel to which the innkeeper's duties apply as an establishment 'held out by the proprietor' as offering food, drink and, if so required, sleeping accommodation to 'any traveller presenting himself who appears able and willing to pay a reasonable sum for the services and facilities provided and who is in a fit state to be received' (s. 1(3)). Arrival alone has never been enough. A neighbour 'who is no traveller' has no claim (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 32b), and a full house excused refusal (Lane v. Cotton 1701: 485, Holt CJ dissenting). The duty is held by the proprietor: the innkeeper's duties 'attach to the proprietor of such an hotel and shall not attach to any other person' (Hotel Proprietors Act 1956, s. 1(1)), and the proprietor answers for 'a default in him or his servants' (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33a). The legal history does not settle why innkeepers were bound in this way. Bogen calls the explanation from holding out 'not so much a theory as a tautology' (1996: 52), and the most persuasive account of the duty's rationale lies elsewhere, in the exposure of travellers 'away from their home communities and in unfamiliar surroundings' (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63). The law therefore tells us when the obligation comes into force and who holds it, but not why it exists.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Commercial hospitality has long divided the holder's position among several firms, and the law has had to say which of them answers to the guest. In Crinkley v. Holiday Inns, the owners of a franchised motel, the company that ran it day to day and the franchisor whose brand it carried were all held liable to guests assaulted in their room. The franchisor's liability was vicarious, reached through apparent agency: the jury could find that the brand had presented the property as its own and that the guests had relied upon that presentation, although the appeals court itself described the evidence of reliance as 'marginal' (Crinkley v. Holiday Inns 1988: paras 7, 9, 49–52). We read this single decision, under North Carolina law, as an illustration rather than a rule. A second line comes from English law. A party whose 'own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance' is not liable for how it is performed (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 25(3)). Phygital arrangements multiply such arrangers, chiefly the vendors that supply the systems which price, verify and allocate. We propose that the commercial host's obligation is held by whoever takes up the role of receiving the guest, and that holding out is how a party takes it up. The party that holds itself out as host answers to the guest, alongside the party that controls the operation, while a party that only arranges a service, such as a technology vendor or a pure intermediary, answers to the host rather than to the guest. This allocation is our proposal, not a holding of any court.

### 2.4 What can and cannot be delegated

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The work of hosting can be delegated to staff, contractors or systems, but the duty, and the answering for it, stay with the holder. The law states this structure plainly. In Woodland, the UK Supreme Court held that the work required to perform certain duties 'may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant's' (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 7). Such a duty arises from 'an antecedent relationship' and is 'a positive or affirmative duty to protect' (para 7). Morgan states the principle in a single line: 'No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be' (2015: 7). Woodland concerns schools and hospitals, not hotels, and we borrow its structure rather than claiming that the law already places hotels in its category. The court noted, however, that the responsibilities of fee-paying providers are already non-delegable 'because they are contractual', with contracting out of them 'limited by legislation' (para 25(5)), and the paying guest stands in that position. Delegation is nothing new to hospitality: the innkeeper of 1584 already answered for the defaults of his servants (Calye's Case 1584: fol. 33a). What algorithmic delegation changes is the performer. The servant who erred could be found and named; the system that errs may be one that no guest can address, supplied by a firm that no guest can reach.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
This account differs from the familiar argument that deploying an autonomous system does not diminish the deployer's responsibility. Kong grounds that retained responsibility in the deployer's control over the act of delegating, and concludes that 'one who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it' (2026: 4–5, 10). We agree with the conclusion, but our duty has a different shape. It is not a responsibility for harm that the delegated system might cause; it is a first-order duty owed to a particular person, the guest, from the moment of reception onwards, whether or not anything goes wrong. It rests, in Woodland's terms, not on control over the environment but on a relation of charge over the person received (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 24). Kropf et al. reach a compatible position from the ethics of care: a robot can occupy a narrow role without accountability, its answerability 'is currently inconceivable', and blame stays with the humans who deploy it (2026: 7, 9). The role of hosting can therefore be performed by a machine, but the duty to the guest, and the answering for its performance, remain with the party that took up the role.

### 2.5 What is distinctive about hospitality

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The mechanism we have described is general, and hospitality cannot claim it as its own. Banks may not discriminate when consumers apply for or access a payment account (Payment Accounts Regulations 2015, reg. 18(1)); every service provider in Great Britain must not discriminate by refusing a person the service (Equality Act 2010, s. 29(1)); hospitals that participate in Medicare must screen anyone who comes to the emergency department and asks (Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act 1986, § 1395dd(a)). Inaccessible decision-makers, weak appeals and costly exit trouble banking and e-commerce as much as hospitality. What remains distinctive of hospitality is narrower and more specific. The duty to receive all comers survived, among the common callings, only for innkeepers and carriers (Burdick 1911: 523). What the host receives is the stranger's person and belongings, overnight, in a house that is not the stranger's own, so that the duty is owed to someone 'away from their home communities and in unfamiliar surroundings' (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 62–63) for the length of a stay, where a hospital's statutory duty begins with an emergency and ends at stabilization. And once the guest is received, the duty is fixed by status rather than contract (Maese 2015: 501). The failure we describe below therefore bites hardest where these three meet, at admission and during the stay, and we turn this into boundary conditions in section 6.

## 3. Guest standing

### 3.1 Standing held and standing exercised

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
If the host's obligation persists under delegation, so does the guest's standing, provided standing is defined from the obligation and not from accountability. Darwall defines an obligation owed to someone through the obligee's authority to make demands of the obligor and to hold the obligor personally accountable (2013: 36). Taking standing from that account and then defining accountability as answering someone with standing makes each term depend on the other. Thompson offers a way out. His forms of directed duty, 'X has a duty to Y to do A' and 'X has a right against Y' (2004: 335), fix who owes what to whom without mentioning answering, and he contrasts them with 'merely monadic' duties, such as a duty simply to do something, which have no particular claimant (2004: 338). We therefore define the guest's standing as the guest's position as the one to whom the host's directed duties are owed. Standing in this sense is held whether or not it is claimed. As Darwall himself concedes, 'an obligee or right holder need not actually claim her right or demand compliance with it in order for the right to exist' (2013: 35). Exercising standing is something else: the guest's pressing of that claim, by asking, objecting or requesting an exception, to a party able to receive it. The law draws the same line, asking 'who has the right?' separately from 'who can enforce a right?' (Liau 2023: 1) [CHECK: Liau read in the introduction only; eprint page].

<!-- v4, edited: first and last sentences rewritten so that the four terms are defined in order from the obligation, and the baseline "these four sit in one person" removed (R2-3, R3). -->
We define four further terms in the same order. Obligation is what the host owes the guest: the directed duties of section 2.2. Responsibility is the holder's retained duty to see that the obligation is performed, by whoever does the work (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013: para 7; Morgan 2015: 7–8). Authority is the power, assigned by the organization, to change a decision or the rule behind it; we stipulate this sense, because the word carries technical meanings elsewhere that we do not want here. Accountability, or answerability, is the arrangement through which the holder answers to the guest for how the obligation was performed: in Bovens' terms, a relation in which an actor must explain and justify its conduct to a forum that can pose questions and pass judgement (2007: 450, 452). Defined in this order, standing does not refer to accountability, and accountability is the last term rather than the first. The diagnosis this paper offers can then be stated without circularity. Under algorithmic mediation, the guest still holds standing, because the host's obligation persists. What fails is its exercise, because the accountability arrangement offers no one with the authority to receive the guest's claim.

### 3.2 What guests owe

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Hospitality has always run in two directions, and the guest has duties as well as claims. Pitt-Rivers states the classical rule with precision: 'while a host has rights and obligations in regard to his guest, the guest has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host' (2012: 514). The guest infringes that rule by insulting or rivalling the host, by 'making himself at home' and by making 'claims or demands' (2012: 515), and any infringement 'entitles the person slighted … to relinquish his role' (2012: 517). Guests, in Lashley's summary, 'are to be protected by hosts but at the same time, expected not to overstay their welcome, misbehave, or endanger the hosts' (2015: 2). In commercial hospitality the guest's duties narrow to payment and reasonable conduct; one widely cited formulation holds that the commercial guest's 'only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably' (King 1995, as quoted in Golubovskaya et al. 2017: 1286) [CHECK King 1995]. The law of innkeepers matches this. The traveller must appear 'able and willing to pay a reasonable sum' and be 'in a fit state to be received' (Hotel Proprietors Act 1956, s. 1(3)), and hotels remove guests who will not comply with their rules after repeated requests (Xiang et al. 2022). The guest's side of the relation is therefore stated, and conditional; what the field lacks is an instrument for it, since the validated scale of experienced hospitality dropped its items on choice and independence (Pijls et al. 2017).

### 3.3 Where the power to press a claim comes from

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The guest's power to press a claim does not come from hospitality's own norms, which tend to withhold it. In Pitt-Rivers' law of hospitality, a dissatisfied guest 'has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out' (2012: 513). The same restraint appears in the commercial home. In Di Domenico and Lynch's study, hosts held full authority to set rules, admit and eject, and a guest staying with them still withheld a complaint, noting that 'in a hotel one might complain about the beds being uncomfortable, but as the guest in the home one does not, it's not the done thing' (2007: 333). The evidence is one researcher's note, but it suggests that a reachable party with authority is necessary for a guest to exercise standing, but not sufficient. A claim must also be proper to press, and in commercial hospitality what makes it proper is the commercial and legal relation. Commercial hosts 'must meet the desires and demands of the paying guest' (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 335), and the traveller who was refused could sue for damages (Sandoval-Strausz 2005: 64). It also follows that the conditions for exercising standing are supplied by institutions, not by guests. Users build working theories of the systems they meet (Eslami et al. 2016), but understanding a platform does not let them move it (Lin 2025), and knowing how to appeal is not being answered (Vaccaro et al. 2020). Competence is learned; the occasion and the addressee for a claim are provided, or withheld, by the institution that holds the host's role.

### 3.4 Negotiation as the exercise of standing

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Negotiation between host and guest is where standing is exercised, and the hospitality literature shows it changing outcomes. During the pandemic, a hotel guest who arrived without a test report was received after a front-office employee checked 'with hotel management', and the hotel paid for the test; a guest refused at the desk took his complaint the next day to 'the general manager and the group head office' (Xiang et al. 2022). Requests also shape what hospitality becomes. In Lugosi's study of a bar, regulars' requests produced house cocktails that disappeared when the requests stopped, and managers' allowance 'only created opportunities' that patrons then used (2009). Even the classical law of hospitality knows a bounded form of the guest's protest: Pitt-Rivers' diplomatic guest turned his plate over to register an objection 'without showing any discourtesy to his host' (2012: 513–514). In each case a request or complaint succeeds when it reaches someone with the authority to grant it, and it creates value for the guest when it does. What the guest can expect from the host's obligation is therefore a hearing by someone with authority, not the exception itself. The next section shows how phygital arrangements change the path by which a claim reaches that authority.

## 4. The authority–answerability split

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
By the authority–answerability split we mean an arrangement in which the party that can change a decision or its rule is not a party the guest can reach, so that the guest's claim cannot travel from the person who receives it to the person who could grant it. The split did not begin with algorithms. Authority in commercial hospitality has always been graded and organizational. Studies of hotel service recovery find it 'carefully stage-managed and "staircased"', with 'empowerment strongly related to hierarchical level' (Hewagama et al. 2019) [CHECK full text], and in five-star hotels empowerment amounted to 'increased employee responsibility rather than greater choice over how work was done or more voice in organizational decisions' (Hales and Klidas 1998) [CHECK full text]. The property at the centre of Crinkley was shared, decades before any algorithm, among owners, an operating company in day-to-day control and a franchisor (Crinkley v. Holiday Inns 1988: para 7). The image of a host in whom obligation, responsibility, authority and answerability all sit together fits the owner of a small commercial home (Di Domenico and Lynch 2007: 332–335), not the staffed hotel. What phygital arrangements change is therefore not a complete frontline authority that once existed. It is the path from the guest to graded authority, which an algorithmic system can lengthen, obscure or remove.

<!-- v4, edited: opening clause rewritten to tie the chain to the split just defined; final sentence kept. -->
The chain along which a claim travels makes the change visible. Commercial hospitality already bends the line between host and guest, slipping the company and its employee between the guest and whoever sets the terms, so the person facing the guest is acting for an institution whose rules they did not write. Phygital hospitality bends it again, dropping an algorithmic system in between. Now the guest's claim has to travel down a chain, guest to employee to system to whoever configured the system, and at each link the person in front of them holds a little less of the authority their claim is really aimed at. Physical moments are constantly turned into digital records, and digital rulings are constantly turned back into physical facts, a key card that works or does not, a price that appears on the screen at the desk. The frameworks call this movement fluidity (Batat 2024). It is just as accurate to call it a relay, and a relay raises a management question that the word fluidity hides: who holds the authority, and who keeps the room to use judgement, at each handoff. The problem is not the length of the chain. It is a break in it: the point where the decision gets made can come apart from any point where the guest can be answered.

<!-- v4, edited: shortened; "AI"/"algorithm" made consistent with the fixed vocabulary; vendor clause and Cobbe et al. added; Spektor et al. 2025 retained from v4. -->
Within the arrangement as a whole, we divide agency into three things, because they need not travel together: knowledge, discretion and authority. Knowledge is about who is placed to understand what is happening. It drifts towards the system, because the system can hold the guest's history, preferences, spending and loyalty status at the same time as the property's inventory and constraints (Rahman 2021; Calo and Rosenblat 2017). Discretion is about who can depart from the prescribed answer. It splits: staff keep some freedom to act, but more and more of their day goes to reading decisions made elsewhere and working around categories set elsewhere (Pedersen and Pors 2023; Spektor et al. 2025). Authority is about who can change the rule itself, or answer for it. It drifts towards whoever designs, configures or controls the rules (Scott and Orlikowski 2012), and in algorithmic supply chains that is often a vendor which keeps the right to 'change, withdraw, or cancel products and services at will' (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1195). Discretion acts on the single case; authority acts on the rule. A guest can meet someone with the first power and never reach anyone with the second, and the five-star employees who gained responsibility without choice or voice (Hales and Klidas 1998) show the two powers coming apart even before an algorithm arrives.

<!-- v4, edited: merged from v4 §4 (craft) and §6 (translation); "sitting ducks" and second-person address removed (R3). -->
The employee caught in the split does work that is easy to miss. Skilled front-desk employees often manufacture a guest's sense of control through small acts of discretion, appearing to defer while steering the options and heading off trouble (Bendoly 2013). Under algorithmic direction that work becomes translation, and it runs both ways: hotel managers describe carrying the system's decisions to the people they affect (Jianu et al. 2025), and the employee at the desk carries the guest's request the other way, into terms the system will accept. The translation is fragile. When a system quietly revises its rules or categories, the knowledge it rests upon depreciates overnight, and no one tells the employee or the guest that it has happened (Hatherley 2025). Staff are still asked to produce the guest's sense of control while no longer holding the controls that would produce it (Fuller and Smith 1991; Möhlmann et al. 2021), and employees fall silent when speaking up looks futile (Morrison 2014). Frontline hospitality employees aware of artificial intelligence in their jobs report more silence (Cheng et al. 2025), and platform workers rarely get the chance to contest the decisions that govern them (Duggan et al. 2026: 1448). Designing the employee out of the point where the guest's request meets the system removes the translator along with the task.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The split takes different shapes across hospitality formats, which is why a theory built only on the hotel front desk would not hold. In short-term rentals the platform divides the host's role into primary hosts, co-hosts and listing administrators (Bosma 2022: 601), and self-check-in can turn hosting into what Bosma calls ghosting, with no one present at the point of arrival (2022: 605). Hosts themselves resist the hotel role, insisting 'I am not a butler though nor am I a receptionist in a hotel' (Kaniadakis and Farmaki 2022: 14), while the platform passes the guest-facing duties of hosting to them (Germann Molz 2018: 243–245). In restaurants, by contrast, technology can be added to an encounter while staff go on hosting it (Batat 2021). Across these formats, the question is the same: whether a guest's claim can still reach someone with authority over the rule that produced the outcome.

## 5. Three conditions and a test

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The test we propose has a narrow purpose. It is meant to tell, at a given touchpoint, whether the conditions under which a guest can exercise standing survive the delegation of hosting work to an algorithmic system. Researchers can use it to analyse an arrangement, and managers can use it to audit one before or after deployment. It does not measure satisfaction, and it does not measure harm; a touchpoint can satisfy guests and harm no one while failing the test, because what the test examines is whether a claim the guest might make still has somewhere to go. The test rests on three conditions. Their names come from the study of coordination in organizations, which identifies 'three integrating conditions for coordination: accountability, predictability, and common understanding' (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009: 483). Their content here comes from the host's duties and the guest's standing. In Okhuysen and Bechky's usage, accountability means knowing who is responsible for which task. Here it keeps the sense of section 3.1: an arrangement through which the holder of the host's role answers to the guest. Table 1 sets out how each condition follows from the host's obligation and what fails when it is absent.

**Table 1. From the host's obligation to the conditions at the touchpoint**

| What the host's obligation requires | Condition at the touchpoint | What fails when it is absent |
|---|---|---|
| To receive and attend to this particular guest (section 2.2) | Common understanding | Guest and employee cannot see what the system is doing, so the guest cannot be recognized as an exception to the standard case |
| To hold the terms of the stay steady enough to be negotiated (section 3.4) | Predictability | The terms change without notice, so no request can be framed against them |
| To answer, through someone with authority, for how the duties were performed (section 3.1) | Accountability | No one with authority can receive the guest's claim, so standing is held but cannot be exercised |

<!-- v4, edited: "on or off" sentence kept; Batat's non-compensability added from outline v1; Hemmer et al. sentence cut for length. -->
It follows that mediation is not simply 'helpful' or 'substitutive,' on or off. Each of the three conditions can be present or absent, and present in degrees, so the pattern across them tells us how much of the hosting relationship survives at a given touchpoint. The clearest case of replacement is when all three are gone at once: nothing intelligible, nothing predictable and no one to answer. But that is not the only way hosting fails. A guest who understands a system perfectly, and could even predict it, has still been hosted by no one if no party is obliged to answer them. Accountability cannot be bought back with the other two. Batat makes the parallel claim for her own framework of phygital experience, holding that failures by human connectors 'cannot be fixed or improved by improving other connectors' (2024: 1234). It also yields a result that sounds backwards at first. An opaque algorithmic system can still support hospitality, if its rules are reasonably steady and an employee with authority can question or override it. A transparent one can still replace hospitality, if no one is obliged to answer for it and no one has the power to change it. Transparency is worth having, but transparency alone cannot create accountable systems (Ananny and Crawford 2018: 984).

<!-- v4, edited: "you" replaced; each question now carries a sentence on what it reveals (R3: Table 2 needs explanation); table renumbered. -->
The test can be run at any touchpoint with six questions, which Table 2 sets out. The first asks what rule or actor determined the outcome, and it comes first because the most basic thing to establish is whether the decision can be located at all. The second asks whether the guest could have anticipated the outcome and is told when the rule changes, which examines predictability. The third asks who can see how the outcome was produced, which examines common understanding. The fourth asks who can override the outcome, which shows where discretion sits in the case at hand. The fifth asks who must answer if the outcome is disputed, which examines accountability and is the question on which the others depend. The sixth asks what refusing the system costs the guest, because a guest who cannot refuse the automated route without paying in money, effort or embarrassment has no real alternative, and an alternative is what gives a complaint its weight (Hirschman 1970). Together the questions cover the three conditions, and they separate the power over the single case (question four) from the duty to answer for the rule (question five).

**Table 2. Six questions for any phygital touchpoint**

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| What rule or actor determined this outcome? | Whether the decision can be located at all |
| Could the guest have anticipated it, and are they told when it changes? | Predictability |
| Who can see how it was made? | Common understanding |
| Who can override it? | Where discretion sits |
| Who must answer if it is disputed? | Accountability |
| What does refusing the system cost the guest? | Whether a real alternative exists |

Four cases, chosen to span different technologies and formats, show the questions at work. In hotel pricing, a study of algorithmic recommendations found them pitched away from the price the algorithm itself judged best, in order to move managers who repriced too rarely (Garcia et al. 2026). The operative rule was not one the guest, or even the staff, could anticipate, and no one at the desk could explain the price; the touchpoint fails on predictability and on accountability, although its legal weight is moderate unless the price is high enough to bar a guest from the service altogether (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 22). At check-in, a facial-recognition system that confirms a guest's identity does a clerical job, while one that weighs the guest's history to decide how they will be received is deciding admission (Xu et al. 2021); the six questions bear hard on the second and barely on the first. In a short-term rental with self-check-in, the app may answer the first three questions well, yet when a code fails at night no one with authority may be reachable (Bosma 2022: 605). When a restaurant projects an animated chef onto the table while staff go on hosting the meal, the technology adds to an encounter whose rules stay steady and whose staff keep their discretion (Batat 2021), and the touchpoint passes. Guests' acceptance of automation varies in the same way: little rides on directions at an information desk (Schmidt et al. 2025), and much on admission, identity and price.

## 6. Boundary conditions

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
Not every touchpoint needs a person with authority at the moment of decision, and the framework is stronger for saying which ones do. Baum and Laux distinguish forms of human involvement in automated systems by when intervention can occur relative to the decision's effect. What matters is 'not elapsed time but whether intervention can occur before the token output becomes effective' (Baum and Laux 2026: 6) [CHECK: arXiv v2 page; LNCS chapter unread]. Synchronous intervention can change the decision a person is subject to, asynchronous review shapes future decisions, and for decisions too fast for any person the only human control is a limit set in advance and kept open to revision (Baum and Laux 2026: 6–8). Applied to hospitality, this gives a boundary that follows the host's obligation. Where a decision takes effect on the guest's person, access or contract during the stay, and cannot be undone before the harm lands, the guest needs a reachable party with authority before the decision takes effect. Where a decision can be deferred or undone, a reachable appeal is enough. Where a decision is too fast for anyone, the host answers for the rule it set in advance. Table 3 places common hospitality decisions against this boundary. The placements are our own reading of the sources cited; no study makes them for hospitality.

**Table 3. Hospitality decisions by stakes, reversibility and timing**

| Decision | Stakes | Reversible in time? | Recourse it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Admission or refusal at the door or at check-in; a booking not honoured | High: shelter for the night; cancelling a booking is a legal effect (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 21) | No | A reachable person with authority before refusal takes effect |
| Safety and access on the premises (lock-out, alarm, removal) | Highest | Often no; some too fast for any person | A reachable person where one can act in time; otherwise limits set in advance by the host, and kept revisable |
| Identity verification | Low as verification; high if failure blocks entry | Yes, if a staffed route exists | A staffed fallback; a reachable person if failure would refuse entry |
| Room assignment and allocation | Low to moderate; high where a guest's need makes a room unusable | Mostly | A later appeal by default; a reachable person where the room cannot be used |
| Pricing | Usually moderate; significant if a price bars access (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 22) | Yes | A later appeal, and rules the host can explain |
| Complaints and service recovery | Varies with the failure | Partly | A reachable person with authority; timing matched to the guest |
| Routine requests and information | Low | Yes | Automation acceptable; rules set in advance |

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The law sets a floor beneath this boundary rather than a ceiling. Under the General Data Protection Regulation, a person subject to a solely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects has 'at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision' (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 22(3)), and the reviewer must be someone 'who has the appropriate authority and capability to change the decision' (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 27). The right states no timing, and many hotel decisions may fall within the exception for decisions necessary to a contract (art. 22(2)(a)), so the law secures a later review rather than a person at the threshold. The framework therefore explains most where the guest is on site and the decision is high in stakes and hard to reverse, and less where decisions are remote and reversible. And it reaches its edge at the line between commercial and household hosting. United States public accommodation law exempts an establishment with 'not more than five rooms for rent or hire' that is 'actually occupied by the proprietor of such establishment as his residence' (Civil Rights Act 1964, § 2000a(b)(1)); short-term rentals in a host's own home can fall on the household side of that line, where the role obligation we describe is weakest.

## 7. Designing for interruptible hospitality

<!-- v4, edited: opening rewritten to drop the rhetorical question (register); Mosca 2026a and De Vos et al. 2026 sentences kept. -->
The design lesson starts from a real disagreement in the field about whether guests should see the technology at all. One study of luxury production found customers so uneasy about technology that it advised keeping its use hidden, to protect the sense of authenticity (Mosca and La Rosa 2019). More recent work backs transparency and human oversight for high-stakes systems (Mosca 2026). Concealment answers the unease by making the system harder to see, while leaving the unease exactly where it was. We take the opposite line, and we organize it around a split the argument has already prepared: some conditions can be delivered at the interface, and some can only be built into the institution. Three principles live at the interface, and design, communication and training can largely handle them. First, explain a decision where it lands; guests prefer little disclosure at check-in and fuller explanation later in the stay (Pigac et al. 2026: 286). Second, let guests adjust the settings that matter, because a path the system alone has authored is choice architecture and not choice (Yeung 2017). Third, make significant rule changes visible, because when rules move in silence the knowledge guests and staff have built becomes a moving target (Hatherley 2025). These three improve the interaction. None of them decides who is answerable within it.

<!-- v4, edited: Martin and Waldman corrected per CITATION_FIXES; WP251, Baum and Laux, Wilson-Nash et al. added; Choi and Chao, Li and Sun cut for length. -->
The other two principles are institutional, and they secure accountability. The fourth is reachable authority to reverse. A person must stay reachable who can actually reverse or revise the decision, and keeping a human in the loop is not enough: a human who 'rubberstamps without genuine judgment does not thereby become an overseer' (Baum and Laux 2026: 12), and oversight must be carried out by someone with 'the authority and competence to change the decision' (Article 29 Working Party 2018: 21). The deploying organization is best placed to reverse a decision (Lyons et al. 2021); algorithmic governance needs 'escalation pathways and human oversight' (Shabnam et al. 2026); and of five governance mechanisms, only appeal raised the perceived legitimacy of an adverse algorithmic decision (Martin and Waldman 2023). What guests seem to need is not a human presence as such, but someone with the power to change the answer. The fifth principle is a real alternative. Digital captivity is 'coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable' (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 976); even a downgraded analogue route preserves a choice (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 978), and refusing the system 'may carry a personal cost and loss' (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 979). That cost is easy to overlook because it is usually paid in effort, not cash, and research on mandatory self-service already counts the work it shifts onto the customer as unpaid shadow work (Kim et al. 2025).

<!-- v4, edited: seamlessness paragraph condensed; Mameli et al. 2026 dropped pending citation check; "interruptibility" now scoped by section 6. -->
These two principles also locate interruptibility. Smoothness has become a central goal in accounts of phygital hospitality: good technology strips out queues, repetition, uncertainty and wasted effort so the guest glides through the journey (Batat and Hammedi 2023; Batat 2024: 1220, 1233). The field is not uncritical of its tools (Weaver 2025), but smoothness itself has stayed a goal, and it bears directly on standing. A system built never to interrupt the guest can become a system the guest cannot interrupt (Star and Ruhleder 1996), and frictionless design can remove not only annoyance but the occasion for a claim. By interruptibility we mean a point at which the guest can stop the process, ask, redirect or be treated as an exception, with a counterparty there to receive the interruption. Interaction design has long argued for revealing a system's seams (Chalmers and Galani 2004), but a visible seam is no use to a guest unless somebody with authority stands behind it. Section 6 shows where interruptibility is owed. Interrupting and reversing are different things: synchronous interruption belongs to the decisions that take effect on the guest on site and cannot be undone in time, while a later appeal with authority to reverse serves the rest. Voice offered without such authority can even mislead, because voice offered after a decision, with the decision unchanged, still raised judgements of fairness (Lind et al. 1990). The counterparty has to be real.

<!-- v4, edited: roadmap paragraph rewritten to tie to Tables 2 and 3; stage 4 corrected to Bovens' actor/forum relation (R3, CITATION_FIXES). -->
For organizations, these principles line up as a roadmap of rising commitment, which Table 4 sets out. A property can start by auditing its phygital touchpoints with the six questions of Table 2 and naming the decisions taken inside them. It can then specify what guests and employees are able to understand, adjust and challenge at each touchpoint, and design the capabilities that support interpretation and adjustment. The harder stages are institutional. The fourth stage constitutes accountability in Bovens' sense: a forum before which an identifiable actor with authority to change outcomes must explain and justify the organization's decisions, and which can question that actor and pass judgement (Bovens 2007: 450, 452). In a hotel, the forum is the place a guest's claim can be lodged; the actor is the manager, operator or brand that holds the host's role. The fifth stage preserves a meaningful alternative for guests who decline the automated route. Each stage costs more than the last, because responsibility cannot be automated as easily as service can, and Table 3 tells an organization where the fourth stage must operate in real time and where a later appeal will do. A property that stops after better interfaces and better-trained staff may have automated its hospitality without ever deciding who answers for it.

**Table 4. Five stages from interface fixes to institutional answerability, in rising order of cost**

| Stage | What the organization does | Condition it secures | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Map touchpoints with the six questions (Table 2) and name the decisions taken in them | Common understanding | Attention |
| 2. Specify | Establish what guests and employees can understand, adjust and challenge | Common understanding, predictability | Analysis |
| 3. Design | Build the capabilities that support interpretation and adjustment | Predictability | Design and training |
| 4. Constitute | Establish a forum before which an accountable actor with authority to change outcomes must answer, in real time where Table 3 requires it | Accountability | Governance and staffing |
| 5. Preserve | Maintain a meaningful alternative for guests who decline the automated route | Accountability, through exit | Ongoing operational commitment |

## 8. Discussion

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The paper makes three contributions. The first is a theory of the commercial host's obligation under delegation (section 2). Against accounts that locate the obligation in hosts' motives or index it to harm, we have argued that the obligation is a role obligation, imposed on whoever takes up the role of receiving travellers and taken up by holding out; that it comes into force when a traveller who can pay and is fit to be received presents themselves; that its holder answers alongside the operator in control, while those who merely arrange a service answer to the holder; and that its work can be delegated while the duty cannot. The second contribution is an account of guest standing that does not depend on accountability (section 3). Defining standing from the host's directed duties, and accountability last, removes the circularity between the two and allows the central diagnosis of the paper to be stated precisely: under algorithmic mediation the guest still holds standing, but cannot exercise it when no one with authority can receive the claim. The third contribution is a test and a roadmap scoped by type of decision (sections 5 to 7). The three conditions and six questions of Tables 1 and 2 examine whether a touchpoint keeps the conditions for exercising standing; Table 3 says where those conditions must hold at the moment of decision and where a later appeal will do; and Table 4 orders what an organization must do to secure them.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
These contributions place the paper among neighbouring fields rather than inside any one of them. Service management asks whether an encounter delivers value to the customer. Algorithmic accountability asks who answers when a delegated system causes harm, and it has shown that answerability stays with the deployer (Kong 2026: 5, 10) and that, in chains of providers, responsibility is partial and unequally distributed (Cobbe et al. 2023: 1193). The role account asks a third question: what the holder of a public calling owes the person it has received. That question catches an arrangement that works exactly as designed and harms no one, yet leaves the guest with no one to address. Regulation leaves much of this to firms. Data protection law secures only a later review (section 6). The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act requires that high-risk systems allow those assigned to oversee them to intervene or interrupt the system 'through a "stop" button' (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, art. 14(4)(e)), but that is a design requirement owed to overseers, not a right held by guests, and the Act's list of high-risk uses names no hospitality application. How a guest reaches someone with authority is therefore, for now, largely a matter for hospitality organizations themselves.

<!-- v4, edited: well-being paragraph rewritten to name the distinction and state the link (R3; editor's request); "two levels" and "direction of travel" removed. -->
The distinction between holding and exercising standing also bears on well-being, which transformative service research asks of service ecosystems at both the individual and the collective level (Anderson and Ostrom 2015; Batat 2022). For the individual guest, a touchpoint that withholds any reachable authority converts a claim into a private cost. Refusing a system that has become unavoidable 'may carry a personal cost and loss' (Wilson-Nash et al. 2026: 979), and the guest who cannot press a claim bears the outcome of a decision alone, whatever the quality of the service around it. For communities, the evidence suggests a range rather than a verdict. The same digital mediation can enchant, disenchant or re-enchant a service encounter depending on the surrounding arrangements (De Vos et al. 2024), and our framework identifies one of those arrangements: whether the path from the guest to authority survives. Where many touchpoints withhold that path, what a community can expect from its hospitable institutions changes with them. None of this is a case against the technology. Phygital arrangements bring real gains in responsiveness, and in how much a small team can offer a large number of guests. The framework's purpose is to help organizations keep those gains without giving up the relationship that makes them worth having.

## 9. Limitations and a research agenda

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
This is a conceptual paper, and its cases illustrate the distinctions rather than test them. Three limits deserve stating. First, the legal sources on which section 2 draws are English, United States federal and European Union law, together with one decision of a United States federal appeals court, and the account of when the host's obligation comes into force and who holds it may not travel unchanged to other jurisdictions. Second, two claims about the organizational grading of frontline authority rest on studies we could read only in abstract (Hewagama et al. 2019; Hales and Klidas 1998) [CHECK], and the typology in Table 3 is our own reading of sources that do not address hospitality directly. Third, we have borrowed the structure of non-delegable duty from a judgment about schools and hospitals (Woodland v. Essex County Council 2013), and whether courts would place commercial hosts in that category is a question for legal scholarship, not one this paper can settle.

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
The framework points to three studies, each starting from a gap we documented. The first would follow a guest's request or complaint up the path from the person who receives it to the party with authority over the rule that produced it, and record whether it was heard and what changed; we found no such study in hotels, restaurants or bars, and the nearest, Xiang et al. (2022), reports two cases. The second would examine unstaffed and app-only hotels and short-term rentals with self-check-in, asking whom a guest can reach when a decision goes against them and who answers. The studies of unstaffed hotels we found measure acceptance and satisfaction, and none asks who owes the guest anything. The third would map how hotel management agreements, franchise agreements and platform terms allocate duties to guests among owner, brand, operator, platform and technology vendor, testing our proposal that the party which holds itself out answers alongside the party in control. Whether employees' silence and guests' silence reinforce one another under algorithmic direction remains untested.

## 10. Conclusion

<!-- drafted by Claude -->
This paper asked where the obligation of hosting goes when phygital arrangements delegate the functions of hosting to algorithmic systems, and what an arrangement must preserve for the guest to remain a guest. The obligation does not go into the machine, and it does not dissolve. It stays with the party that took up the role of receiving the guest, by holding itself out, alongside whoever controls the operation, and it can be delegated only in its performance. What algorithmic mediation removes is not the obligation but the path from the guest to anyone with the authority to answer for it. To preserve that path, an arrangement must keep a reachable party with authority to change the outcome, before the decision takes effect wherever it acts on the guest on site and cannot be undone in time, and through a real appeal elsewhere, together with a real alternative for guests who decline the automated route.

<!-- v4, edited: final paragraph of v4 §9 kept in substance; closing epigram replaced by the agenda (R3). -->
The question for phygital hospitality is therefore not whether technology can perform the jobs a host used to perform. Increasingly, it plainly can. The harder question is where, in an arrangement that has scattered those jobs across guest, employee, brand, vendor and platform, the obligation of hosting still has an address, and whether the guest can reach it. Answering that question for particular arrangements is empirical work: following claims along the path to authority, studying the formats in which no host is present, and reading the contracts that allocate the host's duties among firms. Each of these studies would test whether the guest's standing, which we have argued survives delegation, can still be exercised where it matters most.
