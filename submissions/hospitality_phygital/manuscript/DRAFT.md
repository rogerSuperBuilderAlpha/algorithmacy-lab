# Who Hosts the Guest?

## 1. Introduction: Who is responsible for the welcome?

Research on phygital hospitality often presents artificial intelligence, apps and automated
interfaces as tools for creating smoother, more personalized and more guest-centred experiences
(Batat 2024, 2026). That makes sense from a service perspective, but it does not capture what
hospitality involves. Hospitality is more than the efficient delivery of a service. It is a
relational practice built around welcome, recognition, care, presence and the negotiation of access
(Lashley 2000; Lynch et al. 2011). A guest is not a consumer receiving a bundle of services. The
guest enters a relationship in which someone is expected to receive them (Hemmington 2007).

This distinction exposes a gap in the way hospitality research describes technology. AI and other
digital systems are called tools, mediators, facilitators, co-creators, or forms of social presence
(Bitner et al. 2000; Li et al. 2021). Each term describes what the technology can do; none
identifies what, if anything, it owes the guest. The language of hospitality is different, because
the idea of the host contains an obligation. A host does not merely perform tasks. A host receives
the arriving stranger and assumes responsibility for the conditions of that person's welcome
(Lashley 2000). Nor does that responsibility end at the door. Hospitality is a spatial practice
continuing once the guest has crossed, exercised through the tactics by which a house contains a
guest who does not simply comply (Bulley 2015), and the mediation examined here does most of its
work after admission.

Service research has tended to place the employee in the position traditionally occupied by the host
(Larivière et al. 2017; Odekerken-Schröder et al. 2022). Yet employee and host are not identical
roles. An employment contract specifies responsibilities for delivering services; it does not
explain the relational obligation between host and guest. That matters once automated systems take
over functions human hosts once performed. Hospitality scholarship has already asked what happens
when platforms make the human host less visible, and has named the condition algorithmic hostility
(Riordan 2024). The phrase 'guests without hosts' was coined for something adjacent, the blurring of
hosting and guesting in network hospitality, and has lately been extended to an algorithmic
governance holding hosts and guests in extractive relations, and to the replacement of human
hospitality workers by robots and AI (Germann Molz 2026). Both ask what becomes of hospitality as
the human host recedes. A further question follows. If the host can be erased because a system now
performs the recognizing, the routing and the admitting, we need to know whether that system has
also taken on what the host owed. The question is not new to hospitality theory's own agenda. The
founding statement of the field's theorizing programme set out hospitality and virtuality as a
research direction, and discussed technology as at once guest and host, people and machines hostages
of each other (Lynch et al. 2011). What it did not settle is which of the two owes the welcome. This
paper asks whether a system that takes over the functions of hosting also inherits the obligations
of the host. The argument is that it does not, and that the gap matters for how phygital hospitality
is understood and designed.

## 2. Hospitality is more than service

Being a guest is not a description of the level of service a person receives. It is a position
within a relationship, and it carries a particular kind of standing. A guest can ask questions,
decline an offer, request an explanation, challenge a decision, or ask to be treated as an exception
to an ordinary rule (Lynch 2017). These possibilities are what distinguish hospitality from service
delivery, and a stay can be efficient, convenient and free of failures without anyone having
meaningfully received the guest (Lugosi 2008).

Experience research in the phygital tradition comes close to these ideas, insisting that value is
created with the guest and placing empathy, emotional connection and the effort to understand the
customer in her own terms at the centre of design (Batat 2024). Hospitality theory adds that these
are traces of a relationship in which one party holds obligations towards the other, which becomes
consequential once the party doing the recognizing is no longer a person.

This relational dimension matters because hospitality has traditionally involved obligations flowing
between host and guest, even where the relationship is unequal or temporary (Derrida and
Dufourmantelle 2000; Lashley 2000). Commercial hospitality simplifies it by concentrating on what
the organization provides. Research has consequently become very good at measuring whether guests
feel welcomed, satisfied or cared for, and much less curious about what flows the other way. The
point is sharper than neglect. When a validated scale of experienced hospitality was built it
retained inviting, care and comfort, and dropped its two agency items, having choice and feeling
independent, at the confirmatory stage for loading too weakly (Pijls et al. 2017). The field did not
merely overlook the guest's standing. It tried to measure it, and the items did not hold.

The studies that come closest stop short. A study of luxury lodges documents guests who tidy their
rooms for housekeeping, return glasses to the bar and praise employees in reviews, and describes the
conduct in terms of altruism, generosity and fictive kinship rather than obligation (Manfreda and
Harkison 2025). A validated scale measures what residents of tourist destinations gain from
interacting with visitors (Shi et al. 2025). Neither asks what a guest owes a commercial host.
Service research does measure conduct running from the customer, in scales of participation and
citizenship behaviour, but those instruments count what she contributes to the firm's outcome rather
than what she owes the party receiving her. The guest's side of the commercial relationship remains,
for practical purposes, untheorized.

Algorithmic mediation makes the weakness visible, because it can leave the guest's standing without
a counterparty. She may still have someone to complain to, and the organization may still compensate
her, while she loses access to anyone with authority over the rule being challenged. A front-desk
employee may explain that the system assigned this room, rejected the upgrade, moved the price or
required this form of identification, and have no authority to alter any of it. The guest is left in
an unusual position: admitted as a customer, with nobody to negotiate with as a guest.

Platform accommodation showed an early version of this problem, well before hotels automated their
own front desks. Research on Airbnb has documented hosts working to satisfy rating systems they
cannot see (Cheng and Foley 2019), a platform's ideal of hospitality shaping what hosts feel they
must provide (Roelofsen and Minca 2018), and guests excluded on the basis of records they did not
author and cannot inspect (Edelman et al. 2017; Cui et al. 2020). Hospitality scholarship, in other
words, was already registering that a third party had unsettled the question of who hosts while the
service literature was formalizing the triad. It has not yet asked what that third party owes, and
that is the question this paper takes up.

## 3. The three-party arrangement

Phygital hospitality increasingly creates a three-party relationship between the guest, the
hospitality employee or organization, and an algorithmic intermediary (Larivière et al. 2017; Li et
al. 2021; Gursoy 2026). The intermediary does more than transmit information between the other two.
It may classify guests, determine prices, manage queues, allocate rooms, personalize offers, control
access, or decide which options appear at all. Many of these activities were previously the
practical work through which hospitality was enacted.

These encounters are phygital in a precise sense. Physical interactions are turned continuously into
digital records, and digital determinations back into physical arrangements: a room key that works
or does not, a price that appears on the screen at the desk (Batat 2024). The guest crosses between
the two realms repeatedly within a single stay. The frameworks call this movement fluidity. It can
as accurately be called a relay, and a relay raises the question the fluidity language obscures,
namely who holds authority at each handover.

Recent research has begun to place machines in something like the host's role, but it has
consistently asked a different question. Studies of robotic hospitableness examine whether a robot
can produce feelings of welcome, attentiveness, and reassurance in guests (Liu et al. 2026), and an
emerging theory of AI consciousness in hospitality asks whether guests can experience genuine
hospitality from a machine at all (Lee and Lu 2024). Both ask what guests perceive. Whether a party
is perceived as hospitable and whether it occupies a position that owes the guest something are
different matters, and only the second is independent of what the guest happens to believe. Guests
themselves decline the attribution, holding a robot less responsible than a human employee for a
service failure and routing responsibility to the firm behind it (Belanche et al. 2020). Two lines
of work come nearer. One asks whether service robots should be afforded rights and responsibilities
at all, and places its emphasis on governance and risk: liability, safety, data protection, consent,
and regulatory expectation (Sharma and Mattila 2026). The other, the phygital service programme
itself, reports that existing frameworks explain value co-creation without explaining how digitally
enforced rules redistribute the labour of interpretation (Shabnam et al. 2026). The first concerns
what a firm must answer for. The second names the redistribution and stops there. Neither asks which
party, once the rules have done the redistributing, still owes the guest a welcome. Each
responsibility has a different source. Care is owed because someone is in need, fiduciary duties
because someone has been trusted, governance duties because a system creates risks (Kropf et al.
2026; Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021). A welcome is owed because someone has arrived, and arrival
is the barest of these triggers, asking nothing of the guest except that she be there.

Commercial hospitality qualifies this, and the qualification is the point. No hotel owes a welcome
to everyone who arrives, and the commercial domain is defined by the conditions it places upon entry
(Lashley 2000). Those conditions govern admission rather than reception. Once the guest is inside,
the house is not supposed to keep recalculating the terms of her welcome against what she is worth
to it. Algorithmic mediation reopens that condition on the inside of the door and holds it open.

Whether the duty travels with the function is the question this paper began with, and the strongest
case against a negative answer should be stated first. Care robots have been argued to hold role
responsibility grounded in the social function they occupy rather than in any consciousness they
lack (Kropf et al. 2026). A system performing recognition and admission might, on the same grounds,
hold role responsibility for welcome. The answer lies in that argument's own limit. Role
responsibility of this kind is proposed without displacing human accountability, and accountability
is a relation to a forum: an obligation to explain one's conduct before a party who can question it,
judge it, and attach consequences (Bovens 2007). A system can perform a welcome. It cannot stand in
a forum and answer for one. The duty can be discharged by a machine and cannot be owned by one, and
it is ownership rather than performance that makes the role.

Philosophy of technology has considered the machine as a guest to whom hospitality might be extended
(Introna 2010). Across the hospitality and service literatures we could locate no treatment of the
machine as a keeper of the threshold.

As algorithmic systems take on these functions, knowledge, discretion, and authority are
redistributed (Mosca et al. 2026b). Knowledge moves towards the system, which may hold the guest's
history, preferences, loyalty status and the property's constraints at once (Rahman 2021; Calo and
Rosenblat 2017). Discretion is divided rather than simply transferred. Staff retain some freedom to
act, but an increasing part of their work is interpreting machine decisions and working within
categories established elsewhere, and in other expert occupations practitioners decouple from
algorithmic evaluation and buffer their judgement against it, which is a struggle over discretion
rather than evidence that discretion has moved (Christin 2017). The evidence is uneven. The clearest
documentation comes from back-of-house work, where housekeeping systems have been observed to fix
task sequences and, in their most limited configurations, to withhold the day's full task list from
the workers carrying it out (Spektor et al. 2023, 2025). Front-desk work appears different: revenue
systems there anticipate that staff will deviate from their recommendations, and the deviations are
durable and to some extent designed for (Bendoly 2013; Garcia et al. 2026). The guest-facing half of
the question has barely been studied. Authority, meanwhile, moves towards whoever controls the rules
by which the system operates (Scott and Orlikowski 2012).

This redistribution produces the paper's central asymmetry. The party best placed to know, decide
and revise the rules may also be the party least available to answer the guest, and this is not a
malfunction: the asymmetry arises when the technology works exactly as intended (Rahman 2021). The
problem is institutional rather than technical, because the arrangement separates the capacity to
shape decisions from the obligation to answer for them (Bovens 2007). Stakeholder research shows
that roles and responsibilities can be reallocated across parties without the responsibilities
dissolving (Casalegno et al. 2020). What algorithmic mediation raises is what happens when the
reallocation carries the functions and leaves the obligation behind.

This separation also matters for the craft of hospitality. Skilled front-desk employees create a
guest's sense of control by appearing to defer while quietly managing the options, anticipating
difficulties, and steering away from choices likely to disappoint. The craft depends on their
holding some of the power they appear to surrender, and algorithmic direction breaks it at precisely
that point. Employees are still expected to produce the guest's sense of control while no longer
controlling the decisions that produce it (Fuller and Smith 1991).

## 4. When technology helps and when it replaces

Whether technology supports hospitality or substitutes for it cannot be settled by asking how
sophisticated it is (Raisch and Krakowski 2021). An advanced system may support a hospitable
relationship; a simple automated process may undermine one. The useful distinction concerns what
becomes unavailable to the guest under mediation, and three conditions matter: common understanding,
predictability and accountability (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009).

Common understanding weakens when neither guest nor employee can form a working account of what the
system is doing. Predictability becomes one-sided when the system forecasts the guest's behaviour
while changing its own rules without her being able to anticipate the change (Hatherley 2025).
Accountability weakens when no identifiable person or forum is required to answer for a decision
that is challenged (Bovens 2007). Accountability is the condition that decides the case. Mediation
becomes substitutive wherever it is withheld, whatever happens to the other two, and the other two
determine how much the guest can do with the standing accountability gives her. This is a different
test from the one human-AI collaboration research applies, which asks whether the pair outperforms
either party alone and has no place for what remains available to the person the decision is about
(Hemmer et al. 2025).

The pair of terms is not new here; the difference is in what they are applied to. Service research
asks whether a robot augments or substitutes the employee in producing value for the customer
(Odekerken-Schröder et al. 2022; Raisch and Krakowski 2021). The question here is whether the
mediation augments or substitutes the relationship, and the criterion is what remains available to
the guest rather than what she gets.

The result can seem counterintuitive. An opaque algorithm can augment hospitality if its rules are
stable and an empowered employee can override it, while a perfectly transparent one can substitute
for hospitality if nobody is required to answer for its decisions or has the authority to revise
them (Vaccaro et al. 2020). Transparency is valuable. It cannot by itself produce hospitality.

Table 1 puts the distinction to work as five diagnostic questions for any phygital touchpoint.

**Table 1. Five diagnostic questions for any phygital touchpoint.**

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| What made this decision? | whether the decision can be located at all |
| Who can see how it was made? | common understanding |
| Who can override it? | where discretion actually sits |
| Who must answer if it is disputed? | accountability |
| What does refusing the system cost the guest? | whether an alternative genuinely exists |

Two examples show how the questions work. Hotel pricing is the clearest case of one-sided
predictability. A study of algorithmic pricing found the system biasing its own recommendations
because it anticipated that revenue managers would adjust them, so the rule in operation was not the
rule the staff believed they were working with (Garcia et al. 2026). Nobody at the desk could have
explained that decision accurately. The managers kept their discretion; what they lost was any
account of the rule they were adjusting. The study's authors read the result the other way, as a
case for automating the adjustment out of the process. The mechanism is not in dispute. What differs
is whether a rule nobody at the desk can state is an inefficiency to engineer away or an
accountability gap to close. Augmentation is equally concrete. When a restaurant projects an
animated chef onto the table while human staff host the meal, the technology adds to an encounter
whose rules stay stable and whose staff keep their discretion (Batat 2021). The same test that
condemns the first arrangement approves the second.

The questions also explain why findings about guest acceptance should be read carefully. Guests may
be indifferent between a human employee and a robot at an information desk because little is at
stake (Schmidt et al. 2025). That tells us little about encounters in which technology determines
admission, pricing, room allocation, or exceptions to a rule. What matters is not whether guests
prefer humans in general, but what authority and consequences attach to a particular encounter.

Check-in is the clearest example. A system that confirms a guest is who she says she is performs a
clerical task faster than a person could. A system that weighs her history in deciding how she will
be received is doing something categorically different, however alike the two interfaces look.
Guests appear to sense it: willingness to accept identification at the hotel door is modelled as a
trade-off between privacy and benefit, a calculation nobody makes over mere convenience (Xu et al.
2020). The questions apply with full force to the system that scores the guest, and hardly at all to
the one that merely confirms her.

## 5. What people can learn, and what they cannot

Guests and employees are not passive recipients of algorithmic decisions. Through repeated
encounters they develop working theories about how systems operate, learning which requests are
likely to succeed, how preferences must be expressed, and how to tell when the system has changed
(Eslami et al. 2016; Jhaver et al. 2018; Cotter 2019). A review of 169 studies of algorithm literacy
finds that field still without agreed core competencies, and calls for accounts specific to a task
and a domain (Gagrčin et al. 2024). What follows is such an account. These practices can be
understood through three related competences: interpreting what the system is doing, specifying
wishes in terms the system can act upon, and adapting when its rules change.

Hospitality workers frequently supply these competences on the guest's behalf. An employee may
recognize that a guest's ordinary way of describing a need does not match the categories available
in the system, a pattern reported in phygital service work though only in outline and without a full
study behind it (De Vos et al. 2023). She reformulates the request into terms the system will take,
lending the guest a capacity that belongs to her. This is an easily overlooked form of hospitality
work, and a fragile one. The competence being lent is a moving target, since a system that revises
its categories without announcement depreciates accumulated knowledge overnight and tells nobody
(Hatherley 2025). The lending also depends on the employee remaining where the guest's request meets
the system. A design that removes her automates a task and removes the person who was quietly
translating.

There is a limit to what competence can accomplish, because skill cannot manufacture a counterparty.
A guest may understand a pricing system perfectly and still have no influence over its decision, and
knowing how to submit an appeal is not the same as holding a right to be answered (Vaccaro et al.
2020). Some approaches to algorithmic competence blur the two. A validated scale developed with
platform workers measures understanding, embracing, leveraging and remediating algorithmic
management, and remediating is measured by what the worker does alone or with peers: solving the
problem herself, asking other workers, and using the platform's own reporting and appeal functions
(Zhou et al. 2025). Every validated item locates the remedy in the worker, and most route around the
institution rather than through it. What such a scale cannot measure is whether anyone is obliged to
consider the appeal, since that obligation belongs to the institution and not to any capacity of the
worker. Lodging an appeal is an action a person can learn. Being answered is not.

The distinction can be stated simply: competence is learned, while standing is conferred. We name
the two halves accordingly. Hospitality algorithmacy is the situated capacity to recognize,
interpret and work an algorithmic mediation in the hospitality encounter, and it is what guests and
employees can be taught. Coordinative sovereignty is the standing to be answered within a
coordination one cannot leave without cost, and it is what an institution must confer. The same
distinction applies to employees, who can become steadily more knowledgeable about the systems they
work with while losing authority over their outcomes. Those who understand most clearly how little
influence they have may become less willing to speak about it, though the evidence is adjacent
rather than direct. Awareness of artificial intelligence raises frontline silence through a
perceived breach of the psychological contract (Cheng et al. 2025), and algorithmic direction,
scheduling and monitoring depress voice while algorithmic feedback raises it (Lin et al. 2026).
Neither measures competence, so the step from understanding a system to declining to speak about it
is a proposition this framework offers rather than a finding it reports. It meets an existing
problem in hospitality work, where frontline silence is already well documented (Papadopoulos et al.
2021). Greater technological competence does not produce greater voice.

Whether the two sources of silence compound one another is an open question. A workforce already
inclined towards silence might be expected to respond differently to algorithmic direction than one
with a tradition of voice, but the interaction has not been tested: the study that comes closest
carries no measure of workplace climate at all (Lin et al. 2026). This is one of the clearer
empirical openings the framework identifies.

## 6. Five design features

Researchers disagree about whether guests should see the technology at all. One study of luxury
production found customers so uneasy about it that it recommended keeping its use undisclosed, to
protect the sense of authenticity (Mosca and La Rosa 2019). More recent work endorses transparency
and human oversight for consequential systems (Mosca 2026a), and employees resist technology whose
purpose is obscured (De Vos et al. 2026). Concealment answers customer unease by making the system
harder to see while leaving the unease in place. The features below take the opposite approach.

The argument leads to five design features. First, decisions should be explained where they take
effect, rather than in a distant policy document the guest must go and find (Edwards and Veale
2017). Second, guests should be able to adjust meaningful parameters without leaving the
relationship, so that a system which has misread a preference can be corrected (Morosan and DeFranco
2016). Third, significant rule changes should be made visible, since systems that silently revise
their rules turn accumulated knowledge into a moving target (Hatherley 2025).

The fourth principle is that a person should remain reachable who can actually reverse or revise a
decision (Yurrita et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2019). Preserving human contact is not enough. An employee
who can listen sympathetically but change nothing becomes a place where frustration is deposited
rather than a source of accountability (Sterz et al. 2024). What guests need is someone with the
power to change the answer, which is a matter of authority rather than of a human face. The fifth
principle is that guests should retain a genuine alternative. An option that exists on paper but
carries a financial, practical or social penalty is not one, and the penalty is easy to miss because
it is usually paid in effort. When self-service is formally optional but the staffed route means a
longer queue or the sense of having caused inconvenience, guests describe the digital path as work
assigned to them rather than service received (Kim et al. 2025). A guest who declines it is
exercising a preference the organization claims to honour, and should not be treated as a problem to
be managed.

The principles divide. The first three can be addressed through interface design, communication and
training; the final two cannot be supplied by individual competence, however skilled the guest or
employee becomes. The first three extend hospitality algorithmacy. The final two are matters of
coordinative sovereignty, requiring institutional decisions about staffing, authority, review and
responsibility (Alfrink et al. 2023). Which mechanism is chosen matters. Where governance
arrangements have been compared, an appeal that reaches the decision raised the perceived legitimacy
of an adverse algorithmic outcome, while oversight and audit lowered it (Martin and Waldman 2022).
The fourth feature asks for the first and not the other two. An organization that answers a
contestability problem with more guest education has misdiagnosed its condition. Some problems
require people to become more capable; others require institutions to become more answerable.

## 7. Against frictionless design

Seamlessness has become an important goal in many accounts of phygital hospitality (Batat and
Hammedi 2023; Batat 2024; Mameli et al. 2026). From this perspective, good technology removes
queues, repetition, uncertainty, and unnecessary effort so that the guest moves smoothly through the
hospitality journey. These improvements clearly have value. Yet the assumption that all friction
should be eliminated deserves closer examination because some forms of friction provide the occasion
for hospitality itself.

The phygital literature is increasingly willing to criticize itself. Recent work examines
technology-driven acceleration in hospitality (Weaver 2025), and the field's own research agendas
criticize the opacity and rigidity of algorithmic systems (Shabnam et al. 2026). Smoothness has
remained a stated goal throughout. Extended reality frameworks describe their purpose as creating 'a
continuum in terms of customer value from physical to digital settings and vice versa' (Batat and
Hammedi 2023), the founding framework of phygital customer experience aims at 'fluidifying' the
guest's journey (Batat 2024), and the most recent integrative framework for phygital tourism, built
from 57 studies and 84 industry cases, contains no discussion of friction at all (Mameli et al.
2026). The argument here is narrow: the field is far from uncritical, and the claim is only that
this one design value has escaped the criticism applied to everything around it.

Negotiation requires a moment in which negotiation can take place. A system designed never to
interrupt the guest may become a system the guest cannot interrupt (Star and Ruhleder 1996), and a
decision designed to pass unnoticed is hard to question because she never meets a recognizable point
at which it could be challenged. Frictionless design removes inconvenience and opportunities for
voice together.

The value of voice does not depend entirely on whether the guest gets what she wants. Voice offered
after a decision has been taken, with the decision explicitly unchanged, still raises judgements of
both procedural and outcome fairness (Lind et al. 1990; Goodwin and Ross 1992). The effect has a
boundary worth stating: an invitation to speak that was never going to bear on anything lowers those
judgements instead, and the same research warns that voice can be used to manufacture a sense of
fairness. That is the argument for a real counterparty rather than the appearance of one. Value also
depends on there being a point at which speaking, declining or requesting an exception is possible,
and that matters most for guests whose circumstances fall outside the categories the system
anticipates. Guests are in effect scripted before they arrive, and when the assumption misses, a
person on the spot has traditionally noticed and improvised (Nguyen et al. 2025). Algorithmic
personalization industrializes the scripting and removes the improviser. A guest may fail to fit
because of her body, her documents, her language or her circumstances that day, and she meets a
system with no surface on which the misfit can be negotiated (Costanza-Chock 2020). She loses the
pause at the desk at which someone might once have seen that the rule did not fit.

The concern sharpens because AI travel systems reproduce biases and struggle with relational,
contextual and local knowledge (Andreev et al. 2025). That knowledge is not peripheral in
hospitality. It is often exactly what allows a host to see when an ordinary rule should not be
applied in the ordinary way.

The natural remedies deserve scrutiny too, because they deepen the mediation rather than reducing
it. When platform reviews were shown to reduce discrimination against guests, the lesson drawn was
to expand the role of reputational records (Cui et al. 2020). That remedy works through the same
instrument as the harm. It relocates disadvantage onto the guest with no record, and the records
carry distortions of their own, including inflation over time (Filippas et al. 2022). A remedy
requiring the guest to have been legible all along is not available to the guest whose problem is
that the system cannot read her. The aim should therefore not be the elimination of friction, but
the removal of pointless friction while the moments in which recognition, negotiation and exception
remain possible are preserved (Padigar et al. 2025; Phillips et al. 2024). We call the property
those moments provide interruptibility: the arrangement holds a point at which the guest can stop it
and be answered. Interaction design already argues for revealing a system's seams, so that people
can work with it as well as through it (Chalmers and Galani 2004). The claim here goes further. A
visible seam is of no use unless somebody stands behind it.

## 8. Discussion

The paper makes three main contributions. First, it repositions AI from a neutral service technology
to a constitutive mediator of hospitality relationships. By redistributing knowledge, discretion and
authority, technology changes the relationship rather than merely accelerating it (Mosca et al.
2026b; Casalegno et al. 2020). The question is not only what AI does within hospitality, but what
kind of hospitality becomes possible once AI participates in it. Human-centricity, on this account,
concerns what the arrangement protects or withholds: guest agency, employee discretion, and
relational judgement. Keeping humans visible in the encounter is neither necessary nor sufficient
for any of the three.

Second, the paper proposes a checkable distinction between mediation that augments hospitality and
mediation that substitutes for it. Common understanding, predictability and accountability give a
way of examining what remains available at a particular touchpoint, with accountability deciding
between the two (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009; Bovens 2007). This shifts analysis away from whether
automation is good or bad and towards the institutional arrangements through which it operates.

Third, the paper separates competence from standing, hospitality algorithmacy from coordinative
sovereignty. Guests and employees can learn to interact effectively with algorithmic systems, but
individual skill cannot create institutional rights, authority, or responsibility (Zhou et al.
2025).

These distinctions bear directly on well-being. Transformative research already asks how service
ecosystems affect individual and collective well-being across economic, cognitive, emotional, and
social dimensions (Anderson and Ostrom 2015; Batat 2022). The outcomes are best understood as a
range rather than a direction, since the same mediation can enchant, disenchant, or re-enchant
depending on the surrounding arrangements (De Vos et al. 2024). The two levels have rarely been
studied together where automation is concerned. A systematic review of digital transformation and
employee well-being covers algorithmic systems entirely at the individual level (Moganadas et al.
2026), while research connecting hospitality design to community well-being makes the connection
through physical space rather than through technology (Park et al. 2026). The framework offers one
way of joining them, since the withholdings that diminish an individual guest's standing can, in
aggregate, shape what a community may expect from its hospitable institutions.

None of this is a case against the technology. Phygital arrangements deliver real gains in
responsiveness and in what a small team can offer many guests, and the framework exists to help
organizations keep those gains without surrendering the relationship that makes them worth having.
For practitioners the contributions translate into the roadmap in Table 2. Its stages ascend in
institutional cost, because responsibility cannot be automated as easily as service delivery, and a
property that stops after improving interfaces may have automated its hospitality without deciding
who answers for it (Bovens 2007).

**Table 2. A phygital hospitality transformation roadmap.**

| Stage | What the organization does | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Map phygital touchpoints and identify the decisions taking place within them | attention |
| 2. Specify | Establish what guests and employees can understand, adjust, and challenge at each touchpoint | analysis |
| 3. Design | Build the capabilities that support interpretation, specification, and adaptation | design and training |
| 4. Constitute | Create a forum required to answer challenges, with genuine authority to act | governance and staffing |
| 5. Preserve | Maintain a meaningful alternative for those who decline the automated route | ongoing operational commitment |

This is a conceptual paper, and its limits follow from that. Its examples illustrate the
distinctions rather than test them, and every relationship proposed between the five design features
and guest or employee outcomes awaits empirical examination. Three studies would advance matters.
The first would ask guests directly about the working theories they hold of the systems that
recognize and route them; the folk-theory literature has barely been extended into hospitality
settings, and we could locate no study of this kind. The second would operationalize the three
competences of Section 5 against the validated competency scale now available (Zhou et al. 2025).
The third would test whether contestability provisions change how guests experience fairness in a
commercial setting, since the existing evidence comes almost entirely from public-sector and
platform contexts.

## 9. Conclusion

Guest-centred phygital hospitality does not depend on making technology invisible, nor does good
hospitality require every encounter to remain human. The question is whether guests and employees
retain meaningful agency over how technology participates. Better interfaces, clearer explanations
and stronger algorithmic competence support that agency without providing all of it.

Some of that agency has to be built into the institutional arrangement. Guests need real
opportunities to question decisions, employees need the authority to respond, and organizations need
to accept responsibility for the rules through which automated systems act. Hospitality requires
more than a technically effective interaction between a guest and a system. It requires someone who
can recognize a claim, answer it, and when necessary make an exception.

The central question is therefore not whether technology can perform functions once performed by
hosts. Increasingly it can. The harder question is who remains responsible when it does. Who hosts
the guest? Not the system, which performs the welcome without being able to answer for it. The host
is whoever can be called to account for the terms on which the guest is received, and phygital
design either keeps that party reachable or quietly disposes of them. However sophisticated,
personalized or seamless the technology becomes, somebody still owes the guest a welcome.
