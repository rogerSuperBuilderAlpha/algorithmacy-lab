## 1. Parhi (2026), 'AI governance and digital transformation in hospitality firms' (abstract only)

**Q1. Question, design, evidence base.** From the abstract: "This study systematically reviews literature on AI governance and digital transformation in hospitality firms and develops a sector-specific governance architecture." The review followed "PRISMA 2020 principles using Scopus as the sole database, resulting in a final corpus of 162 peer-reviewed articles published through August 2026", analysed by "bibliometric analysis using VOSviewer and Biblioshiny, alongside qualitative thematic synthesis". It proposes "the ADAPT-H Framework, comprising seven interconnected pillars: Accountability, Data Stewardship, Algorithmic Transparency, People-Centred Governance, Technology–Strategy Alignment, Regulatory Harmonisation, and Ecosystem and Vendor Governance."

**Q2. Who is accountable, and on what ground.** The abstract says only that "governance mechanisms ensuring accountability, transparency, data stewardship, and responsible innovation remain fragmented", and it lists Accountability and Ecosystem and Vendor Governance as pillars. The gap search reports, from a targeted reading of the HTML, that §6.6 spreads accountability across "the brand owner (franchisor), the property operator (franchisee), and the technology vendor (third party)", and that §6.3.2 treats governance as "a shared obligation embedded contractually across the franchise and vendor network". I have verified none of this.

**Q3. What the guest is owed.** The abstract does not say. Whether 'People-Centred Governance' concerns guests, staff or both cannot be told from it.

**Q4. Escalation, recourse, correction.** Not in the abstract. The gap search reports a brand-wide incident protocol "requiring franchisees and vendors to disclose algorithmic harms within a defined window" (§6.3.2, unverified). That is disclosure among firms, not a route from the guest.

**Q5. Seamless language.** Not in the abstract.

**Q6. What the paper may and may not say.** At the gap sentence the paper may say, on the abstract alone, that Parhi (2026) reviews 162 studies of AI governance in hospitality firms, finds accountability mechanisms 'fragmented', and proposes a framework whose pillars include 'Accountability' and 'Ecosystem and Vendor Governance'. It may not yet say anything about franchisors, franchisees, vendors or contracts in Parhi's name, nor that Parhi offers no ground such as holding out or control, nor give any locator. The gap search's own caution stands: it advises citing Parhi 'for the paradox, not for the empirical gap', because the claim about franchise templates rests on a source (Arif et al. 2026) whose abstract concerns something else. Whether the gap search overstates or understates Parhi cannot be judged until the full text is read.

## 2. Ariyogi, Mulyawan and Junita (2026), 'From AI disclosure to human handoff'

**Q1. Question, design, evidence base.** A structured evidence review plus a conceptual framework. "This structured evidence review examines AI disclosure, human escalation, trust, and organizational accountability. An access-limited synthesis mapped 19 empirical reports published between 2019 and July 2026, supplemented by relevant reviews and conceptual studies." (p. 77). The search ran on one day, by web discovery and Crossref: "Three queries returned 75 records, including 74 distinct DOI records. The first query failed with a rate-limit response" (p. 80). "No Scopus or Web of Science search, preregistration, independent dual screening, or complete PRISMA assessment is claimed here." (p. 79). Four of the nineteen reports were read in full; fifteen were coded from a "publisher abstract or author-affiliated institutional summary" (Table 1, p. 83). The authors present the framework and its five propositions "as a research agenda supported by partial mechanisms, not a validated intervention" (p. 78).

**Q2. Who is accountable, and on what ground.** The authors put recovery with the organization and separate it from both the robot and the employee. "Transparency can tell guests which agent they face, but accountability requires a service organization capable of answering for what happens next." (p. 77). "The present framework therefore assigns recovery ownership at organizational level while distinguishing the employee who performs corrective work from the actors responsible for system design and resource allocation." (p. 79). "An effective response must connect the failure explanation to an identifiable service obligation. The review proposes organizational recovery ownership to describe that connection." (p. 82). "Responsibility for system limitations should remain traceable within the organization" (p. 86).

The grounds are evaluation, attribution and effectiveness. "The guest evaluates the branded service organization through its interfaces and staff, even when technical components are supplied elsewhere." (p. 78). Leo and Huh (2020) "report that customers assign less responsibility to robots than humans for equivalent failures, while assigning more responsibility to the firm when a robot fails. This is a psychological attribution result, not a statement of legal liability." (p. 82). P4 "predicts an effect of ownership communication and corrective capacity, not a transfer of liability." (p. 85). No passage grounds ownership in a role, in holding out, in invited reliance or in control of the guest's space. Other firms enter only in the p. 78 sentence about suppliers of "technical components". (My reading.)

**Q3. What the guest is owed.** The article comes close to directed language once and stops short. Its definition of accountability, "arrangements through which an organization explains decisions, accepts an obligation to respond, and provides a remedy" (p. 79), names an obligation but not to whom it runs or why. "A guest should have an intelligible route to help" (p. 82) is a design 'should', offered among "proposed operational criteria, not validated thresholds" (p. 82) and assessed partly through the guest's "organizational assurance" (pp. 84–85). The words owe, duty, right and entitle do not occur. The article frames the route as good practice and a hypothesis, not as the guest's claim. (My reading.)

**Q4. Escalation and correction.** This is the article's centre, and the overlap with the paper's third duty is close. "Handoff means that responsibility for handling a request moves from one agent to another. An effective handoff therefore requires more than the presence of a staff member: someone must receive the problem, obtain enough information, and be able to act." (p. 79). The framework's four elements are "intelligible identity and capability information; an accessible route to human help; continuity of relevant information across the transfer; and an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery." (p. 84). Intervention "can be triggered by severity, repeated failure, uncertainty, guest preference, or a request outside system authority" (p. 82). P3 expects the benefit of human access to "weaken when the transfer requires substantial repetition or leads to an employee without decision authority." (p. 84). "Managers should also identify who can authorize corrective action. A concierge who receives an escalated complaint but cannot amend a booking may provide emotional reassurance without completing recovery." (p. 86). The authority at the end is "an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery" inside the organization (p. 84). Timeliness is left open: "None of the summarized comparisons establishes a universal maximum waiting time" (p. 82), and time appears only as a field measure, "time to remedy" (p. 87).

**Q5. Seamless language.** None. Seamless, frictionless, friction and smooth do not occur. 'Continuity' always means continuity of information across a transfer (pp. 84–86). The article questions efficiency once: "An efficient conversation that leaves a guest confused about authority is not necessarily successful." (p. 81).

**Q6. What the paper may and may not say.** The paper may say that Ariyogi et al. (2026: 79) assign "recovery ownership at organizational level", that they define an effective handoff as one in which "someone must receive the problem, obtain enough information, and be able to act" (2026: 79), and that they propose "an accessible route to human help" and "an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery" (2026: 84) as testable features. It may say that they rest ownership on how the guest evaluates "the branded service organization" and on attribution findings (2026: 78, 82), and that they deny it settles liability (2026: 79, 82, 85). It may not say that they allocate responsibility among several firms, that they state a duty owed to the guest, that they ground anything in the host's role, holding out, reliance or control, or that they require the route to be timely.

The gap search overstates the article in one respect and understates it in another. It says the article "names one bearer among several firms, the branded organisation the guest deals with". The article never poses the several-firms question: its contrasts are robot against firm (p. 82) and organization against employee (p. 79), and the brand appears only as what the guest evaluates (p. 78). It also scores the route to correction "T4 yes" without noting that the article declines any timing standard (p. 82). In the other direction, the gap search does not quote the handoff definition (p. 79). That sentence is the nearest published wording to the paper's "reaches someone able to correct it", and the paper should engage it directly: the novelty lies in the ground, the bound party and timeliness, not in the route.

## 3. Suh, Nasta, Pirolo and Magnanelli (2026), 'Phygital customer experience and the adoption of immersive technologies in hospitality'

**Q1. Question, design, evidence base.** An exploratory survey with a Bayesian network. "This study aims to explore immersive-technology adoption in hospitality through the Phygital Customer Experience (PH-CX) framework." (abstract, PDF p. 1). "Data was collected from 208 participants exposed to an AI-supported immersive hotel journey scenario. The analysis is exploratory and identifies conditional associations rather than causal effects or latent-variable relationships." (abstract, PDF p. 1). Respondents came through Prolific (§3.2, PDF p. 6) and were "predominantly aged 25–34 (62.0%) and male (71.6%)" (§3.3, PDF p. 8). The scenario "measures anticipated engagement rather than actual adoption behavior" (§3.2, PDF p. 7). The safeguards, roadmap and propositions are conceptual additions, not findings.

**Q2. Who is accountable.** Nobody, in those terms. Accountability, liability, duty and obligation do not occur. 'Responsib-' occurs only as "responsible phygital service design" (abstract), "more responsible and inclusive service innovation" (§6, PDF p. 13) and "responsible automation" (§7, PDF p. 15). The actors told to act are "Hotels" and "firms" (§6.2), "hospitality managers" (§6) and, once, "Policymakers" (§7). No vendor, platform operator or second firm appears; the AI concierge and cross-platform system are described as the hotel's own (§6.2). (My reading.)

**Q3. What the guest is owed.** The article states what guests should get, but as principles and a condition of legitimacy. "A seventh principle, ethical safeguards, is necessary to preserve the human-centric orientation of PH-CX." (§2.3, PDF p. 5). "Operationally, ethical PH-CX design requires four principles. Transparency requires clear disclosure when guests interact with AI or when data is collected. Agency requires meaningful opt-in choices and the ability to decline phygital features without service penalties. Equity requires non-digital alternatives that maintain comparable service access and quality. Privacy requires data minimization, secure handling and guest control over the deletion of sensitive or biometric information." (§2.3, PDF p. 5). Their status: "PH-CX therefore treats ethical safeguards not as an implementation afterthought but as a condition for legitimate and sustainable phygital value co-creation." (§2.3, PDF p. 5). The guest is the beneficiary; the safeguards are not framed as the guest's claim, and no reason is given why a guest is owed them. (My reading.)

**Q4. Escalation and correction.** "AI concierge systems should provide a visible route to human assistance and establish escalation procedures for inaccurate, unsafe or sensitive responses." (§6.2, PDF p. 14). P3 makes the concierge's benefit depend on escalation: its "positive effect will weaken as service-failure severity, emotional sensitivity or the need for discretionary human judgment increases, unless immediate human escalation is available." (§6.2, PDF p. 14). Escalation is also a metric: "escalation rates to employees" (§6.2, PDF p. 14). The article does not say who receives an escalation or what authority they hold, and it mentions no complaint, redress or correction of a failure.

**Q5. Seamless language.** Mostly endorsed, with qualifications. Endorsed: PH-CX is "an integrated customer experience model that seamlessly merges physical and digital touchpoints" (§1, PDF p. 2); it aims "to deliver seamless, co-created and contextually adaptive service encounters" (§4, PDF p. 9); the roadmap's features "enable a seamless, adaptive and inclusive phygital hospitality experience" (§6.2, PDF p. 14). Qualified: "AI-enabled efficiency may reduce friction while eroding the warmth and discretionary care central to hospitality" (§2.3, PDF p. 5); "while omnichannel frameworks emphasize seamless coordination, PH-CX highlights embodied presence and continuity of meaning" (§2.3, PDF p. 5); "The value of phygital service design is contingent rather than automatic." (§6.1, PDF p. 13).

**Q6. What the paper may and may not say.** The paper may say that Suh et al. (2026) add 'ethical safeguards' to PH-CX as a seventh principle comprising transparency, agency, equity and privacy for guests, that they treat these as "a condition for legitimate and sustainable phygital value co-creation", and that they ask hotels to provide "a visible route to human assistance" and "escalation procedures for inaccurate, unsafe or sensitive responses". The article prints no page numbers, so cite it by section or as the journal's style requires. The paper may not say that phygital research leaves unsaid to whom safeguards are owed (Suh et al. name guests and hotels), nor that the article frames them as duties or rights, nor that it says who receives an escalation, nor that its safeguards have empirical support.

The gap search reads the article accurately on substance, with three corrections. "Policymakers have a role to play" is in §7 (Limitations, PDF p. 15), not §6. The gap search expected the PDF to supply page numbers, but it prints none. And it does not note that P3 already conditions the concierge on "immediate human escalation" (§6.2), so immediacy, as a moderator in a proposition, is in the phygital literature; what is missing is immediacy as something owed. One more point bears on the manuscript's wording. The current gap claim speaks of safeguards for "trust, fairness and well-being". Suh et al.'s safeguards protect "transparency, customer agency, equity, privacy and experiential authenticity" (§2.3, PDF p. 5). Neither 'fairness' nor 'well-being' occurs, and 'trust' occurs only as "trustworthy service innovation" (abstract), in a cited study's findings (§2.2) and in P2. If Suh et al. are cited for that sentence, the list should match theirs.

## 4. Wirtz, Kunz, Hartley and Tarbit (2023), 'Corporate digital responsibility in service firms and their ecosystems'

**Q1. Question, design, evidence base.** A conceptual article. "Using the lens of corporate digital responsibility (CDR), we examine these risks and their mitigation in service firms and make five contributions." (Abstract). CDR is "the principles underpinning a service firm’s ethical, fair, and protective use of data and technology when engaging with customers within their digital service ecosystem." (Introduction). The evidence base is a synthesis of "key literature on ethics, privacy, and fairness in the social sciences, information systems, law and government, management, marketing, and service" (Ethics, Privacy, and Fairness Across the Data and Technology Life-Cycles), with input from academics and industry experts (Acknowledgments). No data are collected. General service, not hospitality.

**Q2. Who is accountable, and on what ground.** The focal service firm, on grounds of orchestration and power, with shared governance and regulation behind it. The authors start from the point that "in addition to the focal service firm often several other players are involved" (Origins of CDR Issues in Digital Service Ecosystems). The ecosystem has "the service firm in the center orchestrating value creation, exchange and capture, and governing the ecosystem of customers, complementors, and external business partners." (The Nature of Digital Service Ecosystems). Because vendors' customer-facing technologies "often dictate how a service firm interacts with its customers", "CDR risks need to be discussed between service firms and these partners." (Complementing Partners). They warn that "as service firms have effectively become private regulators of their ecosystems, they are at risk of abusing that position" (External Business Partners). "Particularly large service firms that can exert power within an ecosystem should be “responsible for the conduct and treatment of users throughout the chain” (Martin 2015, p. 75)." (Business Partner Ecosystem). Table 2 adds "Bilateral sharing of digital governance between partners" and "Transparent shared policies on CDR responsibilities need to be disseminated across the ecosystem". When the calculus of profit against responsibility is too negative, "regulation will have to step in." (A Service Firm’s CDR Calculus).

**Q3. What the customer is owed.** The register is principles and trade-offs. The authors "advance that there is a need to make these behaviors and decisions accountable to moral norms and ethical considerations" (Why is CDR So Important in Service?). The one rights sentence reports a view: "Ethical and fair treatment of consumers is increasingly viewed as a fundamental human right, especially in services such as healthcare and utilities" (Customer Interface). The authors add that "the widely discussed normative (i.e., deontological) approach that tells companies what they should do needs to be supplemented by a utilitarian perspective" (Conclusions and Implications for Theory). Customers are beneficiaries of firm principles; the article does not frame any safeguard as a claim a customer holds against a particular firm. (My reading.)

**Q4. Escalation and redress.** Firm-side tools only, all from Table 2: "Formalize compensation to affected consumers when ethics, privacy, or fairness violations occur"; "Empower employees to handle ethical breaches and establish norms of challenging decisions"; "Ensure human oversight is embedded into algorithmic decision-making". In the text, "safeguard systems that respond to any grey areas and CDR concerns that arise should be made explicit." (Internal Service Firm Design). The article describes no route by which a customer raises a failure and no recipient with authority to correct it; escalation, complaint, recourse and redress do not occur.

**Q5. Seamless language.** 'Seamless' does not occur. The one 'friction' is between partner firms, which shared governance should reduce (Business Partner Ecosystem). The article questions convenience: "these indisputable customer benefits lead directly to CDR concerns as customers find it increasingly difficult to opt-out" (The Front-end: Customer and Service Firm Exchanges).

**Q6. What the paper may and may not say.** The paper may say, where it introduces the several firms, that Wirtz et al. (2023) observe that "in addition to the focal service firm often several other players are involved"; that they place the focal firm "in the center orchestrating value creation, exchange and capture, and governing the ecosystem"; that customer-facing technologies come from vendors whose designs "often dictate how a service firm interacts with its customers"; and that service firms have "effectively become private regulators of their ecosystems". Page numbers must come from the PDF. The paper may not say that the article concerns hospitality, grounds responsibility in a role, holding out, reliance or control of space, makes the focal firm the sole bearer, or describes a customer's route to correction. It must also not present the Martin (2015) quotation as Wirtz et al.'s own words.

The gap search's summary, that Wirtz et al. "make the focal service firm the orchestrator and governor of CDR across its vendors and partners", is right but slightly overstated: the article pairs the focal firm's lead with shared governance among partners and with regulation. The gap search is right that the ground is orchestration and power. That makes the article more than background: it is a rival ground for why one firm answers for the chain, and the paper should say what holding out, reliance and control of the guest's space add for hosting.
