## 1. Batat (2024a), "What does phygital really mean?" (PH-CX)

### Q1. Definition and framework

Batat gives the definition at the end of her review of three perspectives: "phygital can be defined as: a holistic and integrative ecosystem that adopts a consumer standpoint as a starting point and then integrates a combination of physical, human, digital and media content elements, platforms, technologies, and extended realities, among others; the goal of phygital is to offer unique and compelling customer experiences that should guarantee a coherent continuum in the delivery process of consumer value (intrinsic/extrinsic) provided from digital to physical and vice versa" (p. 1229).

She restates the goal at the head of the framework: "The goal of phygital is to facilitate the delivery of consistent consumer value in hybrid settings: physical (e.g. stores) and digital (e.g. e-commerce websites, apps, and social media)" (p. 1230), and "Phygital allows companies to provide compelling experiences in hybrid settings by fluidifying how customers navigate through digital spaces and physical places" (p. 1230).

The dual-place process: "The customer's experience is created and shaped through two processes: shifting from physical to digital ('digitalization') and from digital to physical ('physicalization.')" (pp. 1230–1231). She defines each in the summary: "First, the digitalization process is meant to transform physical customer experiences into digital ones" and "Second, the physicalization process refers to the transformation of digital customer experiences into physical ones" (p. 1236). Digitalization should enhance "immersivity, sensoriality, and affectivity"; physicalization focuses on "technicality, practicality, and sociability" (p. 1236), "thus humanizing the contact with customers" (p. 1237).

Framework parts: "three key components: driving forces, connecters [sic], and pillars" (p. 1231); connectors "can be classified into four key categories: media, digital, physical, and human" (p. 1232); "six main pillars of the phygital customer experience: practicality, sociability, immersivity, technicality, sensoriality, and affectivity" (p. 1234).

### Q2. Safeguards, obligations, design requirements

- Technology: "The technology related to phygital should be easy to use, intuitive, and reliable to guarantee a satisfying and compelling customer experience and thus prevent irritation and negative feelings on the part of the customer" (p. 1235); "before a technology is implemented, how ready customers are to adopt it should be investigated" (p. 1236).
- Employees: employees' "empathy capital, which should be an integral part of a company's human resource strategy" (p. 1233).
- Human contact as a requirement that nothing else can replace (p. 1234; quoted under Q4).
- Negative emotions: "It is, therefore, vital to identify the different types of emotions generated in the digital sphere and how positive emotions can be replicated and negative ones prevented" (p. 1236).
- Privacy appears once, as a reason for market failure rather than as a duty: "facial recognition payments did not conquer the market in Europe due to data privacy issues and resistance by consumers (Kroet, 2021)" (p. 1235).
- Risk appears once, reported from others: Veer and Dobele "invite scholars to not only focus on the positive side of phygital experiences but also develop a better understanding of the risks phygital solutions might create" (p. 1227).
- Not found after full read: trust, fairness, transparency, accountability, inclusion or accessibility as requirements. "Well-being" occurs only in the Veer and Dobele summary (p. 1227) and in "food well-being" (p. 1236).

**Reader:** PH-CX prescribes design qualities (usable, reliable, empathetic, consistent), not safeguards for trust, fairness or well-being. Against this text, the first clause of the gap statement overstates.

### Q3. To whom, which party, division among firms

Batat does assign responsibility, but inside a single firm: "the core of the company's overall transformation strategy involves different functions, departments, and skillsets internally and externally" and "Phygital cannot be the sole responsibility, IT, human resources, or marketing departments [sic]. It must be the task of all organizational functions unified, where each function meets a specific need of customers (e.g. functional, social, emotional, technical)" (p. 1237). Human connectors are "the company's employees and staff who are in direct contact with customers (salespeople, customer-service employees, waiters, delivery personnel, and so forth)" (p. 1233). The nearest she comes to several parties is a report of Jacob et al., who name as a managerial problem "the extent, and heterogeneity of the organizational and human resources involved" and find that phygital management "also requires solid governance of resources" (p. 1229).

Not found after full read (all sections, pp. 1220–1238, including the future-research section and the conclusion): any statement of what is owed to the customer as a matter of duty, any party other than "the company" (or "companies"/"brands") bearing a requirement, or any division of responsibility among firms. "Externally" (p. 1237) is mentioned once and left undeveloped.

**Reader:** Batat answers "who" with "the company, all departments together", and "to whom" with "customers' needs". She never treats the provider as plural.

It recurs on pp. 1220, 1221, 1222, 1223, 1226, 1229–1235 and 1237, always as a goal: "Such a framework guarantees a continuum in value delivery by connecting offline and online offerings" (p. 1220); "The goal is to offer compelling phygital experiences that prevent consumer value disconnections" (p. 1231); "The goal is to prevent a disconnect between a positive image displayed on social media, and the real experience lived when interacting with a company's employees in a physical setting" (p. 1233). Feedback occurs once, reported from the omnichannel literature: "These customers are more willing to provide their feedback" (p. 1225). Not found: handover, escalation, complaint, redress.

### Q5. Guest agency; employees; power, control, governance, space

- Agency: the customer is the design's starting point ("the starting point for designing phygital experiences is consumers and their perceptions of the value they can derive from their consumption experiences in the physical setting", p. 1231). Empowerment appears only in the report of Mishra et al. (pp. 1227–1228).
- Employees: the human connector (p. 1233) and its "power" ("This finding reveals the power of human connectors in the phygital customer experience framework, which should not be neglected", p. 1234).
- Space: a setting for experience ("digital spaces and physical places", p. 1230). In the physical space "socialization is left to consumers themselves" (p. 1235).
- Governance: only in the Jacob et al. report (p. 1229). Power or control over guests: not found.

### Q6. Contradicts, narrows, or grounds v8

- **Grounds v8.** The p. 1234 passage is the editor's own ground for the route duty. Her framework holds that a person repairs what the digital connectors break, and that nothing repairs a human failure. v8 can build on it directly: if a human interaction is the fix for a digital failure, someone must keep that human reachable, and when several firms supply the digital connectors the question becomes which of them. (The batat2024phcx card already flagged this passage.)
- **Narrows.** The p. 1237 sentence shows that Batat does talk about responsibility, though she distributes it across the departments of one company. The gap statement should claim the multi-firm case, not the absence of responsibility talk.
- **Wording for v8.** DRAFT_v7 (line 73) paraphrases PH-CX as built "to make experience move smoothly". "Smooth" never appears in PH-CX; Batat's words are "fluidifying" (pp. 1220, 1230, 1235) and "prevent consumer value disconnections" (p. 1231). Using her word would remove a point a referee could pick at. The decision rests with the author.

## 2. Batat and Hammedi (2023), the ERT framework

### Q1. Definition and framework

Batat and Hammedi adopt Batat's definition: "At the center of the ERT framework lies the phygital experience, which is defined as a holistic and integrative concept that adopts a consumer perspective as a starting point. It then integrates a combination of physical, human, digital and media content elements, platforms, technologies and extended realities, among others, to offer unique and compelling experiences that should guarantee a coherent continuum in the delivery process of consumer value provided from the digital to the physical setting and vice versa (Batat, 2022a)" (p. 14).

ERTs are "a set of technological devices, platforms and tools that are integral to the design satisfying [sic] and engaging customer and service experiences within phygital settings, which, in turn, lead to a unified connection between physical and digital environments" (pp. 13–14). They are sorted into four extension processes: cognitive and behavioural (self-tracking, AI, IoT; §3.1, pp. 15–17), social (avatars, chatbots, voice assistants; §3.2, pp. 17–18), sensory (3D visualization, 3D audio, haptics, digital taste and smell; §3.3, pp. 18–19) and contextual (VR, AR, XR; §3.4, pp. 20–22). The stated goal: "Our goal is to offer a new framework ... which can help service researchers and practitioners to design engaging phygital experiences that guarantee a continuum in customer value from physical to digital settings and vice versa" (p. 11).

### Q2. Safeguards, obligations, design requirements

- Human service: "customers also strongly value the personalized attention provided by humans, such as front desk and salespeople (Adam et al., 2021). That is why companies should never replace services delivered through humans by virtual agents, even if they are highly humanized. Moreover, prior research has indicated that virtual agents should not be placed at the beginning of a relationship with customers (Scherer et al., 2015)" (p. 18).
- Failure: the incorporation of social virtual agents "should consider two main aspects: customer skepticism and technical failures linked to the immature aspect of this emerging technology" (p. 18).
- Well-being: "Service research is a human-centered science that aims to elevate [sic] suffering and pain and enhance customer and employee well-being" (p. 25); "solving consumers' vulnerabilities is a top priority in the service field (Rosenbaum et al., 2017)" (p. 25).
- Privacy and security: "the immersive aspects of ERTs take place in digital environments where security and privacy are critical"; "unless safe and protected spaces are offered, ERTs could be highly risky and create serious threats to both consumers and service providers" (p. 26).
- Agenda questions (Table 1, p. 23): "How can ERTs create value for minorities and vulnerable groups (e.g. disabled people)?"; "How do security and privacy issues affect the well-being of customers and employees?"; "What are the downsides of ERTs? How can we mitigate the risks generated by ERTs?"
- Employees: the design goal is experiences "that positively contribute to both the customer's experience and the employee's experience" (p. 11); without good work design "the metaverse could lead to high employee overload, stress and anxiety" (pp. 26–27); engagement technologies "When imposed, ... may increase the stress, anxiety and disengagement of frontline employees" (p. 27).
- Not found after full read: trust, fairness, transparency, accountability or human oversight as requirements. "Fairness" appears only in a reference title.

### Q3. To whom, which party, division among firms

The one requirement with a named bearer is addressed to "companies" ("companies should never replace services delivered through humans by virtual agents", p. 18), and "a company needs to consider from a strategic perspective whether it is better to use a matching or mismatching servicescape" (p. 27). The safety requirement has no bearer at all: "unless safe and protected spaces are offered" (p. 26). In the same sentence the authors cast service providers as potential victims ("serious threats to both consumers and service providers"), not as the party that owes protection. The nearest approach to multiple parties is the remark that "consumers will be interacting with digital agents representing service providers and not only machines" (p. 24), which assumes one provider behind each agent.

Not found after full read (all sections, pp. 10–28, including Table 1 and the conclusion): any statement of what is owed to whom as a duty; any vendor, platform or third party distinct from "the company" or "service provider"; any division of responsibility among firms. "Responsibility", "accountability" and "obligation" do not occur.

**Reader:** p. 26 is the gap in one clause. The authors name the safeguard ("safe and protected spaces") and the people at risk, then use an agentless passive where the duty-bearer would go.

Fluidity and continuum carry the same goal: "Flawless interactivity and connectivity are thus necessary to guarantee an enjoyable and emotionally engaged customer and service experience" (p. 14); "designing fluid and memorable phygital experiences" (p. 17); sensory technologies improve experiences "by preventing disconnects in their shopping journeys" (p. 18); "fluidify shoppers' journeys" (p. 18); "holistic, fluid and memorable experiences" (p. 24). Continuum terms recur on pp. 10, 11, 13, 14, 18, 19, 21, 22 and 24.

Not found: handover, escalation (other than "escalation of the sense of objectification"), complaint, recourse, correction, redress, feedback. The nearest passage is the rule that virtual agents "should not be placed at the beginning of a relationship with customers" (p. 18).

### Q5. Guest agency; employees; power, control, governance, space

- Agency: "QS phygital experiences can cognitively empower or disempower customers" (p. 16), so that customers may "improve their well-being and regain control and autonomy" (pp. 16–17); "the active role of users in ERT-driven service experiences is undeniable. Thus, co-creating with consumers is then enabled and eased" (p. 24).
- Employees: "both employees and customers ... tend to see themselves as just interchangeable sources of profit for organizations" (p. 24); automated frontline employees "can be deployed anytime and anywhere without risks of fatigue, exhaustion, or absence, and of course, at lower costs ... it raises several questions about managing these virtual avatars/automated frontline employees" (p. 27); "The commingling of human/real-world employees and virtual employees is also an area that requires more investigation" (p. 27).
- Relationship: technology "substantially reduced people's direct interaction within the marketplace" (p. 22).
- Space: the servicescape ("special attention should be devoted to the design of virtual servicescapes", p. 27) and "safe and protected spaces" (p. 26). Governance and power over guests: not found.

### Q6. Contradicts, narrows, or grounds v8

- **Grounds v8.** The disintermediation passage (p. 24) and the human-replacement rule (p. 18) are the two sentences v8 can build on. Batat and Hammedi say that removing the human intermediary objectifies, and that companies should never replace human service, and the front desk is their example. They say this to "companies" in the singular. v8 adds the case in which the agent, the channel and the rule come from different firms.
- **Illustrates the gap.** "unless safe and protected spaces are offered" (p. 26) is the cleanest single instance of a prescribed safeguard with no bearer.
- **Narrows.** The text prescribes well-being, vulnerability and privacy concerns mostly as research questions (Table 1, p. 23), not as design requirements, and does not mention trust or fairness.

## 3. Shabnam et al. (2026), Transformative Phygital Service Research (TPSR)

### Q1. Definition and framework

"TPSR conceptualises phygital services as multi-level socio-technical activity systems in which transformative outcomes arise from the structured interaction between system configurations, interactional mechanisms and well-being outcomes" (p. 7). The system configuration has four parts: "artefacts", "rules, encompassing both institutional norms and algorithmically enforced protocols", "communities, consisting of hybrid collectives of human and technological actors" and "divisions of labour, which specify how cognitive, emotional and technical tasks are distributed between humans and intelligent systems" (p. 7). The paper names three discontinuities: "algorithmic mediation of agency, digitally enforced rules and non-human interpretive labour" (p. 1; developed p. 2). On phygitality itself the authors write: "Recent scholarship conceptualises phygitality not merely as channel integration but as the blending of embodied and digital affordances that structure perception, participation and value creation (Batat, 2024a; Batat, 2025; Mele et al., 2024)" (p. 4).

### Q2. Safeguards, obligations, design requirements

Shabnam et al. prescribe more than the other four texts together.

- Managerial (p. 14): "organisations should prioritise transparent artefact design. Adaptive interfaces that allow user adjustment and feedback enhance perceived agency, whereas opaque automation risks alienation. Managers should therefore implement explainable AI practices and participatory design processes. Secondly, algorithmic governance must remain contestable. Digitally enforced rules should incorporate escalation pathways and human oversight to prevent rigid exclusion. Hybrid governance models combining automation with human judgement can preserve fairness and trust. Thirdly, managers should cultivate hybrid communities of support in which human and digital actors complement rather than replace one another. Training staff to collaborate with intelligent systems enhances collective learning and customer confidence."
- Policy (p. 14): "policymakers must ensure that digital infrastructures support equity and dignity. Regulatory frameworks should encourage transparency and accountability in algorithmic governance, particularly in sectors such as healthcare, finance and public services. Policies promoting inclusive access and digital literacy are essential to prevent new forms of vulnerability." And: "Policymakers should consider how automation reshapes employment, responsibility and relational care."
- Propositions: P1a, "When algorithmic decision systems are transparent and allow consumer override, consumers' perceived autonomy and decision confidence increase" (p. 11); human mediation as a condition, "When algorithmic rules override embodied judgement without compensatory human mediation, dignity may decline and perceptions of injustice may intensify" (p. 9); alternative access, "hybrid protocols that reintegrate physical touchpoints, assisted services or alternative access pathways" (p. 11), against "mandatory online registration without alternatives" (p. 12).
- Privacy: "if a brand introduces a digital application to streamline consumer information and checkout without addressing perceived ease of use or potential privacy concerns, it generates systemic disturbance" (p. 10).

### Q3. To whom, which party, division among firms

**Found, in part, and this is the strongest counter-evidence in the set.** Shabnam et al. name who should act: "organisations", "Managers", "firms" (p. 14), "a brand" (p. 10) and "policymakers" and "Regulatory frameworks" (p. 14). They name the beneficiaries as consumers and, for public services, citizens (p. 11). They also talk explicitly about responsibility being redistributed:

- "This redistribution reshapes responsibility structures and relational expectations within service encounters" (p. 6).
- Table 2 names what existing theory cannot explain: AI systems that "diagnose, classify, recommend or triage autonomously, thereby reshaping responsibility, accountability and emotional labour" (p. 5).
- P4: "responsibilities are dynamically allocated between human actors and algorithmic artefacts. Consumers may first interact with chatbots or recommender systems before escalating to human agents, illustrating how expertise, authority and responsibility are redistributed" (p. 12); "hybrid human–AI collaboration may fail when role boundaries are unclear" (p. 12).
- Communities "coordinate knowledge, responsibility and problem-solving between subjects and the service object" (p. 11); positive outcomes require "active cooperation among stakeholders" (p. 11).
- Accountability is left open: "Research should examine whether algorithmic divisions of labour reproduce inequalities, weaken accountability or reshape professional identities" (p. 15).

What is not there, after a full read (all sections, pp. 1–15, including Tables 1–4, the boundary conditions, implications and future research): any division of responsibility among several firms; any statement that a safeguard is owed to the consumer, as against good for the consumer's well-being; any answer to which party keeps the escalation pathway. Responsibility in TPSR divides between human and algorithmic actors, or between managers and policymakers, never between firms. The "organisation" in the managerial implications is singular. The contestability sentence ("algorithmic governance must remain contestable", p. 14) has no bearer.

**Reader:** Table 2 (p. 5) names "responsibility, accountability" in the column of what existing theory cannot explain, but TPSR's resolution in the next column links redistributed labour "to dignity, trust, relational warmth and emotional well-being". It does not carry responsibility or accountability across. v8 can occupy that space.

### Q5. Guest agency; employees; power, control, governance, space

- Agency is TPSR's central variable: "Transparent algorithms enhance autonomy and trust, while opacity reduces control" (p. 1); "When algorithmic actors dominate decision-making without offering adaptable levels of user control" (p. 12).
- Power: TPSR offers an architecture "for understanding how hybrid service ecosystems generate contradictions, power asymmetries and transformation pathways" (p. 6). Governance: "Phygital systems redistribute decision authority, embed governance within code and intertwine human and non-human actors in ways that directly affect autonomy, dignity and well-being" (p. 2).
- Employees: frontline staff and designers as "physical stakeholders" (p. 11); "Training staff" (p. 14); employment (p. 14).
- Space: physical access points removed and restored in public services ("reduced traditional physical access points and eliminated direct contact with civil servants", p. 11; "the reopening of physical counters", pp. 11–12). Hospitality appears once, in a list of sectors (p. 4).

### Q6. Contradicts, narrows, or grounds v8

- **Contradicts (part of the gap statement).** Against this text, "says neither to whom they are owed nor which ... owes them" cannot stand as worded: Shabnam et al. address safeguards to organisations, managers, firms and policymakers (p. 14). What survives is the narrower claim that none of these is one of several firms, and nothing is framed as owed to the guest.
- **Grounds v8.** p. 14 (contestability, escalation pathways, human oversight) and p. 11 (the kiosk button with no human behind it) are v8's nearest neighbours. v8 should cite them and say what it adds: who must keep the escalation pathway when the rules come from one firm, the channel from another and the room from a third; and why the guest can claim that pathway, not merely benefit from it. p. 15 ("weaken accountability") is an open question v8 answers.
- **Context.** "This research was conducted as part of the American Phygital Association Summit 2025" (p. 15). The same association runs the CFP, so the editor will probably know this paper. One co-author, Roten, gives his affiliation as EM Normandie (p. 1), as does Batat in the 2023 and 2024 papers.

## 4. Mameli et al. (2026), "A comprehensive framework for phygital tourism experiences"

### Q1. Definition and framework

In the conclusion, Mameli et al. define phygital as "a distinct experiential form characterised by the seamless integration of sensory, spatial, and technological elements that place visitors at the centre of real-world experiences" (p. 25). Earlier they set it apart "through its holistic and human-centred integration of physical and digital touchpoints" (p. 4); Table 1 gives the core concept as "Holistic integration of physical and digital elements into one integrated experience" (p. 4). The framework presents "three main elements destinations, but also other service suppliers should consider [sic]: 1) phygital design components; 2) types and features; and 3) strategic elements and stakeholders' engagement" (p. 23), arranged on a physical–digital "ratio" continuum after Milgram and Kishino (p. 23).

### Q2. Safeguards, obligations, design requirements

- Table 5 makes two safeguards into defining features (p. 10): "Accessibility | Ensures independent, equitable, and dignified functioning for individuals with diverse access needs (mobility, vision, hearing, cognitive) by providing universally designed products, services, and environments" and "Integrity | Consistency, coherence, stability, permanence, and ethical alignment, emphasising prioritisation of customers security." "Technicality" requires "user-friendly, intuitive, and reliable technology to ensure a positive customer experience" (p. 10).
- Accessibility in practice: "'accessibility' appeared to be the least prominent feature, indicating that despite the potential of technology to provide more inclusive experiences, organisations still face barriers to achieving broad accessibility" (p. 20).
- Privacy and ethics as gaps: "there is a general need to expand the discussion on the opportunities to adopt phygital experiences to increase accessibility and handle customers' ethical and data privacy concerns" (p. 22). Table 7 lists, as knowledge voids or action–knowledge conflicts, "Front staff and operational employees' perspectives", "Privacy and security (regulation) of phygital experiences" and "Inclusivity and accessibility" (p. 22).

## 5. Zheng et al. (2025), "The Phygital Tourism Experience Triad" (AAM pages)

### Q1. Definition and framework

The abstract: "we propose the phygital tourism experience triad (PTET) framework, which conceptualizes experience formation as the alignment of cognitive and embodied actions, technological affordances, and spatial configurations spanning virtual spaces, physical tourism sites, and users' self-location (e.g., home)" (AAM p. 2). The body names the triad itself as three spaces: "The proposed phygital tourism experience triad (PTET) framework integrates three spatial layers— physical tourism space, virtual tourism space, and self-location space—and provides a comprehensive model to analyse the value transition from virtual to physical and vice versa" (AAM p. 18); restated in the conclusion as "a triadic structure, comprising (1) virtual spaces, (2) physical spaces depicted in virtual content, and (3) self-location spaces where virtual experiences occur" (AAM p. 23).

Zheng et al. take PH-CX as their lens and gloss Batat: "The term 'phygital' describes a holistic, integrative ecosystem designed to facilitate seamless connections between physical and digital realms, thereby delivering consistent and enriched experiential value within hybrid environments (Batat, 2024)" (AAM p. 4). They also adopt the dual-place process: "a holistic framework that incorporates the digitalization of physical tourism and the physicalisation of virtual tourism is important" (AAM p. 18); "the virtual‒physical interaction includes mutual transformation processes (Batat, 2024): the virtualization of the physical tourism environment (e.g., the digital twin of tourist attractions) and the physicalisation of the virtual tourism environment (e.g., cyberculture in physical tourist attraction design)" (AAM pp. 20–21).

### Q2. Safeguards, obligations, design requirements

Almost none. The one protective design rule: "distance-calibrated storytelling adapts narrative perspectives to users' emotional thresholds. For example, exploring Van Gogh's illness through the lens of a physician's daughter can elicit empathy while preserving psychological safety" (AAM p. 23). Constraints are engineering problems: "constraints such as limited space, vertigo ..., and disorientation ... may disrupt this integration. Solutions such as omnidirectional platforms and room-scale tracking help bridge this gap" (AAM p. 20). Research questions in Table 5 (AAM p. 22): "What are the possible negative effects of phygital tourism experience on users?" and "How does social interaction with other avatars influence users' well-being in the virtual or physical world?" Trust appears only as a mediating variable ("trust in the destination", AAM p. 8; meta-analytic results, AAM pp. 10–11), never as something to safeguard. Not found: fairness, transparency, privacy, ethics, accountability, human oversight, employees or staff.

### Q3. To whom, which party, division among firms

The texts' actors are "Destinations", which "must embrace this phygital transformation" (AAM p. 3), and "Managers", who "can consider five key spatial fusion modes" and "should adopt spatial configurations that are aligned with their digital maturity, resource availability, and experiential objectives" (AAM p. 21). Not found after full read (all sections, AAM pp. 2–23, including Tables 1–5 and Figure 4's caption): anything owed, to whom, or by which party; any division of responsibility among firms. **Reader:** the self-location space may belong to a third party ("whether it is the user's home or a VR experience centre", AAM p. 18), but the authors treat the space only as a mediator of experience.

## 7. Verdict on the gap statement for these five texts

The strongest counter-evidence is Shabnam et al. (2026). They prescribe safeguards for trust, fairness and well-being, and they say who should provide them: "organisations should prioritise transparent artefact design", "Managers should therefore implement explainable AI practices", "algorithmic governance must remain contestable" with "escalation pathways and human oversight", and "policymakers must ensure that digital infrastructures support equity and dignity" (p. 14). They also note that AI is "reshaping responsibility, accountability and emotional labour" (p. 5) and that consumers escalate "to human agents" (p. 12). Batat (2024a) allocates responsibility explicitly, though across the departments of one company (p. 1237), and Batat and Hammedi (2023) address "companies" (p. 18). So the clause "says neither to whom they are owed nor which ... owes them" fails as worded for Shabnam and is shaky for the other two. The opening clause overstates too: of the five texts only Shabnam et al. prescribe safeguards for trust and fairness. Batat (2024a), Mameli et al. and Zheng et al. never mention fairness, and Batat and Mameli never mention trust (Zheng uses trust only as a mediating variable). Well-being enters Batat and Hammedi and Mameli mainly as a research question or a design label. What survives every full read is the multi-firm claim and the claim about what is owed. In all five texts the duty-bearer, when there is one, is a single "company", "organisation", "brand", "destination" or set of "managers", or else a regulator. None says which of several firms supplying one experience owes a safeguard; Mameli's "stakeholders ... come together" (p. 23) is the nearest they come, and it allocates nothing. None frames a safeguard as something the guest can claim rather than something good for the guest. Shabnam et al. leave accountability open as a question ("weaken accountability", p. 15), and Batat and Hammedi leave the bearer of "safe and protected spaces" (p. 26) unnamed. A narrower statement holds against all five texts (my wording, offered for the author to accept or reject): "Phygital research designs for a continuum of value across physical and digital settings and, where it prescribes safeguards, addresses them to a single firm or to policymakers; it does not say which of several firms owes them, or what the guest may claim." The best two sentences for v8 to build on are Batat's "the negative effects generated by the three connectors, including media, digital, and the physical, can be fixed by positive human interactions" (2024a: 1234), which is the editor's own ground for keeping a correcting person reachable, and Shabnam et al.'s "algorithmic governance must remain contestable. Digitally enforced rules should incorporate escalation pathways and human oversight to prevent rigid exclusion" (2026: 14), the nearest neighbour, whose agentless "must remain" is where v8 places its question of which party.
