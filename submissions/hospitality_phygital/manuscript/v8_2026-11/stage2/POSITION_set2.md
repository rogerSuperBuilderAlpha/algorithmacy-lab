# Positioning reader, set 2: five texts against the v8 gap statement

**The gap statement under test** (working wording from the brief, not a quotation):

> *Phygital research prescribes safeguards for trust, fairness and well-being, but says neither to whom they are owed nor which of several firms owes them.*

**Texts.** Gursoy (2026), *JHMM* 35(2): 151–165; Mosca (editorial), *Journal of Emerging Perspectives* 2: 3–7; Mosca, Civera, Chiaudano and Shakil (2026), *Journal of Macromarketing* 46(3): 426–445; Liu, Benckendorff and Walters (2026), *IJHM* 135: 104640 (pp. 1–11); Sharma and Mattila (2026), *JHTR* 50(6): 904–920. I also read the call for papers (`cfp2025apa`).

**How I read them.** I read each card first and then the whole text page by page, using `pdftotext -f N -l N`, with layout extraction for single-column pages and reading-order extraction for two-column pages, so that every locator is the printed journal page. I viewed the four image-only figures (Mosca et al. Fig. 1; Liu et al. Figs 3–4; Sharma and Mattila Fig. 1) as rendered pages. Q4's term counts come from a regular-expression sweep over every page, with line-end hyphens rejoined, which I then checked against my reading. The call for papers PDF has four unnumbered pages (text on pp. 1–3, references on p. 4), not two, so I cite it as "CFP p. N" by PDF page.

**One finding that frames all five.** Only one of the five texts uses the word *phygital*. Mosca et al. (2026) use it throughout. Gursoy writes "hybrid hospitality", Liu et al. write "robotic hospitableness", and neither Sharma and Mattila nor Mosca's editorial uses either term. The editorial never mentions hospitality, tourism or guests; its one "customer service" is in banking (p. 6). The gap statement's subject, "phygital research", therefore rests on Mosca et al. and the call for papers alone. The other three hospitality texts are adjacent hospitality-AI research.

## 0. Call for papers (cfp2025apa)

The call prescribes safeguards in the passive of design, and its two *must* sentences have no party as their subject. "Phygital hospitality must therefore prioritize human-centric strategies that enhance, rather than dictate, guest agency, satisfaction, and well-being" (CFP p. 2). "As in phygital consumer environments, hospitality settings must support seamless transitions between physical and digital touchpoints, ensuring continuity, trust, and emotional engagement" (CFP p. 2). It names the parties only as people to be centred in design: "By placing all stakeholders, including guests, hosts, employees, and communities, at the center of the experience design process" (CFP p. 2). It raises power: "emerging hierarchies of access and participation, potentially reinforcing or disrupting existing power structures within hospitality organizations" (CFP p. 2). It treats feedback as an input to design: "how guest preferences, emotional needs, and feedback shape phygital hospitality design" (CFP p. 2), and "approaches that center guest feedback and lived experience" (CFP p. 3). One listed topic is "Power structures and guest-staff relationships in phygitally mediated hospitality" (CFP p. 3).

My reading: the call endorses seamlessness and continuity as requirements, and it hosts the paper's question without asking it. Its subjects are "phygital hospitality" and "hospitality settings", never a firm. Feedback appears as something that shapes design, never as the guest's route to someone who can correct a failure in the guest's own case.

## 1. gursoy2026reconceptualizing

### Q1. Definition and framework

Gursoy never uses *phygital* (0 occurrences after a full read). His construct is hybrid hospitality: "Hybrid hospitality, defined as the co-creation of guest experiences through the intertwined agency of humans and AI-enabled systems, represents a profound theoretical shift in hospitality studies" (p. 154). It replaces the dyad with "a reframing toward a triadic model involving customers, employees, and intelligent technologies" (p. 152), and it "reframes experience co-creation as an emergent property of human-machine assemblages rather than a dyadic interaction between employees and guests" (pp. 154–155). Gursoy treats welcome as an experience: "the ontological core of hospitality, the embodied sense of being welcomed, recognized, and cared for" (p. 162).

### Q2. Safeguards prescribed

- Governance: "Responsible governance therefore requires institutionalized mechanisms such as algorithmic audits, transparency protocols, and explicit guest consent" (p. 155). "Continuous auditing, transparent decision pathways, and cross-functional accountability structures help safeguard fairness, guest autonomy, and experiential integrity" (p. 159). "Ethical governance structures must be institutionalized rather than improvised: algorithmic auditing, responsible data stewardship, explainability protocols, and accountability mechanisms should become core organizational capabilities" (p. 162).
- Transparency and privacy: "Ethical transparency must become a foundational principle, requiring firms to clearly communicate how AI is used, what data it collects, and how guests’ interests and privacy are safeguarded" (p. 158). Guest control: "offering opt-in personalization, which allows guests to determine the extent of AI-driven tailoring they are comfortable with" (p. 158).
- Legitimacy conditions: "Privacy, fairness, transparency, and authenticity are not optional safeguards but foundational conditions for the legitimacy of AI-enabled hospitality" (p. 161).
- Human fallback: "Hybrid models that integrate AI diagnostics with human-led resolution can improve response speed while preserving empathy and accountability" (p. 157). Firms should "enable scenario planning, risk mitigation, rapid human intervention during technological failures, and resilience during crises" (p. 159). Systems should "allow guests to choose their preferred level of human or AI engagement" (p. 157).
- Employees: AI "must be accompanied by training, emotional support structures, and an organizational culture that values human competencies" (p. 156).
- Accessibility appears once, as a benefit: "Thoughtfully implemented AI can enhance accessibility" (p. 153).

### Q3. To whom, by which party, divided how

Partly found. Gursoy names a single bearer, the hospitality firm, and he implies an addressee, the guest, through a firm–guest relation.

- Bearer: "As AI becomes embedded in service encounters, hospitality firms transition from being providers of human-centric service to stewards of algorithmic governance" (p. 155). "More importantly, hospitality firms must acknowledge that the introduction of AI is not ethically neutral: it redistributes power, shifts responsibility, and reframes the moral landscape of service delivery" (pp. 155–156).
- Addressee: AI "reshapes the social contract between hospitality firms and their guests" (p. 158), and firms must say "how guests’ interests and privacy are safeguarded" (p. 158). He closes the agenda on "safeguarding guest dignity in hybrid environments" (p. 160).
- Division inside the firm: "Hybrid systems that consciously allocate competence to AI and warmth to human employees may mitigate these tensions" (p. 155). "human employees assume heightened responsibility for relational, symbolic, and emotionally nuanced forms of engagement that remain technologically inimitable" (p. 161). Because AI touches "operations, human resources practices, marketing, IT, and revenue management simultaneously, firms must adopt coordinated systems-theoretical approaches" (p. 159).
- The question left open: "Algorithmic governance shifts authority from interpersonal judgment to computational logics, raising questions about accountability when harm, bias, or exclusion arise" (p. 160). He names the theory gap as theories that "insufficiently account for algorithmic decision-making, distributed responsibility, and technologically mediated emotion work" (p. 154).
- **Division among several firms: not found after full read.** No vendor, supplier, platform provider or third party appears anywhere. Gursoy names "networks of human actors, algorithms, data infrastructures, cultural norms, and institutionalized practices" (p. 161) as the unit of future theory, not as parties that owe. Sections checked: Introduction; Reframing co-creation; Challenges (all six subsections); Ethical considerations; Theoretical and managerial foundations; Research agenda; Conclusions (pp. 151–162).

My reading: Gursoy answers "which party" with one firm, and "to whom" with the guest by implication. He names "distributed responsibility" as the gap (p. 154) and then assigns everything to the firm.

### Q4. Seamless, friction, continuity; interruption and recourse

| Term | Page | Stance |
|---|---|---|
| "seamless yet uncanny" | 156 | Questioned: seamlessness paired with a cost ("can feel efficient but emotionally hollow, or seamless yet uncanny", p. 156) |
| "removing friction" | 156 | Endorsed: "AI may enhance the flow of an experience by removing friction, thereby enabling more meaningful human interactions elsewhere" (p. 156) |
| "reducing friction in service journeys" | 162 | Endorsed |
| "streamline operations"; "streamline decision processes" | 153; 154 | Endorsed |

*Frictionless*, *smooth* and *continuity* do not occur. Disruption is a harm, not a design point: "algorithmic errors, biased recommendations, or context-insensitive responses can introduce forms of experiential disruption that are not easily mitigated through embodied empathy or improvisation" (p. 154). His service-recovery section (p. 157) comes closest to recourse. AI "performs well in diagnosing issues, routing problems, or offering standardized remedies, but often struggles with ambiguity, emotional nuance, and ethical judgment" (p. 157). "Poorly calibrated systems may escalate rather than resolve emotional tensions" (p. 157). "Crisis moments such as unexpected disruptions, interpersonal conflicts, safety concerns require interpretive empathy, moral reasoning, and improvisation qualities that remain uniquely human" (p. 157). Complaint, feedback, correction and redress do not occur.

My reading: Gursoy is the nearest precursor of the paper's in-stay route. He recommends human-led resolution and "rapid human intervention during technological failures" (p. 159) as designs that "can improve" outcomes. He does not say the guest is owed them.

### Q5. Agency, staff, power, space

- Guest agency is conditional: "These dynamics render customer agency conditional upon design characteristics, data infrastructures, and the sociocultural resources that shape digital literacy" (p. 154). "Surveillance-based optimization may normalize intrusive forms of monitoring that compromise guest autonomy" (p. 155). Guests "become more active co-designers of their service trajectories, exercising agency through AI-mediated interfaces" (p. 161).
- Power and pricing: "AI systems collect, infer, and act upon guest data in ways that reshape power relations across the service ecosystem. Recommendation engines guide choices; dynamic pricing algorithms shape access" (p. 155). "These practices shift agency toward centralized technological systems that often operate opaquely from the perspectives of both guests and frontline employees" (p. 155).
- Staff: "algorithmic management tools, such as performance dashboards, customer sentiment analytics, automated task allocation, can create new pressures, reducing employee autonomy or amplifying managerial surveillance" (p. 156).
- Space: "As AI intervenes in spaces once governed by embodied attentiveness" (p. 160).
- Guest feedback as voice: not found.

### Q6. Against the gap; usable ground

- **Narrows the gap.** Gursoy names a bearer (hospitality firms, pp. 155, 158) and a firm–guest "social contract" (p. 158). "Says neither to whom" overstates the case for him. What he lacks is any division among several firms.
- **Ground v8 should cite.** (a) p. 160, authority moving to "computational logics" and the open accountability question, as the card already advises. (b) p. 155: "dynamic pricing algorithms shape access" and systems that "operate opaquely from the perspectives of both guests and frontline employees". This passage supports both the paper's pricing case and its claim that mediation can move the person able to correct a failure out of the guest's path. (c) pp. 157 and 159: human-led resolution and "rapid human intervention during technological failures", as the design-side precursor that the paper turns into a duty.

## 2. mosca2026ai (Mosca, editorial essay, *Journal of Emerging Perspectives* 2: 3–7)

### Q1. Definition and framework

The essay does not use phygital, hospitality or guest. Its frame is strategic: "the essay argues that AI should be understood not merely as a productivity-enhancing technology, but as a general-purpose strategic infrastructure that reconfigures the very nature of managerial cognition and organizational design" (p. 3).

### Q2. Safeguards prescribed

- Oversight as a condition of value: "AI systems tend to deliver the greatest benefits when integrated into end-to-end processes with clear human oversight, feedback loops, and accountability mechanisms" (p. 4).
- Managerial competencies: "Managers must therefore develop new competencies in AI literacy, model governance, and ethical oversight" (p. 5).
- Governance as advantage: "Firms that can integrate AI within robust risk management, model validation, and accountability frameworks are better positioned to capture value while maintaining trust and regulatory compliance" (p. 6).
- Ethics as risk: "These are not externalities; they are core business risks. AI systems can replicate and amplify existing biases, spark serious concerns about privacy and surveillance, and introduce dangerous forms of systemic risk" (p. 6).
- Regulation reported, not endorsed: "Such frameworks are imposing tough new obligations: transparency, accountability, and stringent human oversight, especially for high-risk systems. For global firms, this is where strategy gets complicated" (p. 6).
- Labour: AI's "effects are brutally uneven across occupations and regions. Firms face strategic choices regarding large-scale reskilling and workforce redesign" (p. 6).
- Resilience: "Firms must develop resilience strategies that include diversified suppliers, continuous monitoring, and integration of AI risks into enterprise risk management" (p. 6).

### Q3. To whom, by which party, divided how

- Bearer: firms and managers (pp. 4–6, above).
- **To whom: not found after full read.** No customer, guest or user appears as the party owed. Sections checked: Introduction; AI and the transformation of managerial processes; Global investment landscape; Sectoral frontiers; Ethical, regulatory, and societal challenges; Future competitiveness; Managerial and research implications (pp. 3–7).
- Several firms: seen, and treated as strategy. "Ecosystem dynamics become central. A small number of platform providers control critical AI infrastructures, while downstream firms integrate these capabilities into sector-specific applications. This creates new dependencies and bargaining power asymmetries, alongside opportunities for collaboration and co-innovation. Managing these relationships is a core strategic task" (p. 6). Elsewhere he names "a dangerous dependency on a handful of providers" (p. 5), and "Firms must therefore make strategic choices about where to build proprietary AI assets and where to rely on external platforms" (p. 6).
- Accountability divided, inside one organization: "In healthcare, AI-assisted diagnostics reshape professional roles, decision rights, and accountability structures among clinicians, radiologists, and support staff" (p. 4). Agentic systems bring "new risks related to error propagation, accountability, and control" (p. 5).

My reading: a special-issue editor describes the stack the paper studies, with platform providers upstream and downstream firms building on them, and he treats it as a question of bargaining and resilience. He does not ask who answers the end user. That supports the gap's second clause in an editor's own words, but the essay is not phygital research and prescribes no guest-facing safeguard.

### Q4. Seamless, friction, continuity; interruption and recourse

The essay uses friction once, as a cost to firms and not to users: "Regulatory fragmentation across jurisdictions creates immense friction, demanding highly differentiated deployment and governance strategies" (p. 6). *Seamless*, *frictionless*, *continuity* and *smooth* do not occur; "disruption" (pp. 4, 5) means industry disruption. Recourse: "feedback loops" (p. 4) appear as a property of a process. Complaint, escalation, redress and correction do not occur.

### Q5. Agency, staff, power, space

- Control: "Strategic leadership in the AI era increasingly entails the ability to design human–AI systems that balance autonomy and control" (p. 5).
- Power: "bargaining power asymmetries" (p. 6).
- Staff and tasks: "AI systems redistribute tasks between humans and machines and often require deep re-engineering of workflows rather than simple automation of existing routines" (p. 4). "Strategic work shifts from information generation to framing, validation, and interpretation" (p. 4).
- Governance: the agenda includes "the governance of AI ecosystems" (p. 7).
- Guest agency, guest feedback, space: not found after full read.

### Q6. Against the gap; usable ground

The essay does not contradict the gap; it cannot count toward the gap's first clause, since it prescribes nothing to guests. v8 can cite p. 6 for a guest editor's own description of several firms behind one service, and pp. 4–5 for human oversight and accountability, as a position the editor holds. It is not evidence about phygital hospitality.

## 3. mosca2025phygital (Mosca, Civera, Chiaudano and Shakil 2026, *Journal of Macromarketing*)

### Q1. Definition and framework

This is the only text in the set that is phygital research by name. It defines phygital museums twice on p. 427. The first definition follows Batat: "phygital museums - holistic ecosystems of humans, digital platforms, technology, and contents where digital and physical elements converge to create seamless, multilayered experiences that should guarantee a coherent continuum in the delivery, circulation, and capturing of value (Batat, 2024)" (p. 427). The second is the authors' own: "The definition conceptualizes phygital museums as dynamic, human-centric, tri-dimensional systems where physical and digital spaces merge into a seamless continuum in the delivery of value while enhancing museums’ effectiveness, innovation, and positive impact" (p. 427). For phygital in general they write that it "entails the blend and the symbiosis of physical and digital realms in a coherent continuum experience" (p. 430), and Table 1 glosses phygitalization as "A holistic ecosystem where physical and digital dimensions converge into a seamless continuum of value creation" (p. 433). The framework has three levels (micro motivations, meso catalysts, macro outcomes) and three dimensions (space, time, scope), running from value capturing through co-creation to value amplification (Fig. 1, p. 436). Amplification "refers to the museum’s enhanced capacity to generate and diffuse cultural, social, technological, and relational value well beyond the boundaries of the institutional space and the temporality of the visit" (p. 438). They fault the phygital literature, Batat's PH-CX among it (pp. 430–431), for "a narrow customer focus resulting from considering phygitalization as a pure company’s channel marketing strategy" (p. 427).

### Q2. Safeguards prescribed

- Human facilitation: "curators and technologists must ensure that visitors are assisted (empowered) by human facilitators in the use and understanding of technology throughout the whole visit to avoid abandoning the experience" (p. 434). "Visitors must be guided—not overwhelmed—by technology" (p. 437).
- Inclusion and well-being: "At the micro level, phygital museums foster individual well-being by enhancing accessibility, learning opportunities, and inclusive participation" (p. 439).
- Ethics and inequality: "value amplification is not a given consequence of phygitalization. On the contrary, it requires deliberate strategy, continuous evaluation, and ethical sensitivity. Without these, the use of digital technologies risks reinforcing existing inequalities" (p. 438).
- Transparency, as authenticity: curators "are expected to safeguard authenticity through practices of phygital curation: clearly distinguishing between what is original and what is digitally reconstructed, and opening up access to multiple perspectives. This transparency aligns with ICOM’s (2022) call for inclusive and ethical participation" (p. 440).
- Co-design: "phygital museum experiences should be co-designed with visitors and a range of stakeholders, including managers, curators, exhibition designers, and digital technologists" (p. 440).
- **Privacy: not found after full read.** The article presents visitor tracking only as a gain: "the museum uses visitor interaction tracking and real-time data collection to estimate its educational impact, visitor engagement, and traffic flow for future exhibition design" (p. 437).

### Q3. To whom, by which party, divided how

Found within one institution. The article names bearers and the visitor as the party served.

- The museum: "empowering visitors and stakeholders with the tools and knowledge to understand how technology and artifacts reinforce each other is the museum’s responsibility" (p. 437).
- Professionals inside it: "curators and technologists must ensure that visitors are assisted (empowered)" (p. 434). Stakeholders' motivations "must be identified and addressed by managers, who are responsible for aligning institutional strategies with stakeholder needs (see Freeman et al., 2020) and for building partnerships with schools, communities, and sponsors" (p. 440). For exhibition designers and technologists, "Their task is to avoid what we refer to as 'siloed doing'" (p. 440).
- Several organizations share value and burden, not duties. "the feats achieved by the Deep Time exhibition are not those of the Smithsonian National Museum alone but also belong to their design partners, the digital experience design team, paleontologists, research labs, and other collaborating stakeholders" (p. 437). Phygital museums rely on "interactive digital platforms that distribute the burden and ownership of content creation" (p. 438), and "neither the organization nor the stakeholders possess all the necessary resources to be able to create value independently or in isolation" (p. 436).
- Fig. 1 lists "Shared responsibility" as a macro-level social outcome (p. 436). The phrase appears nowhere in the body text, so the article never says who shares what.
- **Division of obligations to the visitor among several organizations: not found after full read.** Sections checked: Introduction; Theoretical Foundations (both subsections); Conceptualizing Phygitalization (all three criticisms); Research Design; Framework (micro, meso, macro; Propositions 1–6); Implications for Theory and Practice; future research (pp. 426–441).

My reading: the one phygital text answers "to whom" (visitors) and "which party" (the museum and its staff) for a single institution. When it meets several organizations, it divides credit and the "burden ... of content creation", not what anyone owes the visitor.

### Q4. Seamless, friction, continuity; interruption and recourse

Every use endorses seamlessness or continuity; none questions it.

| Page | Use |
|---|---|
| 427 | "seamless, multilayered experiences that should guarantee a coherent continuum" (with Batat); own definition "a seamless continuum in the delivery of value"; "This continuum amplifies relational, social, cultural, and technological value" |
| 429 | time "spans a continuum that includes prior experiences, in-the-moment engagement, and post-visit reflection" |
| 430 | "a coherent continuum experience"; Nofal et al. reported: experiences are more memorable "when digital contexts seamlessly integrate and overlay physical ones" |
| 433 | Table 1: "a seamless continuum of value creation" |
| 437 | "The convergence of stakeholders and resources into a seamless visitor journey requires deliberate design"; Batat's connectors "ensuring continuity between online and offline touchpoints" |
| 439 | Proposition 5: "A coherent hybrid space ensures continuity in the visitor journey and enables engagement to develop seamlessly across both realms"; "uninterrupted narrative"; "The phygital principle demands a strong plot, a coherent continuum in the delivery process of value co-creation" |
| 440 | "coherent and continuum experiences of phygitalization" |
| 440–441 | "a seamless continuum in which physical exhibits, digital content, and human mediation are intertwined" |

Interruption is a failure of the whole: "a compelling, immersive, larger-than-life, and uninterrupted narrative. However, if at any point the plot falters, it becomes irrelevant how splendid the individual elements are" (p. 439). Complaint, escalation, handover, redress and correction do not occur. Feedback appears only as a research and design method, "capturing iterative feedback, refining phygital strategies" (p. 441), and as the authors' own conference feedback (p. 435). The nearest thing to visitor voice is dialogue: curators encourage audiences "to question, compare, and co-interpret it in dialogue with the museum" (p. 440).

My reading: Mosca et al. place human mediation inside the seamless continuum (pp. 440–441), not at a break where the visitor reaches someone. A faltering plot is a design failure (p. 439), not the moment something is owed to the visitor.

### Q5. Agency, staff, power, space

- Voice and empowerment: "stakeholders must possess the means, the knowledge and the tools to be fully aware of the organization’s purpose, to align (or not) with the organization’s values and be able to express their voices, ideas, creativity" (p. 434). Visitors "should be included even in defining how the catalysts, such as resources and knowledge, for phygitalization must be combined" (p. 437).
- Staff: "the role of mediators, educators, and guides becomes essential in empowering by facilitating interpretation and encouraging dialogue" (p. 437).
- Power: value is amplified through "the reduction of power asymmetries among stakeholders" (p. 431). "Unlike traditional museums, which often centralize curatorial authority, phygital museums rely on cooperation" (p. 438).
- Agency and space: phygitalization "reconfigures the spatial and relational boundaries of cultural experience, extends its temporal reach, and redistributes agency among institutional and non-institutional actors" (p. 426).
- Governance: the article offers "actionable insights on how to design, govern, and evaluate phygital strategies" (p. 426).

### Q6. Against the gap; usable ground

- **Narrows the gap.** The one phygital text does name a bearer (the museum, its managers, curators and technologists) and a beneficiary (visitors) (pp. 434, 437, 440). The open part is narrower: dividing duties among several organizations, and what is owed at a break.
- **Ground.** (a) pp. 427, 439 and 440–441: the field's commitment to a seamless continuum, in a paper co-authored by a special-issue editor (Mosca), with Batat as its Associate Editor (p. 441), and written "as part of the American Phygital Association Summit 2025" (p. 441). (b) The undefined "Shared responsibility" outcome (Fig. 1, p. 436) is an opening v8 can fill.

## 4. liu2026hospitableness (Liu, Benckendorff and Walters 2026, *IJHM*)

### Q1. Definition and framework

Liu et al. never use *phygital*. They take hospitableness from Tasci and Semrad, "a construct to initiate emotional response in guests based on hosts’ traits, attitudes, and behaviors when dealing with guests" (p. 2), and stress that it is "fundamentally unidirectional (even though rapport building can be the downstream effect of hospitableness): a host’s disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity" (p. 2). Their construct: "we define robotic hospitableness as the capacity of robots to deliver hospitality experiences through emotionally intelligent, context-aware, and personalized interactions" (p. 8). Its three dimensions are Robot-Mediated, Robot-Assisted and Robot-Personified Hospitableness (abstract, p. 1; Fig. 4, p. 9). They locate it in the guest: "robotic hospitableness is relationally experienced by guests rather than internally possessed by the machine" (p. 9).

### Q2. Safeguards prescribed

- Data: "This tension requires trustworthy personalization: transparency in data use, guest control over personal information, and revocable consent as core expressions of care" (p. 8).
- Space and intrusion: "True hospitableness requires calibrating spatial behavior to individual preferences – being available without being intrusive" (p. 6).
- Staff for hard cases: robot-assisted hospitableness works "while preserving human touchpoints for complex interactions" (p. 7).
- Inclusion, from a participant: "Vibrations could help people with vision impairment perceive the interaction better" (P11, p. 7), which the authors gloss as "a form of inclusivity" (p. 7).
- Cultural respect: honorifics as "expressions of cultural hospitableness" (p. 6).

### Q3. To whom, by which party, divided how

The guest is the party served throughout. A bearer appears only as attribution: the robot's conduct counts as the hotel's.

- "Using appropriate honorifics signals that the robot (and by extension, the establishment) recognizes and respects the guest’s cultural identity" (p. 6).
- "When robots and staff are visibly aligned, guests experience the organization as caring and well organized" (p. 7).
- The subheading "Effort reduction as host responsibility" (p. 7) uses duty language for what a host does for a guest.
- For practice: "operators can identify suitable configurations based on guest expectations and available AI capabilities" (p. 9).
- One participant touches the several-systems problem: "If you tell one robot something, will every robot know it?" (P12, p. 8). The authors treat it as a privacy-personalization paradox.
- **Accountability, liability, who answers when the robot fails, division among firms: not found after full read.** Sections checked: Introduction; Literature review (2.1–2.3); Methodology (3.1–3.5, Tables 3–4); Findings (4.1–4.3); Conclusions, limitations (pp. 1–9).

### Q4. Seamless, friction, continuity; interruption and recourse

- "Service coordination involves seamless collaboration between robots and staff" (p. 7). Endorsed.
- "relational continuity" (p. 8, three times; p. 9), for example "AI Memory enables relational continuity by linking service encounters across visits" (p. 8). Endorsed as the top of the hierarchy.
- A participant warns: "Missing these signals could disrupt the interaction flow" (P12, p. 8).
- *Frictionless* and *smooth* do not occur.
- Recourse: another participant warns that a robot that misses discomfort "could become the biggest annoyance and result in negative feedback" (P14, p. 8). The video stimulus's farewell includes a "24/7 availability reminder; contact information" (Table 3, p. 4), which the findings never analyse. Escalation, complaint, redress and correction do not occur.

### Q5. Agency, staff, power, space

- Guest space: "This tension reveals a key hospitableness paradox: proactive welcome versus respect for guest space" (p. 6).
- Guest control over data: p. 8 (above).
- Staff: "AIs and robots could do maybe 60 % of basic functions at a hotel check-in… but for the remaining 40 %, which is about hospitality" (P10, p. 7). The authors identify "how robots help redistribute emotional labor" (p. 7).
- Power appears only as method: "Online interviewing broadened participation and reduced power asymmetries" (p. 4).
- Governance: not found.

### Q6. Against the gap; usable ground

Liu et al. do not narrow the which-firm clause; the study is bounded by "a single service touchpoint (check-in)" (p. 9) and rests on a designed video, not deployed robots (p. 9). Usable ground: (a) "As robots increasingly occupy frontline roles, the question shifts from whether they can perform tasks to whether they can meaningfully host" (p. 9), a question v8 answers by treating hosting as a position that owes. (b) "the robot (and by extension, the establishment)" (p. 6). My reading: this is a hospitality-research precedent for attributing a machine's welcome to the party that holds itself out, which v8's holding-out step can cite.

## 5. sharmamattila2025rights (Sharma and Mattila 2026, *JHTR*)

### Q1. Definition and framework

Sharma and Mattila never use *phygital*. They examine "whether—and with what consequences—hospitality service robots should be afforded rights and responsibilities" (p. 904) through stakeholder theory with moral, ethical and legal lenses. They define service robots by "two essential characteristics: (1) they perform a service-related function involving engagement with customers, and/or the public, and (2) they can perform these interactions with a certain level of autonomy" (p. 906). "A robot can therefore be considered a civil, cultural, economic, and legal/organizational entitlement, as well as a responsibility assigned as a duty, with accountable oversight, both governed by moral and ethical expectations for human and non-human entities" (p. 906). Their framework (Fig. 1, p. 914) sets nine themes around "Robotic rights & responsibilities", listed on p. 913.

### Q2. Safeguards prescribed

- A full list: "businesses will need to adopt more specific metrics that measure ethical and moral compliance in robotics centered around key principles of diversity, non-discrimination and fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy and data governance, societal and environmental well-being, technical robustness and safety, and human agency and oversight (Palumbo et al., 2024)" (p. 913).
- Firm practice: "hospitality enterprises need to establish clear policies regarding the use of robotics and AI. They also need to safeguard sensitive data and regularly monitor the use of robots and AI" (p. 913).
- Data: "whether the user has been provided with appropriate information about the types of data being collected, the levels of security in place to protect against a breach, and who would be responsible for a data breach" (p. 911).
- Access: robotic technology "would not be easily accessible to customers of all ages and demographics" (p. 910).
- Bias: "hospitality companies would need to assess how to prevent human biases from being fed into service robots" (p. 912).
- Employees: "How will companies provide opportunities for retraining and reskilling employees in the new context of robotization?" (p. 911).

### Q3. To whom, by which party, divided how

This is the closest text in the set, and it contradicts the gap statement as worded. Sharma and Mattila do three things.

**(a) They say to whom, and what is owed.** "From the customer’s perspective, responsibilities relate to safety, security, and delivering the promise of service quality and reliability" (p. 911). "one of those responsibilities could be that employees must ensure guest safety by following specific operational protocols" (p. 915). My reading: these are the paper's two core duties, care for the guest's safety and diligent performance of what was undertaken, named in a hospitality journal as responsibilities toward the customer.

**(b) They pose the several-parties question, and leave it open.**
- "In the case of an accident due to a robot's malfunction, which cannot be attributed to human error, the question of who to hold responsible will eventually have to be answered" (p. 905).
- "If an undercooked batch of burgers leads to an outbreak of illness, who should be blamed? The restaurant company, the robotic manufacturer who provided the robot, or the algorithm creators who made it? Or was the manager on duty responsible for ensuring the robot was functioning properly?" (p. 911)
- "Service businesses are concerned about third-party liability issues (Nevejans, 2016) resulting from damages caused by autonomous robots. Such damages could be due to design, construction, software, operator issues, and interactions with third parties" (p. 909). They think it "possible that hospitality and tourism businesses will outsource much of this development to technology companies" (p. 909). "While much of the liability is still being directed at companies and individuals that create, develop, and operate robots, increased autonomous operations could challenge that approach" (pp. 908–909).
- "If a service robot is given the responsibility to ensure guest safety (e.g., a security robot), who is liable if it fails to prevent harm to a customer or a coworker? How should management and owners prepare for such questions?" (p. 915)
- On staff: "Retraining and retooling employees for alternative employment opportunities will have to be someone’s or a shared responsibility" (p. 905).

**(c) They land on the robot and on the firm's risk.** The robot becomes the bearer: "Robots would be responsible for ensuring effective and safe customer service" (p. 910); cooking robots "would now be responsible for ensuring safe food preparation, and, more broadly, food safety" (p. 911). The firm becomes the manager of its own exposure: "hospitality businesses must be aware of the risks associated with hardware and software" (p. 909); "to safeguard themselves against legal exposure, hospitality and tourism companies should collaborate with legal experts" (p. 913); and the conclusion that "thinking about robots' rights and responsibilities is essentially about business governance and risk management for the hospitality and tourism industries" (p. 915). The managerial checklist (Table, p. 915) names "robot manufacturers" and "Policy makers, industry associations", but in a column headed "Stakeholder Impact". My reading: the table lists who is affected by each step, not who owes the guest what.

They also state the correlation of claim and duty, for employees: "The claim has a counterclaim attached to it, which relates to the duty of the person against whom the moral right is being asserted, such as the employer's duty towards employees. Therefore, it can be viewed as a reciprocity of claim and responsibility" (p. 907).

### Q4. Seamless, friction, continuity; interruption and recourse

*Seamless*, *frictionless*, *continuity* and *smooth* do not occur after a full read. Recourse appears three times, none as the guest's route. Escalation, redress and correction do not occur.

### Q5. Agency, staff, power, space

- Consent and profiling: "customers are passively allowing consent for the use of this data" (p. 911); "In a subtle manner, this could represent a case of guest profiling by default" (p. 911).
- Space and privacy: consumers report "interpersonal privacy concerns arising from the robot's physical presence" (p. 911). Hotels may need "robot-friendly facilities in both design and theme" (p. 910).
- Staff: "a key concern is how the industry will responsibly reassign employees who will no longer be needed to complete tasks that can be automated" (p. 911); "managers may well end up being the arbitrators on the front line of hospitality and tourism businesses" (p. 911).
- Consumer power: "consumers may be the ones who ultimately determine whether robots should have rights or not" (p. 915).
- Governance: the abstract's emphasis is "governance and risk-management for the industry for eventually clarifying accountability (liability, safety, data protection, consent), enhancing guest trust, and preparing for imminent regulatory expectations" (p. 904).

### Q6. Against the gap; usable ground

- **Strongest counter-evidence in the set.** Sharma and Mattila say to whom (customers and guests) and what (safety, security, the promised quality) (pp. 911, 915), and they name the several candidate parties (pp. 909, 911). The gap statement's "says neither to whom ... nor which of several firms" is false of them as written. They are not phygital research, though.
- **What survives.** They never answer the which-party question. They put it after harm ("who should be blamed?", p. 911; "who is liable", p. 915), and they convert it into the hospitality firm's liability and risk management (pp. 909, 913, 915). Their only answer gives the duty to the robot (pp. 910, 911).
- **Ground v8 should cite.** (a) p. 911, the burger passage, as the field's own plain statement of the several-firms question, left open. (b) pp. 911 and 915 for the two duties toward the guest. v8 can say it agrees on what is owed and answers who owes it before harm. (c) p. 907 for the correlation of claim and duty in hospitality research's own rights talk, as a bridge to the guest's claim. (d) p. 915, "handle situations where robot “responsibilities” fail", as another design-side precursor of the in-stay route.

## Verdict on the gap statement for these five texts

The strongest counter-evidence comes from Sharma and Mattila. They say to whom responsibilities run and what they are: "safety, security, and delivering the promise of service quality and reliability" from "the customer’s perspective" (p. 911), and "ensure guest safety" (p. 915). They also list the several parties who might answer: "The restaurant company, the robotic manufacturer who provided the robot, or the algorithm creators who made it? Or was the manager on duty responsible ...?" (p. 911). Gursoy names the hospitality firm as "stewards of algorithmic governance" (p. 155), within "the social contract between hospitality firms and their guests" (p. 158). Mosca et al., the one phygital text, make visitors' empowerment "the museum’s responsibility" (p. 437) and charge curators, technologists and managers by name (pp. 434, 440). So "says neither to whom they are owed" fails for three of the five, and "phygital research" describes only one of them and the call. What survives is narrower and sharper. No text allocates duties toward the guest among the several firms behind a service. Sharma and Mattila come nearest, and they pose the question only after harm and leave it to liability and risk management (pp. 909, 913, 915). Mosca's editorial sees the platform providers and downstream firms and calls their relations "a core strategic task" (p. 6). Mosca et al. share credit and "the burden and ownership of content creation" among partners (pp. 437–438), and they list "Shared responsibility" in a figure without defining it (p. 436). No text treats a break in service as the point at which the guest is owed a route to someone who can correct it. The phygital texts endorse seamlessness throughout (CFP p. 2; Mosca et al. pp. 427, 437, 439, 440–441), and Gursoy alone questions it, once ("seamless yet uncanny", p. 156). Human fallback appears only as design advice that "can improve" outcomes (Gursoy, pp. 157, 159; Sharma and Mattila, p. 915).

A restatement that these five texts support (drafted by Claude for the authors to accept, change or discard):

> *DRAFTED BY CLAUDE.* Phygital and hospitality-AI research prescribes safeguards for trust, fairness and well-being, and when it names who owes them, it names one organization (the hotel firm, the museum) or the robot itself. Where it notices the several firms behind a service, it asks who should be blamed after harm and leaves the answer to liability and risk management. It treats a break in the guest's experience as a design failure to be smoothed away, not as the moment the guest is owed a route to someone who can correct it.

This restatement keeps the user's abstract ("it rarely specifies which party owes what", `USER_ABSTRACT_2026-11-13.md`) and drops the claim that the field never says to whom.
