## Summary

The King's Bench resolved that an innkeeper is not liable for a horse stolen from pasture where the guest asked for it to be put out, and used the case to set out, from the words of the original writ, when the innkeeper answers for a guest's loss: only a common inn, only travellers, only goods within the inn, only by default of the innkeeper or his servants. The strict liability enforced by the writ extended to the guest's goods, "and not his person" (fol. 33b): the report construes *bona et catalla* to restrain the writ to moveables, so a guest beaten in the inn has no remedy under it. The report does not say the innkeeper owed nothing to the guest's person under any other action.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Only the common innkeeper is bound; a private host is not: "if a man be lodged with another (who is not an innholder) upon request, if he be robbed in his house by the servants of him who lodged him, or any other, he shall not answer for it" (fol. 32a) [full-text]
- Only travellers: "common inns are instituted for passengers and wayfaring men" (fol. 32b) [full-text]
- Strict custody of goods: "for the innkeeper is bound in law to keep them safe without any stealing or purloining" (fol. 33a) [full-text]
- Answers for his servants: "the innholder shall not be charged, [unless] there be a default in him or his servants, in the well and safe keeping and custody of their guest's goods and chattels within his common inn" (fol. 33a; OCR "un'eſs") [full-text]
- Not the person, under the writ: "the innholder ought to keep the goods and chattels of his guest, and not his person" (fol. 33b). The passage construes the writ against hostelers: *bona et catalla* "restrain the latter words to extend only to moveables" (the sentence runs from the foot of fol. 33a to the head of 33b), so "if the guest be beaten in the inn, the innkeeper shall not answer for it" by the writ (fol. 33b) [full-text]
- The guest's own fault limits it: where the guest ignores the innkeeper's request to lock goods away, "the innkeeper shall not be charged, for the fault is in the guest" (fol. 33a) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Qualifies the Fable lead (FABLE_REVIEW §4(d)) that the innkeeper's duty "contains ... a duty to take reasonable care for the guest's safety". Calye shows only that the innkeeper's *strict* liability under the custom of the realm extended to the guest's goods, "and not his person" (fol. 33b). It is not authority that the innkeeper owed the guest's person nothing. Two held sources report liability for harm to guests: bogen1996innkeeper, p. 82 n. 160 ("innkeepers were liable for third-party injuries done to their guests"), and maese2015rethinking, p. 499, citing Beale 1906 (a duty to "exercise reasonable care in the protection of the guest" and to "protect the guest from injury by third parties"; n. 166: not "the insurer of his guest's safety"). Whether, and since when, a negligence-based duty to the person existed is a question this report does not answer. Neither of the two sources dates it to 1584: Bogen's remark is an uncited aside in a footnote about "assault or robbery by third parties", and Maese restates Beale's 1906 treatise on "the common law" without a period.
- Liability runs to "default in him or his servants" (fol. 33a): the holder answers for the servants who perform. This is a located statement of performance delegated, liability retained, which H2 should weigh.
- The duty to receive is not in this report; it comes from Year Book dicta and later cases (wyman1904law, pp. 158-159; bogen1996innkeeper, pp. 77-78).
- Read in an eighteenth-century translation, from OCR; a verifier should check each quotation against the page images.
