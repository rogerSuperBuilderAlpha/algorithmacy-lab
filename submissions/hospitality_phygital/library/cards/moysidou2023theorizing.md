## Summary

Theorizes gendered power relations in homestays (au pair, work-exchange, language immersion) from two earlier qualitative studies. Rejects the assumption that hosts hold all the power, and proposes that a guest's mobility stands in for the guest's power. Two figures sort what enables and constrains a guest into tangible and intangible.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Complaint as power: "Knowing how to complain, and feeling that the complaint will be acted upon, are latent forms of power." (accepted manuscript p. 12) [full-text]
- A guest who cannot leave does not complain. An au pair: "What if I complain about something and I make the things uncomfortable?' Cause I have to be here, I cannot leave. ...So that's probably why I haven't complained about little things." (Ane, accepted manuscript p. 7) [full-text]
- The authors' reading of that excerpt: those who feel indebted and/or infantilized "are stripped of agency, resulting in a sense that they 'cannot complain'" (accepted manuscript p. 13) [full-text]
- Leaving as power: tangible affordances "mean that guests are not beholden to hosts, so they can leave if things go wrong" and "part of power is the choice of whether or not to stay" (accepted manuscript p. 12) [full-text]
- The thesis: "we reject a model that assumes, a priori, that hosts are powerful and guests are powerless" and "mobilities seem to operate as proxy for power" (accepted manuscript p. 15) [full-text]
- The received view of the host's power, through Derrida: welcoming "denotes sovereignty over the space and a level of power over the visitor (Caputo in Leung and Stone 2009)" (accepted manuscript pp. 4-5) [full-text]
- The settings: "A lack of formal contract, ethical accountability and clear communication can often exacerbate these issues" (accepted manuscript p. 5, citing Wengel et al. 2018) [full-text]
- Method: Study 1, 50 participants interviewed (26 hosts, 24 guests), from 2017; Study 2, 120 participants in interviews and focus groups, 2013-2016, predominantly Guatemala and Nicaragua (accepted manuscript pp. 5-6) [full-text]
- Crossref: Hospitality & Society 13(3), pp. 241-263, 2023 [metadata]

## Critical notes & threats

- Homestays, not hotels. Several of the settings are work exchanges in which the guest is also a worker, so the guest's dependence is partly an employee's.
- Page numbers are the accepted manuscript's (1-17), not the journal's (241-263). A direct quotation in the paper needs the journal page.
- The authors' claim concerns gender and mobility. Its use for a guest's standing to complain is this paper's reading; they do not use the word standing.

## rr-2026-11-k: bearing on the keystone (R9)

*Added by a Claude (Opus 5.5) subagent, reader R9, 11 Nov 2026, after re-reading the saved accepted manuscript whole (manuscript/rewrite_2026-11/journal/corpus/moysidou2023/; reference list scanned). Locators are the manuscript's pages, as above.*

- **The host's power is a condition of hosting (question a).** "the very act of welcoming a person into one's home is an expression of control and conditionality, as it denotes sovereignty over the space and a level of power over the visitor (Caputo in Leung and Stone 2009). Yet the host needs to retain this power if they are to offer hospitality to the guest (Derrida 1999). Thus, power and control over the space and, to an extent, over the guest are necessary conditions to the offering of hospitality." (pp. 4-5) Rules and filtering in the data: an au pair's written house rule of "No male company" (Faye, p. 8); a host "After filtering [the au pair profiles], I decided I didn't want a male" (Ellis, p. 10).
- **The guest's means, in the data (questions a, g).** Leaving: "part of power is the choice of whether or not to stay" (p. 12). Silence: Irene, made a 'housemaid' on a WWOOF farm, says "it was sort of a situation I couldn't really say anything" and "We might have actually left a few days early because of it" (p. 11). Complaint to the intermediary: Pauline, refused as a couple, "also complained to Workaway" (p. 10). The authors' later gloss credits "complaining to the homestay-organising agency" to Alice (p. 14), but the excerpt that reports such a complaint is Pauline's (p. 10); Alice's excerpt reports no complaint (p. 8). Negotiation ranged "from indirect and passive approaches (when the person feels they have limited power in the relationship) to more direct and assertive approaches (when the perception of power is high)" (p. 6). The intermediary's remedy the authors name is exit: "Workaway offers an Emergency Help Plan that covers accommodation if a guest faces difficult or dangerous situations with their hosts" (p. 16).
- **Several hosts (question b).** The homestays are arranged through agencies and platforms (au pair agencies, Workaway, HelpX, WWOOF; pp. 2, 5); the authors address their practical findings to "intermediary organisations" (p. 16). They do not ask which party answers to the guest.
- **Derrida (question e).** The article quotes *Of Hospitality* p. 81 on the law and the laws: "'both contradictory, antinomic and inseparable. They both imply and exclude each other, simultaneously' (Derrida and Dufourmantelle 2000: 81)" (p. 4). It is the only carrier of this sentence among the texts R1 and I read.
- **Workers (question f).** Several guests are also workers (au pairs, work-exchange volunteers), and the authors note that "hosting in small family owned Bed and Breakfasts tended to be seen as women's work", after Lynch (2005) (p. 14).
- **Bearing (S1, S6).** The article's sentence "Knowing how to complain, and feeling that the complaint will be acted upon, are latent forms of power" (p. 12) treats a guest's effective complaint as the guest's power within hospitality, not as information for the host. That is the strongest footing in this set against the S1 reading of the channel as making the guest a sensor, but only if the paper grounds the channel in the guest's position rather than in the host's need to perform; the article states no host duty. For household hosts, the complaint route in the data runs to the intermediary and the named remedy is exit, which supports S6's suggestion that the route for a household guest be assigned to the platform or agency.

Corrected by the lead (Claude Opus 5.5) on 11 Nov 2026 after independent verification CV-H (`manuscript/keystone_2026-11/VERIFY_CARDS_H.md`): the one complaint to an intermediary in the data came from an applicant refused before arrival (Pauline, p. 10); it shows a route at admission, not during a stay. The Emergency Help Plan is the organisation's offer (p. 16).
