## Summary

A German credit information agency, SCHUFA, computed a score for OQ and sent it to a lender, which refused her a loan. SCHUFA told her it only supplied information and that its contractual partners took the decisions. The Court of Justice held that the automated establishment of such a probability value is itself "automated individual decision-making" under Article 22(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation where the third party that receives it "draws strongly" on it to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship. The Court read Article 22(1) as a prohibition in principle that the data subject need not invoke, lifted only in the three cases of Article 22(2) and then subject to safeguards, which include at least the right to obtain human intervention, to express a point of view and to contest the decision. It gave as a reason that any narrower reading would leave a gap: the score's maker would owe the data subject nothing under Article 22, and the lender could not explain a score it did not make. The second question, on German law, was left unanswered.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The case**

- Court and date: Court of Justice, First Chamber, 7 December 2023; reference from the Verwaltungsgericht Wiesbaden "made by decision of 1 October 2021" (header). Parties: OQ v Land Hessen, with SCHUFA Holding AG as intervener (header). [full-text]
- The proceedings were public-law proceedings against a regulator. OQ challenged the refusal of the Hesse data protection commissioner "to order SCHUFA Holding AG (‘SCHUFA’) to grant an application lodged by OQ seeking to access and erase personal data concerning her" (para 2). She did not sue the lender or SCHUFA for the refusal of credit. [full-text]
- The facts: "OQ was refused the granting of a loan by a third party after having been the subject of negative information established by SCHUFA and transmitted to that third party." (para 15) [full-text]
- SCHUFA's position: it "stated that it limited itself to sending information to its contractual partners and it was those contractual partners which made the actual contractual decisions." (para 16) [full-text]
- What a score is: SCHUFA "establishes a prognosis on the probability of a future behaviour of a person (‘score’), such as the repayment of a loan, based on certain characteristics of that person, on the basis of mathematical and statistical procedures." (para 14) [full-text]
- Article 22 is set out in full at para 11, including Art. 22(3): "In the cases referred to in points (a) and (c) of paragraph 2, the data controller shall implement suitable measures to safeguard the data subject’s rights and freedoms and legitimate interests, at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision." [full-text]

**The test for Article 22(1)**

- Three conditions: "The applicability of that provision is therefore subject to three cumulative conditions, namely, first, that there must be a ‘decision’, secondly, that that decision must be ‘based solely on automated processing, including profiling’, and, thirdly, that it must produce ‘legal effects concerning [the interested party]’ or ‘similarly significantly [affect] him or her’." (para 43) [full-text]
- 'Decision' is wide: the concept "is not defined by that regulation" (para 44) and "is broad enough to encompass the result of calculating a person’s creditworthiness in the form of a probability value concerning that person’s ability to meet payment commitments in the future." (para 46) [full-text]
- The effect on the person: on the referring court's findings, "an insufficient probability value leads, in almost all cases, to the refusal of that bank to grant the loan applied for." (para 48) [full-text]
- The vendor's output is the decision: where the value "plays a determining role in the granting of credit, the establishment of that value must be qualified in itself as a decision producing vis-à-vis a data subject ‘legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly [affecting] him or her’ within the meaning of Article 22(1) of the GDPR." (para 50) [full-text]

**A prohibition, with exceptions and safeguards**

- "That provision lays down a prohibition in principle, the infringement of which does not need to be invoked individually by such a person." (para 52) [full-text]
- "the adoption of a decision based solely on automated processing is authorised only in the cases referred to in that Article 22(2)", namely necessity for a contract, authorisation by Union or Member State law with suitable safeguards, or explicit consent (para 53). [full-text]
- The safeguard inside the contract and consent exceptions: "the data controller is to implement at least the right of the data subject to obtain human intervention, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision." (para 54) [full-text]
- The same floor for decisions authorised by national law: such measures "include, moreover, at least the right for the data subject to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to challenge the decision taken in his or her regard." (para 66) [full-text]
- The consequence for the score: it "is prohibited unless one of the exceptions set out in Article 22(2) of that regulation is applicable and the specific requirements provided for in Article 22(3) and (4) of that regulation are complied with." (para 64) [full-text]
- The purpose of Article 22 is "protecting individuals against the particular risks to their rights and freedoms represented by the automated processing of personal data, including profiling." (para 57) [full-text]
- Accountability: "The controller must be able to demonstrate compliance with those principles, in accordance with the principle of accountability set out in Article 5(2) of that regulation" (para 67). [full-text]

**Why the vendor must be reached: three parties and a gap**

- "in circumstances such as those at issue in the main proceedings, in which three stakeholders are involved, there would be a risk of circumventing Article 22 of the GDPR and, consequently, a lacuna in legal protection if a restrictive interpretation of that provision was retained, according to which the establishment of the probability value must only be considered as a preparatory act and only the act adopted by the third party can, where appropriate, be classified as a ‘decision’ within the meaning of Article 22(1) of that regulation." (para 61) [full-text]
- On that narrow reading the score "would escape the specific requirements provided for in Article 22(2) to (4) of the GDPR, even though that procedure is based on automated processing and that it produces effects significantly affecting the data subject" (para 62). [full-text]
- Neither party could then explain it: the data subject could not obtain the Article 15(1)(h) information from the agency, and the lender "would not be able to provide that specific information because it generally does not have it." (para 63) [full-text]
- The referring court had put the same point: "the third party to whom the probability value is communicated could not provide that additional information because it does not have it." (para 23) [full-text]

**The ruling and what it left open**

- Operative part and para 73: Article 22(1) "must be interpreted as meaning that the automated establishment, by a credit information agency, of a probability value based on personal data relating to a person and concerning his or her ability to meet payment commitments in the future constitutes ‘automated individual decision-making’ within the meaning of that provision, where a third party, to which that probability value is transmitted, draws strongly on that probability value to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship with that person." [full-text]
- Whether an exception applied was left to the national court: "it is for the referring court to verify whether Paragraph 31 of the BDSG can be classified as a legal basis authorising, under Article 22(2)(b) of the GDPR, the adoption of a decision solely based on automated processing." (para 72) [full-text]
- "Given the answer to the first question, there is no need to answer the second question." (para 74) [full-text]
- The judgment does not say when human intervention must take place, who must supply it as between vendor and user, or what power the intervening person must have. The phrase "the decision taken in his or her regard" (para 66) speaks of a decision already taken; the Court draws nothing from it. The words 'hotel', 'accommodation' and 'guest' do not occur (whole-text search, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The holding is tied to its facts.** The ruling concerns "a credit information agency" and a probability value about ability "to meet payment commitments" (para 73). The reasoning is general (paras 44-46, 50, 61-63): a third party's automated output is the decision where the user "draws strongly" on it. Applying it to a hotel's fraud score, identity check or risk rating is an extension the Court did not make.
- **'Draws strongly' was a finding of fact made below.** The Court took it from the wording of the question and the referring court's findings (para 48). It set no threshold.
- **The 'solely automated' condition was common ground** (para 47). The judgment gives no guidance on how much human involvement takes a decision outside Article 22(1).
- **Nothing on timing.** Paras 54 and 66 restate the Article 22(3) floor. The existing card `gdpr2016art22` records that the Article states no timing; this judgment does not add one.
- **A prohibition is stronger than a later appeal, and narrower than it looks.** Outside the three exceptions a solely automated significant decision may not be taken at all (paras 52, 64). Inside them, including the contract exception a hotel is likely to invoke, the data subject has the three safeguards and no more (para 54).
- **Public enforcement route.** The case reached the Court through a complaint to a supervisory authority and judicial review of its decision (paras 17-19, 32-34). The judgment does not deal with damages.
- **Short name.** The brief calls the case *SCHUFA Holding (Scoring)*. Neither the saved judgment nor the Publications Office notice uses that name; the judgment itself cites its companion as "SCHUFA Holding (Discharge from remaining debts)" (para 34). The name should be checked against the Court's own listing before it is printed.
- Later treatment not checked. The scout lists C-203/22 *Dun & Bradstreet Austria* as not searched; I did not read it.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K9 (mediation, before reception): supports.** K9 says a system that decides admission can decide that the host's duty never arises, and names that as a separate failure. The Court treats the automated step taken before any contract exists as a decision in law: the score counts because the user draws on it "to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship" (para 73), and its example is a refusal to contract (paras 15, 48). EU data protection law therefore already attaches consequences to the pre-contract decision, and attaches them to the system's maker as well as its user.

**K8 (holder under delegation; several firms): supports by analogy, in another body of law.** The Court refused to let a three-party arrangement leave the affected person with no answerable party: the maker said the user decided (para 16), the user could not explain (para 63), and the Court called the result "a lacuna in legal protection" (para 61). That is the paper's divided-host problem, decided for data protection. The Court's answer was to bind the vendor. It did not hold the user answerable for the vendor's system, and it did not say the user was released.

**K7 (channel from the guest): supports the floor, narrows the reading 'later appeal only', adds nothing on timing.** Where a solely automated significant decision is permitted, the person must be able "to obtain human intervention", to be heard and to contest (paras 54, 66). That is a route to a person, on the person's request. The judgment does not require the intervention before the decision takes effect and does not say what the intervening person must be able to do.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Court of Justice reads Article 22(1) of the GDPR as "a prohibition in principle", which the data subject need not invoke (para 52).
- A solely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effect is authorised only where it is necessary for a contract, authorised by law with suitable safeguards, or based on explicit consent (para 53).
- In the contract and consent cases the controller must provide at least the right to obtain human intervention, to express a point of view and to contest the decision (para 54).
- An automated score made by one firm is itself such a decision where another firm "draws strongly" on it to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship (para 73).
- The Court reasoned that a narrower reading would leave "a lacuna in legal protection" in arrangements with three parties, because the firm using the score "generally does not have" the information needed to explain it (paras 61, 63).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Court required a human to be involved before an automated decision takes effect. The judgment states no timing.
- That the Court decided anything about hotels, bookings or guests. The ruling is about a credit score (para 73).
- That the lender, or any user of a vendor's system, was held answerable for the vendor's score. The Court bound the vendor.
- That every hotel decision supported by a vendor's score falls under Article 22(1). The test needs a solely automated value on which the user "draws strongly" and a legal or similarly significant effect (paras 43, 48-50).
- That EU law forbids automated decisions on admission. A decision necessary for entering into a contract is one of the exceptions (para 53).
- That the judgment's short name is 'SCHUFA Holding (Scoring)' on this card's authority.
