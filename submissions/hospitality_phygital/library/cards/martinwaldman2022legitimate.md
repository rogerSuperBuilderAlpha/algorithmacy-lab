## Summary

Factorial vignette survey (nine Mechanical Turk runs, slider rating "This decision is legitimate") on what makes a firm's algorithmic decision seem legitimate. Five governance options were tested against a human-decision null: notice, impact assessment, external audit, human in the loop, and appeal to an internal review board. Only the appeal carried a legitimacy dividend; impact assessment, human in the loop and audit scored below mere notice; the appeal softened but did not erase the penalty of a bad outcome; arbitrary or race-based factors erased most dividends.

- The five governance options: "transparency, in which the organization notifies individuals that a computer program made the decision"; "impact assessment"; "audit governance, in which an external entity completed an annual independent audit"; "human governance, or so-called 'human in the loop'"; and "appeals, in which the decision can be appealed by the individual to an internal review board" (Study Design, 'Governance') [full-text]
- The lowering result, and its comparator: "offering an appeal process provides a legitimacy dividend for decisions with no outcome or arbitrary factors included (Survey 5)… (β = 4.54, p < 0.01). However, alternative governance mechanisms such as an impact assessment, including a human in the loop, and having the program audited, lower the legitimacy of the decision compared to mere notice. This was a surprise and counter to the hypothesis." (Results, 'Governance and Legitimacy') [full-text]
- The bad-outcome result is about appeal alone: "for bad outcomes, the use of an appeal process does provide a legitimacy dividend (t = −12.53; p < 0.0001)"; "the dividend did not 'make up' for the penalty of the bad outcome" (Results, 'Outcomes and Governance') [full-text]
- "The only form of procedural governance that carries a legitimacy dividend is the most robust that we studied: a formal appeal to a human decision maker. Those more commonly in practice or in legislative proposals today—notice and impact assessments—either have no impact or carry a legitimacy penalty." (Discussion and Conclusion) [full-text]
- "Only an appeal to a human authority is generally capable of legitimizing algorithmic systems, all other factors held constant." (Discussion, 'Legitimacy') [full-text]
- Scope the authors set: "this study does not seek to normatively defend the practice of using ADM… Nor does it suggest that adoption of any particular form of governance makes the use of ADM legitimate in a democratic society." (Introduction) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- v4 sentence: "One study compared appeal, oversight, and audit as remedies for an adverse algorithmic outcome: an appeal that reached the decision raised its perceived legitimacy, while oversight and audit lowered it (Martin and Waldman 2023)." SUPPORT: PARTIAL at full text (upgraded from the abstract-level verdict; verifier_2's reading confirmed by Fable). Right: appeal was the only mechanism with a legitimacy dividend, and it is the one the authors describe as reaching a human decision maker. Wrong in three places. (1) The study compared five mechanisms, not three; "oversight" is the paper's "human in the loop". (2) The lowering is relative to mere notice, measured in the survey without outcomes (Survey 5), not against no governance and not as a remedy for an adverse outcome. (3) The adverse-outcome test (H4b) was run for appeal only; the paper reports no bad-outcome result for audit or human in the loop. A faithful wording: "of five governance mechanisms, only an appeal to a human review board raised perceived legitimacy, and it softened the penalty of a bad outcome; impact assessment, a human in the loop and an external audit scored below mere notice."
- Legitimacy here is a perception measured on a slider; the authors disclaim any normative verdict on governance (Introduction). Do not cite it as showing that appeal makes a decision legitimate.
- Vignette survey; legitimacy perceptions, not behaviour.
