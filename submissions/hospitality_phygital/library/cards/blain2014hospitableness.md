## Summary

Builds a 13-item instrument to measure "genuine" (altruistic) hospitableness in individuals, for use in recruiting staff to commercial settings. The introduction extends host-guest relations by metaphor to many service roles and states that the host is obliged to keep guests safe and welcome them unconditionally. The theory section relies heavily on Telfer (2000), read as making hospitableness a matter of motive; externally imposed obligation is treated as at odds with genuine hospitableness.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Host-guest relations extended to service roles: "Host and guest relationships can be seen to apply in situations beyond bars, hotel and restaurants to include tour guides, air stewards, conductors and drivers on coaches, staff in museums and at attractions, and many more." (p. 1) [full-text]
- The host's obligation, guests' lesser obligations: "Most significantly, the host is obliged to make the guests safe and to ensure that guests are unconditionally welcome. Whilst there are some obligations on guests, the chief responsibilities which concern us here lie with hosts. The welcome for the guest has to be unconditional." (p. 1) [full-text]
- Obligation as social and variable: "The study of hospitality reveals that societies do have changing levels of obligation to be hospitable" (p. 1, abstract) [full-text]
- Hospitableness as individual motive: "there are differences amongst individuals in the commitment to being hospitable for the pleasure it gives them personally. They are not motivated by ulterior motives, personal gain, or the threat of punishment." (p. 1, abstract) [full-text]
- The obligation accepted by hosts, then set against genuine hospitableness: "At the same time there is an obligation, accepted by hosts, to care for and protect guests." Religious obligations "represent an obligation which may be at odds with Telfer's notion of hospitableness because the host is behaving in a way that has been externally imposed and may be seen to have an ulterior motive." (p. 2) [full-text]
- Commercial use is recruitment: "Commercial organisations could benefit from employing individuals who possess and demonstrate the qualities of hospitableness"; the problem is that "there is no instrument for recognising the qualities of hospitableness in applicants for work in commercial contexts" (p. 2) [full-text]
- Role-holders discharge the welcome through feeling: "Hence the hospitable tour guide or air steward will have the ability to make visitors or passengers welcome and safe, because they experience this desire as a genuine emotion." (p. 2) [full-text]
- Telfer as reported (Telfer pages as Blain and Lashley give them): hospitality's "central ides [sic] ... remains that of sharing one's own home and provisions with others" (Telfer p. 39); her motives include "allegiance to what one sees as duties to be hospitable, a duty help one's friends or a duty to help those in trouble" (Telfer p. 42); "what good hosts are good at is making their guests happy" (Telfer p. 40) (all p. 2-3 of this paper) [full-text]
- One retained item: "I enjoy taking responsibility for the wellbeing of guests" (p. 7) [full-text]
- Disposition is distributed: "It is clear that this quality of hospitality is naturally spread across the population." (p. 8) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- The p. 1 sentence is the strongest statement in hospitality studies, among sources read in this run, that a host in a service role is *obliged* to keep guests safe and welcome them unconditionally. It is asserted in one sentence, not argued, and the paper then moves the whole question into individual motive. It names no organisation as holder; the "host" is whoever occupies the service role.
- "Unconditionally welcome" runs against the law's conditional trigger (HPA s 1(3)) and against Lashley 2015 p. 1 ("only so long as they could pay the price"). The paper should not borrow "unconditional" from here.
- Telfer is reported, not read. Lashley (2015, p. 5) gives a different version of the same Telfer list, without "duties". Use neither as Telfer's words.
- The sentence "Guests are offering hospitality because they are obliged to do so" (p. 2) reads as a slip for "hosts"; do not quote it.
- Small, unrepresentative sample; the instrument is a recruitment tool, not a theory of obligation.
