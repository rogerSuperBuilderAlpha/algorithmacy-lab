## Summary

Xu and Greiffenhagen video-recorded check-in at two self-service hotels of one Chinese chain (the 'L-hotel') over 15 months: more than 70 hours from two lobby cameras, 674 cases. Guests open the door with a code, place an ID card on a kiosk and pass a facial-recognition 'liveness' routine that tells them to close their eyes, shake their head and open their mouth. Using ethnomethodological video analysis, the authors argue that such guests are not only 'working customers' but 'disciplined customers', who work on their own bodies to be recognised. For this run the data matter more than the thesis. The 'automated' hotel kept a service worker by the door. When the kiosk could not process a guest at all (13 of 623), the guest looked for that worker, who checked the guest in by hand. When the kiosk was down for two days, the worker approached all 51 arriving guests. When recognition failed, the kiosk repeated its instructions and guests changed their own behaviour until it succeeded.

## Critical notes & threats

- **Not unstaffed.** The hotels were sold as 'robotic', but a worker sat by the door (pp. 5022, 5035). The study shows a human retained as fall-back, not a guest with no one to turn to.
- **Three kinds of failure, three routes.** A system-wide outage reached staff without the guest (p. 5021). Single-case failures of data or hardware reached a person because the guest went to find one (p. 5035). Recognition failures were fixed by the guest's own repeated compliance (pp. 5027, 5030). Only the second kind fits K7's P4 squarely.
- **Power over the output is my gloss.** The worker "manually checked them in" (p. 5035), that is, registered the guest without the kiosk's result. The authors do not discuss the worker's authority, and the paper reports no counts of staff help during recognition failures.
- **No outcome measures.** No satisfaction, complaint or recovery measures; no interviews.
- **Denominators shift**: 674 recorded, 623 used the kiosk, 610 completed recognition (pp. 5021, 5027, 5035).
- **Scope.** Two hotels of one chain in China, where facial recognition is routine (pp. 5014-5015). The "legal and technical reasons" are not specified (p. 5035); do not supply them.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P4, first half: supports, narrowed.** For the 13 single-case failures, the guest's own approach was how the failure reached a person (p. 5035). For the system-wide outage, staff knew and acted without any report from a guest (p. 5021). The guest's report carried failures specific to the guest's case, not failures of the system as a whole.

**K7, P4, second half: supports, narrowed.** Where the system could not process the guest, correction needed a worker who could check the guest in by hand (p. 5035). Where recognition failed, the guest corrected it alone by complying with repeated instructions (pp. 5027, 5030). The claim holds for failures the guest cannot cure by retrying.

**K9, empirical side: first half supported; second half not shown, and this case runs against it.** The guest's unresolvable problem was resolved by a person (p. 5035). Automation did not remove that person: the chain kept workers on site "for legal and technical reasons" (p. 5035). It moved the person behind the machine, since guests walked past the worker and the worker sent those who asked back to the kiosk (p. 5022). What the case shows is demotion to fall-back, not removal.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In a video study of 674 check-ins at two automated self-service hotels in China, Xu and Greiffenhagen (2024: 5035) report that the 13 guests whom the kiosk could not process at all went to find a service worker, who checked them in by hand.
- The chain kept service workers on site "for legal and technical reasons" although guests could register on their own (Xu and Greiffenhagen 2024: 5035), and the workers sent guests who approached them back to the kiosk (5022).
- When the kiosk was out of service for two days, the worker met every arriving guest (Xu and Greiffenhagen 2024: 5021).
- When facial recognition failed, the kiosk repeated its instructions and guests changed their own behaviour until it succeeded (Xu and Greiffenhagen 2024: 5027, 5030).

**What it may not say**

- That the hotels were unstaffed or that no human was reachable (pp. 5022, 5035).
- That the worker's authority, guests' satisfaction or recovery outcomes were measured. None were.
- That guests prefer a human; most walked past the worker to the kiosk (p. 5022).
- What the "legal" reasons were (p. 5035), or anything beyond two hotels of one chain in China.
