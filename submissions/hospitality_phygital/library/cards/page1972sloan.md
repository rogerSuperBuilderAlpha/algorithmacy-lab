## Summary

A paying guest at the Ocean Isle Motel died when an 82-gallon electric water heater in the utility room next to his room exploded. Weeks earlier, after guests complained of too little hot water, the owners had called a plumber, who replaced a 2,500-watt heating element with a 4,500-watt one without noticing the wattage; a licensed electrician testified that a larger element could weld the thermostat's points so that it no longer controlled the temperature. The trial court gave the owners summary judgment; the Court of Appeals reversed; the Supreme Court of North Carolina affirmed the reversal. Huskins J stated that an innkeeper is not an insurer, that he must use due care to keep the premises reasonably safe and to warn of hidden perils, and that these duties "are nondelegable, and liability cannot be avoided on the ground that their performance was entrusted to an independent contractor". He listed three bases on which the owners "could be liable" depending on the evidence at trial: negligent selection of a plumber for electrical work; the non-delegable duty, making them liable for the plumber's negligence "as if they had made the repairs themselves"; and res ipsa loquitur. The case went back for trial. North Carolina law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The question: "Did the Court of Appeals err in reversing summary judgment entered by the trial court in favor of defendants? Answer depends upon whether defendants, in light of an innkeeper’s duty to a guest, have borne the burden which the law places upon a movant for summary judgment." (702) [full-text]
- The guest and the heater (pretrial stipulations, treated as admissions): the deceased "was accepted as a paying guest at the Ocean Isle Motel and assigned a corner room which adjoined the utility room in which an electric water heater was located. At about 6:30 a.m. on 29 August 1964 said water heater exploded with tremendous force and violence and the explosion resulted in the death of Channing Nelson Page." (700, stipulation 3; the court treats the stipulations "as admissions", 705) [full-text]
- A complaint from guests reached the owners and led to the repair: "In June or July 1964, due to a complaint of insufficient hot water by motel guests, George Sloan and Rea Sloan called plumber Olaf Thorsen to check the water heater." (700, stipulation 6). He "removed the lower heating element of the water heater (2500-watt size), obtained a replacement from Shallotte Hardware Company (4500-watt size), and installed same" (701, stipulation 8). [full-text]
- An omitted statutory inspection: when installed in 1962 the heater "was not inspected by the Boiler Inspection Division of the North Carolina Department of Labor as required by G.S. 95-54 et seq; but the installation was inspected by the Brunswick County Inspector." (701, stipulation 7) [full-text]
- The owners' plea of ignorance: they answered that any defects "were not known to defendants and could not have been known to them prior to the explosion by reason of the fact that the electric water heater was in a sealed container with no external controls." (699) [full-text]
- The standard of care: "An innkeeper is not an insurer of the personal safety of his guests. He is required to exercise due care to keep his premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn his guests of any hidden peril." (702, citing *Barnes v. Hotel Corp.*, 229 N.C. 730 (1949)) [full-text]
- Non-delegability, in words the court adopts from an encyclopedia: "The duties thus imposed upon an innkeeper for the protection of his guests “are nondelegable, and liability cannot be avoided on the ground that their performance was entrusted to an independent contractor.” 40 Am. Jur. 2d, Hotels, Motels and Restaurants § 81. Compare the English rule, Chapman, Liability for the Negligence of Independent Contractors, 50 L.Q. Rev. 71 (1934)." (702) [full-text]
- The ground: "The rule of nondelegability is grounded on the premise that an innkeeper’s duty to use due care for the safety of his guests is a responsibility so important to the public that he should not be permitted to transfer it to another." (702) [full-text]
- Restatement (Second) of Torts § 425, as quoted by the court: "One who employs an independent contractor to maintain in safe condition land which he holds open to the entry of the public as his place of business, or a chattel which he supplies for others to use for his business purposes or which he leases for immediate use, is subject to the same liability for physical harm caused by the contractor’s negligent failure to maintain the land or chattel in reasonably safe condition, as though he had retained its maintenance in his own hands." Its illustration 2, as quoted: "A operates a hotel. He employs B as a plumber to install a shower bath. B negligently transposes the handles so that the hot water pipe is labeled cold. C, a guest, deceived by the label, turns on the hot water and is scalded. A is subject to liability to C." (702-703) [full-text]
- The rule applied where a maintenance contractor had taken over inspection, as the court reports out-of-state cases: "Even where the company which manufactured and installed the elevator had by contract assumed responsibility for the inspection, repair and maintenance of the elevator, the rule was applied and defendant owner of the premises was held liable." (703, citing *Otis Elevator Co. v. Bond*, Tex. Civ. App. 1963) [full-text]
- The three alternative bases, conditional on the evidence at trial: "Thus, depending on the evidence offered at the trial, defendants in this case could be liable on any of the following bases:" (703; the OCR's comma after 'depending' is a speck on the scan). [full-text]
- Basis (1), negligent selection, with a constructive-knowledge test: failure "to exercise reasonable care to employ a competent and careful contractor" (Restatement 2d § 411, as quoted); "If defendants knew, or in the exercise of due care should have known, that a plumber was not competent to do such work and if the plumber’s negligence was a proximate cause of the explosion and ensuing death of plaintiff’s testate, defendants would be liable." (703) [full-text]
- Basis (2), the non-delegable duty, with no knowledge condition: "Since the duties imposed upon an innkeeper for the protection of his guests are nondelegable and liability cannot be avoided on the ground that their performance was entrusted to an independent contractor, defendants would be subject to the same liability for an injury or death caused by the plumber’s negligent failure properly to repair the electrical heating element on the water heater as if they had made the repairs themselves." (704) [full-text]
- Basis (3): "Application of the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur." (704) [full-text]
- The holding: "We hold that defendants have failed to carry the movant’s burden of proof." (706). The issues left for trial: "Were defendants negligent in selecting a plumber instead of an electrician to repair an electrical element on the water heater? ... Was the plumber negligent in making the repairs? Was his negligence a proximate cause of the explosion and ensuing injury?" (708) [full-text]
- The court's own limit on what it decided: "Whether res ipsa [loquitur] applies and whether this case should be submitted to the jury under that doctrine or under some other theory of law, or dismissed, are questions for determination by the trial judge at the close of the evidence. It would be inappropriate at this juncture, upon affidavits, to determine any of those matters. Our comments are intended merely to demonstrate defendants’ failure to carry the burden of showing they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law." (708; the CAP OCR reads 'loquilv/r' where the bracketed word stands) [full-text]
- The plumber's evidence: "I didn’t pay enough attention to this particular one to notice the wattage stamped on the side of it." (707). The electrician's: "Any time you alter whatever was shipped with the unit, you should add additional safety devices." (707) [full-text]
- Disposition: the Court of Appeals' decision "reversing the entry of summary judgment in favor of defendants is Affirmed." (709) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Alternative ground on a preliminary ruling: confirmed.** The non-delegable duty is the second of three bases on which the owners "could be liable" "depending on the evidence offered at the trial" (703-704). The decision holds only that summary judgment was wrongly granted (706, 708-709), and the court says that which theory goes to the jury is for the trial judge "at the close of the evidence" (708). The statement of non-delegability is the court's own statement of the law, not a finding of liability.
- **The authority is thin.** For non-delegability the court quotes 40 Am. Jur. 2d § 81 and Restatement 2d § 425 with its hotel illustration, and cites out-of-state elevator cases (702-703). It cites no earlier North Carolina decision for the rule; *Barnes* (1949) is cited for the standard of care only (702). It contrasts "the English rule" without stating it (702).
- **What is non-delegable, and what is not.** The duties named are "to exercise due care to keep his premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn his guests of any hidden peril" (702). The case says nothing of other duties (aid, attention, admission). The innkeeper remains "not an insurer" (702): liability under basis (2) still requires the contractor's negligence.
- **Section 425 is not about innkeepers as such.** As quoted, it covers anyone who holds land "open to the entry of the public as his place of business" (702). Its reach to restaurants and shops is therefore wider than the innkeeper rule, which matters for K4 and K8 but was not tested here.
- **Contrast with English law read in this run.** *Maguire v Sefton* (card) let a leisure-centre operator escape where a maintenance contractor had inspected badly. The elevator cases reported here impose liability on the owner even where the contractor "had by contract assumed responsibility for the inspection, repair and maintenance" (703). The comparison is mine.
- **Later treatment** of *Page v. Sloan* in North Carolina not checked. Whether it remains good law, or was confined, is open.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports the limb 'without that defence where the duty is non-delegable', for an innkeeper's premises-safety duties to a paying guest, in North Carolina in 1972.** The court states that the innkeeper's duties to keep the premises reasonably safe and to warn of hidden perils are non-delegable and that the innkeeper is liable for an independent contractor's negligent repair "as if they had made the repairs themselves" (702, 704). It states the ground: the duty is "so important to the public that he should not be permitted to transfer it to another" (702). It narrows K8 in three ways: one jurisdiction; one of three alternative bases at summary judgment (703, 708); and the premises and warning duties only.

**K7, P3: shows where the knowledge question drops out, and where it stays.** Under the non-delegable duty the holder's knowledge is no condition of liability (704), so the owners' plea that defects "could not have been known to them" because the heater was a sealed unit (699) is no answer on that basis; arranging not to know would gain such a holder nothing. Under negligent selection the test is what the owners "knew, or in the exercise of due care should have known" (703), a negligence standard wider than wilful blindness. The facts also show a channel from guests that worked: a complaint about hot water reached the owners, who sent for a repair (700). What failed was the delegate's work, not the route from the guest.

**What the paper may say**

- The Supreme Court of North Carolina stated in 1972 that an innkeeper's duties "to exercise due care to keep his premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn his guests of any hidden peril" are non-delegable, so that "liability cannot be avoided on the ground that their performance was entrusted to an independent contractor" (*Page v. Sloan*, 281 N.C. 697, 702 (1972), quoting 40 Am. Jur. 2d).
- The court grounded the rule on the premise that the innkeeper's duty of care "is a responsibility so important to the public that he should not be permitted to transfer it to another" (at 702).
- The court listed this as one of three bases on which the motel owners could be liable at trial, and held only that summary judgment for them had been wrongly granted (at 703-704, 706, 708).
- On the negligent-selection basis, the owners would be liable if they "knew, or in the exercise of due care should have known" that the plumber was not competent for electrical work (at 703).
- The repair that preceded the explosion was ordered "due to a complaint of insufficient hot water by motel guests" (at 700, stipulation).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the court held the motel liable. It sent the case to trial (708-709).
- That non-delegability was the ground of decision. It was one of three possible bases (703).
- That North Carolina, or United States law generally, treats every duty of an innkeeper as non-delegable. The court named the premises and warning duties (702).
- That the rule still stands in North Carolina. Later treatment was not checked.
- Anything on the authority of Restatement § 425 or § 411, or of the elevator cases, beyond 'as quoted in' or 'as reported in' *Page v. Sloan*.
