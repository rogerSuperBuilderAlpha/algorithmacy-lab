## Summary

Herstein surveys the theory of legal rights. On form he follows Hohfeld: four positions, each a relation between a right-subject and a right-object, with the claim-right defined by the duty owed to its holder. He records doubts about strict correlativity, but only in one direction: claim-rights for which no duty has yet vested. On function he reports the will, interest and demand theories and calls the debate unsettled. A closing section lists what is special to legal rights as institutional rights: they can be validated, adjudicated and enforced; they include procedural rights; a few are 'imperfect', held but unenforceable; and they come in two tiers, primary rights and the remedial rights and rights of action that their violation grounds.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The four correlative pairs: "Altogether, Hohfeld identifies four pairs of correlatives: claim/duty; privilege/no-claim; power/liability; and, immunity/disability." (§3.1, para. 2) [full-text]
- Claims and powers sit at different levels: "Claims and privileges are first-order norms, respectively determinative of what the relevant parties ought to do or may do. Powers and immunities are second-order norms" (§3.1, para. 3) [full-text]
- The claim-right and its direction: "X has a claim-right against Y with respect to some φ if and only if Y is under a duty to X to φ. Claim-rights are, therefore, “directed”—the claim of the right-subject is directed at the right-object, whose correlative duty is owed to (directed at) the right-subject." (§3.1.1, para. 1) [full-text]
- A complex private-law right taken apart: "creditors, for instance, hold claims against debtors to the payment of their debts; privileges to collect or not to collect debts; power to forgive debts; and, immunities from other private parties unilaterally waiving or annulling those respective claims, privileges, and powers." (§3.1.5, para. 1) [full-text]
- Claims and privileges come apart in recognised cases: "In Hohfeldian terms, rights to do wrong are claim-rights without privileges (Waldron 1981; George 1993: 118–122)." (§3.2, para. 4) [full-text]
- Correlativity doubted, in the direction from right to duty only: "Hohfeldian correlativity suggests that (claim-)rights are analytically or formally equivalent with duties, so that a (claim-)right of one person entails a correlative duty of another (Feinberg 1973: 63–64; Sumner 1987: 24–25). It is not clear, however, whether this is in fact true." (§3.3, para. 1); "it is arguably both conceptually possible and practically not unheard off for claim-rights to obtain without correlative duties." (§3.3, para. 3; 'unheard off' is the entry's spelling) [full-text]
- Rights against a definite party: "Rights in personam are rights the content of which designates a specific and definite right-object. These rights are typically associated with the law of obligations (contracts, trusts, and parts of torts)" (§3.4, para. 1) [full-text]
- The function debate: "While each of the leading two theories captures important instances of rights, neither manages to encompass the full parameters of rights discourse. The debate remains unsettled, with ever-growing nuance and ingenuity." (§4, para. 7) [full-text]
- Will theory, as reported: "right-holders control as a matter of choice the duties owed to them by others, holding the Hohfeldian power to annul, waive, enforce, or transfer such duties (Hart 1982: 183–4)." (§4.1, para. 1) [full-text]
- Private law fits it best: "The branch of law most hospitable to the Will Theory is private law." (§4.1, para. 2) [full-text]
- Interest theory, as reported: "unlike Will Theorists, Interest Theorists tend to emphasis claim-rights more than powers (Kramer 2013: 246; 2017: 49)." (§4.2, para. 1; 'emphasis' is the entry's wording) [full-text]
- Demand theory, as reported, is a theory "emphasizing the capacity or power of right-holders to claim or demand" (§4.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Feinberg's thought experiment, as reported: duties that benefit people without being owed to them. "Yet, these duties are public duties not owed to those individuals that they are designed to benefit. It is, therefore, a legal system of just duties without correlative rights (at least non held by individual beneficiaries)." (§4.3, para. 1; 'non' is the entry's spelling) [full-text]
- What such a world lacks: "In a world without rights no one is legally owed justice, benevolence, or care, and, therefore, no one is in a position to claim or demand just treatment under the law." (§4.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- A violated right keeps its force: "Violated rights (or their underlying reasons) continue, in one way or another, to project normative force, thereby still counting as legal reasons. This is prominently manifested in remedial rights and correlative remedial duties justified by the violation of other rights" (§8.6, para. 2). The sources given there include "Gardner 2011: 28–37, 45–46" (§8.6, para. 2) [full-text]
- A remedy is not guaranteed: "violated rights do not always succeed in grounding a remedy and can be overridden by other considerations in this regard." (§8.6, para. 2) [full-text]
- Legal rights among other institutional rights: "Legal rights also exhibit features absent or more muted in rights found in normative systems of non-legal institutions, such as social clubs, trade unions, or universities." (§10.1, para. 2) [full-text]
- Procedural rights are a legal category of their own: "Examples include rights to a hearing, trial by jury, confront witnesses, notice of accusation, receive reasons for official decisions, finality, appeal, evidentiary rights, and many more." (§10.2, para. 1) [full-text]
- Rights that cannot be enforced: "law distinguishes between “perfect” legal rights, which are enforceable, and imperfect legal rights, which are not (Fitzgerald [Salmond] 1966: 233). Although there are imperfect legal rights (e.g., unenforceable contracts, certain immunities, and claims barred by lapse of time), these are not the norm" (§10.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- "Imperfect moral rights are not similarly scarce." (§10.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- What violation of a primary right brings: "when a primary right is violated law typically provides right-holders with a right to recourse (Stevens 2011: 133–134; Gardner 2019: 18–19) or, at the very least, with a right of action to pursue recourse against the wrongdoer" (§10.4, para. 1) [full-text]
- Enforcement first, repair where enforcement fails: "Remedies can take the form of enforcement of the violated (primary) right, either through self-help, policing, or a court order. Yet, enforcement is sometimes less favored, impossible, impractical, inequitable, or too costly. If so, law typically provides victims of rights violations with secondary rights, which are remedial rights grounded in the violation of the primary right." (§10.4, para. 2) [full-text]
- Who holds the remedy: "Importantly, remedial rights and rights of action against the party who violated a primary right, and are typically held exclusively by the party who held that right (Zipursky 1998: 4)." (§10.4, para. 2; the sentence is ungrammatical in the entry) [full-text]
- Morality has no counterpart: "lacking institutions of adjudication and enforcement, morality does not obviously involve formalized straightforward rights of action to pursue recourse for rights violation." (§10.4, para. 3) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What the entry can carry.** It reports the theories and describes legal rights in general. It names no jurisdiction, case or statute for the remedial structure in §10.4; 'typically' is its own hedge. The paper's legal limb still needs each duty's primary source (K4, K5). Cite Herstein for the general form and for the existence of the theoretical dispute, with the theorist named.
- **Two sentences cannot be quoted as they stand.** The key sentence of §10.4 is ungrammatical ('remedial rights and rights of action against the party who violated a primary right, and are typically held exclusively'). Paraphrase it. §3.3 has 'unheard off' and §4.3 'non held'.
- **The doubt about correlativity runs the wrong way for K5.** §3.3 gives claim-rights without a vested duty (MacCormick's estate example). The customary limb of K5 needs the converse, a duty owed without a claim, and the sections read contain no example of it. The nearest thing is Feinberg's world of duties 'not owed to those individuals that they are designed to benefit' (§4.3), which is a world of undirected duties, not of duties owed without rights.
- **Imperfect rights cut both ways.** They show that a legal right can exist with no action to enforce it (§10.3), so the absence of a guest's action does not prove the absence of a guest's right. They are also 'not the norm'. K5 should argue from the guest's action to the directed duty and not from its absence to the duty's absence.
- **Rights to a hearing and to reasons are procedural rights against officials** (§10.2). The entry does not say that a private-law right-holder has any such right against the duty-bearer. It gives the stage-0 tort lawyer's objection no answer.
- **Version-of-record pages for Gardner.** §8.6 cites Gardner (2011) at pp. 28–37 and 45–46. Those ranges match, in order, section 5 and the sixth doubt of the author's typescript (gardner2011what, typescript pp. 33–45 and 54–55). The match is this card's inference and is unverified against the journal.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K5 (supports the legal limb; narrows the test).** A legal claim-right is defined by the duty owed to its holder (§3.1.1), and the remedial rights and the right of action are typically that holder's alone (§10.4, para. 2). A guest's own action on a duty is therefore good evidence that the duty is owed to the guest. The converse inference is barred by §10.3.
- **K6 (supports).** 'The debate remains unsettled' (§4, para. 7). Control over the duty, including the power to enforce, is the will theory's mark (§4.1, para. 1); the demand theory's is the capacity to claim (§4.3); the interest theory stresses the claim itself (§4.2). Legal 'standing' in the sense of a right of action is a remedial right distinct from the primary claim (§10.4), which fits K6's wish not to rest on a theory of standing to demand.
- **K7 (narrows).** The law's first remedy is enforcement of the primary right; secondary, remedial rights come where enforcement is 'less favored, impossible, impractical, inequitable, or too costly' (§10.4, para. 2). That is the legal shape of K7's line between performing and repairing. The entry gives no duty on the duty-bearer to be reachable, and says that morality has no formal rights of action at all (§10.4, para. 3).
