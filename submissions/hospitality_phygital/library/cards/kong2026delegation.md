## Summary

A conceptual argument that the responsibility-gap thesis for principal-deployed AI agents rests
on a "delegation illusion": inferring reduced answerability from the deployer's causal and
epistemic distance from the act. Separating attributability, answerability and accountability,
Kong argues that autonomy, opacity and adaptivity reduce only attributability. Answerability is
conserved and relocated to the prior act of delegation, grounded in the deployer's guidance
control over that act, and graded by type-level foreseeability of harmful dispositions. Because
the machine bears no responsibility at all, answerability concentrates on the deployer.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The principle, stated in full: "When an agent voluntarily delegates a task to a subordinate process — human or artificial — by specifying an objective and releasing the process to pursue it across a domain of foreseeable moral risk, the agent's answerability for the foreseeable types of outcome within that domain is neither divided nor diminished by the autonomy of the process. It is relocated to the prior act of delegation: the choice of objective, the choice of constraints, and the choice to release the process under those terms." (p. 5) [full-text]
- "answerability is a quantity delegation cannot make disappear, only displace in time and structure." (p. 5) [full-text]
- Answerability defined: an agent is answerable "when she is the correct address for the question 'why did this happen, and what have you to say for it?'"; it "tracks not authorship but a normative standing within a web of obligations." (p. 3) [full-text]
- Role-based answerability (the captain): one can be answerable for outcomes one did not author "provided one occupied a role, undertook a task, or created a risk that rendered those outcomes one's normative business." (p. 3) [full-text]
- Ground: "the deployer's standing to answer for what an agent does is grounded in her guidance control over the act of delegation [12]" (p. 4) [full-text]
- Roles are derivative, not foundational: "A role matters derivatively rather than intrinsically: it bundles competence, control, and epistemic access, and assuming it is itself an exercise of control." ... "Roles are not an alternative to control-based responsibility; they are how it is distributed across a division of labour." (p. 4) [full-text]
- What the role does do: "What the role contributes is a specification of the domain over which control and knowledge are exercised, and thereby of the outcomes that are hers to explain" (p. 4) [full-text]
- Kong's own position on the machine, stated in a reply to an objection: the account "insists the machine is not a responsibility-bearer in any sense" (p. 10) [full-text]

- Non-answerable delegate concentrates answerability: "One who elects a delegate that cannot answer thereby elects to answer for it." (p. 10) [full-text]
- "a diminished answerability that is nonetheless located somewhere is not a gap. It is a smaller quantity with an address." (p. 10) [full-text]
- Legal personality as a manufactured gap: conferring personality as a liability shield "does not close a gap: it manufactures one" (p. 11) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**What the principle is and what grounds it (settled).** The principle (p. 5) says the deployer's answerability for *foreseeable types* of outcome is "neither divided nor diminished" by the delegate's autonomy and is "relocated to the prior act of delegation". Its ground is guidance control over that act (p. 4), connected to later outcomes by a standard tracing structure (pp. 5–6, after Fischer and Tognazzini), and graded by the deployer's ex ante access to the deployment's dispositional profile (E2), not to the particular act (E1) (p. 8). Roles enter only derivatively (p. 4).

**Why this matters for the paper.** Kong reaches the paper's *Transfer* conclusion and even its vocabulary ("addressee", "a smaller quantity with an address", pp. 10, 13). A referee who knows it will ask what the paper adds. The full text supplies four answers, each with a locator:

1. *Ground.* Kong grounds answerability in control over delegation and treats roles as derivative (p. 4). The plan grounds the obligation in the role constituted by the arrival. The two grounds differ, and the paper should say which it holds and why. Kong is direct evidence *against* the plan's claim that ownership "moves only with the role" as a *ground*. The role is not idle on his account: it specifies "the domain over which control and knowledge are exercised, and thereby of the outcomes that are hers to explain", and roles "are how [control-based responsibility] is distributed across a division of labour" (p. 4). A weaker version of the plan's claim, that roles mark where control-based answerability lands, has support on p. 4; the stronger version, that the role itself grounds ownership, does not.
2. *Content.* Kong's answerability is indexed to "foreseeable types" of *harm* (p. 5) and his scope is harm to third parties (p. 2). Nothing concerns an obligation owed before harm, or an exception owed to a particular arrival. The WS_A *Distinctness* reading ("indexed to 'harmful dispositions'") holds.
3. *Reachability.* Kong separates being answerable (a normative fact) from being held answerable (an institutional practice) and argues only the first (p. 4). The paper's diagnosis, that the answerable party exists but cannot be *reached*, sits on the second side. Kong's own conclusion asks governance "to hold that addressee in view" (p. 13), which is the paper's design problem stated from the other end.
4. *Fiduciary analogy.* Kong sets fiduciary duty aside as "a different matter" (p. 4), so he does not license the plan's trustee/successor comparison.
