## Summary

Conceptual article arguing that outdoor adventure guides are hosts, and that three borrowed concepts (dwelling, communitas, hygge) describe the comfort they deliver. No data of its own. Section 2 defines hospitality from the field's standard sources.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The host's historical obligation, on King's authority: "Historically, the host had an obligation not only to provide a place for and assure the safety of their guests, but also to assure their comfort (King 1995)." (section 2, second paragraph) [full-text]
- Hospitality as a relationship: "Hospitality is most often defined as a relationship between hosts and guests." (section 2, first paragraph) [full-text]
- Commercial hosting carries the same imperative of availability: "In order to be a good host, one must be unconditionally available to the guest. Commodified hosting, including adventure guiding, creates a similar imperative." (section 2, second paragraph) [full-text]
- The commercial motive, stated plainly: commercial hospitality "implies a provision of services in order to make guests welcome and comfortable, which is aimed at extracting surplus monetary value for the service provider (Ashness and Lashley 1995)." (section 2, third paragraph) [full-text]
- Lashley's three domains, as the authors report them: "Lashley's (2000) work distinguishes between three apparently independent domains in which hospitality activities occur: social, private and commercial." (section 2, fourth paragraph) [full-text]
- The article's question: "The article asks the broad question of how the sense of temporary belonging and comfort might be achieved on guided adventure tours." (section 1, last paragraph) [full-text]
