## Summary

J.L. alleged that, aged seventeen, she was trafficked for commercial sex in rooms rented by her trafficker at hotels in the Denver Tech Center, including a Best Western Plus, a La Quinta and a Sheraton. She sued the brand companies (Best Western, Wyndham, Marriott and a Hyatt entity), not the hotel owners, under the civil remedy of the Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1595(a), which lets a victim sue the perpetrator "or whoever knowingly benefits ... from participation in a venture which that person knew or should have known has engaged in" trafficking. The court described two routes. On 'direct liability' the defendant's own acts, omissions and state of mind must establish each element; on 'indirect liability' the acts and state of mind of an agent are imputed under the federal common law of agency, here the Restatement (Third) of Agency. The court held that room revenue and royalties pleaded the 'knowingly benefited' element and that no overt act was required. But it held that the plaintiff pleaded no facts showing that any brand knew or should have known of her trafficking: general awareness of trafficking at hotels was not enough, and she did not allege that staff heard and ignored her pleas or that she alerted any staff member. On indirect liability, the allegations of day-to-day control (rules of operation, employment decisions, training, wages, bookings on the brand's domain) were sufficient to plead actual agency for Best Western and Marriott, but agency liability failed because the hotels were not plausibly liable under § 1595 either; apparent agency failed for want of alleged reliance. All claims were dismissed with prejudice.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The statute and the two routes**

- § 1595(a) as quoted: "An individual who is a victim of a violation of this chapter may bring a civil action against the perpetrator (or whoever knowingly benefits, financially or by receiving anything of value from participation in a venture which that person knew or should have known has engaged in an act in violation of this chapter) in an appropriate district court of the United States and may recover damages and reasonable attorneys fees." (pg 10-11 of 46). [full-text]
- "A plaintiff may satisfy these elements in one of two ways. She may show that the defendant’s own acts, omissions, and state of mind establish each element. Alternatively, she may impute to the defendant the acts, omissions, and state of mind of an agent of the defendant. The former is referred to as “direct liability” and the latter as “indirect liability.”" (pg 11). [full-text]

**Direct liability: benefit, venture, knowledge**

- Benefit: the court agreed with cases holding that "the rental of a hotel room ... constitutes a financial benefit sufficient to meet this element" and found the element pleaded (pg 13, as to Wyndham; pg 33, as to Best Western). [full-text]
- No overt act: "The Court agrees with plaintiff that an “overt act” is not required under the TVPRA." (pg 15); "plaintiff is not required to establish an overt act in furtherance of or actual participation in sex trafficking under § 1595." (pg 16). [full-text]
- General awareness is not enough: "General knowledge of commercial sex activity occurring at hotels across the United States is insufficient on its own to demonstrate that Wyndham participated in the trafficking of plaintiff." (pg 19). [full-text]
- Best Western: "The complaint, however, fails to allege facts as to how Best Western International – the parent company or franchisor of this hotel – was aware of these facts. First, plaintiff’s allegations are insufficient to establish actual knowledge because plaintiff does not allege that any member of the hotel staff heard and ignored her pleas or that she alerted any staff member to her need for help." (pg 35). [full-text]
- "Second, even when construed in the light most favorable to plaintiff, her allegations do not plausibly establish that Best Western, the parent company or franchisor, should have known about plaintiff’s sex trafficking at one of its hotels." (pg 35). Conclusion: "plaintiff has not alleged facts sufficient to state a claim for direct liability under the TVPRA against defendant Best Western." (pg 35). [full-text]
- Marriott: the plaintiff "makes no allegations about the state of the room, how she looked when she arrived at check-in, the number of buyers front desk staff may have seen enter her room, whether she spoke to a staff member, or whether her pleas resulted in any noise complaints that could have put hotel staff on notice." (pg 25). [full-text]

**Indirect liability: agency**

- "Because the TVPRA is silent, courts have held that the federal common law of agency should apply. ... Tenth Circuit courts apply common law agency principles from the Restatement (Third) of Agency." (pg 20). [full-text]
- "To state a claim for vicarious liability under an agency theory, a plaintiff must plausibly allege that (1) defendants and their corresponding hotels were in an agency relationship, and (2) the hotels or hotel staff are plausibly liable under § 1595." (pg 20). [full-text]
- Best Western, control: the plaintiff alleged that "Best Western does maintain day-to-day control over its member hotels, including by setting rules of operation, making employment decisions, training employees, setting hours of operation, setting employee wages, and hosting bookings on the Best Western domain." The court "finds plaintiff’s allegations sufficient to show an actual agency relationship between Best Western and the Best Western branded hotel where plaintiff was trafficked." (pg 36). [full-text]
- Best Western's own position: its membership agreement states it has "no control over or responsibility for any decision relating to or affecting the employment or suspension of any person employed at or providing services in connection with the hotel" (pg 36, the court's report of Best Western's argument). [full-text]
- Marriott, control: alleged control including "hosting online bookings on Defendant Marriotts’s [sic] domain; requiring Sheraton hotels to use Defendant Marriott rewards program(s); setting parameters on employee wages; . . . making employment management decisions" would, if proven, "show control over the day-to-day operations of the Tech Center Sheraton hotel sufficient even under Marriott’s agency theory." (pg 26-27). [full-text]
- But no agent liability: "Nevertheless, as with Wyndham and Marriott, plaintiff fails to state a claim under an agency theory because the complaint does not plausibly allege that Best Western is liable under § 1595." (pg 36). "While plaintiff alleges that she had a visible injury, appeared malnourished, received male guests, and that there were used condoms in the room, she does not allege that hotel staff noticed these to be signs of trafficking." (pg 37). [full-text]
- Apparent agency, from the Restatement (Third) of Agency § 2.03 cmt. c as quoted: "Apparent authority holds a principal accountable for the results of third-party beliefs about an actor’s authority to act as an agent when the belief is reasonable and is traceable to a manifestation of the principal." The plaintiff "has not alleged that she relied on any representation by Wyndham or La Quinta" (pg 22). [full-text]
- The contrasting cases: the court compared "cases where courts have found plaintiffs’ allegations sufficient. A.B., 455 F. Supp. 3d at 189–90 (alleging staff were aware of “loud altercations” as well as “constant” attacks on the plaintiff); H.H., 2019 WL 6682152, at *1 (claiming hotel staff discovered her chained up in the bathroom and ignored her plea for help)." (pg 37). [full-text]
- Disposition: "plaintiff’s first, second, third, and fourth claims for relief are DISMISSED with prejudice" and "judgment shall enter for defendants and against plaintiff on all claims" (pg 46). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **'Direct liability' here is a statutory beneficiary theory, not a brand's tort duty to guests.** It asks whether the brand's own knowledge and benefit satisfy § 1595(a) (pg 11). The red team's phrase that the order "recognises a theory of 'direct liability' for hotel brands under 18 U.S.C. § 1595(a), although it dismissed the claims on their facts" (T12) is accurate.
- **A.B. v Marriott is cited as support, not as going the other way.** REDTEAM_TORT.md lead 12 says the order cites *A.B. v. Marriott Int'l*, 455 F. Supp. 3d 171 (E.D. Pa. 2020) "as going the other way on § 1595 elements". In the order it is cited for the elements (pg 11), for rejecting the criminal definition of participation (pg 15), and as a case where allegations were sufficient (pg 27, 37). The many citations of "A.B., 2020 WL 5371459" are to a different case, A.B. v. Hilton Worldwide Holdings (D. Or. 2020) (pg 11).
- **Not a guest.** The plaintiff was a trafficking victim held in rooms her trafficker rented (pg 2). The order concerns a federal trafficking statute, not an innkeeper's or occupier's duty.
- **Pleading stage.** Every holding is on the sufficiency of allegations. The findings of actual agency are findings that agency was plausibly pleaded (pg 21, 27, 36).
- **Knowledge runs through what reached staff.** Actual knowledge failed because the plaintiff did not allege that staff "heard and ignored her pleas" or that "she alerted any staff member to her need for help" (pg 35); constructive knowledge failed because she did not allege that staff noticed the signs (pg 37). The order measures the brand's and the hotel's knowledge by what reached staff, as R7 found of the knowledge triggers in hosts' law (ARGUMENT.md, Step 6, P3).
- District court; persuasive only. Appeal and later history not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation), the brand row: narrows 'Control: No' and confirms that brand liability is derivative or knowledge-based.** At the pleading stage, a brand alleged to set rules of operation, make employment decisions, train staff and host bookings is plausibly the hotel's principal (pg 36; Marriott, pg 26-27). Agency liability still requires that the hotel or its staff be liable (pg 20, 36). Direct statutory liability requires the brand's own knowledge or reason to know of this victim, which general awareness does not supply (pg 19, 35).

**K7 (channel from the guest): a legal illustration of the premise, not support for the conclusion.** The order treats a victim's alerting staff, or staff hearing and ignoring her pleas, as what would have made out actual knowledge (pg 35; and pg 25 for Marriott). That fits P4: what reaches the holder turns on the person affected reaching staff. It imposes no duty to keep a channel; the claims failed because nothing reached staff.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In *J.L. v. Best Western International* the District of Colorado held, at the pleading stage, that allegations that a brand set rules of operation, made employment decisions, trained employees, set wages and hosted bookings were "sufficient to show an actual agency relationship" with the branded hotel (pg 36).
- The court held that a brand's general knowledge of trafficking at hotels was "insufficient on its own" to show its participation in the trafficking of the plaintiff (pg 19).
- It held actual knowledge unpleaded because the plaintiff did not allege that hotel staff "heard and ignored her pleas or that she alerted any staff member to her need for help" (pg 35), and dismissed all claims against the brands with prejudice (pg 46).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the court held any brand liable, or held any brand a principal on evidence. It decided motions to dismiss.
- That the case concerns a guest's injury, an innkeeper's duty, or English or EU law.
- That *A.B. v. Marriott* goes the other way on the elements of § 1595.
