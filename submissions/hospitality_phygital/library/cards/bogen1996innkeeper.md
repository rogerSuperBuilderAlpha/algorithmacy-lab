## Summary

Traces the innkeeper's duties from a 1345 London bill, through post-Black-Death strict liability for guests' goods, to the settled seventeenth-century duty to receive. Argues that the duty to serve arose as a support for strict liability and for crime control, not from monopoly (Wyman), an implied public undertaking (Burdick) or an analogy to public office; that the duty became part of the *status* of innkeeper once imposed; and that its modern significance is anti-discrimination.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Against the undertaking theory: "Innkeepers made no express promise to serve all comers, and an invitation to the public to request services does not necessarily imply a promise to provide them." (p. 52) [full-text]
- Status, not promise: "When the court said innkeepers must serve the public, the obligation became an element of the status of an innkeeper; thereafter, the act of becoming an innkeeper included undertaking that obligation. Why the court imposed the obligation in the first place remains a question." (p. 52) [full-text]
- "The analogy to public officeholders, and thus the idea of a public calling, was a product of duties imposed rather than a cause of them." (p. 52) [full-text]
- His thesis: "It was, however, concern for criminal activity in the inn, rather than any monopoly or innkeeper promise, that led to the obligation to serve the public." (p. 53) [full-text]
- "English law, not the innkeeper's express commitment, imposed the duty to serve the public as a standard of performance that the common innkeeper must meet." (p. 75) [full-text]
- Hospitality and commerce: "To some extent the inns were heirs to a medieval tradition of hospitality, [n. 133] but the innkeepers' hospitality was not free." (p. 76) [full-text]
- Holding out by sign: Chamberlaine J (1623) "noted that simply by putting up a sign and lodging travelers, an individual became liable to an action on the case for refusing someone shelter" (p. 88, Bogen's paraphrase of the 1623 report, 81 Eng Rep 842, pin cite 843 per n. 183, "Id. at 843") [full-text]
- Against monopoly: "innkeeping was not a monopoly" (p. 89 n. 192) [full-text]
- Newton v Trigg (1691), Eyre J, as quoted: "Inns are of necessity; the keeper is chargeable to the public, and compellable to lodge all comers, he cannot refuse whom he pleases" and innkeepers "do not deal upon contracts as others do" (p. 90) [full-text]
- Conclusion: "The duty to serve the public was incidental to the innkeeper's liability for losses of his lodgers." (p. 91) [full-text]
- "The main significance of the right of access today is to protect against discrimination." (p. 91) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- The paper cannot say the law shows *why* a commercial host is bound. On Bogen's reading the reasons offered later (holding out, monopoly, public office) were rationalisations. What the law does show is that, once the duty existed, it attached to a *status* entered by going into the trade (p. 52; Newton v Trigg at p. 90). That supports Position C (role obligation) more than Position B (public calling as ground).
- Bogen's own origin story (crime control, strict liability for goods) has nothing to do with welcome or care. Using the legal duty as evidence for a duty of welcome needs that caveat.
- The duty was always conditional: room available (pp. 89-90), traveller, ability to pay.
- Law review, single author, 1996. Sandovalstrausz2005travelers cites it as the introduction to innkeeper law (p. 64 n. 16).
