## Summary

A district council engaged a demolition contractor on terms forbidding sub-contracting without consent. The contractor brought in two brothers who demolished the building by an unsafe method, and their workman fell and was paralysed. The workman sought a new trial against the council as occupier. The House of Lords dismissed his appeal. Lord Keith, with whom Lords Brandon and Griffiths agreed, held that section 2(4)(b) of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 covers dangers created by a contractor while the work is going on; that an occupier who has engaged a contractor it reasonably believes competent is not ordinarily expected to supervise the contractor; and that it may be reasonable to expect the occupier to act where it knows or has reason to suspect that the contractor is working unsafely. The evidence did not show that the council knew or ought to have known. Lord Goff reached the same result by holding that the injury did not arise from the workman's use of the premises, and declined to accept that knowledge alone could make an occupier liable. English law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: House of Lords, 29 October 1987, [1987] UKHL 14, heard 6 and 7 July 1987 (order sheet). Lords Keith of Kinkel, Brandon of Oakbrook, Griffiths, Oliver of Aylmerton and Goff of Chieveley (p. 1). [full-text]
- Parties and claim: the appellant "sustained an accident, which left him paralysed from the waist downwards as a result of a broken back, while he was engaged on demolition work on a building" on a site owned by Sedgefield District Council (p. 1). He was employed by the sub-contractors, sued them, the contractor and the council, and appealed only against the refusal of a new trial against the council (pp. 3, 5). He was a contractor's workman, not a customer; the defendant was a public authority. Damages had been agreed at "£150,000" (p. 3). [full-text]
- The council's own controls on delegation, in the tender: "Prior approval must be obtained from the engineer for the time being of the council before the employment of a sub-contractor upon site. Any approved sub-contractor shall secure public liability insurance cover to the satisfaction of the council before being engaged on site." (p. 1) [full-text]
- Why the workman needed the council: "The Welsh brothers are men of straw, and the prospects of Mr. Spence (who had no relevant insurance at the time of the accident) being able to satisfy an award of damages against him to the tune of £150,000 are probably remote." (p. 5) [full-text]
- The starting point: "It would be going a very long way to hold that an occupier of premises is liable to the employee of an independent contractor engaged to do work on the premises in respect of dangers arising not from the physical state of the premises but from an unsafe system of work adopted by the contractor." (pp. 6-7, Lord Keith) [full-text]
- The purpose of s. 2(4)(b): "The enactment is designed to afford some protection from liability to an occupier who has engaged an independent contractor who has executed the work in a faulty manner." (p. 7) [full-text]
- Work in progress is covered: the pluperfect in "the work had been properly done" might suggest only completed work, but "That would, however, in my opinion, be an unduly strict construction, and there is no good reason for narrowing the protection afforded so as not to cover liability from dangers created by a negligent act or omission by the contractor in the course of his work on the premises." (p. 7) [full-text]
- The inference Lord Keith draws: "It may therefore be inferred that an occupier might, in certain circumstances, be liable for something done or omitted to be done on his premises by an independent contractor if he did not take reasonable steps to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work was being properly done." (p. 7) [full-text]
- No ordinary duty to supervise; a duty on knowledge: "It would not ordinarily be reasonable to expect an occupier of premises having engaged a contractor whom he has reasonable grounds for regarding as competent, to supervise the contractor's activities in order to ensure that he was discharging his duty to his employees to observe a safe system of work. In special circumstances, on the other hand, where the occupier knows or has reason to suspect that the contractor is using an unsafe system of work, it might well be reasonable for the occupier to take steps to see that the system was made safe." (p. 7) [full-text]
- The question the case turned on: "The crux of the present case therefore, is whether the council knew or had reason to suspect that Mr. Spence, in contravention of the terms of his contract, was bringing in cowboy operators who would proceed to demolish the building in a thoroughly unsafe way." (p. 7) [full-text]
- Selection: "It is entirely reasonable that a council occupying premises where demolition work is to be executed should take steps to see that the work is carried out by reputable and careful contractors. Here, however, the council did contract with Mr. Spence subject to the condition that sub-contracting without their consent was prohibited." (p. 8) [full-text]
- What others saw is not what the holder knew: two people who visited the site and complained of dust were the architects' clerk of works and an architect. "The architects were independent contractors and there is no evidence that they or anyone in their employment informed the council, before the accident, about anything which they observed in the course of the site visit." (p. 8) [full-text]
- Common law adds nothing: the alternative case "based on the ordinary common law duty of care does not raise any considerations of a different nature to those applicable to the statutory case" (p. 8). [full-text]
- A case not run: the pleaded case "that the council negligently employed an incompetent contractor" was "dropped at the conclusion of the trial" (pp. 8-9). [full-text]
- Disposition: "for these reasons I would dimiss the appeal" [so in the transcript] (p. 9). Lord Brandon and Lord Griffiths agreed for Lord Keith's reasons (p. 9). The claim against the council failed. [full-text]
- Lord Oliver: "It is possible to envisage circumstances in which an occupier of property engaging the services of an independent contractor to carry out work on his premises may, as a result of his state of knowledge and opportunities of supervision, render himself liable to an employee of the contractor who is injured as a result of the defective system of work adopted by the employer. But I incline to think that his liability in such case would be rather that of joint tortfeasor than of an occupier." (p. 9) [full-text]
- Lord Goff, on a different route: the injury "arose not from his use of the premises but from the manner in which he carried out his work on the premises. For this simple reason, I do not consider that the Occupiers' Liability Act has anything to do with the present case." (p. 11) [full-text]
- Lord Goff's reservation: "I do not, with all respect, subscribe to the opinion that the mere fact that an occupier may know or have reason to suspect that the contractor carrying out work on his building may be using an unsafe system of work can of itself be enough to impose upon him a liability under the Occupiers' Liability Act, or indeed in negligence at common law, to an employee of the contractor who is thereby injured" (p. 11). His example is "the ordinary householder who calls in an electrician": he "cannot see that, in ordinary circumstances, the householder should be held liable under the Occupiers' Liability Act, or even in negligence, for failing to tell the man how he should be doing his work." (p. 11) [full-text]
- Lord Goff accepts the ordinary visitor's case: where a contractor's work makes premises unsafe for a lawful visitor, "as when a brick falls from a building under repair onto the head of a postman delivering the mail", "the occupier may be held liable to the postman, though in considering whether he is in breach of the common duty of care there would have to be considered (inter alia) the circumstances specified in section 2(4)(b) of the Act." (p. 11) [full-text]
- The speeches do not use the term "non-delegable" and do not mention hotels or guests (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **How much is holding.** The House decided that the fresh evidence did not justify a new trial against the council (pp. 8-9). Lord Keith's reading of s. 2(4)(b) is his route to that result and has the support of three of the five (Lords Brandon and Griffiths in terms; Lord Oliver agreed with the reasons while doubting the label of occupier's liability). His statements of when an occupier "might" be liable or when action "might well be reasonable" (p. 7) are guarded on their face. Lord Goff did not join them.
- **The claimant was the contractor's own workman.** Lord Keith's sentence against supervision is about supervising a contractor's "duty to his employees to observe a safe system of work" (p. 7). A guest is nearer Lord Goff's postman: a visitor endangered by the contractor's work, to whom the occupier may be liable subject to s. 2(4)(b) (p. 11). The case therefore understates neither the occupier's exposure to visitors nor the entrustment defence; it applies the defence in a setting less favourable to claimants than a guest's.
- **Knowledge is the hinge, and the judgment does not require the means of getting it.** Liability turned on whether the council "knew or had reason to suspect" (p. 7). The House imposed no duty to inspect the site or to arrange to be told; it held that what the architects' people saw did not count because no one "informed the council" (p. 8). The council had contractual controls (approval of sub-contractors, insurance) and was not faulted for failing to police them.
- **The statute's second limb is treated as real.** Lord Keith reads "satisfy himself ... that the work had been properly done" as extending to work "being properly done" (p. 7). So the entrustment defence is not selection alone. How far "reasonable steps" go is left to the circumstances.
- **Later treatment, as far as read.** *Naylor v Payling* [2004] EWCA Civ 560 and *Maguire v Sefton MBC* [2006] EWCA Civ 316 apply s. 2(4)(b) without citing *Ferguson* (searched both saved texts). Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, premise P3: limits, and leaves one opening.** P3 says a duty to see that something is done includes having the means to learn that it is not being done and to correct it. For an occupier's duty of care, the House of Lords says the opposite about the ordinary case: an occupier who reasonably believes its contractor competent is not expected to supervise (p. 7). This is the source K7's 'refutes or narrows' list predicted. The opening is the second sentence of the same passage: once the occupier "knows or has reason to suspect", it may be reasonable to expect it "to take steps to see that the system was made safe" (p. 7). On this authority a guest's report matters because it supplies knowledge, and knowledge is what turns reasonable entrustment into a duty to act. The authority does not go further and require the occupier to keep a way of being told.

**K8 (holder under delegation): narrows.** For dangers a contractor creates on the premises, the occupier's liability is conditional: it depends on reasonable entrustment and on reasonable steps as to competence and the work (p. 7, applying s. 2(4)(b)). The holder does not simply 'stay liable'.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- The House of Lords read s. 2(4)(b) of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 as covering dangers created by a contractor while the work is in progress, so that an occupier "might, in certain circumstances, be liable" if it "did not take reasonable steps to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work was being properly done" (p. 7, Lord Keith).
- Lord Keith said it "would not ordinarily be reasonable to expect an occupier" who reasonably regards its contractor as competent "to supervise the contractor's activities" (p. 7).
- He said that where the occupier "knows or has reason to suspect" an unsafe system, "it might well be reasonable for the occupier to take steps to see that the system was made safe" (p. 7).
- Lord Goff did not accept that knowledge or suspicion "can of itself be enough" (p. 11).

**What it may not say.**
- That English occupiers must monitor their contractors. The House said the contrary of the ordinary case (p. 7).
- That the occupier must have a means of learning what its contractor is doing. The council was not faulted for lacking one (p. 8).
- That Lord Keith's knowledge proposition is a unanimous holding. It is guarded, and Lord Goff, who concurred in the result, declined to subscribe to it (p. 11).
- That the case concerned a visitor in a guest's position. The claimant was the sub-contractor's workman (pp. 1, 3).
