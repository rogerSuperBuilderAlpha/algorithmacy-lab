## Summary

Thirty-five semi-structured interviews (Feb-Apr 2018) with individual Airbnb hosts in European countries, corporate hosts excluded. Hosts map a "practice architecture" of responsibilities toward the platform, themselves and neighbours, guests and the local community. The authors read this as responsibilisation: the platform moves responsibility for transactions onto hosts while it shifts strategy toward guests-as-hotel-customers ("hotelisation"), and hosts react against it.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Responsibility moves from platform to participants: "responsibility of online transactions and interactions is increasingly removed from the digital platform owners (and the state) and transferred to individuals who participate and engage with those platforms" (p. 2) [full-text]
- A "responsibility vacuum": as Airbnb bypasses "existing institutions and regulatory frameworks ... there is a responsibility vacuum that is created (Codagnone et al., 2016)" (p. 2) [full-text]
- Who should hold what: responsibility "should be divided and balanced between the platform owner (architects of the online environment), participants (individuals responsible for their behaviour in these online environments) and the government (overall ground rules for these interactions) (Gorwa, 2019)", but "increasingly seems to be removed from institutional agents and transferred to individuals" (p. 6) [full-text]
- The platform as invisible infrastructure: the transfer "benefits platform leaders, who ... establish themselves as an infrastructure that invisibly supports operations ... while simultaneously minimising its presence as a business corporation (Van Doorn, 2020)" (p. 6) [full-text]
- Sample and method: 35 informants, hosts "in various European countries"; "We decided to focus on individual hosts and exclude corporate hosts" (p. 8) [full-text]
- Hosts' duties to guests, in their words: basic provisions "such as cleanliness, toiletries and towels, safety (e.g. have working fire alarms)" plus "good communication, friendliness, local info" (p. 12); "Responsibility is about keeping your word" (Irish host, p. 12) [full-text]
- Table 2, "Responsibility towards guests": "To provide good quality services"; "To be accurate, decent and honest" (p. 16) [full-text]
- Hosts refuse the hotel role: "I am not a butler though nor am I a receptionist in a hotel" (host, Athens, p. 14); "Airbnb has no protection for hosts. They favour guests" (UK host, p. 14) [full-text]
- Hosts' view of the platform: "Airbnb as a platform leader and orchestrator of the guest–host relations do tend to treat guests as customers and hosts as Airbnb employees" (p. 14) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Data are 2018, which the authors call "a historical snapshot" (p. 3); the platform's terms and EU law have changed since (EU Short-Term Rental Regulation not read in this run).
- Host perceptions only; no guest data and no platform documents. It shows what hosts think they owe, not what is owed.
- Several of its strongest framing sentences are the authors' summaries of others (Van Doorn 2020; Gorwa 2019; Codagnone et al. 2016). Attribute them as such.
- Its reference list cites "Edelman and Geradin (2015)" as a Harvard Business School working paper, confirming that the Fable lead exists in some form (see WS_H3 report).
