## Summary

A comparative essay (Eskimo greeting duels, Homeric and Arab hospitality, Andalusian begging and
treating) that derives a general "law of hospitality" from the problem of how a community deals with
a stranger. Hospitality converts a potentially hostile stranger into a guest whose hostility is held in
abeyance. The relation is reciprocal but never equal: the host ordains, the guest complies. Each side
has duties, the law is best seen in its infringements, and any infringement ends the roles and
returns the parties to hostility. Written by an anthropologist of the Mediterranean; first published
in 1968 under a longer title, collected in 1977, reprinted in HAU in 2012 with the original
pagination marked.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The guest's duties and rights, in one sentence: "while a host has rights and obligations in regard to his guest, the guest has no right other than to respect and no obligation other than to honour his host." (p. 514 [1977: 109]) [full-text]
- Asymmetry of roles: "It is always the host who ordains, the guest who complies." (p. 513 [1977: 107]) [full-text]
- Reciprocal but unspecific: "Host and guest must pay each other honour. ... Their mutual obligations are in essence unspecific, like those between spiritual kinsmen or blood-brothers; each must accede to the desires of the other. To this extent the relationship is reciprocal. But this reciprocity does not obscure the distinction between the roles." (p. 513 [1977: 107]) [full-text]
- A guest's recourse is exit: "the guest who is dissatisfied with his treatment has no recourse but to retire from his role altogether by walking out." The diplomatic convention of turning one's plate over let a guest register a protest "without showing any discourtesy to his host. To complain openly would infringe the host's prerogative in the placing of his guests" (pp. 513-514 [1977: 107-108]) [full-text]
- Guest infringements, listed: "A guest infringes the law of hospitality: 1. If he insults his host or by any show of hostility or rivalry; he must honour his host. 2. If he usurps the role of his host. ... If he makes claims or demands, he usurps the host's right to ordain according to his free will, even where custom lays down what he should wish to ordain. ... 3. If, on the other hand, he refuses what is offered he infringes the role of guest." (p. 515 [1977: 109]) [full-text]
- Host infringements, listed: insult or rivalry; "If he fails to protect his guest or the honour of his guest"; "If he fails to attend to his guests, to grant them the precedence which is their due, to show concern for their needs and wishes" (p. 516 [1977: 110]) [full-text]
- How the claim is lost: "failure to return honour or avoid disrespect entitles the person slighted in this way to relinquish his role and revert to the hostility which it suppressed. ... Once they are no longer host and guest they are enemies, not strangers." (p. 517 [1977: 111]) [full-text]
- Pressing a claim destroys it: "the claim of the beggar is paradoxically one which is lost if it is asserted as a right ... By pressing his claim too hard the would-be guest destroys its basis and falls back into the role of hostile stranger. By asserting his rights he denies his status, for even though a diffuse obligation exists towards the beggar, he is not endowed with any corresponding right." (p. 509 [1977: 102]) [full-text]
- Guest status excludes making demands: the author was kept a guest, "where I had no rights", and never allowed into "that of community member where I might assert myself, make demands and criticisms" (p. 512 [1977: 105]) [full-text]
- Territory bounds the role: "A host is host only on the territory over which on a particular occasion he claims authority. Outside it he cannot maintain the role." (p. 514 [1977: 108]) [full-text]
- Time bounds the role: "it is always recognised that it is an abuse to outstay one's welcome" (p. 516 [1977: 111]) [full-text]
- General law versus local codes: "a 'natural law' of hospitality deriving not from divine revelation like so many particular codes of law, but from sociological necessity" (p. 515 [1977: 109]) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**What it supports.** It is the located, field-standard statement that hospitality carries duties on
both sides, that the host's duties include protection and attention to the guest's "needs and wishes"
(p. 516), and that a guest who breaks the guest's duties loses the relation (p. 517). That answers
R3.10 with a source instead of an absence claim, and it supplies the "when does a guest lose the claim"
condition the paper lacked: by insult or rivalry, by usurping the host's role, by refusing what is
offered, or by outstaying (pp. 515-517).

**What it cuts against.** The classical law gives the guest no right to make claims. Making "claims or
demands" is itself an infringement (p. 515); a claim "asserted as a right" is lost (p. 509); open
complaint "would infringe the host's prerogative" (p. 514); and the dissatisfied guest's only recourse
is to walk out (p. 513). On this account the guest's standing to address claims is not part of
hospitality; it is what hospitality withholds. The paper cannot cite Pitt-Rivers for "the guest's
authority to address claims" (PO §3). It can cite him for the opposite baseline, and then say what
the commercial relation adds (payment, contract, public calling) that licenses the guest's claim.

**Scope.** Private, honour-based hospitality to strangers; no commercial setting. The essay is avowedly
speculative in part ("on the basis of no evidence whatsoever, I should like to speculate", p. 503) and
gendered in its examples (the host's wife, p. 515). Cite the infringement lists and the role
asymmetry, not the Eskimo reconstruction.

**Text note.** The PDF's text layer renders some 'l' as '1' ('c1aims', p. 515; 'c1assical', p. 501) and uses ‗ ‘ for quotation marks; quotations above restore the letters and are otherwise verbatim.

**Lineage.** Lugosi (2009, accepted manuscript, section "Hospitality") summarises the same position for
hospitality studies: guests "have obligations to respect the rules of the host and to reciprocate".
Golubovskaya et al. (2017, p. 1286), via King (1995), give the commercial counterpart: the guest's
"only obligation is to pay and behave reasonably".
