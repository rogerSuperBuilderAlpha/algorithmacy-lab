## Summary

The authors argue that reactive human oversight (intervening on an agent's single actions, or on aggregate patterns after the fact) reaches structural limits with agentic AI, and must be complemented by an anticipatory mode. In that mode the principal sets, before the agent acts, a 'normative agenda' of purposes, authorisation boundaries, considerations and escalation triggers, and refines it through a specification-runtime-inspection spiral. Escalation triggers are the points at which the agent defers to a human, so the anticipatory mode calls the reactive mode back in on terms fixed in advance. The agenda's content comes largely from the principal's roles. Section 4 argues that occupying this mode simply is the discharge of a role-grounded prospective obligation to guard against harms one's activities impose on others. Backward-looking responsibility then takes the form of 'strict moral answerability' (after Kiener 2024): an obligation to justify or explain, owed relationally to parties with standing, and holding even where the harm was unforeseeable, because the principal had a prior opportunity to take precautions against that type of harm. Where principal and provider both answer, answerability is shared, not divided. Multi-principal and institutional principals are left for later work.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The thesis: "occupying the anticipatory mode is the discharge of a role-grounded prospective obligation, and backward-looking responsibility takes the form of strict moral answerability—rationalistic, relational, and holding regardless of fault, in virtue of the principal's prior opportunity for precaution." (pp. 1-2, abstract) [full-text]
- Escalation to a human is part of the agenda: "Escalation triggers specify the conditions under which the agent is to defer to a human or hand control back: uncertainty thresholds, novelty conditions, stake-related flags, and trajectory-level drift indicators." (p. 11) [full-text]
- Affected parties enter at inspection: "In the inspection phase, the principal—with tool support, and with input from stakeholders the deployment affects—examines those trajectories against the considerations the agenda makes explicit" (p. 14). The agenda "must be contestable—open to challenge by other reasonable parties on terms the principal can meet." (p. 14) [full-text]
- Contestability needs more than design: inspectability "alone does not deliver contestability. The latter additionally requires institutionally recognised standing for the parties who would contest and the resources for those parties to engage." (p. 17) [full-text]
- The task as closing a control gap: "the substantive task is better described as closing a control-gap: not locating someone to blame for harm but building systems whose level of risk is morally acceptable [49]" ([49] is Hindriks and Veluwenkamp 2023). (p. 8) [full-text]
- The prospective obligation: "In the context at hand, this means, primarily, the obligation to guard against a type of harm, together with the claim that one holds such an obligation in virtue of a role. It is qua driver that one must guard against collisions, qua surgeon against operative harm." (p. 19) "So the first premise is that prospective responsibility is the role-grounded obligation to guard against the harms one's activities impose on others." (p. 20) [full-text]
- Its source in the literature: note 11 cites Van de Poel (2011) "on forward-looking responsibility as the duty to see to it that something is the case" and Duff (2007) for the dependence of retrospective on prospective responsibility. (p. 19, n. 11) [full-text]
- What discharges it: the agenda represents "her role-typical commitments, the institutional norms her deployment context puts in force, the obligations to others her position generates." "There is no further thing the principal must do to discharge the obligation, over and above specifying, monitoring, and refining the conditions under which her agent acts." (p. 20) [full-text]
- Identity, not analogy: "occupying the anticipatory mode is not analogous to discharging a prospective obligation; it is the discharging of one. The relation is identity, not resemblance." (p. 20) [full-text]
- Deferral: "where a system cannot be made genuinely overseeable, the obligation directs deferral rather than deployment" (p. 20) [full-text]
- Other parties' obligations: "The deployer's obligation to assign a competent overseer and the provider's to ship an overseeable system are real, separately grounded, and discharged otherwise" (p. 20) [full-text]
- Answerability as a Hohfeldian liability: "As Kiener argues, to be answerable is to be liable, in Hohfeld's sense, to a demand for justification; the correlative of that liability is a power, held by certain others, to put the answerable party under an obligation to respond." (p. 22) [full-text]
- Who holds standing: "standing is settled by the deployment context: by whose interests the agenda was meant to protect, grounded in the role obligations of the principal and of others involved in the deployment." In the running example, "these parties are identifiable, e.g. the under-supervised student, the collaborator whose confidential work was mishandled, and each holds the power to demand that the principal answer." (p. 22) [full-text]
- Replies available: a trajectory "may be justified, excused, but also exempted (the harm falls outside what the principal's role required her to guard against) or denied (the agent's action did not in fact produce it)" (p. 22) [full-text]
- Prior opportunity (Kiener's reply to Shoemaker): "One is situational: the chance, at the moment of action, to register and respond to a reason against it. The other is prior: the chance to take meaningful precautions against a type of harm." "So wherever a person has a prospective obligation, she also has an opportunity, in the second sense, to respond to the reasons that ground it, even when, on the specific occasion, the harm was unforeseeable and the situational opportunity is absent." (p. 23) [full-text]
- Strictness: "Answerability is strict in the analogous sense: it holds independently of mens rea, and thus independently of fault." "The agenda is her meaningful precaution against the types of harm her role obliges her to guard against." (p. 23) [full-text]
- Shared, not divided: "Answerability under (i) and (iii) is shared, not distributed: principal and provider are each fully answerable for the harm, not each answerable for a fraction of it." (p. 25) [full-text]
- Answerability is not liability: "Strict answerability closes the gap on its accountability dimension: it guarantees, for any harm within the scope of the principal's role, a party who owes an account. Liability and remedy—who must compensate, and how—are a distinct question" (p. 25) [full-text]
- Scope: "(iv) Multi-agent and multi-principal deployments. Out of scope here." (p. 25) The framework "does not extend, without further work, to multi-agent systems in which agents negotiate jointly, to multi-principal deployments in which several human deciders share oversight responsibility, or to deployments in which the principal is herself an institution rather than a person." (p. 27) [full-text]
- Affected parties' preconditions: "parties affected by the agent's action—recipients of automated decisions, third parties whose information passes through the agent, communities subjected to deployments they did not consent to—additionally require institutionally recognised standing and the resources to engage." (p. 27) [full-text]
- The AI Act mapping: the authors map modifiability and external containment onto art. 14(4)(d)-(e) and read deployer obligations under art. 26 as the likely home of agenda-setting (p. 28). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A single human principal.** The authors exclude, in terms, institutions as principals and deployments with several principals (pp. 25, 27). A hotel firm, and a brand, operator and platform hosting together, fall outside the stated scope. Any application to a host is an extension the authors decline to make.
- **Harm, not performance.** The prospective obligation is to "guard against the harms one's activities impose on others" (p. 20). The dossier's C concerns performance of a service owed to the guest. The two overlap where failed performance harms the guest, but the paper does not speak of duties to perform.
- **The prospective obligation is not said to be owed to anyone.** The relational element attaches to answerability, the backward-looking side: a liability whose correlative power is "held by certain others" (p. 22). The paper does not say that the prospective obligation itself is owed to those parties.
- **No real-time route from the affected party.** Affected stakeholders give "input" at inspection (p. 14), and contestability presupposes standing that the framework does not secure (pp. 17, 26-27). Hand-back to a human is triggered by conditions in the agenda (p. 11), not by the affected party. Nothing in the paper requires a channel from the affected party to a person during operation.
- **Answerability is not blame and not liability** (pp. 21-22, 25). What strictness yields is an obligation to give an account.
- **Agentic AI.** The argument is built for systems acting over long horizons with streams of micro-actions (pp. 2-3). Hotel kiosks and room systems are mostly discrete-decision systems, for which the authors call reactive oversight "empirically fragile, yet increasingly well understood" (p. 1).
- **Preprint under review**, version 1; the SSRN version was not seen.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3: supports a neighbouring principle with a different ground.** A role-holder who delegates to a system has a prospective obligation, grounded in the role, whose discharge consists in "specifying, monitoring, and refining the conditions under which her agent acts" (p. 20), with escalation back to a human built in (p. 11). That is P3's 'means of learning and correcting', grounded in role rather than derived from a duty to perform. It does not supply the guest's route.

**K7, C: supports 'someone with power', not the channel from the guest.** Escalation triggers hand control back to a human (p. 11); stakeholder input comes at inspection (p. 14). The paper gives no premise for a route from the affected party during operation.

**Red team S5 (unforeseen failures): supports in part.** On Kiener's account, as the authors adopt it, a prospective obligation carries a "prior" opportunity "to take meaningful precautions against a type of harm", and answerability holds "even when, on the specific occasion, the harm was unforeseeable" (p. 23). That makes the single unforeseen failure something the role-holder answers for, without fault, if its type falls within what the role required her to guard against (p. 22, 'exempted'). It is a claim about answerability, not about a duty to keep a fall-back.

**Red team S1 and K6 (to whom): supports for answerability only.** The parties with standing are those "whose interests the agenda was meant to protect" (p. 22). The guest would be such a party *(my inference)*. The paper does not say that the prospective obligation is owed to them.

**K8 and red team S9 (several firms): little help.** Principal and provider are "each fully answerable" (p. 25), and their obligations are "separately grounded" (p. 20), but multi-principal and institutional cases are out of scope (pp. 25, 27).

**Novelty neighbour.** The paper's 'role-grounded prospective obligation' with escalation to a human, and its relational answerability, sit close to the paper's keystone. The difference the paper can claim: a route from the person owed the duty, during performance, to someone with power over the single case.

**What the paper may say**

- Baum, Kiener, Langer and Laux argue that a principal who delegates to an agentic system holds a "role-grounded prospective obligation" to guard against harms her activities impose on others, and that setting and refining the system's agenda, including escalation to a human, is what discharging it consists in (pp. 11, 19-20).
- They argue that her backward-looking responsibility is "strict moral answerability", owed to parties whose interests the agenda was meant to protect, and holding even for unforeseeable harms within the scope of her role (pp. 22-23).
- They leave institutional principals and multi-principal deployments outside their account (p. 27).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the authors require a channel from affected parties to a person during operation.
- That they say the prospective obligation is owed to the affected parties.
- That their account covers firms or several firms hosting together.
- That they treat answerability as liability to compensate (p. 25).
