## Summary

Three sisters from the United Arab Emirates, staying with their family at the Cumberland Hotel in London, were attacked with a hammer in their rooms in the early hours of 6 April 2014 by an intruder who had walked in from the street. They sued the hotel company for damages for personal injury. Dingemans J tried liability as a preliminary issue. He held that the hotel owed its guests a duty to take reasonable care to protect them against injury caused by the criminal acts of third parties, and he placed the duty among the cases in which a defendant has assumed a responsibility: the hotel "invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests" (para 187). He held the attack reasonably foreseeable but its likelihood "extremely low" (para 195), measured the hotel against "a reasonable 4 star London hotel" (para 196), and found no breach. The claim failed.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court, date, judge: High Court, Queen's Bench Division, Dingemans J, 21 June 2019, after a ten-day trial (7-22 May 2019); England and Wales (cover sheet). [full-text]
- The question: "whether a hotel proprietor owes a duty to guests to take reasonable care to protect against injury caused by the criminal actions of third parties, and if so whether the duty was breached in this case" (para 1). [full-text]
- Who sued whom: the injured guests and family members "bring a claim for damages against the Defendant the Cumberland Hotel (London) Limited"; the hearing was of "a preliminary issue as to liability and contributory negligence" for the three direct victims (para 7). [full-text]
- The defendant is one company in a group: the head of security worked for "GLH Hotels Limited (“GLH Group”) which is the Group in which the Cumberland hotel is a subsidiary" (para 14); the group operated 15 hotels in London and 3 regional hotels (para 39). Only the hotel company was sued. [full-text]
- The pleaded duty and the admission: the claimants alleged a duty "to take such care as in all the circumstances of the case was reasonable to see that their person and property were kept reasonably safe, whilst they were staying at the hotel" (para 8); "The Defendant admits that it owed the duty to its guests but contends that the duty did not include a responsibility to protect guests from the criminal acts of a third party such as Mr Spence" (para 9). [full-text]
- The general rule the duty is an exception to: "Lord Reed reaffirmed the general reluctance of English law to impose liability in tort for pure omissions"; one exception is where "A has assumed a responsibility to protect B from that danger" (para 182, the judge reporting *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4, para 34). [full-text]
- The innkeeper's law as the starting point: "it is necessary to start off from the fact that the Defendant operates a hotel. The common law developed special rules for those who are InnKeepers, now called hotel proprietors by the Hotel Proprietors Act 1956" (para 183). [full-text]
- Goods against person in the old law: "the law imposed stricter duties for the protection of the goods of guests than the duties for the protection of guests. Under the old common law it seems that the InnKeeper was not liable for any criminal assault on his guests" (para 183, citing *Calye's Case*, which the judge dates 1604). [full-text]
- How the law moved: "The position at common law evolved and by 1917 in MacLenan v Segar it was held in the context of a fire that a hotel proprietor owed a common law duty “to take reasonable care to prevent damage to the guest from unusual danger which the occupier knows or ought to know of”" (para 184). [full-text]
- Holding on the duty (issue 1): "the Cumberland Hotel owed the claimants, as guests of the hotel, a duty of care “to take reasonable care to protect guests at the hotel against injury caused by the criminal acts of third parties”" (para 187; repeated as conclusion (1), para 240). [full-text]
- The basis, in the court's words: "This is because the hotel invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests." (para 187). [full-text]
- The test used: "I have found the duty to exist by reason of the assumption of responsibility test set out in Robinson rather than by the use of the Caparo test, although I should record that in my judgment the imposition of such a duty of care accords with the reasonable expectations of both hotel proprietors and guests" (para 187). [full-text]
- The judge's own gloss on the old rule: "It is clear that the common law relating to hotel proprietors has developed since 1604." (para 187). [full-text]
- A third party's crime does not break the chain: "This is because the duty is to take reasonable care to protect guests against injury caused by the criminal acts of others." (para 190). [full-text]
- Foreseeability (issue 3): "it was reasonably foreseeable to the Cumberland hotel that a third party might gain entry to the hotel and might injure the guests by a criminal assault"; but "the likelihood of such an attack occurring was extremely low, which is relevant to what steps ought reasonably to be taken by the hotel to prevent such an attack" (para 195). [full-text]
- Standard of care: "It was common ground that when assessing what is reasonable the relevant standard is that of a reasonable 4 star London hotel." (para 196). [full-text]
- No breach (issue 4): "the hotel did take reasonable care to protect the Claimants against the injuries caused by Mr Spence"; "The duty was to take reasonable care to prevent the attacks, it was not an absolute duty to prevent an attack." (para 229). [full-text]
- What the duty did not require: "The hotel did not need to monitor continuously CCTV cameras."; "The hotel did not need to have an alarm system to alert security staff to open guest doors." (para 229); no notice or leaflet telling guests to shut their doors (para 230); no second lobby officer and no greeting of every guest after 11 pm (para 232); no key-card access to the lifts (para 233). [full-text]
- Reporting, review and supervision were weighed as parts of reasonable care: a system of reporting through departmental and weekly meetings (para 205); past incidents "were investigated properly using CCTV and key card interrogation and considered" (para 209); the security manager "did work closely with his security officers, and had a good idea about their abilities" (para 213). [full-text]
- Disposition: "(4) the hotel did not act in breach of any duty of care to the Claimants"; "I therefore determine that there was no liability on the part of the Cumberland hotel to Ohoud, Khaloud and Fatima for the attack carried out by Mr Spence." (para 240). Contributory negligence was not decided (para 239). [full-text]
- Agency-supplied staff: the lobby security officer had been trained "as shown by the documents provided by the security agency who employed him and who supplied him to the hotel" (para 63); the back-of-house officer "was formally employed by Assist Security who supplied him to the hotel" (para 64). The judgment assesses their conduct as the hotel's own (paras 213-232) and raises no contractor point. [full-text]
- The group's standards were not the ground of the duty: the "Security Brand Standards" said "the protection of our internal and external guests and their property is of paramount importance", and the judge records that this "was not a statement advertised to guests or which formed part of any contract to stay at the hotel" (para 43). [full-text]
- Evidence, not a finding, on a guest's earlier report: a guest whose locked room a man had entered in 2013 "said that she was not given any care by the hotel when she reported it" (para 92). [full-text]
- Evidence, not the court's ground, on dependence: the group's head of security "noted that guests were particularly vulnerable when asleep in their beds" (para 40). [full-text]
- On goods: the strict liability for guests' goods "was limited to comparatively small sums save in specified circumstances including “default, neglect or wilful act of the proprietor”, see section 2(3)" of the Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 (para 184). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Holding, dictum, result.** The existence and basis of the duty were decided as issue (1) of the preliminary issue (paras 187, 240(1)). The claimants then lost on issue (4), breach. The judgment is authority that the duty exists and for the basis the judge gives; it is not a case in which a hotel was held liable. The causation findings (paras 235-236) answer a hypothetical: what would have followed had a given measure been required.
- **First instance, and the appeal did not test the basis.** The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal on breach alone and recorded that there was "no challenge to the finding" on duty (alnajar2020cumberland, para 19). The statement of basis at para 187 is Dingemans J's alone.
- **The basis is invitation and assumed responsibility.** The judge does not rest the duty on control of the premises, on the guest's dependence, on the contract of stay or on the defendant's status as an innkeeper under the 1956 Act. He starts from innkeeper law (para 183), notes that it did not protect the person, and reaches the duty through the general law of negligence. The paper cannot cite this case for a duty that flows from innkeeper status.
- **Invitation is general here, reliance is not required.** No finding of reliance by these guests on any statement was made; the brand standard was not advertised (para 43). The duty arose from inviting guests to stay. That helps K3's first ground (assumption) and gives nothing to the second (invited reliance) beyond the phrase "reasonable expectations" (para 187).
- **Content is set by practice and likelihood.** What reasonable care required was fixed by the practice of comparable hotels and the low likelihood of attack (paras 197, 229, 233). Continuous monitoring and door alarms were not required. A reader looking for a legal duty to maintain a live channel will not find one here; the facts concern an intruder, not a guest's unanswered report.
- **Silence on contractors.** Two of the three security officers on duty were agency staff (paras 63-64). Nobody argued that the hotel could shelter behind the agency, and the court did not consider it. This is silence, not a ruling.
- **Slips in the judgment.** It dates *Calye's Case* "(1604)" (para 183); the library's card and McCardie J's footnote in *MacLenan* give 1584. It cites para 34 of *Robinson* at para 182 and para 35 at para 187. It prints "Everett v Komojo" (para 187), "Human Rights Act 1988" (para 180) and "4 April 2019" for 2014 (para 132). None affects the reasoning.
- **Later treatment.** Appeal dismissed: [2020] EWCA Civ 1716 (read; alnajar2020cumberland). Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (reception tier): supports, for a hotel.** English law imposes on a hotel a duty of reasonable care for the staying guest's person, extending to third parties' crimes (paras 187, 240). The standard is reasonable care, not insurance (para 229).
- **K3 (grounds): supports the first ground, in the court's own terms; narrows the others.** Assumption by invitation is the stated basis (para 187). Dependence appears only in a witness's evidence (para 40) and reliance not at all.
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** The injured guests sued in their own names on a duty owed to them "as guests of the hotel" (paras 7, 187).
- **K7 (channel): narrows.** The court treated reporting systems, incident review and supervision of officers as elements of reasonable care (paras 205, 209, 213), which fits P2. It refused to require continuous monitoring or alarms (para 229), and the remedy sought was damages after the event.
- **K8 (holder): weak support by silence.** The hotel company answered for agency-supplied security staff without argument (paras 63-64, 213-232). The group was not sued.

**What the paper may say**

- In English law a hotel owes its guests a duty to take reasonable care to protect them against injury caused by the criminal acts of third parties (para 187; para 240(1)).
- The High Court grounded that duty in an assumption of responsibility: the hotel "invited guests to come and stay at the hotel and thereby assumed a duty to take reasonable care to protect guests" (para 187).
- The judge recorded that the old innkeeper's law protected the guest's goods more strictly than the guest, and that the common law "has developed since" (paras 183, 187).
- The duty is one of reasonable care, "not an absolute duty to prevent an attack" (para 229), and the guests' claim failed because the hotel had taken reasonable care (paras 229, 240).
- The hotel admitted a duty to see that its guests' "person and property were kept reasonably safe" while they stayed (paras 8-9).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the hotel was held liable, or that the guests recovered. They lost (para 240).
- That the Court of Appeal approved the assumption-of-responsibility basis. It recorded only that the duty was not in dispute (alnajar2020cumberland, para 19).
- That the duty flows from the hotel's status as an inn or from the contract of stay. The judge used neither.
- That the law requires a hotel to monitor continuously or to keep a person on hand for each guest. The judge refused each such measure on these facts (paras 229-233).
- That the case decides who is liable when security is contracted out. The point was not raised.
- Anything about *Robinson*, *Stansbie* or *Chordas* beyond what this judgment says of them.
