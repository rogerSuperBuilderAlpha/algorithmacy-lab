## Summary

Argues that American public-accommodations law grew from the common law of innkeepers, whose duties existed to protect travellers away from home; that those duties were "bed, board, and hearth"; that they were obligations and privileges in a "well-regulated society", not individual rights; and that nineteenth-century courts and Reconstruction legislators turned the traveller's privileges into rights, which the 1964 litigation then relied on.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Ground: "Underlying all common law protections of travelers was a basic assumption: people who were on a journey, away from their home communities and in unfamiliar surroundings, were particularly vulnerable and therefore deserved a special legal status." (pp. 62-63) [full-text]
- Content: "There were three basic components of innkeeper law, customarily condensed into the phrase “bed, board, and hearth.”" (p. 63) [full-text]
- Duty to receive: "Innkeepers were not at liberty, in other words, to pick and choose their clientele, accepting some as guests while turning others away." (p. 63) [full-text]
- Its limits: "(This rule was not absolute: innkeepers could establish reasonable rules for the behavior of guests and thus exclude, for example, known criminals, people who were drunk and disorderly, or those with contagious diseases" (p. 63) [full-text]
- Holder answers for others: "The innkeeper’s liability was in fact so strict that he was answerable not only for the acts or negligence of his servants, but also those of other guests or any unidentified party." (p. 65) [full-text]
- Not rights: "these were not “rights” in any proper sense of the term." (p. 66) [full-text]
- "Innkeeping is defined not simply as a private business pursued by a profit-seeking individual, but as a public calling subject at all points to regulation by the community." (p. 66; ligature normalised) [full-text]
- "travelers enjoyed privileges and innkeepers bore obligations, but nobody possessed automatic entitlements." (p. 68) [full-text]
- On Singer, who holds that the public calling and not travel grounds the duty to serve; Sandoval-Strausz disputes the history, not the public-calling ground: "I agree with Singer’s normative argument and recognize the existence of public callings, but the historical record indicates that travelers and strangers did indeed have a special status." (p. 63 n. 15) [full-text]
- "its duties and privileges had to be turned into rights" (p. 54) [full-text]
- The traveller could nonetheless sue: "Innkeepers were thus compelled by two separate sets of regulation—the public law of the state and the private law of damages on suit—to serve the public without discriminating among guests." (p. 64) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Three rival grounds for the same duty now sit in the held literature: holding out (burdick1911origin), dependence or monopoly (wyman1904law) and traveller vulnerability (this article), with bogen1996innkeeper's crime-control account against all three. The paper should report the dispute, not pick one silently.
- The traveller-vulnerability ground fits Position A (the guest in another's space) better than Position B. But in law the holder was the innkeeper as such, not whoever controlled the premises.
- "Bed, board, and hearth": hearth is custody of property (p. 65), not care for the person.
- p. 68 is the most useful sentence for R2.3a: the guest's position was a privilege correlated with the innkeeper's obligation, not a free-standing right to be answered.
- American evidence; English roots cited through Bogen.
