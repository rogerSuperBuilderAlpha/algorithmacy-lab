## Summary

Robbins, a registered guest at a Howard Johnson motor lodge owned and operated by Orlando Executive Park (OEP), was attacked on the premises by an unidentified man. A jury found OEP and the franchisor, Howard Johnson Co. (HJ), liable for breach of the duty of reasonable care for her safety and awarded $750,000 compensatory damages against them jointly. The district court of appeal affirmed, holding that the jury could find OEP in breach and could extend liability to HJ under apparent agency. The Florida Supreme Court approved that decision. On OEP, it held that the absence of industry standards did not insulate an innkeeper where there was credible evidence of measures reasonably available, and that what precautions are required is a question for the jury. On HJ, it approved the three elements of apparent agency (representation, reliance, detrimental change of position) and held there was enough evidence for the jury: HJ's signs, national advertising and uniform design, and the fact that HJ "rather than OEP, operated the restaurant, lounge, and adult theater at the motel". Boyd J dissented on causation: nothing showed that the extra guards the plaintiff's expert proposed would have prevented the assault.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Parties and verdict**

- "An unidentified man attacked Robbins while she was a registered guest at a Howard Johnson (HJ) Motor Lodge owned and operated by Orlando Executive Park, Inc. (OEP). Robbins sued for damages, claiming that HJ and OEP violated their legal duty to exercise reasonable care for her safety while she was a guest on the premises." (at 492). [full-text]
- "The jury agreed and awarded her $750,000 compensatory damages against the defendants jointly and $500,000 punitive damages against each defendant separately. The trial court vacated verdicts for OEP and HJ on the'punitive damages" (at 492; "the'punitive" is the copy's OCR). [full-text]
- The district court of appeal held "that the jury could properly find that OEP breached its duty of care and that liability could be extended to HJ under the doctrine of apparent agency." (at 492). [full-text]

**The innkeeper's duty (OEP)**

- "An innkeeper owes the duty of reasonable care for the safety of his guests ... and it is “peculiarly a jury function to determine what precautions are reasonably required in the exercise of a particular duty of due care.”" (at 493, quoting Holley, 382 So. 2d at 100-01). [full-text]
- The district court, approved: "absence of industry standards does not insulate the defendants from liability when there is credible evidence presented to the jury pointing to measures reasonably available to deter incidents of this kind, against which the jury can judge the reasonableness of the measures taken in this case." (at 493, quoting 402 So. 2d at 447). [full-text]
- The evidence: "numerous episodes of eriminal activity, of which the management had knowledge, had occurred during the immediately preceding six-month period and that the situation required the presence of security guards." (at 493; "eriminal" is the copy's OCR). [full-text]

**The franchisor (HJ): apparent agency**

- The district court, approved: "While OEP might not be HJ’s agent for all purposes, the signs, national advertising, uniformity of building design and color schemes allows the public to assume that this and other similar motor lodges are under the same ownership." (at 494, quoting 402 So. 2d at 450). [full-text]
- "As HJ concedes, the district court correctly set out the three elements needed to establish apparent agency: “(1) a representation by the principal; (2) reliance on that representation by a third person; and (3) a change of position by the third person in reliance upon such representation to his detriment.”" (at 494). [full-text]
- "Robbins presented sufficient evidence to allow the jury to conclude that HJ represented to the public that it could find a certain level of service at this motel. Besides the evidence pointed out by the district court, we note that HJ, rather than OEP, operated the restaurant, lounge, and adult theater at the motel. The complex was an integrated commercial enterprise, and HJ’s direct participation was significant. The district court also correctly pointed out the sufficiency of Robbin’s evidence regarding her reliance on HJ." (at 494). [full-text]
- The oil-company line of cases (no apparent authority from trade name and product supply) was limited "to their facts" (at 494). "We therefore approve the instant district court opinion." (at 494). [full-text]

**The dissent on causation (Boyd J)**

- "I must respectfully dissent because there is no evidence that the defendants’ negligence actually contributed to causing the plaintiff’s injuries. That is to say that even if the defendants had provided the security precautions which plaintiff’s expert witness testified were needed, there was no evidence that such additional precautions might have deterred or prevented this particular crime." (at 494, Boyd J). [full-text]
- The expert, on cross-examination: "Q. You are not testifying here today that your proposals, your hypothetical proposals would have in any way affected or prevented the assault on Mrs. Robbins, are you? A. No." (at 495, Boyd J). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The ground was apparent agency.** HJ's operation of the restaurant, lounge and theatre is offered as further evidence that HJ "represented to the public that it could find a certain level of service at this motel" (at 494). The court does not hold that operating those components made HJ an actual principal, or liable for its own negligence in operating them. "HJ’s direct participation was significant" is said within the apparent-agency analysis.
- **Concurrent liability.** Owner-operator and franchisor were held liable "jointly" (at 492). One holder was not picked; both answered.
- **Causation was not answered by the majority.** The majority treats the question as "one of foreseeability" (at 493). Boyd J's dissent is the only passage addressing whether the omitted measures would have prevented the harm (at 494-496). Its point is the same causation problem Gregg v Scott raises in England (card gregg2005ukhl).
- **Florida law, 1983.** Later Florida treatment not checked here; Mobil Oil v Bransford (card mobil1995bransford) is a later Florida Supreme Court decision on franchisor agency that preserves this case.
- OCR errors in the copy ("the'punitive", "eriminal", "Robbin’s") are reproduced as printed.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation), the brand row: refutes the row as written.** The dossier's brand row says 'Control: No' and that a brand that lends its name answers, if at all, for the operator's fault, and that a brand running a guest-facing system is a supplier to the holder. Here the brand answered to the guest jointly with the owner-operator on apparent agency (at 492, 494), and its own operation of guest-facing components at the motel counted towards the representation the guest relied on (at 494). The row must say that a brand which holds the property out as its own and runs parts of the guest's experience may answer to the guest directly, as an apparent principal, in at least one US jurisdiction.

**K4 (legal tiers): supports.** "An innkeeper owes the duty of reasonable care for the safety of his guests" (at 493).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Supreme Court of Florida approved the district court of appeal's decision upholding liability of both a motel's owner-operator and its franchisor, Howard Johnson, to a guest attacked on the premises (at 492, 494). (Narrowed after CV-G: the Court approved the appeal court's decision, not 'a verdict'.)
- It held the franchisor answerable on apparent agency, whose elements are "(1) a representation by the principal; (2) reliance on that representation by a third person; and (3) a change of position by the third person in reliance upon such representation to his detriment" (at 494).
- The court noted that the franchisor, "rather than OEP, operated the restaurant, lounge, and adult theater at the motel", that "The complex was an integrated commercial enterprise", and that the franchisor's "direct participation was significant" (at 494).
- A dissenting justice would have reversed for want of evidence that the omitted security measures would have prevented the assault (at 494-496, Boyd J).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the court held the franchisor liable for its own negligence, or held it an actual principal because it ran components. The holding is apparent agency.
- That the case states English or EU law, or a general US rule.
- That the court resolved causation; only the dissent addresses it.
