## Summary

Title 4.9 of the Digest gathers seven fragments of Ulpian, Gaius and Paul on the praetor's edict that gave an action against seamen (*nautae*), innkeepers (*caupones*) and stable-keepers (*stabularii*) who failed to restore what they had received for safekeeping. The liability covers things only. It is strict: the one who received answers even without fault, save for irresistible force. Ulpian gives the reasons: travellers must generally trust such people with their things, the people of this trade are otherwise tempted to collude with thieves, and the rule is not hard on them because they are free to receive nobody. A guest who stays without paying keeps the action. The operator answers for his staff and for other travellers. The title says nothing of a duty to admit, and nothing of the guest's bodily safety.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- **Which texts.** Latin from The Latin Library (headed "Dig. 4.9.0. Nautae caupones stabularii ut recepta restituant."); English from S. P. Scott, *The Civil Law*, vol. III (Cincinnati, 1932), where the title is headed "Sailors, innkeepers, and the proprietors of stables, must restore property entrusted to them." The fragments are Ulpian *ad edictum* 14 (frr. 1, 3), Gaius *ad edictum provinciale* 5 (frr. 2, 5), Paul *ad edictum* 13 (fr. 4) and 22 (fr. 6), and Ulpian *ad edictum* 18 (fr. 7). [full-text]
- **The edict.** "nautae caupones stabularii quod cuiusque salvum fore receperint nisi restituent, in eos iudicium dabo" (D. 4.9.1 pr.). My gloss: against seamen, innkeepers and stable-keepers who do not restore what they have received from anyone on the footing that it will be safe, I will give an action. [full-text]
- **The ground, and the freedom to refuse.** "Maxima utilitas est huius edicti, quia necesse est plerumque eorum fidem sequi et res custodiae eorum committere. ne quisquam putet graviter hoc adversus eos constitutum: nam est in ipsorum arbitrio, ne quem recipiant, et nisi hoc esset statutum, materia daretur cum furibus adversus eos quos recipiunt coeundi, cum ne nunc quidem abstineant huiusmodi fraudibus." (D. 4.9.1.1). My gloss: the edict is most useful, because one must generally rely on their good faith and commit things to their custody; nobody should think it hard on them, for it is in their own discretion to receive no one; and without it they would have occasion to combine with thieves against those they receive, since even now they do not refrain from such frauds. [full-text]
- **Scott's rendering of the same clause.** "No one should think that this Edict imposes any hardship upon them, for they have the choice of refusing to receive anyone" (D. 4.9.1.1, Scott) [full-text]
- **A second statement of purpose.** Pomponius, as Ulpian reports him, supposed the praetorian action was added to the civil ones "ut innotesceret praetor curam agere reprimendae improbitatis hoc genus hominum" (D. 4.9.3.1). My gloss: so that it should be known that the praetor took care to repress the dishonesty of this kind of men. [full-text]
- **What it covers.** Things: "hoc est quamcumque rem sive mercem receperint" (D. 4.9.1.6), extended to clothing and things of daily use. No fragment speaks of injury to the guest's body. [full-text]
- **Strict liability and its one exception.** "at hoc edicto omnimodo qui receperit tenetur, etiam si sine culpa eius res periit vel damnum datum est, nisi si quid damno fatali contingit"; and for the inn, "idem erit dicendum et si in stabulo aut in caupona vis maior contigerit" (D. 4.9.3.1). My gloss: under this edict the one who received is liable in any case, even if the thing perished or was damaged without his fault, unless by unavoidable accident; the same where irresistible force occurs in a stable or inn. [full-text]
- **Who is the innkeeper.** "Caupones autem et stabularios aeque eos accipiemus, qui cauponam vel stabulum exercent, institoresve eorum. ceterum si qui opera mediastini fungitur, non continetur, ut puta atriarii et focarii et his similes." (D. 4.9.1.5). My gloss: those who run an inn or stable, and their managers; a person doing a menial's work is not included, such as doorkeepers and cooks. [full-text]
- **Only in the course of the business.** "Eodem modo tenentur caupones et stabularii, quo exercentes negotium suum recipiunt: ceterum si extra negotium receperunt, non tenebuntur." (D. 4.9.3.2) [full-text]
- **The innkeeper answers for other travellers.** Of the ship, Ulpian holds that the operator must answer for the acts "non solum nautarum ... sed et vectorum" (D. 4.9.1.8); Gaius adds "Sicut et caupo viatorum." (D. 4.9.2). Scott: "Just as an innkeeper is liable for the acts of travellers." [full-text]
- **The pay is not for custody.** "Nauta et caupo et stabularius mercedem accipiunt non pro custodia, sed nauta ut traiciat vectores, caupo ut viatores manere in caupona patiatur, stabularius ut permittat iumenta apud eum stabulari: et tamen custodiae nomine tenentur." (D. 4.9.5 pr.). My gloss: the innkeeper is paid to let travellers stay in the inn, not for custody, and is liable for custody all the same. [full-text]
- **A guest who pays nothing keeps the action.** "Licet gratis navigaveris vel in caupona gratis deverteris, non tamen in factum actiones tibi denegabuntur, si damnum iniuria passus es." (D. 4.9.6 pr.) [full-text]
- **Who may sue.** The person with the interest in the thing's safety, whether owner or not: "parvi referre, res nostras an alienas intulerimus, si tamen nostra intersit salvas esse" (D. 4.9.1.7). The action is the guest's own; no public prosecution appears in the title. [full-text]
- **Staff.** "Debet exercitor omnium nautarum suorum, sive liberi sint sive servi, factum praestare: nec immerito factum eorum praestat, cum ipse eos suo periculo adhibuerit." (D. 4.9.7 pr., of the ship's operator). My gloss: he must answer for the acts of all his crew, free or slave, and rightly, since he took them on at his own risk. [full-text]
- **Checking those he takes on.** The operator is liable in his own name, "culpae scilicet suae qui tales adhibuit", and "cum alienos adhibet, explorare eum oportet, cuius fidei, cuius innocentiae sint" (D. 4.9.7.4, of the ship's operator). My gloss: for his own fault in employing such people; when he employs others' slaves he ought to find out how trustworthy and how honest they are. [full-text]
- **Those placed in a post bind the operator.** An action lies against the operator for what his appointed guards received, "quia is, qui eos huiusmodi officio praeponit, committi eis permittit" (D. 4.9.1.3). My gloss: because he who sets them to such a duty allows things to be entrusted to them. [full-text]
- **A warning the passengers accept removes liability.** "item si praedixerit, ut unusquisque vectorum res suas servet neque damnum se praestaturum, et consenserint vectores praedictioni, non convenitur." (D. 4.9.7 pr., of the ship's operator). My gloss: if he has given notice that each passenger is to look after his own things and that he will not answer for loss, and the passengers have agreed to the notice, he is not sued. [full-text]
- **Residents and passing guests.** "In factum actione caupo tenetur pro his, qui habitandi causa in caupona sunt: hoc autem non pertinet ad eum, qui hospitio repentino recipitur, veluti viator." (D. 4.9.6.3). Scott: the innkeeper is liable "on account of those who have lodgings in the inn, but this rule does not apply to a party who is entertained as a transient guest, as, for instance, a traveller". [full-text]
- **Running the inn with the owner's consent.** Where a slave or son runs a ship, inn or stable with the master's or father's consent, Ulpian thinks them liable in full, "quasi omnia, quae ibi contingunt, in solidum receperint" (D. 4.9.7.6). My gloss: as if they had taken on in full everything that happens there. [full-text]
- **What the guest must do.** Nothing by way of notice or deposit is required in the title. Of a ship, things are treated as received by being brought aboard: "omnium eum recipere custodiam, quae in navem illatae sunt" (D. 4.9.1.8). [full-text]
- **May the innkeeper refuse a traveller?** Yes, on the text: "est in ipsorum arbitrio, ne quem recipiant" (D. 4.9.1.1). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Freedom to refuse is one of Ulpian's reasons for the strict liability.** The rule is fair because the innkeeper chose to receive. The Roman text therefore joins a strict duty for goods to an express denial of any duty to admit.
- **Two actions sit in one title.** Fragments 1–5 concern the action on the receipt (Ulpian calls it "de recepto honoraria actione" at 4.9.3.5). Fragments 6–7 concern an action for double (4.9.7.1) for damage or theft by the operator's people. This division is my reading of the text; I read no Roman-law commentary. Several of the passages most useful to the paper (4.9.7 pr., 4.9.7.4) are said of the ship's operator, and their extension to the innkeeper rests on the title treating ship, inn and stable together (4.9.7.6).
- **Selection, not supervision.** The operator's fault at 4.9.7.4 lies in whom he employed and in not looking into them beforehand. The title states no duty to watch staff or to learn of failures during the stay.
- **Scott is not safe to quote as the Digest.** In the title read, he renders "parvi referre" (it matters little) as "it makes a little difference" (4.9.1.7) and "idem ait" (he also says) as "He also asks" (4.9.3 pr.), and the Greek terms at 4.9.1.3 are transliterated in the Latin web text and garbled in the English one. The Latin should be quoted and glossed afresh, or checked against a modern translation, which I did not read.
- **Provenance of the Latin.** The Latin Library gives no editor. A quotation in print should be checked against a critical edition.
- **Dates.** The saved files give no date for the Digest or for the jurists. Any date in the paper needs its own source.
- **"Caupo" is Scott's "innkeeper".** Whether a *caupona* corresponds to a hotel, a tavern with rooms or both is not settled by the text.
- **Later treatment not checked.** How later civil law received the edict was not read here; the scout lists a South African article (Vrancken) as unread.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (two tiers): supports the goods limb of reception outside the common law; refutes a general duty to receive; silent on the person.** The goods duty is Roman (4.9.1 pr., 4.9.3.1). The admission tier is denied in terms (4.9.1.1). Nothing protects the guest's body.
- **K3 (grounds): supports assumption and forced reliance; adds a ground K3 treats as a rival.** Ulpian's reasons are that travellers must rely on the innkeeper's good faith (close to K3's reliance and dependence) and that the innkeeper chose to receive (voluntary assumption). He and Pomponius also give crime control: the edict checks collusion with thieves (4.9.1.1) and represses the dishonesty of people in this trade ("reprimendae improbitatis hoc genus hominum", 4.9.3.1). The duty attaches to running the business, not to payment (4.9.5 pr., 4.9.6 pr.), which fits the role as specification.
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** The praetor gives the guest an action; the person with the interest sues (4.9.1.7); the non-paying guest keeps it (4.9.6 pr.).
- **K8 and K7: supports the holder's answerability for staff; gives no support to a duty of continuing oversight.** The operator answers for those he employs "suo periculo" (4.9.7 pr.) and for those he places in a post (4.9.1.3). The stated fault is in selection (4.9.7.4). An agreed warning could remove the liability (4.9.7 pr.), which the modern civil-law texts forbid (cards bgb701, coe1962hotelkeepers).

**What the paper may say** (as Roman law in Justinian's Digest, citing fragment and section):
- The praetor's edict gave a guest an action against an innkeeper who did not restore what he had received, and the liability did not depend on fault, irresistible force apart (D. 4.9.1 pr.; 4.9.3.1).
- Ulpian explains the edict by the traveller's need to trust the innkeeper with his things and by the risk of collusion with thieves, and says it is not harsh because innkeepers are free to receive no one (D. 4.9.1.1).
- The innkeeper was paid for letting travellers stay and not for custody, and a guest lodged for nothing kept the action (D. 4.9.5 pr.; 4.9.6 pr.).
- The innkeeper answered for the acts of other travellers (D. 4.9.2).
- The title makes a ship's operator answer for the crew he took on "at his own risk" and treats ship, inn and stable together (D. 4.9.7 pr.; 4.9.7.6).

**What the paper may not say:**
- That Roman law obliged an innkeeper to receive travellers. The text says the contrary.
- That the title protects the guest's person or welfare.
- That D. 4.9.7 pr. or 4.9.7.4 speaks of innkeepers by name.
- That the title requires the operator to supervise staff or to be reachable.
- Scott's English as a quotation of the Digest, or any date for the texts, on this card's authority.
