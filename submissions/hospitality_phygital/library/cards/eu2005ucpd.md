## Summary

The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive harmonises the law on commercial practices of traders towards consumers. Article 5 prohibits unfair practices; a practice is unfair if it is contrary to professional diligence and distorts the economic behaviour of the average consumer, and in particular if it is misleading or aggressive. Article 8 defines an aggressive practice as one that, by harassment, coercion or undue influence, significantly impairs the average consumer's freedom of choice or conduct and so causes a transactional decision the consumer would not otherwise have taken. Article 9 lists what must be taken into account in deciding whether a practice uses harassment, coercion or undue influence; point (d) is 'any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract'. The Directive applies 'before, during and after a commercial transaction' (art. 3(1)), and a 'transactional decision' includes a decision 'to exercise a contractual right' (art. 2(k)), so it reaches practices during performance. Enforcement is by persons or bodies with a legitimate interest before courts or administrative authorities (art. 11). Since Directive (EU) 2019/2161 a consumer harmed by an unfair practice must have access to remedies, including compensation, under conditions national law may set (art. 11a, applied from 28 May 2022).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Who and what it covers**

- 'consumer' means "any natural person who, in commercial practices covered by this Directive, is acting for purposes which are outside his trade, business, craft or profession" (art. 2(a)). [full-text]
- 'trader' means "any natural or legal person who, in commercial practices covered by this Directive, is acting for purposes relating to his trade, business, craft or profession and anyone acting in the name of or on behalf of a trader" (art. 2(b)). [full-text]
- 'product' (as replaced by Directive 2019/2161) means "any good or service including immovable property, digital service and digital content, as well as rights and obligations" (art. 2(c), cons.). [full-text]
- 'business-to-consumer commercial practices' means "any act, omission, course of conduct or representation, commercial communication including advertising and marketing, by a trader, directly connected with the promotion, sale or supply of a product to consumers" (art. 2(d)). [full-text]
- 'transactional decision' means "any decision taken by a consumer concerning whether, how and on what terms to purchase, make payment in whole or in part for, retain or dispose of a product or to exercise a contractual right in relation to the product, whether the consumer decides to act or to refrain from acting" (art. 2(k)). [full-text]
- Time: "This Directive shall apply to unfair business-to-consumer commercial practices, as laid down in Article 5, before, during and after a commercial transaction in relation to a product." (art. 3(1)). Recital 13 says the general prohibition "should apply equally to unfair commercial practices which occur outside any contractual relationship between a trader and a consumer or following the conclusion of a contract and during its execution." [full-text]
- What it leaves alone: "This Directive is without prejudice to contract law and, in particular, to the rules on the validity, formation or effect of a contract." (art. 3(2)); "This Directive is without prejudice to Community or national rules relating to the health and safety aspects of products." (art. 3(3)). [full-text]
- It protects economic interests only: the purpose is to approximate the laws "on unfair commercial practices harming consumers' economic interests" (art. 1). [full-text]

**The prohibition and the aggressive-practice test**

- "Unfair commercial practices shall be prohibited." (art. 5(1)). A practice is unfair if "(a) it is contrary to the requirements of professional diligence, and (b) it materially distorts or is likely to materially distort the economic behaviour with regard to the product of the average consumer whom it reaches or to whom it is addressed" (art. 5(2)); "In particular, commercial practices shall be unfair which: ... (b) are aggressive as set out in Articles 8 and 9." (art. 5(4)). [full-text]
- Vulnerable groups: practices likely to distort the behaviour "only of a clearly identifiable group of consumers who are particularly vulnerable to the practice or the underlying product because of their mental or physical infirmity, age or credulity in a way which the trader could reasonably be expected to foresee, shall be assessed from the perspective of the average member of that group" (art. 5(3)). [full-text]
- Article 8: "A commercial practice shall be regarded as aggressive if, in its factual context, taking account of all its features and circumstances, by harassment, coercion, including the use of physical force, or undue influence, it significantly impairs or is likely to significantly impair the average consumer's freedom of choice or conduct with regard to the product and thereby causes him or is likely to cause him to take a transactional decision that he would not have taken otherwise." [full-text]
- Article 9, opening words: "In determining whether a commercial practice uses harassment, coercion, including the use of physical force, or undue influence, account shall be taken of:" (art. 9, identical in the 2005 text and the 27.9.2026 consolidation). [full-text]
- Article 9(d): "any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract, including rights to terminate a contract or to switch to another product or another trader;" (art. 9(d); 2005 Official Journal text, unchanged in the consolidation). [full-text]
- The other factors include "its timing, location, nature or persistence" (art. 9(a)) and "the exploitation by the trader of any specific misfortune or circumstance of such gravity as to impair the consumer's judgement, of which the trader is aware, to influence the consumer's decision with regard to the product" (art. 9(c)). [full-text]
- 'undue influence' means "exploiting a position of power in relation to the consumer so as to apply pressure, even without using or threatening to use physical force, in a way which significantly limits the consumer's ability to make an informed decision" (art. 2(j)). [full-text]
- Recital 16: "The provisions on aggressive commercial practices should cover those practices which significantly impair the consumer's freedom of choice. Those are practices using harassment, coercion, including the use of physical force, and undue influence." [full-text]
- Annex I point 27, aggressive in all circumstances: "Requiring a consumer who wishes to claim on an insurance policy to produce documents which could not reasonably be considered relevant as to whether the claim was valid, or failing systematically to respond to pertinent correspondence, in order to dissuade a consumer from exercising his contractual rights." (Annex I, point 27, cons.). [full-text]

**Enforcement and the individual remedy**

- Enforcement means "shall include legal provisions under which persons or organisations regarded under national law as having a legitimate interest in combating unfair commercial practices, including competitors, may: (a) take legal action against such unfair commercial practices; and/or (b) bring such unfair commercial practices before an administrative authority" (art. 11(1)). Courts or authorities may order cessation "even without proof of actual loss or damage or of intention or negligence on the part of the trader" (art. 11(2)). [full-text]
- Article 11a(1), inserted by Directive 2019/2161: "Consumers harmed by unfair commercial practices, shall have access to proportionate and effective remedies, including compensation for damage suffered by the consumer and, where relevant, a price reduction or the termination of the contract. Member States may determine the conditions for the application and effects of those remedies." Art. 11a(2): "Those remedies shall be without prejudice to the application of other remedies available to consumers under Union or national law." [full-text]
- From when: Member States were to apply the 2005 measures "by 12 December 2007" (art. 19). Art. 11a applies from 28 May 2022 (Directive 2019/2161, art. 7(1): "They shall apply those measures from 28 May 2022."). [full-text]
- Recital 9 (2005): "This Directive is without prejudice to individual actions brought by those who have been harmed by an unfair commercial practice." [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Art. 9(d) is a factor, not a rule.** The words open "account shall be taken of" (art. 9). A barrier to exercising contractual rights counts towards a finding that a practice uses harassment, coercion or undue influence; the practice is aggressive only if it then "significantly impairs" the average consumer's freedom of choice or conduct and causes a transactional decision (art. 8). The red team's gloss (T6) that art. 9(d) treats such barriers "as a mark of an aggressive practice" is fair; any reading that makes art. 9(d) a free-standing prohibition is not.
- **'Imposed' barriers, not missing routes.** Art. 9(d) speaks of barriers "imposed by the trader". Whether a host that simply keeps no reachable person during the stay has 'imposed' a barrier is not settled by the text. I found no judgment applying art. 9(d) (none searched beyond this text).
- **Economic interests only.** The Directive protects "consumers' economic interests" (art. 1) and is "without prejudice" to health and safety rules (art. 3(3)) and to contract law (art. 3(2)). A guest's injury claim does not run through it.
- **Consumers and traders only.** It reaches a natural person acting outside his trade (art. 2(a)); the business traveller, and a companion who is not party to any transactional decision, are outside its protection on the text's terms. That limit matches T7.
- **Who is the 'trader'.** The definition includes "anyone acting in the name of or on behalf of a trader" (art. 2(b)). A system vendor or brand operating the guest's check-in or complaint interface on the host's behalf may on that wording be a trader for this Directive's purposes *(my inference; no case read)*.
- **The individual remedy is shaped nationally.** Art. 11a obliges Member States to provide remedies but lets them "determine the conditions for the application and effects" (art. 11a(1)). It is not a uniform private right of action.
- **Annex I point 27 may be confined to insurance claims.** The sentence ties "failing systematically to respond to pertinent correspondence" to dissuading the exercise of contractual rights, but whether the second limb is limited to insurance claims is a question of construction the text does not settle. Do not rely on it for hotels without a judgment.
- **Not English law now.** The Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, which implemented this Directive in the UK, were revoked by the DMCC Act 2024, s. 251(1) (card uk2024dmcc).
- Art. 6-7 (misleading practices) and Annex I points 1-23j not read. No CJEU judgment on art. 8-9 read.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7 (channel from the guest): narrows, and gives the legal register a correlate of the right kind.** The red team's T4 holds that the law knows channels only as the consumer's own remedial right or as a duty to a regulator. This Directive fits that: it treats obstruction of the consumer's exercise of contractual rights as a mark of an aggressive practice (art. 9(d)), applies during performance (art. 3(1), recital 13; art. 2(k)), and is enforced by public action (art. 11) and, since 2022, by remedies for the harmed consumer under national conditions (art. 11a). It does not require any trader to keep a route to a person with power over the single case. The legal correlate Step 6 may draw from it is negative: a host may not put onerous or disproportionate obstacles in the way of a consumer guest who seeks to exercise contractual rights during the stay *(my inference from arts 2(k), 3(1), 8, 9(d))*.

**K6 (the guest's claim): supports the guest-side framing in the legal register.** The protected person is the consumer exercising "rights under the contract" (art. 9(d)); the remedy runs to "Consumers harmed" (art. 11a(1)).

**K4 (legal tiers): narrows.** What is protected is economic behaviour in a consumer contract, not the guest's person (art. 1, 3(3)).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- EU law counts "any onerous or disproportionate non-contractual barriers imposed by the trader where a consumer wishes to exercise rights under the contract" among the matters to be taken into account in deciding whether a commercial practice is aggressive (Directive 2005/29/EC, art. 9(d)).
- The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive applies "before, during and after a commercial transaction" (art. 3(1)), and a consumer's decision whether to exercise a contractual right is a 'transactional decision' (art. 2(k)).
- Since 28 May 2022 Member States must give consumers harmed by unfair commercial practices "access to proportionate and effective remedies, including compensation" (art. 11a(1), inserted by Directive (EU) 2019/2161; applied from 28 May 2022, art. 7(1) of that Directive).
- The Directive protects consumers' economic interests and is "without prejudice" to rules on the health and safety aspects of products and to contract law (arts 1, 3(2)-(3)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Directive requires a host, or any trader, to provide a channel, a contact person or a person with power to correct performance. It contains no such requirement.
- That art. 9(d) by itself makes a practice unlawful. It is a factor under art. 9 in the test of art. 8.
- That it gives a remedy to a guest who is not a consumer, or who made no transactional decision (a business traveller; a non-contracting companion).
- That it governs injury to the guest. It concerns economic interests (art. 1) and leaves health and safety rules aside (art. 3(3)).
- That it is the law of England. The UK implementing regulations were revoked by the DMCC Act 2024 (uk2024dmcc).
- That Annex I point 27 reaches a hotel's failure to answer complaints. Its scope beyond insurance claims is not settled by the text.
