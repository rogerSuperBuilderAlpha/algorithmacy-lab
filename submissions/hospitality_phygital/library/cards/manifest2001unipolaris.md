## Summary

The *Star Sea*, a ship of the Kollakis fleet, became a constructive total loss after an engine-room fire that her CO2 system failed to put out, because the master did not know how to use it and the funnel dampers could not be closed. Under section 39(5) of the Marine Insurance Act 1906 the insurers were not liable for a loss attributable to unseaworthiness if the ship was sent to sea "with the privity of the assured". The year before, two other ships of the fleet had been lost in engine-room fires for similar reasons; the managers changed crews but took no steps to check the new master's knowledge of the CO2 system and did not instruct anyone to check the dampers. The trial judge found 'blind-eye knowledge'. The Court of Appeal and a unanimous House of Lords held that this was negligence, perhaps gross, and not privity. Lord Clyde, Lord Hobhouse and Lord Scott each state the test: suspicion of the specific facts and a deliberate decision not to confirm them. English law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and composition: House of Lords, 18 January 2001, [2001] UKHL 1. Lord Steyn agreed with Lord Clyde, Lord Hobhouse and Lord Scott (para 1); Lord Hoffmann agreed with Lord Hobhouse and Lord Scott (para 2). The appeal was dismissed on both issues. [full-text]
- The statute, as quoted: "In a time policy there is no implied warranty that the ship shall be seaworthy at any stage of the adventure, but where, with the privity of the assured, the ship is sent to sea in an unseaworthy state, the insurer is not liable for any loss attributable to unseaworthiness." (Marine Insurance Act 1906, s. 39(5), quoted at para 15 and para 86) [full-text]
- Lord Clyde: "Blind-eye knowledge in my judgment requires a conscious reason for blinding the eye. There must be at least a suspicion of a truth about which you do not want to know and which you refuse to investigate." (para 3) [full-text]
- The earlier formulations, as Lord Hobhouse quotes them: Lord Denning MR in *The Eurysthenes*: "If a man, suspicious of the truth, turns a blind eye to it, and refrains from inquiry - so that he should not know it for certain - then he is to be regarded as knowing the truth. This 'turning a blind eye' is far more blameworthy than mere negligence. Negligence in not knowing the truth is not equivalent to knowledge of it." Geoffrey Lane LJ: "I add the word 'believed' to cover the man who deliberately turns a blind eye to what he believes to be true in order to avoid obtaining certain knowledge of the truth." (para 24) [full-text]
- Lord Hobhouse on the test: "All these formulations reject the suggestion that even gross negligence will suffice." "The illuminating question therefore becomes "why did he not inquire?". If the judge is satisfied that it was because he did not want to know for certain, then a finding of privity should be made. If, on the other hand, he did not enquire because he was too lazy or he was grossly negligent or believed that there was nothing wrong, then privity has not been made out." "The test is subjective: Did the assured have direct knowledge of the unseaworthiness or an actual state of mind which the law treats as equivalent to such knowledge?" (para 25) [full-text]
- Branson J in *The Gloria* (1935), as quoted: "But the mere omission to take precautions against the possibility of the ship being unseaworthy cannot, I think, make the owner privy to any unseaworthiness which such precaution might have disclosed." Lord Hobhouse's own statement: "If the shipowner deliberately refrains from examining the ship in order not to gain direct knowledge of what he has reason to believe is her unseaworthy state, he is privy to the ship putting to sea in that unseaworthy state." (para 26) [full-text]
- Whose mind counts for a company: "the privity must be of an individual who is to be identified with the assured" (para 27, Lord Hobhouse). The owner was a Cypriot company; management was delegated to an English company and a Greek company; four individuals' states of mind were considered (paras 27, 88). [full-text]
- What the managers did and did not do after the earlier fires: they changed to Greek officers, "But it was also the case that they took no steps to check his knowledge of the right way to use the CO2 system." (para 30). "So far as the maintenance of the equipment in engine-rooms was concerned, the managers of the fleet did not take any special steps." "However, neither in relation to the Star Sea nor in relation to other vessels did the management specifically require or instruct their superintendents to check the state of the dampers and other engine-room sealing components." (para 31). "the managers were at fault" (para 32). [full-text]
- The trial judge's finding, as quoted: "The assured did not want to know about the competence of the master to use the CO2 system effectively. It is not easy to find a reason for this, since it would not have involved much time or money to ensure that the master was competent." (para 32, quoting Tuckey J) [full-text]
- Why it failed: the judge's phrases were "all postulated upon the inadequacy of the response to the earlier fires not upon any belief that would relate to the Star Sea herself" (para 33); "A finding of negligence to a very high degree did not suffice for a finding of privity." (para 34); "the inadequate response to the previous casualties was evidence consistent with a number of states of mind of those concerned with the management of the fleet and does not without more establish that there was privity in relation to any individual vessel." (para 35, Lord Hobhouse) [full-text]
- Lord Scott on the test: "an imputation of blind-eye knowledge requires an amalgam of suspicion that certain facts may exist and a decision to refrain from taking any step to confirm their existence." (para 112). He quotes Lord Blackburn's contrast between a person "honestly blundering and careless" and one who suspects and does not ask; Lord Blackburn added "I think that is dishonesty" (para 112, quoting *Jones v Gordon* (1877)). [full-text]
- Lord Scott rejects an objective element: "I do not, myself, see how a failure to think about the facts, and hence a failure to realise their implications, can afford the basis for a finding of blind-eye knowledge." (para 114) [full-text]
- "There must be a suspicion of the relevant unseaworthiness, and a decision not to check. Unless there is a decision not to check, not to obtain confirmation of what is suspected, there will, in my opinion, be no privity, no blind-eye knowledge, however seriously negligent the failure to check may be." (para 115, Lord Scott) [full-text]
- The quality of suspicion required: "In summary, blind-eye knowledge requires, in my opinion, a suspicion that the relevant facts do exist and a deliberate decision to avoid confirming that they exist. But a warning should be sounded." "In my opinion, in order for there to be blind-eye knowledge, the suspicion must be firmly grounded and targeted on specific facts." "To allow blind-eye knowledge to be constituted by a decision not to enquire into an untargeted or speculative suspicion would be to allow negligence, albeit gross, to be the basis of a finding of privity." (para 116, Lord Scott) [full-text]
- On the privity test (not a duty of care), the earlier losses did not supply the targeted suspicion blind-eye knowledge needs: "The incompetence of the masters of the Centaurus and the Kastora did not put the Kollakis brothers, Mr Faraklas or Mr Nicholaidis in a position in which they were bound to enquire into the competence of every other master in the Kollakis fleet." (para 117, Lord Scott) [full-text]
- A missing system is negligence, not knowledge: on the dampers, contractors had been hired to overhaul them, and "The judge went on to say that some system should have been in place to ensure that the necessary work had been done." (para 118). "These findings go to negligence, perhaps gross negligence, but they are, in my opinion, no basis for a finding of blind-eye knowledge." The evidence did not show that the four individuals, "whether viewed individually or collectively", sent the ship to sea "believing or suspecting that that might be so" (para 119, Lord Scott). [full-text]
- Not in the opinions read: any reference to a hotel, guest or duty of care owed to a third party (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Register.** Section 39(5) decides whether an insured shipowner loses cover. Blind-eye knowledge here attributes knowledge to defeat the assured's own claim; no one is owed a duty, and the passages say nothing about what an owner must do for others. For a host's duty to a guest the case is an analogy, and the paper must say so.
- **Why it is the closest case.** The facts are a manager's response to known, general risks: two earlier losses, a decision to change crews, maintenance contracted out, no check of the master, did "not take any special steps" (para 31) and gave no instruction to check the dampers (paras 30-31), and a trial judge who thought "some system should have been in place" (para 118). The House held all of that to be negligence and not knowledge (paras 34, 115, 119). A host that designs a service without any route for a guest's report, and has no suspicion about a particular guest, stands where these managers stood.
- **Targeted, not generic, suspicion.** Lord Scott requires suspicion "firmly grounded and targeted on specific facts" (para 116), and Lord Hobhouse requires a belief relating to "the Star Sea herself" (para 33). A host's general awareness that guests sometimes need help would be an "untargeted" suspicion (para 116).
- **Corporate knowledge through individuals.** Privity is that of "an individual who is to be identified with the assured" (para 27). Lord Scott considered the four individuals "individually or collectively" (para 119) and found none with the suspicion. The case gives no route by which a company's design, as distinct from a person's state of mind, supplies knowledge.
- **The second issue.** The section 17 discussion of good faith after a claim (paras 37-80, 90-111) was not read; it does not use 'blind' and is not needed for the knowledge point.
- **Later treatment** not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3 (and the 'knowledge route' to it): refutes the extension that matters.** In English law a party that suspects a specific fact and decides not to check is treated as knowing it (paras 3, 25-26, 112-116), so the principle that one cannot escape knowledge by arranging not to know is law in its narrow form. The same speeches set its limit. Suspicion must be targeted (para 116); the test is subjective (para 25); gross negligence, failure to think, failure to take precautions and the absence of a system to check are not enough (paras 25-26, 114-115, 118-119); and earlier failures did not oblige the managers to enquire into every master (para 117). A host that builds a service with no channel from the guest, and has no particular suspicion, is negligent on this authority, not knowing. If P3 is to reach that host, it must stand as a claim about what care requires, not as a rule about knowledge.

**What the paper may say**

- In English law, "blind-eye knowledge" requires "a suspicion that the relevant facts do exist and a deliberate decision to avoid confirming that they exist" (*The Star Sea* [2001] UKHL 1, para 116, Lord Scott); Lord Clyde required "a conscious reason for blinding the eye" (para 3).
- Lord Hobhouse put the test as "why did he not inquire?": not wanting to know for certain is privity, but "too lazy", "grossly negligent" or a belief that "there was nothing wrong" is not (para 25).
- Lord Scott required that the suspicion be "firmly grounded and targeted on specific facts" (para 116).
- Lord Scott, whose reasons Lords Steyn and Hoffmann adopted, said that findings about managers who, after two similar losses, took "no steps to check" the master's knowledge (para 30) and had no system to check their contractors' work (para 118) "go to negligence, perhaps gross negligence", and were no basis for blind-eye knowledge (paras 118-119). No Lord held the managers negligent; the question was privity.
- Applied to a host, the case is an analogy from the law of marine insurance.

**What the paper may not say**

- That English law treats a failure to set up a means of learning as knowledge. The House held the contrary (paras 118-119).
- That the case imposes a duty to enquire. It decides what counts as privity under s. 39(5) and holds that the earlier losses did not oblige the managers to enquire into every master (para 117).
- That the case concerns hosts, guests or a duty of care to third parties.
- That a company's knowledge can be built from its design or from aggregating what its people knew. The House looked for an identified individual's state of mind (paras 27, 119).
