## Summary

Guests assaulted in their room at a franchised Holiday Inn sued the owners, the management company in day-to-day control (TRAVCO) and the franchisor, Holiday Inns, Inc. The district court rejected actual agency; the jury found Holiday Inns vicariously liable on apparent agency. The Fourth Circuit affirmed: a franchisor that operates its system so that a franchised property appears to be its own, and on whose appearance guests rely in choosing it, can be held to the guest as if it were the operator, whatever the franchise agreement says. Three parties (owner, operator, brand) were each held liable to the same guests.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Three-party structure stated by the court: the owners were sued "as the actual owners of the motel"; TRAVCO "as the entity in actual day-to-day control of the operation of the motel, which was managed under a franchise agreement with Holiday Inns"; Holiday Inns "on the theory of actual authority or, alternatively, apparent agency" (para 7) [full-text]
- The doctrine: "Though no actual agency exists, a party may be held to be the agent of another on the basis that he has been held out by the other to be so in a way that reasonably induces reliance on the appearances." (para 49) [full-text]
- The two-part test: "(1) the alleged principal has represented or permitted it to be represented that the party dealing directly with the plaintiff is its agent, and (2) the plaintiff, in reliance on such representations, has dealt with the supposed agent." (para 50) [full-text]
- What counted as holding out: trade name and marks "on numerous items in and about the motel"; "national advertising that promotes its national system, without distinguishing between company owned and franchised properties"; a directory "also without distinguishing"; "The only indication that the Holiday Inn-Concord was not owned by Holiday Inns was a sign in the restaurant" (para 51) [full-text]
- Contract does not settle it: "the denial of an agency relationship in a franchise agreement is not alone determinative of liability" (para 51) [full-text]
- Holding on appearance: "a jury could reasonably conclude that the Holiday Inn-Concord was operated in such a way as to create the appearance that it was owned by Holiday Inns, Inc. and that this was one of the purposes of the franchise agreement" (para 51) [full-text]
- Reliance: the guest "did not know the difference between a franchise inn and a company owned inn"; "While the Crinkleys' evidence of actual reliance may be marginal, we think it sufficed ... to raise a jury issue" (para 52) [full-text]
- Reliance fails where the guest did not choose: the court distinguished Hayman v. Ramada Inn, where the guest "was required by her employer to stay at the franchised motel" so "necessity rather than reliance on the franchisor's representations dictated the guest's choice" (footnote 3) [full-text]
- Dissent's description of the brand: "an innkeeper not responsible for managing the premises" (Wilkinson, J., para 58); "Holiday Inns played no role in its management" (para 57) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Jurisdiction and generality. One federal circuit applying North Carolina agency law in 1988. Secondary sources in the search results (Terry and Huan 2019 for Australia; Emerson 2019; commentary on Bright v. Sandstone Hospitality and Mobil Oil v. Bransford) indicate that many US and Australian courts now reject brand-only apparent agency. None of those was read; the verifier should not let the draft say "courts hold franchisors liable". It can say "a court has held".
- The duty itself (security for guests) is the innkeeper's or landowner's, which H1 owns; this card is used only for *who* answers when the sign says one name and the operator is another.
- Reliance is the guest's choice of the brand. That ties holding-out to the guest's position, which suits the paper; it also means the doctrine thins where guests do not choose (corporate travel, footnote 3).
- Reporter page 166 not seen; cite by paragraph or obtain a paginated copy before quoting.
