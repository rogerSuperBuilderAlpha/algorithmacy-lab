## Summary

Builds a framework joining hospitality and home (four pillars: type of accommodation, food, host-guest relationship, safety) and applies it to two cases built from news, inquiry and NGO reports: the Glasgow hotels used to house asylum seekers during the Covid-19 pandemic, and a far-right attack on a hotel in Knowsley, Merseyside, in 2023. Finds that inhospitality on each pillar produced a sense of unhome, and that poorly managed hosting arrangements bred fear in hosts and guests.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Question: "how do asylum seekers experience hospitality and home within UK hotel accommodation?" (MS p. 2) [full-text]
- Outsourcing: accommodation providers acquire hotels and manage them "with subcontracted housing companies"; catering, cleaning and security "are subcontracted to other companies" (MS p. 3) [full-text]
- Several hosts: asylum seekers are guests of the state and, within hotels, "also guests of the hotel, the staff, private housing contractors, and the local community", while "it is the Home Office who are paying customers" (MS p. 4) [full-text]
- Glasgow: "A web of hosts was created in Glasgow through complex outsourcing arrangements" (MS p. 11) [full-text]
- Unclear responsibility: "reports highlighted a lack of clarity around what each different agency was responsible for. This generated confusion amongst asylum seekers who did not know where to turn for help" (MS p. 11) [full-text]
- The desk: the first port of call "was often the hotel receptionist", who with others present "lacked the skills and knowledge to deal with complex problems" (MS p. 11, citing the Scottish inquiry, 2022, and the Home Office evaluation, 2020) [full-text]
- Responsibility: the inquiry panel "considered hotel staff as victims of decisions made by management, rather than individually responsible" (MS p. 11) [full-text]
- Crossref: Hospitality & Society 15(1), pp. 23-44, 2025 [metadata]

## Critical notes & threats

- The evidence is secondary: the authors read inquiry, evaluation and news reports; they did not interview residents or staff. Attribute the findings to the reports the authors read.
- The guests are not the hotels' customers and the hotels did not hold themselves out to them; the Home Office pays. Use the card for a divided host's unanswered question, not for the commercial host's role obligation.
- Accepted manuscript: paraphrase, or take a journal page from the version of record before quoting.

## rr-2026-11-k: bearing on the keystone (R9)

*Added by a Claude (Opus 5.5) subagent, reader R9, 11 Nov 2026, after re-reading the saved accepted manuscript whole (manuscript/rewrite_2026-11/journal/corpus/petie2025/). Locators are the manuscript's printed pages (MS p. = PDF p. minus 1), as above. The findings come from inquiry, evaluation, NGO and news reports, so each is attributed to the report the authors cite.*

- **The placed guest's ability to leave or complain (questions a, g).** Housing "is provided on a no-choice basis", and people "can be moved by the Home Office at short notice without their consent" (MS p. 2). In Glasgow residents "were only allowed to come and go from the hotel at certain times, were not allowed to stay overnight outside of the hotel, and were not allowed to 'host' visitors" (MS p. 10, citing Guma et al. 2021). "Asylum seekers approach staff for help with issues ranging from accommodation complaints (Neal 2022) to healthcare concerns (Jones et al. 2022), with staff largely reported as unhelpful" (MS p. 4). Food is "one of the most common issues reported to Migrant Help by hotel residents (Neal 2022)" (MS p. 11). Exit came only by transfer: wellbeing improved "once they transferred back into dispersal accommodation, described as 'freedom' and 'escape'" (MS p. 10), and a High Court ruled that a resident "must be moved" because attacks made the hotel unsafe (MS p. 14).
- **Reports that reached people without power to act (questions a, g).** Badreddin "called the Home Office and other support organisations more than 70 times" (MS p. 9). "Hotel staff were made aware of Badreddin's deteriorating mental health the night prior to the attack but they lacked the knowledge needed to act" (MS p. 11). The receptionist was "often" the first port of call and "lacked the skills and knowledge to deal with complex problems or recognise potential warning signs" (MS p. 11).
- **Contrary, stated with the same weight.** "although the queries lodged by Badreddin at the Park Inn were dealt with appropriately, cumulatively they should have acted as a warning sign (Laing 2020). There was no communication or joined up approach between services, so warning signs were missed" (MS p. 12). The single queries were handled; the failure the evaluation names lies in aggregation across services, and the harm was to mental health, a welfare matter.
- **Voice as part of provision.** "Elsewhere in institutional housing, asylum seekers have been engaged in the design of the menu and consulted about how to ensure food is culturally appropriate, improving their wellbeing (Laing 2020)" (MS p. 11).
- **Several hosts (question b).** Beyond the passages above: "the lack of hospitality offered by the Home Office and its sub-contractors in their role of hosts" (MS p. 10); private contractors "act as a buffer between states and migrants" (MS p. 12, citing López-Sala and Godenau); the local community appears as "(unwilling) hosts" (MS p. 12), and the case shows "what can happen when hosting is imposed, without consultation" (MS p. 13).
- **The host's responsibility, as the authors state it.** "Hospitality can include a responsibility for a guest's welfare and needs, and ensuring they come to no harm (Telfer 2000)" (MS p. 3); "Despite hosts' responsibility to protect asylum seekers and their contractual obligations, it was subsequently evident that some individuals were not safe" (MS p. 12). Telfer is paraphrased, not quoted, three times (MS pp. 3, 12, 13).
- **Workers (question f).** Supporting asylum seekers "requires knowledge and skills not usually required in commercial hospitality (Kritzman-Amir 2011)" (MS pp. 4-5); "Hotel staff overnight effectively became hosts to a set of people with whom they were unfamiliar" (MS p. 11); the inquiry quoted on Mears: "It simply is not good enough to rebrand housing officers as welfare officers and expect them to work skilfully with traumatised individuals" (MS p. 12). Two staff members were among the six stabbed (MS p. 9).
- **Derrida.** The article quotes 'Hostipitality' (Derrida 2010: 14) on the door and the key: "it means someone has a key to them and consequently controls the conditions of hospitality" (MS pp. 10-11). It quotes nothing from *Of Hospitality*.
- **Title and issue.** Crossref (fetched 11 Nov 2026) gives "... in hotel accommodation for asylum seekers in the United Kingdom", 15(1), pp. 23-44, which matches this card's title. The repository cover sheet gives "... in accommodation for asylum seekers in the UK" and 15(1-2); the manuscript's own title page reads "... in hotel accommodation for asylum seekers in the UK". The red team's citation item 7 is right that the sources differ, but Crossref supports the card.
- **Bearing.** The article supports the keystone's problem in the journal's own terms: a web of hosts in which guests "did not know where to turn for help" and the person reached could not act (MS p. 11). It also cuts against the keystone's scope. The paying customer is the Home Office (MS p. 4), so a channel derived from the duty to perform what the host undertook does not reach these guests; the queries that reached the hotel were handled (MS p. 12); and the harm lay in welfare and in aggregation across services, both of which Step 6 excludes. The paper may cite it for the failed path only together with MS p. 12.
