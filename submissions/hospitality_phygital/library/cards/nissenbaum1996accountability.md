## Summary

Argues that computerization erodes accountability, understood as answerability, through four
barriers: the problem of many hands, the problem of bugs, blaming the computer, and software
ownership without liability. Answerability, unlike liability, cannot be spread thin across many
agents, and restoring it means working out new lines of accountability where computers take over
human functions.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Accountability as answerability: the thesis "rests on an intuitive understanding of accountability closely akin to “answerability.”" (pdf p. 3) [full-text]
- Many hands obscure answerability but do not remove it: "they do not imply that answerability can be foregone" (pdf p. 6) [full-text]
- The scapegoat barrier: blaming a computer is a barrier "because, having found one explanation for an error or injury, the further role and responsibility of human agents tend to be underestimated – even sometimes ignored." (pdf p. 10) [full-text]
- When human agents are replaced by systems "it may seem reasonable to hold the systems answerable for harms" (pdf p. 10) — the move she rejects [full-text]
- Where answerability must go: "working out new lines of accountability may point to designers of the system, the human experts who served as sources, or the organization that chooses to put the system to use." (pdf p. 11) [full-text]
- The default if nothing is done: "Unless alternate lines of accountability are worked out, accountability for these important functions will be lost." (pdf p. 11) [full-text]
- Answerability is not divided by numbers: "No matter how many agents there are, each may be held equally and fully answerable for a given harm." (pdf p. 14) [full-text]
- Answerability versus liability: "answerability (which cannot be spread, thinned and offset)" (pdf p. 15); "Acknowledging, or for that matter denying, one’s liability for an outcome does not take care of one’s answerability for it." (pdf p. 15) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Her accountability is backward-looking and harm-centred: who answers for a malfunction, a bug, an injury. The paper's obligation is owed to an arrival before anything goes wrong and includes the capacity to grant an exception. That difference is the *Distinctness* move, and Nissenbaum is the clearest version of the rival to set it against.
- She supports *Transfer* by analogy only: replacing a human function with a system does not move answerability to the system, and without deliberate new lines it is "lost" (pdf p. 11). She does not say the obligation stays with a role; she says it must be re-assigned or it disappears. The paper's claim that ownership "stays with the institution by default" is stronger than hers. Her text supports "is lost unless assigned" — authors must choose which claim they make.
- "The organization that chooses to put the system to use" (pdf p. 11) is one candidate among three (designers, experts, deployer). She does not privilege the deployer; the paper does.
- The locators are preprint pages. Before citing a page, map to journal pp. 25-42 or cite without page.

## Where it enters the manuscript
