## Summary

Liau argues that private law has an implicit "general standing rule" — only the primary right-holder may sue to enforce the right — and that standing, understood as a Hohfeldian *power* to hold another accountable before a court, is distinct from the claim-right it enforces. Rights and standing usually coincide in one holder; exceptions ("right-less enforcers") show they can come apart. Tort law's typical bundle is a primary right, a secondary right to damages, and the standing to sue.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The two questions: "The question “who can enforce a right?” can be conflated with and collapsed into a separate distinct question: “who has the right?”. Two questions are asked, but often, only one answer is given." (pdf p. 1) [abstract]
- Standing defined as a power: the book attends to "‘standing’, conceived as a power to hold another accountable before a court, as a distinct private law concept." (pdf p. 1) [abstract]
- Thesis: "Standing is a distinct and separable private law concept that can, and should, be distinguished more clearly from ‘right’" (pdf p. 2) [abstract]
- Standing is "not a ‘right’ in the sense of a Hohfeldian claim-right" but "a Hohfeldian power to sue and enforce distinct private law rights and duties" (pdf p. 5) [abstract]
- The rule: "Only the primary right-holder has the standing (a power) to sue to enforce his rights (and no one else)." (pdf p. 5) [abstract]
- The triplet in tort: "primary rights, the infringement of which constitute a tort; secondary rights to damages arising post-tort; and the standing (a power) to sue and enforce these rights" — "it is conceptually possible for the three to be split up; even if only exceptionally so." (pdf p. 7) [abstract]
- Liau places Cornell's work as seeking "to detach wrongs, which he associates with the notion of having ‘standing to complain’, from rights" (pdf p. 4). [abstract]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Terminology clash.** Liau's "standing" is the enforcement power — the *exercise* side. The paper's "standing" (outline §3) is the guest's position as the one owed the duty — the *held* side, which is Liau's "right". If the paper keeps its usage, it should say in one clause that legal "standing" names the power to enforce, which the paper calls exercising standing. Otherwise a law-trained referee will read the paper's "standing" as Liau's and find the circle again.
- **Useful structure.** The three-way split (primary right, secondary right, power to enforce) maps onto the paper's need: the duty to receive (first order), the duty to answer for breach (second order), and the guest's power to press either. That mapping is my inference.
- Only the Introduction was read; do not cite chapter content.
