## Summary

A Barcelona taxi drivers' association sued Uber Systems Spain for unfair competition, on the ground that Uber and its non-professional drivers operated without the licences Barcelona requires of taxis. The commercial court asked the Court of Justice whether Uber's service is a transport service, an information society service, or both. The Grand Chamber held that an intermediation service connecting drivers and passengers is in principle a service separate from the transport, but that Uber's is more than intermediation: Uber simultaneously offers urban transport, which it makes accessible through its application and whose general operation it organises. Two findings carried the conclusion. Without the application the drivers would not provide the service and the passengers would not use it; and Uber exercises decisive influence over the conditions under which the drivers provide it, through the fare, the handling of payment and a certain control over vehicles, drivers and conduct that can end in exclusion. The service is therefore an integral part of an overall service whose main component is transport. It is a 'service in the field of transport', outside Directive 2000/31, Directive 2006/123 and art. 56 TFEU, and Member States may regulate it. EU law; the reference came from a Spanish commercial court.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Court, parties, what was at stake**

- Grand Chamber, 20 December 2017, Case C-434/15; composed of "K. Lenaerts, President, A. Tizzano, Vice-President, R. Silva de Lapuerta, M. Ilešič, J.L. da Cruz Vilaça, J. Malenovský and E. Levits, Presidents of Chambers, E. Juhász, A. Borg Barthet, D. Šváby (Rapporteur), C. Lycourgos, M. Vilaras and E. Regan, Judges"; Advocate General M. Szpunar, whose Opinion was delivered on 11 May 2017 (preamble). The Court gives a single judgment; it records no vote and no separate opinion. [full-text]
- The parties: "Asociación Profesional Elite Taxi (‘Elite Taxi’), a professional taxi drivers’ association in Barcelona (Spain), and Uber Systems Spain SL, a company related to Uber Technologies Inc." (para 2). The claimant was a trade association of competitors; no passenger was a party. [full-text]
- The claim: Elite Taxi sought "a declaration from that court that the activities of Uber Systems Spain infringe the legislation in force and amount to misleading practices and acts of unfair competition", an order to cease, and a prohibition for the future (para 13). [full-text]
- The national court's point: "neither Uber Systems Spain nor the non-professional drivers of the vehicles concerned have the licences and authorisations required under the Regulation on taxi services in the metropolitan area of Barcelona" (para 14). [full-text]
- The referring court limited the question: the request "in no way concerns those factual elements but solely the legal classification of the service at issue" (para 17). [full-text]

**The starting point**

- "an intermediation service consisting of connecting a non-professional driver using his or her own vehicle with a person who wishes to make an urban journey is, in principle, a separate service from a transport service consisting of the physical act of moving persons or goods from one place to another by means of a vehicle" (para 34). [full-text]
- Such a service "meets, in principle, the criteria for classification as an ‘information society service’" (para 35). [full-text]

**The test and the facts that met it**

- More than intermediation: "a service such as that in the main proceedings is more than an intermediation service" (para 37). [full-text]
- The platform offers the underlying service: "the provider of that intermediation service simultaneously offers urban transport services, which it renders accessible, in particular, through software tools such as the application at issue in the main proceedings and whose general operation it organises for the benefit of persons who wish to accept that offer in order to make an urban journey" (para 38). [full-text]
- Selection and indispensability: "the intermediation service provided by Uber is based on the selection of non-professional drivers using their own vehicle, to whom the company provides an application without which (i) those drivers would not be led to provide transport services and (ii) persons who wish to make an urban journey would not use the services provided by those drivers" (para 39). [full-text]
- Control: "In addition, Uber exercises decisive influence over the conditions under which that service is provided by those drivers. On the latter point, it appears, inter alia, that Uber determines at least the maximum fare by means of the eponymous application, that the company receives that amount from the client before paying part of it to the non-professional driver of the vehicle, and that it exercises a certain control over the quality of the vehicles, the drivers and their conduct, which can, in some circumstances, result in their exclusion." (para 39). [full-text]
- The conclusion: "That intermediation service must thus be regarded as forming an integral part of an overall service whose main component is a transport service and, accordingly, must be classified not as ‘an information society service’ within the meaning of Article 1(2) of Directive 98/34, to which Article 2(a) of Directive 2000/31 refers, but as ‘a service in the field of transport’ within the meaning of Article 2(2)(d) of Directive 2006/123." (para 40). [full-text]

**What followed from the classification**

- "Directive 2000/31 does not apply to an intermediation service such as that at issue in the main proceedings." (para 42). [full-text]
- "as EU law currently stands, it is for the Member States to regulate the conditions under which intermediation services such as that at issue in the main proceedings are to be provided in conformity with the general rules of the FEU Treaty" (para 47). [full-text]
- The answer: the service "must be regarded as being inherently linked to a transport service and, accordingly, must be classified as ‘a service in the field of transport’ within the meaning of Article 58(1) TFEU" (para 48; operative part). [full-text]
- The third and fourth questions, on authorisation schemes and derogations, were not answered (para 49). [full-text]

**What the judgment does not contain**

- Nothing on liability to passengers, on contract with passengers, or on who answers for a driver's fault. The words describing Uber's position towards the customer are that it "simultaneously offers urban transport services" (para 38) and "receives that amount from the client" (para 39). [full-text]
- No finding about what passengers believed or relied on. The test looks at how the service is organised and controlled. [full-text]

**Later treatment, as far as read**

- Airbnb Ireland, C-390/18 (card airbnb2019cjeu), restates the rule at its para 50, citing para 40 of this judgment, and distinguishes the facts at its paras 65-68, adding that the Uber judgments "were given in the specific context of urban passenger transport to which Article 58(1) TFEU applies" (Airbnb Ireland, para 66). Other later treatment not checked. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A classification for market access.** The consequence of the holding is that Spain may require a licence. The judgment does not say that Uber is the carrier in a passenger's claim, or that it owes passengers a carrier's duties. Using it for 'who answers to the customer' is an inference from a regulatory classification, and the paper must present it as one.
- **Transport is treated specially in the Treaty.** Services in the field of transport are excluded from art. 56 TFEU and from Directive 2006/123 (paras 43-44). Airbnb Ireland marks that context (its para 66). The reasoning carries to accommodation only through the general formula in para 40, which Airbnb Ireland adopted at its para 50.
- **The facts are thin and conditional.** The Court writes "it follows from the information before the Court" and "it appears, inter alia" (para 39). Several governments and the Commission said the order for reference was "insufficiently precise" about the activities (para 22). The service the Court describes throughout is carriage by "non-professional drivers using their own vehicle" (paras 2, 33, 39); the judgment does not address a platform for licensed or professional providers.
- **Two limbs, and the judgment does not say whether both are needed.** Para 39 gives selection of drivers with indispensability of the application, and then, opening with the words "In addition", decisive influence. Airbnb Ireland examined both and found neither (its paras 55, 68).
- **The phrase "decisive influence" has another meaning nearby.** Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 defines 'control' as "ownership of, or the ability to exercise decisive influence over, an undertaking" (art. 2(9); card eu2019p2b). That is control of a company in the merger-control sense. The Uber phrase is influence over the conditions of a service.
- The Advocate General's Opinion was not read.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports, as the test; does not reach liability.** K8 lists "tests of 'decisive influence' for platforms" among what would confirm it. The test exists and this is its source: a platform that selects the providers, without whose tool the service would not be supplied, and that fixes the price, takes the payment and polices quality with a power to exclude, is treated as offering the underlying service itself. That lines up with K8's second ground, the party that controls. It does not line up with the first, the party that held out: the Court asks nothing about how the service was presented to the customer. For a hotel context the test suggests where a booking platform or a system vendor would have to stand before EU law treated it as part of the accommodation service: setting the price, choosing the providers and controlling how the service is delivered.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Court of Justice has held that an intermediation service is in principle separate from the service it arranges, but is part of that service where it forms an integral part of an overall service whose main component is the arranged service (paras 34, 40).
- The Court found Uber's service to be such a part because Uber selected the drivers, supplied an application without which the rides would not take place, and exercised decisive influence over the conditions of the service (para 39).
- The facts the Court named for decisive influence were that Uber set at least the maximum fare, received the fare from the client before paying the driver, and exercised a certain control over vehicles, drivers and their conduct, which could lead to exclusion (para 39).
- The Court described Uber as simultaneously offering urban transport services whose general operation it organises (para 38).
- The case was brought by a taxi drivers' association and concerned whether Uber needed a licence (paras 13-15).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Court held Uber liable to passengers, or held anything about liability to customers.
- That the test turns on what the customer was led to believe. It turns on selection, indispensability and control.
- That the Court applied the test to accommodation here. It did so two years later in Airbnb Ireland and found it unmet.
- That "decisive influence" is defined. The Court lists facts "inter alia" and gives no threshold.
- That the judgment makes a platform a 'host' or a 'carrier'. Its words are "a service in the field of transport" (para 40).
- Anything on the Advocate General's reasoning. The Opinion was not read.
