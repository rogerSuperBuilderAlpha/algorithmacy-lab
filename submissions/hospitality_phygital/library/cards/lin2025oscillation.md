## Summary

A walkthrough of Douyin's affordances plus a week of screen-recorded video diaries and follow-up
interviews with 31 young urban Chinese users (aged 21-30). Users who say they are aware of and
annoyed by algorithmic simplification, commercial exploitation and political agenda-setting
"oscillate" between resisting and not, and mostly resist through the platform's intended
features. Non-resistance comes from social and entertainment needs, from satisfaction with the
system, and, for political content, from digital resignation that the platform's own educational
videos about regulation and censorship cultivate.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Sample and method: 31 participants aged 21-30, recruited across Chinese platforms; daily screen recordings for a week, then interviews; over 52 hours of recordings (p. 5) [full-text]
- Central paradox: "although respondents said they were algorithmically aware and annoyed by algorithmic irritations ... they often behaved in ways that contradict those claims with limited resistance or even no resistance" (p. 10) [full-text]
- Constraint: "most of their resistance behaviors remain constrained within the dominant use of technological affordances" (abstract, p. 1); resistance "remains predominantly confined to the dominant use of technological affordances, lacking the transformative capacity to subvert the ideological structures" (p. 11) [full-text]
- Users can bend non-political curation: "Every time I pressed dislike, the relevant content was reduced" (Ruoxi, p. 9); against political content, "there is no use to 'dislike' politically relevant information" (p. 9) [full-text]
- Some non-resistance is chosen: users "conducted limited or no resistance to algorithms when they also felt satisfied with the system"; the author calls this "active passivity", "a deliberate response" (p. 10) [full-text]
- Resignation is cultivated by the platform: "users' apathy toward resisting algorithms stems not only from a lack of algorithmic awareness or folk theories ... but also from the learned algorithmic knowledge through educational videos about platform regulations and censorship" (p. 11) [full-text]
- Limits: young, urban users; does not capture less algorithmically aware groups (pp. 6, 11) [full-text]
- *Social Media + Society* 11(1), January-March 2025, 1-13 [metadata]

## Critical notes & threats

- (rr-2026-11) Supports the structure of the competence-standing claim, with two limits. Awareness does not convert into transformative influence (pp. 10-11). But users do steer non-political curation by ordinary means (p. 9), and some do not try because they are satisfied (p. 10). The paper shows influence bounded by the platform's affordances and by state-platform power, not influence absent.
- The resignation finding is tied to China's censorship regime (pp. 9-11). That strengthens the point about the counterparty (users fear the state, not their own ignorance) but weakens transfer to a hotel.
- (rr-2026-11) The abstract-era fact "Competence and its non-conversion into influence are documented in the same participants" holds for awareness versus resistance behaviour (p. 10); Lin measures awareness and folk theories, not competence.

## Support verdicts against v4 and the revision plan (rr-2026-11)

- v4 §6 (line 60): "Users who understand a platform's curation well still cannot bend it, and their resistance stays inside what the platform allows (Lin 2025)." PARTLY. The second clause is SUPPORTED (abstract p. 1; p. 11). The first overstates: users did bend entertainment and commercial curation by pressing dislike or reporting (pp. 8-9); what they could not do was bend political curation or the platform's power structure. A faithful version: "Users who understand a platform's curation still resist only through the features it provides, and cannot move what the platform and the state protect (Lin 2025)."
- Revision plan §6 ("no skill conjures a counterparty"): SUPPORTED in structure by pp. 10-11, from social media, not hospitality.
