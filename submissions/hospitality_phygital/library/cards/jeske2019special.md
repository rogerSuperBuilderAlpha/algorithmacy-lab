## Summary

Jeske defines special obligations as those owed to some subset of persons, sets them against consequentialist duties and natural duties, and organises the debate around two objections that Scheffler named: the voluntarist objection, pressed from the side of the person bound, and the distributive objection, pressed from the side of outsiders. Voluntarists hold that special obligations arise only from the obligor's voluntary acts; they limit special obligations to promises and contracts, or reduce the rest to tacit contract, or treat them all as voluntary commitments. Anti-voluntarists defend obligations of role or position that nobody chose, by five routes: Dworkin's true community, Hardimon's reflective acceptance, a justice condition, Scheffler's appeal to value, and Sandel's constitutive commitments. Jeske argues against each of the five. A later section surveys grounds: raised expectations and reliance, the worth of the friend, psychological connection, and Ross's intuitionism. The entry never treats vulnerability or dependence as a ground.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Definition: "Special obligations are obligations owed to some subset of persons, in contrast to natural duties that are owed to all persons simply qua persons." (preamble) [full-text]
- The grounds the entry announces: "Attempts to ground special obligations have appealed to diverse states of affairs, including the intrinsic nature of the special relationship, the nature of the party to whom the obligations are owed, the expectations of the party to whom the obligations are owed, and the nature of persons as psychological continuants bound by relations between mental states." (preamble) [full-text]
- Natural duties and position: in the poisoned-drink case, "My proximity to the person and my knowledge of the poison in her drink facilitate my being able to fulfill my (natural) duty of mutual aid, but it is not a part of the fundamental explanation of why I have such a duty in the first place." And: "Anyone else in the same causal and epistemic position would similarly have a duty to warn of the poison in the drink. I occupy no peculiar status or role and have performed no prior actions such that if I did not occupy that role or had not performed those actions, I would have no duty to warn of the poison in the drink." (§3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Nearness: "nearness in space cannot be an ultimate ground of an agent-relative reason", because it works "by affecting our causal efficacy in providing aid" (§2, para. 6, 'Notice that on this definition') [full-text]
- What marks a special obligation: "the relationship between me and my obligee is fundamental to any explanation of my special obligations to that obligee." A defender "owes an account of what sorts of status or position or what types of acts performed are such that, as a result of being in such a position or having performed such acts, one has obligations that are not derived from some more general obligation that one has to all persons considered as such." (§3, para. 2) [full-text]
- The voluntarist thesis: "The voluntarist worries support the view that special obligations can only be acquired through the voluntary actions of the agent whose obligations they are." Jeske adds that "voluntarism is a thesis about special obligations alone." (§4, para. 2) [full-text]
- Promise and contract as the agreed case: "the voluntarist will grant that special obligations are acquired through promises and contracts, because, in making a promise or contract, the agent voluntarily agrees to bear a certain burden for the person to whom she makes the promise or contract." (§4, para. 2) [full-text]
- Three voluntarist strategies: "(i) they limit special obligations to the straightforwardly contractual or promissory, (ii) they attempt to show that all of the special obligations that they defend really are contractual or promissory in nature, or (iii) they show that while not all special obligations are contractual, they nevertheless are similar in a relevant respect." (§4, para. 3) [full-text]
- Tacit consent has conditions. Jeske reports Simmons (1979): an act or omission counts as consent only when "the person must be aware that she is in a situation in which consent is appropriate and/or expected, it must be made clear in some way what must be done or not done in order to give consent, it must be clear when action is to be taken or avoided, and the consent-giving action or failure to act must be possible and not extraordinarily costly." (§4, para. 6) [full-text]
- Voluntary assumption without a promise: on the third strategy "All special obligations, according to this account, are forms of voluntary commitments undertaken by the individual moral agent", and "a commitment may be undertaken via a temporally extended series of events, each of which, in and of itself, has no moral significance: it is only the series taken as a whole that generates or grounds special obligations." (§4, para. 8) [full-text]
- Role obligations introduced: "Our understandings of what it is to occupy a certain role often involves certain normative assumptions about the person’s obligations in virtue of occupying that role." And: "So role obligations, i.e., obligations that we have in virtue of occupying some role (whether or not voluntarily assumed), appear to be special obligations: they are usually owed to some limited class of persons and one acquires them through some actions of one’s own or in virtue of one’s status, not as a result of intrinsic features of the obligee or of the state of affairs to be promoted." (§4, para. 9) [full-text]
- Moral and non-moral obligations of role: "But, of course, we always need to distinguish between moral and non-moral (or reason-giving and non-reason-giving) obligations of role (or position)." The commandant of a concentration camp had obligations of role in a sense that "does not seem to be a moral or reason-giving sense". "Such language of obligations of roles seems to involve merely others’ expectations of persons holding such positions. Why, a voluntarist (or anyone else, for that matter) will ask, should we suppose that such expectations are sufficient for the having of obligations in the reason-giving or moral sense?" (§4, para. 10) [full-text]
- The five anti-voluntarist routes: "(i) the ‘true community’ view, (ii) the ‘reflective acceptance’ view, (iii) the ‘justice view’, (iv) the appeal to value, and (v) the ‘constitutive commitment’ view." Jeske notes that the names "are not necessarily used by those who take one or the other of these options". (§4, para. 11) [full-text]
- Hardimon, as Jeske reports him: "Hardimon aims to offer an account of obligations attached to certain institutionally defined roles, namely “political, familial, and occupational roles” (334), where a role is a “[constellation] of institutionally specified rights and duties organized around an institutionally specified social function” (334). A role obligation, according to Hardimon, is “a moral requirement, which attaches to an institutional role, whose content is fixed by the function of the role, and whose normative force flows from the role” (334)." (§4, para. 16) The page numbers are Jeske's, to Hardimon (1994). [full-text]
- Hardimon on unchosen roles, as Jeske quotes him: "To say that a social role is reflectively acceptable is to say that one would accept it upon reflection. Determining whether a given social role is reflectively acceptable involves stepping back from that role in thought and asking whether it is a role people ought to occupy and play. Determining that a given social role is reflectively acceptable involves judging that it is (in some sense) meaningful, rational, or good. (348)" (§4, para. 16, block quotation) [full-text]
- Jeske's gloss: "Thus, on Hardimon’s account, it is hypothetical rather than actual consent that grounds unchosen special obligations." And "a view such as Hardimon’s is not a voluntarist view, as Hardimon himself readily acknowledges." (§4, para. 17) [full-text]
- Jeske's objection to Hardimon: "The obvious question for Hardimon’s view is why we need to appeal to the notion of reflective acceptance at all." What does the work is the role's being ‘meaningful, rational, or good’: "But then the hypothetical judgment is really epiphenomenal with respect to the justification and we are left with (iii), the justice view: one has obligations in virtue of occupying a role if and only if one’s filling that role and the institution in which one would be filling that role meet certain conditions of morality or justice." (§4, para. 18) [full-text]
- The justice view is then denied as sufficient, by the West Side Reading Group, a just group with good ends to which a resident belongs by where she lives: "But why suppose that I have special obligations to other members of the reading group simply because those others have decided to describe me in a certain way, even if their group is a good group with just and worthy goals in which I could participate without violating any requirements of justice or morality?" (§4, para. 19) [full-text]
- Dworkin: associative obligations are “the special responsibilities social practice attaches to membership in some biological or social group, like the responsibilities of family or friends or neighbors” (196, as quoted, §4, para. 12); on his account "mere social expectation is not sufficient to create associative obligations" (§4, para. 14). Scheffler: "relationships give rise to special obligations only when persons have reason to value the relationships" (§4, para. 20). Sandel: certain roles "are ‘constitutive commitments.’" (§4, para. 22). Each is reported and then criticised (§4, paras 15, 21, 23-24). [full-text]
- A duty to ensure, reported from Archard: he distinguishes "having “an obligation to ensure that someone acts as a parent to the child” and having an obligation to act as a parent oneself", so that "a parent could, according to Archard, fulfill her parental obligations that result from conceiving and bearing a child, by finding adoptive parents who will assume parental responsibilities for the child." (§6.2, para. 2, citing Archard 104-105, 127) [full-text]
- Professional roles: "Many of us assume obligations when we assume professional roles." The question is left open: "Are these obligations mere obligations of role without moral implications, or are they genuinely moral special obligations?"; "we need to have some account of which professional roles, if any, generate genuinely moral special duties." (§6.3) [full-text]
- The test Jeske sets for any proposed special obligation: "For any proposed special obligation, we need to consider whether it is more plausibly understood as derivative from an agent-neutral reason or a natural duty and so not really special in a fundamental sense." (§6.4, last para.) [full-text]
- Expectation and reliance as a ground: "If you voluntarily lead me to expect that you will perform a given action, then I am likely to make plans on the basis of that expectation. You are obligated not to upset the plans that I have made on the basis of your voluntary actions that you knew would raise my expectations." The account "has been extended into other contexts": "Our friends and family members come to rely upon us to act in certain ways with respect to them, and their reliance is due to our own voluntary past actions." (§7, para. 2) [full-text]
- Its difficulty: "One serious difficulty with this sort of grounding of special obligations is that it appeals to the psychological states of those to whom the obligations are owed." Jeske cites the promisee who does not expect performance ("Scanlon calls this the case of the Profligate Pal") and asks: "If such expectations are not raised, are no obligations created?" An appeal to hypothetical expectations leaves us "still left wondering why I ought to meet expectations that have never actually been raised." (§7, para. 3) [full-text]
- A limit on voluntary acts, reported from Macleod: “Actions … must survive moral scrutiny – which means, in effect, that it must be possible to characterize them as morally permissible actions – if the duties that are generated by the acts in question are to be given recognition as  moral duties ” (132, as quoted, §8, para. 4). [full-text]
- Jeske's closing form of the question: "What matters is whether these demands are actually reasons, or whether they are mere obligations of role or position, where the latter are really just shorthand for social expectations or understandings." (§8, para. 5) [full-text]
- Terminology: "Some philosophers restrict their use of the term ‘obligation’ to refer to those moral reasons that are voluntarily assumed, where ‘duties’ refers only to non-voluntarily assumed moral reasons." Jeske declines the restriction: "nothing seems to ride on one’s choice of terminology." (note 3) [full-text]
- Absent from the entry: vulnerability or dependence as a ground of special obligation. The word 'vulnerable' occurs once, of pets (§9, para. 3), and the bibliography lists nothing by Goodin (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]
- The bibliography gives "Hardimon, Michael, 1994. “Role Obligations,” The Journal of Philosophy, 91: 333–363." It lists Simmons (1979) and Simmons (1996), 'Associative Political Obligations', *Ethics* 106: 247–273, and does not list Simmons's reply to Hardimon, 'External Justifications and Institutional Roles'. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What an encyclopaedia entry carries.** The entry reports positions and gives the author's assessment of them. Jeske is a party to the debate (she writes "As I have said" and cites her own work at §6.1 and §9), and her criticisms of the five anti-voluntarist routes are arguments, not findings. A sentence in the paper may rest on this card for what Jeske says and for what she reports others as saying, with the attribution kept.
- **Hardimon is reported for unchosen roles only.** Jeske brings Hardimon in as an anti-voluntarist and discusses his test for roles nobody chose (§4, paras 16-18). She does not report what he says of roles a person signs on for, which is the case of a commercial host. The words "whose normative force flows from the role" stand in her text without his argument for them. Dowie (2024: section 'Hardimon’s view of roles as contractual'), an open article, gives the words "acquired by signing on for the roles from which they derive" (p. 337 as given there) as the standard view of contractual role obligations as Hardimon explains it, not as his own position; see the second-hand note in WS_P2_R2.md. What Hardimon holds about chosen roles cannot be settled from either.
- **The entry stops in 2019 and omits the reply.** It does not cite Simmons (1996) on Hardimon, Dare, Sciaraffa or the 2023 Oxford collection on social roles that the scout lists. The map is partial on exactly the exchange K3 turns on.
- **No ground of dependence.** A reader who expects the entry to cover Goodin's vulnerability account will not find it. The nearest material is the account of natural duties (§3, para. 1), on which position enables a duty that anyone so placed would have.
- **Individuals throughout.** The role-holders are a daughter, a professor, a citizen. Nothing concerns a firm as the holder of a role, a host, or a commercial relation; §6.3 on professional roles is one paragraph and ends on a question.
- **Typographical state.** The saved text has slips of its own ("that’s role being", "persons’s", a doubled space inside the Macleod quotation). Quotations above reproduce them.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K3 (the role specifies; three external grounds bind): narrows, and supplies the wording that opposes it.** Three points.

1. *The role as specification.* Hardimon's definition, as Jeske quotes it, splits a role obligation into content ("fixed by the function of the role") and force ("flows from the role") (§4, para. 16). K3 accepts the first clause and denies the second. So the definition supports K3's claim that the role fixes content and refutes its claim that the role does not bind, if Hardimon is right. Jeske herself sides against him: role talk may record "merely others’ expectations" (§4, para. 10), reflective acceptance is "epiphenomenal" (§4, para. 18), and a just institution's say-so is not enough (§4, para. 19). Her line is close to K3's first half, but she never says that roles 'specify' while outside grounds 'bind'; that wording is the paper's.
2. *Ground 1, voluntary assumption.* The entry supports it as the least contested ground: even the voluntarist's opponents grant promise and contract (§4, para. 2). It also gives the paper a published description of assumption without a discrete promise, by a series of acts (§4, para. 8), which is what holding out is. It narrows the ground in two ways: tacit consent needs the conditions Simmons sets (§4, para. 6), and acts that are not morally permissible generate no moral duty on Macleod's view (§8, para. 4).
3. *Ground 2, invited reliance, and ground 3, dependence.* The entry states the expectation account and its main difficulty: it makes the obligation turn on what the other party in fact expects (§7, paras 2-3). That narrows ground 2 to guests who did rely. For ground 3 the entry gives no support and one obstacle: on Jeske's account of natural duties, a person's proximity and knowledge "facilitate" the duty and are no part of its explanation, and anyone in the same position would owe the same (§3, para. 1). Dependence so read yields a duty that does not pick out a host as such. The entry's standing test (§6.4, last para.) asks of every special obligation whether it is derivative from a natural duty; K3 has to answer it for ground 3.

**K2 (the duty attaches to the role): narrows.** The phrase "attaches to an institutional role" is Hardimon's, as quoted (§4, para. 16), and Jeske's commandant shows that a duty's attaching to a role does not make it a moral duty (§4, para. 10). K2 is safe as a claim about content and holder.

**K7, P2: one reported defeater.** Archard's distinction, as reported, lets a person with an obligation to ensure that a child is cared for fulfil it by finding others who take the care on (§6.2, para. 2). That is discharge by competent hand-over. Brandt (2017) argues against exactly this view (see brandt2017transfer).

**What the paper may say**

- Philosophers disagree over whether special obligations can be acquired only through the obligor's voluntary acts; Jeske calls the affirmative view the voluntarist thesis (§4, para. 2).
- Even on the voluntarist view, promise and contract generate special obligations, because the agent "voluntarily agrees to bear a certain burden" for the other party (§4, para. 2).
- On one voluntarist account a commitment can be undertaken by a series of acts over time, none of which is a promise (§4, para. 8). Applying this to a host's holding out is the paper's move.
- Hardimon defines a role obligation as "a moral requirement, which attaches to an institutional role, whose content is fixed by the function of the role, and whose normative force flows from the role" (Hardimon 1994: 334, as quoted in Jeske 2019: §4).
- Jeske distinguishes moral from non-moral obligations of role and observes that talk of a role's obligations may record "merely others’ expectations of persons holding such positions" (§4, para. 10).
- Jeske argues that Hardimon's test of reflective acceptability reduces to a condition of justice, and asks whether that condition suffices to bind a person to a role, doubting it by the reading-group case (§4, paras 18-19).
- One account grounds special obligations in expectations a person has voluntarily raised and others have relied on; its standing difficulty is that it makes the obligation depend on the other party's state of mind (§7, paras 2-3).
- On Jeske's account of natural duties, a person's position and knowledge enable the duty of aid and do not explain it, and anyone so placed would owe it (§3, para. 1).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the literature, or Jeske, has established that roles do not bind by themselves. The entry reports five views on which they do and gives one philosopher's objections.
- That Hardimon holds any view beyond the definitions quoted, and in particular any view about chosen or contractual roles. Jeske does not report it.
- That Jeske names vulnerability or dependence among the grounds of special obligation. She does not mention it.
- That the entry states K3's structure, or uses 'specify' and 'bind' as the paper does.
- That the entry concerns hosts, firms or commercial roles.
- That the entry reports Simmons's reply to Hardimon. It is not in the bibliography.
- Any quotation of Hardimon, Scheffler, Dworkin, Sandel, Macleod or Archard without 'as quoted in' or 'as reported in' Jeske.
