## Summary

Conceptual theory-synthesis paper that builds a transformative phygital service research
(TPSR) framework on activity theory. It names three discontinuities phygital systems introduce
(algorithmic mediation of agency, digitally enforced rules, non-human interpretive labour),
gives propositions linking transparency, adaptive governance, immersion and human–AI labour to
well-being, autonomy, dignity and inclusion, and draws managerial implications, including that
algorithmic governance "must remain contestable" through escalation paths and human oversight.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Discontinuities: "digitally enforced rule structures embed governance within code, producing non-negotiable and often opaque constraints that differ fundamentally from socially negotiated institutional norms" (PDF p. 2) [full-text]
- Phygital systems "redistribute decision authority, embed governance within code and intertwine human and non-human actors in ways that directly affect autonomy, dignity and well-being" (PDF p. 2) [full-text]
- Critique of the field's focus: "Most scholarship conceptualise phygitality through immersive experiences, seamless channel integration and engagement optimisation ... The dominant emphasis on efficiency and experience design leaves underexplored the deeper socio-technical mechanisms through which phygital systems transform agency, inclusion and equity." (PDF p. 2) [full-text]
- Opacity and rigidity (abstract, Findings): "Transparent algorithms enhance autonomy and trust, while opacity reduces control. Adaptive governance fosters inclusion; rigid rules intensify exclusion." (PDF p. 1) [full-text]
- Proposition P1b: "When algorithmic systems are opaque and non-negotiable, consumers' perceived autonomy decreases and compliance behaviour increases, leading to reduced psychological well-being." (PDF p. 11) [full-text]
- Friction as something to reduce: replacing long registration forms with QR access "realigns the artefact with user capabilities and concerns, thereby reducing friction and restoring agency." (PDF p. 10) [full-text]
- Escalation: "Consumers may first interact with chatbots or recommender systems before escalating to human agents, illustrating how expertise, authority and responsibility are redistributed." (PDF p. 12) [full-text]
- Managerial implication: "algorithmic governance must remain contestable. Digitally enforced rules should incorporate escalation pathways and human oversight to prevent rigid exclusion. Hybrid governance models combining automation with human judgement can preserve fairness and trust." (PDF p. 14) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

Two things in the full text bear on v4 §7 beyond the abstract.

1. **Overlap with the constitutional principles.** "Algorithmic governance must remain contestable ... escalation pathways and human oversight" (PDF p. 14) is close to v4's principle that "a person must stay reachable who can actually reverse or revise the decision". The paper should cite it there and say what it adds: Shabnam et al. ground contestability in fairness, trust and exclusion, not in an obligation owed to the arrival, and they do not ask who answers.
2. **The seamlessness claim.** Shabnam et al. do fault the field's "dominant emphasis on ... seamless channel integration and engagement optimisation" (PDF p. 2), but for neglecting agency and equity, not because smoothness itself harms. They still treat friction as a cost to remove (PDF p. 10). v4's narrow claim survives, but this source is the nearest counterexample and a referee may raise it.

- v4 §7 (line 68): "the field's own research agendas fault the opacity and rigidity of algorithmic systems (Shabnam et al. 2026)." **SUPPORTED** (PDF pp. 1, 2, 11, 14). "Research agendas" is plural; this is one agenda paper, in *Journal of Services Marketing* rather than a hospitality journal.

#### correction 2026-11-13 (v8 stage 2 reader)

Critical note 1 says Shabnam et al. "do not ask who answers". In part the text does say who answers. A human answers: "cooperation failures become salient when technological affordances, such as a "call a store representative" function on a digital kiosk, are not matched by human responsiveness" (p. 11), and "Consumers may first interact with chatbots or recommender systems before escalating to human agents" (p. 12). The recommendations are addressed to named parties: "organisations", "Managers", "firms", "policymakers" and "Regulatory frameworks" (p. 14). What the text does not say is which of several firms answers, or that anything is owed to the consumer. Responsibility in the paper divides between human and algorithmic actors ("responsibilities are dynamically allocated between human actors and algorithmic artefacts", p. 12), and accountability is left as a research question ("weaken accountability", p. 15).
