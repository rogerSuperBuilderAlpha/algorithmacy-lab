## Summary

The Guiding Principles set out, in thirty-one principles with official commentary, a State duty to protect human rights, a corporate responsibility to respect them, and access to remedy. The corporate responsibility is a "global standard of expected conduct" and is expressed throughout in 'should'. It asks enterprises to carry out human rights due diligence: to assess impacts, act on the findings, track the effectiveness of the response and communicate (Principles 17-21); to remediate impacts they caused or contributed to (22); and, so that grievances can be "addressed early and remediated directly", to establish or take part in operational-level grievance mechanisms open to those who may be affected (29), which must meet eight effectiveness criteria (31). The responsibility reaches impacts linked to an enterprise through its business relationships, in a graded way (13, 19, 22).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- What the document is: "The Human Rights Council endorsed the Guiding Principles in its resolution 17/4 of 16 June 2011." They "were developed by the Special Representative of the Secretary-General on the issue of human rights and transnational corporations and other business enterprises", who annexed them to his final report (A/HRC/17/31). (note, p. iv) [full-text]
- No new legal obligation: "Nothing in these Guiding Principles should be read as creating new international law obligations, or as limiting or undermining any legal obligations a State may have undertaken or be subject to under international law with regard to human rights." (General principles, p. 1) [full-text]
- Whom they address: "These Guiding Principles apply to all States and to all business enterprises, both transnational and others, regardless of their size, sector, location, ownership and structure." (General principles, p. 1) [full-text]
- Their grounds include "(b) The role of business enterprises as specialized organs of society performing specialized functions, required to comply with all applicable laws and to respect human rights;" and "(c) The need for rights and obligations to be matched to appropriate and effective remedies when breached." (General principles, p. 1) [full-text]
- The kind of norm: "The responsibility to respect human rights is a global standard of expected conduct for all business enterprises wherever they operate." (Principle 11, Commentary, p. 13) It "is distinct from issues of legal liability and enforcement, which remain defined largely by national law provisions in relevant jurisdictions." (Principle 12, Commentary, p. 14) [full-text]
- Principle 13: "The responsibility to respect human rights requires that business enterprises: (a) Avoid causing or contributing to adverse human rights impacts through their own activities, and address such impacts when they occur; (b) Seek to prevent or mitigate adverse human rights impacts that are directly linked to their operations, products or services by their business relationships, even if they have not contributed to those impacts." (p. 14) [full-text]
- Principle 13, Commentary: a business enterprise’s “activities” are "understood to include both actions and omissions; and its “business relationships” are understood to include relationships with business partners, entities in its value chain, and any other non-State or State entity directly linked to its business operations, products or services." (p. 15) [full-text]
- Principle 17: "In order to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for how they address their adverse human rights impacts, business enterprises should carry out human rights due diligence. The process should include assessing actual and potential human rights impacts, integrating and acting upon the findings, tracking responses, and communicating how impacts are addressed." (p. 17) Due diligence "(c) Should be ongoing, recognizing that the human rights risks may change over time as the business enterprise’s operations and operating context evolve." (p. 18) [full-text]
- Principle 17, Commentary, before and after: "Potential impacts should be addressed through prevention or mitigation, while actual impacts – those that have already occurred – should be a subject for remediation (Principle 22)." (p. 18) [full-text]
- Principle 17, Commentary, on liability: enterprises "should not assume that, by itself, this will automatically and fully absolve them from liability for causing or contributing to human rights abuses." (p. 19) [full-text]
- Principle 19 on who inside must be able to act: effective integration requires that "(i) Responsibility for addressing such impacts is assigned to the appropriate level and function within the business enterprise;" (p. 20) and that "(ii) Internal decision-making, budget allocations and oversight processes enable effective responses to such impacts." (p. 21) [full-text]
- Principle 19, Commentary, on other firms: "Leverage is considered to exist where the enterprise has the ability to effect change in the wrongful practices of an entity that causes a harm." (p. 21) "If the business enterprise has leverage to prevent or mitigate the adverse impact, it should exercise it." (p. 22) [full-text]
- Principle 20: "In order to verify whether adverse human rights impacts are being addressed, business enterprises should track the effectiveness of their response. Tracking should: (a) Be based on appropriate qualitative and quantitative indicators; (b) Draw on feedback from both internal and external sources, including affected stakeholders." (p. 22) [full-text]
- Principle 20, Commentary: "Tracking is necessary in order for a business enterprise to know if its human rights policies are being implemented optimally, whether it has responded effectively to the identified human rights impacts, and to drive continuous improvement." And: "Operational-level grievance mechanisms can also provide important feedback on the effectiveness of the business enterprise’s human rights due diligence from those directly affected (see Principle 29)." (p. 23) [full-text]
- Know and show: "The responsibility to respect human rights requires that business enterprises have in place policies and processes through which they can both know and show that they respect human rights in practice." (Principle 21, Commentary, pp. 23-24) [full-text]
- Principle 22: "Where business enterprises identify that they have caused or contributed to adverse impacts, they should provide for or cooperate in their remediation through legitimate processes." (p. 24) [full-text]
- Principle 22, Commentary: "Even with the best policies and practices, a business enterprise may cause or contribute to an adverse human rights impact that it has not foreseen or been able to prevent." Where it identifies such a situation, "its responsibility to respect human rights requires active engagement in remediation, by itself or in cooperation with other actors. Operational-level grievance mechanisms for those potentially impacted by the business enterprise’s activities can be one effective means of enabling remediation when they meet certain core criteria, as set out in Principle 31." (p. 24) [full-text]
- Principle 22, Commentary, the limit for linked impacts: "Where adverse impacts have occurred that the business enterprise has not caused or contributed to, but which are directly linked to its operations, products or services by a business relationship, the responsibility to respect human rights does not require that the enterprise itself provide for remediation, though it may take a role in doing so." (pp. 24-25) [full-text]
- Principle 24, on timing: "Where it is necessary to prioritize actions to address actual and potential adverse human rights impacts, business enterprises should first seek to prevent and mitigate those that are most severe or where delayed response would make them irremediable." (p. 26) [full-text]
- 'Grievance' defined: "For the purpose of these Guiding Principles, a grievance is understood to be a perceived injustice evoking an individual’s or a group’s sense of entitlement, which may be based on law, contract, explicit or implicit promises, customary practice, or general notions of fairness of aggrieved communities." (Principle 25, Commentary, p. 27) [full-text]
- Place in the system of remedy: "State-based judicial and non-judicial grievance mechanisms should form the foundation of a wider system of remedy. Within such a system, operational-level grievance mechanisms can provide early stage recourse and resolution." (Principle 25, Commentary, p. 28) [full-text]
- Principle 29: "To make it possible for grievances to be addressed early and remediated directly, business enterprises should establish or participate in effective operational-level grievance mechanisms for individuals and communities who may be adversely impacted." (p. 31) [full-text]
- Principle 29, Commentary, what they are: "Operational-level grievance mechanisms are accessible directly to individuals and communities who may be adversely impacted by a business enterprise. They are typically administered by enterprises, alone or in collaboration with others, including relevant stakeholders. They may also be provided through recourse to a mutually acceptable external expert or body. They do not require that those bringing a complaint first access other means of recourse. They can engage the business enterprise directly in assessing the issues and seeking remediation of any harm." (p. 31) [full-text]
- Principle 29, Commentary, first function: "First, they support the identification of adverse human rights impacts as a part of an enterprise’s ongoing human rights due diligence. They do so by providing a channel for those directly impacted by the enterprise’s operations to raise concerns when they believe they are being or will be adversely impacted. By analysing trends and patterns in complaints, business enterprises can also identify systemic problems and adapt their practices accordingly;" (p. 32) [full-text]
- Principle 29, Commentary, second function: "Second, these mechanisms make it possible for grievances, once identified, to be addressed and for adverse impacts to be remediated early and directly by the business enterprise, thereby preventing harms from compounding and grievances from escalating." (p. 32) [full-text]
- Principle 29, Commentary, the threshold: "Such mechanisms need not require that a complaint or grievance amount to an alleged human rights abuse before it can be raised, but specifically aim to identify any legitimate concerns of those who may be adversely impacted. If those concerns are not identified and addressed, they may over time escalate into more major disputes and human rights abuses." (p. 32) [full-text]
- Principle 29, Commentary, the limits: such mechanisms "can be important complements to wider stakeholder engagement and collective bargaining processes, but cannot substitute for either. They should not be used to undermine the role of legitimate trade unions in addressing labour-related disputes, nor to preclude access to judicial or other non-judicial grievance mechanisms." (p. 32) [full-text]
- Principle 31, the criteria: "In order to ensure their effectiveness, non-judicial grievance mechanisms, both State-based and non-State-based, should be:" "(a) Legitimate: enabling trust from the stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended, and being accountable for the fair conduct of grievance processes;" "(b) Accessible: being known to all stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended, and providing adequate assistance for those who may face particular barriers to access;" "(c) Predictable: providing a clear and known procedure with an indicative time frame for each stage, and clarity on the types of process and outcome available and means of monitoring implementation;" "(d) Equitable: seeking to ensure that aggrieved parties have reasonable access to sources of information, advice and expertise necessary to engage in a grievance process on fair, informed and respectful terms;" "(e) Transparent: keeping parties to a grievance informed about its progress, and providing sufficient information about the mechanism’s performance to build confidence in its effectiveness and meet any public interest at stake;" (p. 33) "(f) Rights-compatible: ensuring that outcomes and remedies accord with internationally recognized human rights;" "(g) A source of continuous learning: drawing on relevant measures to identify lessons for improving the mechanism and preventing future grievances and harms;" (p. 34) [full-text]
- Principle 31, the eighth criterion: "Operational-level mechanisms should also be: (h) Based on engagement and dialogue: consulting the stakeholder groups for whose use they are intended on their design and performance, and focusing on dialogue as the means to address and resolve grievances." (p. 34) [full-text]
- Principle 31, Commentary: "A grievance mechanism can only serve its purpose if the people it is intended to serve know about it, trust it and are able to use it." "The first seven criteria apply to any State-based or non-State-based, adjudicative or dialogue-based mechanism. The eighth criterion is specific to operational-level mechanisms that business enterprises help administer." (p. 34) [full-text]
- Principle 31, Commentary on (h): "Since a business enterprise cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome, these mechanisms should focus on reaching agreed solutions through dialogue. Where adjudication is needed, this should be provided by a legitimate, independent third-party mechanism." (p. 35) [full-text]
- The text never uses 'consumer', 'customer', 'guest', 'hotel', 'algorithm' or 'automated'; 'clients' occurs once, of business clients in a value chain (Principle 17, Commentary, p. 18) (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The kind of instrument and its authority.** This is soft law: a set of principles drafted by a Special Representative of the Secretary-General and endorsed by a resolution of the Human Rights Council (p. iv). It states that it creates no new obligation in international law (p. 1). For States it says 'must' where it restates existing duties (Principle 25, p. 27); for enterprises it says 'should' and calls the responsibility a "standard of expected conduct" (p. 13), "distinct from issues of legal liability" (p. 14). It can show that an authoritative international text expects enterprises to keep a channel for those affected. It cannot show that any enterprise is legally bound to. Whether later statutes have enacted parts of it is outside what I read.
- **The field is human-rights impact.** The responsibility concerns "internationally recognized human rights" (Principle 12, p. 13). A late room, a wrong charge or an unanswered kiosk is not a human-rights impact as such. The commentary lowers the threshold for raising a matter (a grievance "need not" amount to an alleged abuse, p. 32, and may rest on "contract, explicit or implicit promises, customary practice", p. 27), but the mechanism exists to catch concerns that "may over time escalate into ... human rights abuses" (p. 32). Carrying the concept to service failures in a hotel is an analogy the paper must own.
- **It anticipates K7's shape.** Principle 29 joins the two halves that K7's premise P3 joins: the mechanism is how the enterprise learns ("a channel for those directly impacted ... to raise concerns") and how it corrects in time ("remediated early and directly ... thereby preventing harms from compounding") (p. 32). The phrase "being or will be adversely impacted" covers the period before and during harm. The paper's 'reachability' is close to this, for a different relation and a different norm.
- **What it does not contain.** No requirement that a human receive the grievance, or that the person receiving it hold power over the particular decision. The nearest words are Principle 19's: responsibility "assigned to the appropriate level and function" and decision-making that can "enable effective responses" (pp. 20-21). Nor does it argue from a duty of oversight to the channel; it stipulates the channel as part of due diligence and remedy.
- **It qualifies the channel K7 asks for.** An enterprise "cannot, with legitimacy, both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome" (p. 35). On this text a line to a manager who decides alone is not yet an effective mechanism where the matter needs adjudication.
- **Linked firms.** For impacts an enterprise is only linked to through another firm, it should use leverage (p. 22) and need not itself remediate (pp. 24-25). That is weaker than K7's P2 and K8's contract limb.
- **Unread.** Principles 1-10, 15-16, 23 and 26; the report A/HRC/17/31; resolution 17/4; any later interpretive guidance.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7 (the channel): a prior concept, which supports the conclusion in soft law and narrows the claim to novelty.** The 'operational-level grievance mechanism' (Principle 29) is a published name for a channel from those affected to the enterprise, kept so that problems are caught early and put right directly. The paper should credit it and claim what is left: the application to a host and guest, the derivation from the host's own duty, and the requirement of a person with power over the single case.

**K7, P3: supports by enactment, not by argument.** The Principles link learning to correcting. Tracking exists "to verify whether adverse human rights impacts are being addressed" and must draw on "feedback ... including affected stakeholders" (Principle 20, p. 22); the grievance mechanism serves "identification" and early remediation (Principle 29, p. 32); priority goes to impacts "where delayed response would make them irremediable" (Principle 24, p. 26). These state what P3 states. They give no reason for it beyond the standard itself.

**K7, the timing boundary: supports.** "Potential impacts should be addressed through prevention or mitigation, while actual impacts ... should be a subject for remediation" (Principle 17, Commentary, p. 18) is the paper's line between interrupting and repairing.

**K7, P2 and K8: narrows.** The responsibility extends to impacts "directly linked" through business relationships "even if they have not contributed to those impacts" (Principle 13(b), p. 14), but for those the enterprise should seek to prevent or mitigate through leverage and is not required to remediate itself (pp. 22, 24-25). A brand or platform merely linked to a failure gets a lighter responsibility on this text than the firm that caused it.

**What the paper may say**

- The UN Guiding Principles, endorsed by the Human Rights Council in 2011, state: "To make it possible for grievances to be addressed early and remediated directly, business enterprises should establish or participate in effective operational-level grievance mechanisms for individuals and communities who may be adversely impacted." (Principle 29, p. 31)
- The official commentary describes such a mechanism as "a channel for those directly impacted by the enterprise’s operations to raise concerns when they believe they are being or will be adversely impacted", and says it allows impacts "to be remediated early and directly by the business enterprise, thereby preventing harms from compounding" (Principle 29, Commentary, p. 32).
- The Principles ask enterprises to "track the effectiveness of their response", drawing on feedback from "affected stakeholders" (Principle 20, p. 22).
- They distinguish prevention or mitigation of potential impacts from remediation of impacts that have occurred (Principle 17, Commentary, p. 18; Principle 22, p. 24).
- They set eight effectiveness criteria for such mechanisms, among them that the mechanism be accessible and predictable, with "an indicative time frame for each stage" (Principle 31, pp. 33-34).
- They hold that an enterprise cannot legitimately "both be the subject of complaints and unilaterally determine their outcome" (Principle 31, Commentary, p. 35).
- The Principles are not law: they state that nothing in them creates "new international law obligations" (General principles, p. 1), and they concern human-rights impacts. The paper borrows the concept by analogy.

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Guiding Principles bind hotels, platforms or any enterprise in law, or that they give guests a right.
- That they apply to service failures as such. Their subject is adverse human-rights impact.
- That they require a human being, or a person with authority over the individual decision, to receive a grievance. They do not say so.
- That they derive the channel from a duty of oversight or from a duty owed to a particular person. They state it as part of due diligence and access to remedy.
- That they make an enterprise answerable for remediation of harms caused by its business partners. For linked impacts they say the opposite (pp. 24-25).
- That they mention consumers, guests, hospitality, algorithms or automated decisions.
- That the paper's idea of reachability is new without qualification. This text had the core of it in 2011, for another field.
