## Summary

The article traces the transparency ideal and lists ten limitations of it as a route to algorithmic accountability. Transparency can be disconnected from power, harmful, occluding, binary, neoliberal, untrusting, professionally bounded, and privilege seeing over understanding; it also has technical and temporal limits. The authors argue that accountability must look across an assemblage of human and non-human actors, not inside a black box.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- "Transparency can be disconnected from power": visibility "carries risks for the goal of accountability if there is no system ready and 'capable of processing, digesting, and using the information' to create change (Heald, 2006: 35-37)" (p. 978) [full-text]
- Burden on individuals: "The ideal of transparency places a tremendous burden on individuals to seek out information about a system, to interpret that information, and determine its significance" (p. 979) [full-text]
- Seeing is not understanding: "Seeing inside a system does not necessarily mean understanding its behavior or origins" (p. 980) [full-text]
- Conclusion: "making one part of an algorithmic system visible—such as the algorithm, or even the underlying data—is not the same as holding the assemblage accountable. If we recognize that transparency alone cannot create accountable systems ..." (p. 984) [full-text]

- Closing questions: "accountable for what?" and "accountable to whom?" (p. 985) [full-text]
