## Summary

A guest at the Imperial Hotel, Liverpool, was badly injured on the night of 17 June 1916 when a fire broke out and she tried to climb down from her second-floor room on a rope of sheets. She sued the hotel's proprietor, an innkeeper who was its lessee and occupier, on two grounds: breach of an implied warranty that the premises were safe, and his personal negligence. The fire came from a defective kitchen-flue scheme built in 1910 by a builder whom the landlord had employed, at the start of the defendant's lease: the landlord covenanted to spend £2000 'as the defendant should direct', and the defendant 'saw and approved' the plans (p. 328). McCardie J held that the innkeeper does not insure the guest's person, and that as occupier he owes the guest, an invitee, the ordinary duty of reasonable care; but that the contract between innkeeper and paying guest adds an implied warranty that the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill on the part of anyone can make them. Under that warranty it does not matter whether the want of care was the innkeeper's, his servants' or an independent contractor's, or whether it occurred before he took the premises. The jury had also found the innkeeper personally negligent in failing to inquire after an earlier fire. Judgment for the guest for £865 17s.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court, date, judge: King's Bench Division, McCardie J, "FURTHER CONSIDERATION of an action tried by McCardie J. and a jury at the Liverpool Assizes"; argued 2 and 3 May, judgment 4 May 1917 (pp. 325, 327); England. [full-text]
- Who sued whom: "The plaintiff, an unmarried woman, claimed damages from the defendant, a hotel proprietor, for personal injuries sustained by her on the occasion of an outbreak of fire at the defendant’s hotel on June 17, 1916, while the plaintiff was staying there as a guest." (p. 325). [full-text]
- The defendant's position: "The defendant is an innkeeper. He is the lessee and occupier of the well-known Imperial Hotel in the centre of Liverpool. On the night of June 17, 1916, the plaintiff became a guest for reward to the defendant at this hotel." (p. 327). [full-text]
- The two grounds of claim: "She based her claim on two grounds—namely, (a) breach of warranty, and (b) personal negligence by the defendant." (p. 327). [full-text]
- The jury's findings included "(3.) that the defendant was negligent in not having made further inquiry than he did as to the cause of a previous outbreak of fire at the hotel in 1915" (p. 325). [full-text]
- The defect was a contractor's, and not the defendant's contractor: "A builder was employed by the landlord to carry out the plans. Either the architect or the builder or both were admittedly guilty of a serious want of care" (p. 328). [full-text]
- Goods against person: "The liability of an innkeeper for the goods of his guest is that of an insurer, subject to well-known statutory and common law limitations; but the liability of an innkeeper with respect to the personal safety of his guest is less onerous: see Calye’s Case. He does not insure the personal safety of the guest." (p. 328; the footnote dates *Calye's Case* 1584). [full-text]
- Why goods are treated more strictly: "The prevalence of highway robbery and the risk of possible collusion between the thief and the innkeeper of those early days adequately account for the onerous burden on the latter with respect to goods" (p. 329). [full-text]
- The tort duty, from invitation and occupation: "It is obvious that the guest is an invitee. It follows, therefore, that the innkeeper, as the occupier of premises to which he has invited the guest, is bound, as a matter of common law duty, to take reasonable care to prevent damage to the guest from unusual danger which the occupier knows or ought to know of" (p. 329). [full-text]
- Contract adds to it: "Where the plaintiff is a guest at the defendant’s inn the duty of the defendant cannot be less than the duty laid down in Indermaur v. Dames; but it may be substantially greater." (p. 330). [full-text]
- The rule: "Where the occupier of premises agrees for reward that a person shall have the right to enter and use them for a mutually contemplated purpose, the contract between the parties (unless it provides to the contrary) contains an implied warranty that the premises are as safe for that purpose as reasonable care and skill on the part of any one can make them." (pp. 332-333). [full-text]
- Its limit: "the defendant is not to be held responsible for defects which could not have been discovered by reasonable care or skill on the part of any person concerned with the construction, alteration, repair, or maintenance of the premises" (p. 333). [full-text]
- Contractors and predecessors: "But subject to this limitation it matters not whether the lack of care or skill be that of the defendant or his servants, or that of an independent contractor or his servants, or whether the negligence takes place before or after the occupation by the defendant of the premises." (p. 333). [full-text]
- The rule is not special to inns: "It matters not whether the subject be a race-stand, a theatre, or an inn; whether it be a taxicab, an omnibus, or a railway carriage. The warranty in each case is the same, and for a breach thereof an action will lie." (p. 333). [full-text]
- Where the obligation sits: "That rule puts on the defendant a greater obligation than exists with respect to a mere invitee, but a less obligation than is imposed on a man who sells a chattel to another for a specific purpose" (p. 334). [full-text]
- A second, independent ground: the judgment "can well be supported upon another ground, inasmuch as the jury were satisfied that the defendant had been personally negligent in that he had omitted to take such steps and to make such inquiries as would have revealed to him the defects in his structure and the risks of fire thereby occasioned" (p. 334). [full-text]
- A place the guest should not be: McCardie J reads *Walker v Midland Railway* as deciding "that the innkeeper owes no duty to the guest with respect to that portion of a hotel which a guest ought not to enter" (p. 334). [full-text]
- Headnote: "By reason of the contractual relationship existing between an innkeeper and a guest in the inn there is an implied warranty by the innkeeper that the inn premises are, for the purpose of personal use by the guest, as safe as reasonable care and skill on the part of any one can make them" (p. 325). [full-text]
- Disposition: "There must be judgment for the plaintiff for 865l. 17s. with costs." (p. 335). The claim succeeded. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Holding and its two legs.** The warranty is the first ground of decision (pp. 328-334); the innkeeper's personal negligence in not inquiring is a second ground on which the judgment "can well be supported" (p. 334). Either would carry the result, so the statement about independent contractors is one of two reasons for the decision, given at first instance by a single judge.
- **Basis.** For the higher duty the basis is the contract of a guest "for reward" (pp. 327, 330, 332-333). For the lower duty it is invitation and occupation (p. 329). Status as innkeeper grounds neither: the judge applies the same rule to theatres, race-stands and vehicles (p. 333) and says the innkeeper is no insurer of the person (p. 328).
- **The warranty yields to contrary terms.** The rule applies "unless it provides to the contrary" (p. 333). In 1917 the innkeeper could contract out. Today's limits on that (Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, Consumer Rights Act 2015) are recorded on uk1957occupiers from another reader's check; I did not read them.
- **Statute has since spoken to the implied term.** The Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s. 5(1) provides that the duty owed to persons entering under a contract, "in so far as the duty depends on a term to be implied in the contract by reason of its conferring that right, shall be the common duty of care" (uk1957occupiers). On its face that replaces McCardie J's implied warranty with a duty to which the reasonable-entrustment rule in s. 2(4)(b) applies. I read the section; I read no authority on whether *MacLenan* survives it, and the paper should not say that it does or that it does not without one.
- **Later treatment.** Two noter-up slips pasted on p. 325 of the scanned copy read "Distinguished. Shore v. Ministry of Works. [1950] 2 All E.R. 228." and "Not applied. Protheroe v. Railway Executive. [1950] 2 All E.R. 1093." I did not read either case. *Al-Najar* [2019] EWHC 1593 (QB) paras 183-184 (read) cites *MacLenan* for the occupier's duty of reasonable care quoted from p. 329, not for the warranty. Other later treatment not checked.
- **OCR.** The saved text misreads several case names and figures (for example "Karl v. Lubbock" for *Earl v. Lubbock*, "8651. 17s." for "865l. 17s."). The quotations above follow the page images. Line-break hyphens are mended; footnote markers inside quoted sentences are dropped.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (reception tier): supports.** An innkeeper owes a guest reasonable care for the guest's person as occupier and invitor (p. 329), and more under the contract (pp. 332-333). The case also confirms the asymmetry K4 states: insurer of goods, not of the person (p. 328).
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** The injured guest sued in her own name and recovered damages (pp. 325, 335).
- **K8 (holder): supports the contract limb as the law stood in 1917; narrowed since.** Under the contractual warranty the innkeeper answered for an independent contractor's want of care, even a contractor the landlord employed, for work done at the start of his lease under his direction as to spending and with his approval (p. 328); the rule at p. 333 covers negligence 'before or after the occupation by the defendant'. The 1957 Act s. 5(1) cuts across this for implied terms about premises.
- **K7 (channel): modest support for P3.** The second ground makes the holder liable for not taking "such steps" and making "such inquiries as would have revealed to him the defects" after a warning event (p. 334; jury finding 3, p. 325). It is a duty to find out, enforced by damages afterwards. It says nothing about a guest's report.
- **K3 (grounds): supports assumption through contract and invitation; nothing on dependence.**

**What the paper may say**

- In 1917 an English court held that the contract between an innkeeper and a paying guest carries an implied warranty that the premises are "as safe as reasonable care and skill on the part of any one can make them" (pp. 325, 332-333).
- Under that warranty "it matters not whether the lack of care or skill be that of the defendant or his servants, or that of an independent contractor or his servants" (p. 333).
- The innkeeper insures the guest's goods and not the guest's person: "He does not insure the personal safety of the guest." (p. 328).
- The guest recovered £865 17s (p. 335), on the warranty and, independently, on the innkeeper's failure to inquire into an earlier fire (p. 334).

**What the paper may not say**

- That this is the present rule without qualification. The Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s. 5(1) now sets the implied contractual term at the common duty of care.
- That an innkeeper's duty to the person is strict or non-delegable by reason of being an innkeeper. The warranty is one of reasonable care and skill, it is contractual, and it applies equally to a theatre or a taxicab (p. 333).
- That the warranty cannot be excluded. The judge says "unless it provides to the contrary" (p. 333).
- That the case concerns the acts of other people on the premises. It concerns the state of the building.
