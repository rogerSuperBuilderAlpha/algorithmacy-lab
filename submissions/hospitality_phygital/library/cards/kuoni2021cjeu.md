## Summary

The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom asked whether a tour organiser could rely on the defence in the third indent of art. 5(2) of Directive 90/314, an event that neither the organiser nor the supplier could foresee or forestall, where a hotel employee raped a guest, and whether that employee was himself a "supplier of services". The Court of Justice answered no to both. An employee is not a supplier, because he works in subordination to one; but his acts in performing obligations under the package contract are treated as the supplier's, they make the organiser liable under art. 5(1), and they fall within the "sphere of control" of the organiser or the supplier, so they cannot be an event outside foresight or prevention. The Court read the organiser's obligations broadly and the exemption strictly, on the ground of a high level of consumer protection.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The parties and the claim: proceedings "between X, a consumer residing in the United Kingdom, and Kuoni Travel Ltd (‘Kuoni’), a travel organiser established in the United Kingdom, concerning the claim made by X for compensation for damage suffered as a result of the improper performance of the contract relating to package travel" (para 2). [full-text]
- The provision interpreted, art. 5(1) of Directive 90/314 as quoted at para 7: the organiser "is liable to the consumer for the proper performance of the obligations arising from the contract, irrespective of whether such obligations are to be performed by that organiser and/or retailer or by other suppliers of services without prejudice to the right of the organiser and/or retailer to pursue those other suppliers of services." [full-text]
- Art. 5(3), same paragraph: "there may be no exclusion by means of a contractual clause from the provisions of paragraphs 1 and 2." [full-text]
- The premise the referring court supplied: it "should be assumed that guidance by a member of the hotel’s staff of X to the reception was a service falling within the scope of the ‘holiday arrangements’ which Kuoni had agreed to provide and that the rape and assault constituted improper performance of the contract at issue." (para 21). The Court of Justice did not decide that point. [full-text]
- The system: Directive 90/314 "establishes, inter alia, a system of contractual liability for package travel organisers in respect of consumers who have concluded a contract with them for such travel." (para 34). "The only exemptions therefrom which are allowed are those exhaustively set out in Article 5(2) of that directive." (para 34). [full-text]
- Its special feature: "Article 5(1) and (3) of Directive 90/314 thus limits the freedom of the parties to a package travel contract to determine the content of the contractual terms which apply to them, by making the organiser liable to the consumer in so far as concerns the proper performance of that contract. One of the special features of that liability is that it extends to the proper performance of the obligations arising under the package travel contract by suppliers of services." (para 35). [full-text]
- A supplier is "a natural or legal person who provides services for remuneration" (para 38). [full-text]
- An employee is not a supplier: he "has not concluded any agreement with the package travel organiser" and "merely performs work on behalf of a supplier of services" (para 41); "the word ‘employee’ refers to a person who performs his or her work in the context of a relationship of subordination with his or her employer and therefore under the latter’s control." (para 42). [full-text]
- But his acts count as the supplier's: that fact "does not preclude that employee’s acts or omissions from being treated, for the purposes of that system, in the same way as those of the supplier of services employing him or her." (para 43). [full-text]
- The limit of the liability: it "relates solely to the obligations arising from the package travel contract", and "does not, therefore, affect the liability of the parties to that contract or of third parties arising from other obligations or other systems of liability, such as that of criminal liability." (para 44). [full-text]
- The obligations are read broadly: "the obligations arising from a package travel contract, the improper performance or non-performance of which renders the organiser liable, cannot be interpreted restrictively. Those obligations comprise all the obligations associated with the provision of transport, accommodation and tourism services arising from the purpose of the package travel contract, irrespective of whether those obligations are to be performed by the organiser itself or by suppliers of services." (para 45). [full-text]
- A link is required: "there must be a link between the act or omission which caused damage to that consumer and the organiser’s obligations arising from the package travel contract." (para 46). [full-text]
- Suppliers act through employees "who are under their control" (para 47), and "that non-performance or improper performance, although caused by acts committed by employees under the control of a supplier of services, is such as to render the organiser liable, in accordance with Article 5(1) of Directive 90/314." (para 48). [full-text]
- The reason given: without such liability "an unjustified distinction would be drawn" between acts of suppliers and the same acts of their employees, "which would enable an organiser to avoid its liability." (para 49). [full-text]
- The rule stated: "under Article 5(1) of Directive 90/314, the non-performance or improper performance of an obligation, arising from a package travel contract, by an employee of a supplier of services renders the organiser of that package travel liable to the consumer with whom that organiser has concluded that contract, where that non-performance or improper performance has caused damage to that consumer." (para 50). [full-text]
- The exemption is read strictly: "since that ground for exemption from liability derogates from the rule laying down the liability of organisers, enshrined in Article 5(1) of Directive 90/314, it must be interpreted strictly" (para 56). [full-text]
- The sphere of control: the event that cannot be foreseen or forestalled "must be interpreted as referring to a fact or incident which does not fall within the sphere of control of the organiser or the supplier of services." (para 60). [full-text]
- Applied: "the acts or omissions of an employee of a supplier of services, in the performance of obligations arising from a package travel contract, resulting in the non-performance or improper performance of the organiser’s obligations vis-à-vis the consumer fall within that sphere of control" (para 61). [full-text]
- The operative part: where the failure "is the result of the actions of an employee of a supplier of services performing that contract", "that employee cannot be regarded as a supplier of services for the purposes of the application of that provision, and" "the organiser cannot be exempted from its liability arising from such non-performance or improper performance, pursuant to that provision." (para 63 and ruling). [full-text]
- The judgment does not use the terms "vicarious liability" or "non-delegable" in its own reasoning; "vicariously liable" appears once, in the account of the Court of Appeal's judgment (para 17). It says nothing about contact with the organiser or remedies other than compensation. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Repealed instrument.** The ruling interprets Directive 90/314, repealed from 1 July 2018 (Directive 2015/2302, art. 29; card eu2015package). The defence at issue, an event that could not be foreseen or forestalled, is not among the three defences of art. 14(3) of the 2015 Directive on my reading of the two texts. I read no ruling that carries the "sphere of control" test over to the 2015 Directive.
- **The first issue was assumed.** Whether guiding a guest was within the contract, and whether the assault was improper performance, were premises supplied by the referring court (paras 21, 51). The Supreme Court decided them afterwards (kuoni2021uksc, paras 28-35).
- **The "sphere of control" is the organiser's or the supplier's.** The organiser answers for what lies within its supplier's control, which it does not itself control. The test extends the organiser's answerability; it does not say that control is the ground of the liability, which the Court rests on the Directive's system and on consumer protection (paras 34-35, 45, 49).
- **Contract only.** The ruling concerns contractual liability under the package contract and leaves other liabilities, including the supplier's and the employee's own, untouched (para 44).
- The Opinion of Advocate General Szpunar, to which paras 38, 46 and 49 refer, was not read. Later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports, for packages under the former Directive.** The Court states the structure K8 needs in general terms: the law limits the parties' freedom of contract "by making the organiser liable to the consumer" for performance by suppliers (para 35), allows no contractual exclusion (para 34), and counts the supplier's employees' acts as within the "sphere of control of the organiser or the supplier of services" (paras 60-61). It refuses the argument that would let the party who sold the trip escape because the wrong was done two steps down the chain (para 49). The narrowing is the one on card kuoni2021uksc: packages, contract, and an instrument now replaced.

**K3 (grounds), in passing.** The Court's test of what an organiser cannot disclaim is whether the event lay within the control of those through whom it performs. That sits closer to K3's dependence ground than to holding out, and the paper should not cite the case for reliance.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Court of Justice held that an organiser of package travel is liable to the consumer where an employee of a supplier, in performing an obligation under the package contract, performs it improperly and causes damage (para 50).
- The Court held that the organiser could not rely on the defence of an event that could not be foreseen or forestalled, because the acts of a supplier's employee in performing the contract fall within the "sphere of control of the organiser or the supplier of services" (paras 60-62).
- The Court described the organiser's liability as a limit on the parties' freedom of contract, one of whose "special features" is that it extends to performance by suppliers (para 35).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Court of Justice decided the hotel employee's guiding was part of the contract. It assumed so at the referring court's request (paras 21, 51).
- That the ruling concerns Directive 2015/2302, or any contract other than a package.
- That the Court spoke of vicarious liability or of a non-delegable duty.
- That the Court grounded the organiser's liability in the organiser's own control over the hotel.
- That the ruling concerns the hotel's liability to the guest.
