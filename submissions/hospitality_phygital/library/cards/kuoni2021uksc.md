## Summary

Mrs X, on a package holiday in Sri Lanka bought from Kuoni, was raped and assaulted by N, an electrician employed by the hotel, who was on duty and in uniform and had offered to show her a shortcut to reception. She sued the tour operator, not the hotel and not N, for breach of contract and under the Package Travel, Package Holidays and Package Tours Regulations 1992. She lost at trial and, by a majority, in the Court of Appeal. The Supreme Court referred two questions on the statutory defence to the Court of Justice in 2019 and, after the ruling in Case C-578/19, allowed her appeal unanimously in 2021. Lord Lloyd-Jones held that guiding a guest through the hotel was a service within the holiday arrangements Kuoni had contracted to provide, that the assault was improper performance of that contract, and that Kuoni had no defence because the failure was caused by an employee of a supplier performing the contract. Kuoni was liable under the Regulations and for breach of contract. That liability, he added, does not depend on vicarious liability.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Who sued whom, and on what**

- The contract: "the appellant and her husband (“Mr and Mrs X”, anonymity orders having been made in respect of the appellant by the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court) entered into a contract with the respondent tour operator (“Kuoni”) under which Kuoni agreed to provide a package holiday in Sri Lanka" (para 1). [full-text]
- Its terms: "Your contract is with Kuoni Travel Ltd. We will arrange to provide you with the various services which form part of the holiday you book with us." (Booking Conditions, clause 2.2, quoted at para 2). Clause 5.10(b) accepted responsibility "if due to fault on our part, or that of our agents or suppliers, any part of your holiday arrangements booked before your departure from the UK is not as described in the brochure, or not of a reasonable standard" (para 2). [full-text]
- The facts: "She came upon a hotel employee, N, who was employed by the hotel as an electrician and (on the facts found by the judge) known to her as such. N was on duty and wearing the uniform of a member of the maintenance staff. N offered to show her a shortcut to reception, an offer which she accepted. N lured her into the engineering room where he raped and assaulted her." (para 3). [full-text]
- The claim: "In these proceedings Mrs X claims damages against Kuoni by reason of the rape and the assault. The claim is brought for breach of contract and/or under the Package Travel, Package Holidays and Package Tours Regulations 1992" (para 4). [full-text]
- The statutory rule in force for this contract, reg. 15(1) of the 1992 Regulations as quoted at para 6: "The other party to the contract is liable to the consumer for the proper performance of the obligations under the contract, irrespective of whether such obligations are to be performed by that other party or by other suppliers of services but this shall not affect any remedy or right of action which that other party may have against those other suppliers of services." Reg. 15(5), same paragraph: "liability under paragraphs (1) and (2) above cannot be excluded by any contractual term." [full-text]
- No fault of oversight was alleged: "it was no part of her case that there was systemic or organisational negligence on the part of Kuoni or the hotel (such as failure to supervise N or carelessness in selecting N as an employee) causative of the attack. The assault was caused by N alone." (para 8). [full-text]
- Kuoni's admission: it was "responsible to the claimant for the proper performance of the obligations under the holiday contract whether or not such obligations were to be performed by the defendant or another supplier of services" (para 9). [full-text]

**The courts below, as the Supreme Court reports them**

- The trial judge held that the holiday arrangements did not include a maintenance employee conducting a guest to reception, and, obiter, that the statutory defence would apply. "Although it was not necessary to decide the point, he held that the hotel would not have been vicariously liable for the rape and assault as a matter of Sri Lankan law, which it was agreed was the same as English law for these purposes." (para 10). [full-text]
- The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal by a majority (para 11). The majority held that guiding a guest "was no part of the functions for which the employee was employed" (para 12) and saw "no discernible policy reasons for imposing liability on a tour operator when neither it nor the hotel were “at fault”" (para 13). [full-text]
- Longmore LJ, dissenting, as summarised at para 15(5): "In the law of England and Wales, the governing principle is that a person who undertakes contractual liability retains liability for his side of the bargain even if he performs it through others (at para 21)." And at para 15(6): "The whole point of the Directive and the 1992 Regulations was to give the holidaymaker whose holiday had been ruined a remedy against his contractual opposite. It should be left to the tour operator to sort out the consequences of the ruined holiday with those with whom it had itself contracted who could then sort things out further down the line whether with their own employees or their independent contractor (at para 22)." These are Longmore LJ's propositions; the Supreme Court sets them out and does not adopt them in terms. [full-text]

**What the Supreme Court held about the hotel employee's conduct**

- The approach: "the purpose of the agreement, namely to confer an enjoyable experience, encourages a broad, not a narrow, interpretation of the holiday services contracted for." (para 29). [full-text]
- Ancillary services are part of the bargain: "In the case of any contract for a package holiday the provider of the holiday necessarily undertakes to provide not merely transport, accommodation and meals but also to provide other services ancillary thereto." (para 30). [full-text]
- Two such services named: "the obligation to provide the service of cleaning the hotel with reasonable care and skill would be inherent in every such contract. So would the service of looking after and serving holidaymakers courteously in matters relating to their holiday experience." (para 30). [full-text]
- Helpfulness as a term of the standard, in Longmore LJ's words quoted with agreement: "For such a holiday to be a reasonable standard, hotel staff must be helpful to guests when asked for assistance; all the more must a member of staff, who actually offers assistance, assist the guest in a reasonable way." (para 31). [full-text]
- "It is an integral part of the services to be provided on a holiday of such a standard that hotel staff provide guests with assistance with ordinary matters affecting them at the hotel as part of their holiday experience. In my view, guidance by a member of the hotel’s staff of Mrs X from one part of the hotel to another was clearly a service within the “holiday arrangements” which Kuoni had contracted to provide." (para 32). [full-text]
- The employee's job description does not fix the organiser's promise: the scope of the services "is governed by the contract between Mr and Mrs X and Kuoni and not by the contract between the hotel and its employee, N." (para 33). The hotel's house rules in any event "provided that the principal objective of employees should be to look after guests, serve them courteously and make them comfortable." (para 33). [full-text]
- A crime can be improper performance: "N was able to assault Mrs X only as a result of purporting to act as her guide. Furthermore, the assault was a failure to provide that guiding service with due care. Contrary to the submission of Kuoni, the fact that N’s conduct was so grossly egregious does not alter the fact that this was a breach of the package travel contract between Mr and Mrs X and Kuoni." (para 34). [full-text]
- The conclusion on issue 1: "Being accompanied to reception by a member of the hotel staff was a service falling within the scope of the holiday arrangements which Kuoni contracted to provide and the rape and assault committed by N on Mrs X was improper performance of that contract." (para 35). [full-text]

**What the Court of Justice had ruled, and its effect**

- The reference asked whether the defence in the third indent of art. 5(2) of Directive 90/314 could apply where the failure "is the result of the actions of an employee of a hotel company which is a provider of services to which that contract relates", and whether such an employee is himself a "supplier of services" ([2019] UKSC 37, para 23; repeated at para 25). The Court of Justice was "asked to assume that guidance by a member of the hotel’s staff of Mrs X to the reception was a service within the “holiday arrangements” which Kuoni had contracted to provide and that the rape and assault constituted improper performance of the contract." ([2019] UKSC 37, para 22). [full-text]
- The ruling of 18 March 2021, EU:C:2021:213, as set out at para 26: where the failure "is the result of the actions of an employee of a supplier of services performing that contract", "that employee cannot be regarded as a supplier of services for the purposes of the application of that provision, and" "the organiser cannot be exempted from its liability arising from such non-performance or improper performance, pursuant to that provision." [full-text]
- N was therefore not himself a supplier: "the submission on behalf of Mrs X that N should be considered a “supplier of services” within article 5 of the Directive and regulation 15 of the Regulations must be rejected." (para 40). [full-text]
- But Kuoni had no defence. The ruling means "that Kuoni cannot invoke the exemption from liability established by the third indent of article 5(2) of the Directive or the corresponding provision in regulation 15(2)(c)(ii) of the 1992 Regulations", "because that improper performance was caused by the acts of N, an employee of the hotel which was a supplier of services performing those obligations. Kuoni is liable to Mrs X under regulation 15 of the 1992 Regulations." (para 45). [full-text]
- "Furthermore, it follows that Kuoni is liable to Mrs X for breach of the package travel contract." The contractual defence fell with the statutory one because, as the parties agreed, "The defence in contract is coextensive with the statutory defence." (para 46). [full-text]
- "The CJEU has taken a narrow view of the exemption from liability under the third indent of article 5(2) of Directive 90/314. It has no application where a failure of performance of obligations under a package travel contract is the result of acts or omissions of employees of suppliers of services performing those obligations." (para 48). [full-text]
- The disposal: "In these circumstances, Kuoni is liable to Mrs X both under the 1992 Regulations and for breach of contract." (para 49). "For these reasons, I would allow the appeal." (para 51). [full-text]

**What it said about vicarious liability**

- "Finally, I should mention that in arriving at these conclusions it has not been necessary to address issues relating to vicarious liability. In my view vicarious liability is not relevant here. Kuoni is liable both under the Directive as implemented by the 1992 Regulations and in breach of contract because the services it undertook to provide were not provided with care and skill by an employee of the hotel which was a supplier of the services. That liability does not depend on vicarious liability for the acts of an employee." (para 50). [full-text]
- The reason: "to introduce the principle of vicarious liability into the operation of the Directive scheme would defeat its purpose by rendering the pursuit of claims against tour operators unnecessarily complex and expensive. Questions of vicarious liability of a hotel for the acts of its employees would be likely to be governed by the law of the place where the hotel is situated, with the result that differing systems of national law would apply." (para 50). [full-text]
- The rule: "the Directive as interpreted by the CJEU has established a simple rule whereby a tour operator is liable for the non-performance or improper performance of the obligations it has undertaken where those failures are the result of acts or omissions of employees of suppliers of services performing those obligations." (para 50). [full-text]

**What the judgment does not contain** (whole-text searches of the saved file, 11 Nov 2026)

- It does not decide whether the hotel was liable to Mrs X. The hotel was not a party; the only finding on its vicarious liability is the trial judge's obiter view at para 10, which the Supreme Court neither approves nor disapproves (para 50). [full-text]
- The phrase "non-delegable" does not occur, and *Woodland v Essex County Council* is not cited. [full-text]
- Nothing in it concerns contact with the organiser during the holiday, a complaint made at the time, or any remedy other than damages. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Old law.** The case turns on Directive 90/314 and the 1992 Regulations (paras 4-6). The contract was made in 2010. The 2018 Regulations, which replaced the 1992 Regulations for later contracts, are on card uk2018package; I read no judgment that applies *Kuoni* to them. The defence in issue here ("an event which the other party to the contract or the supplier of services, even with all due care, could not foresee or forestall", reg. 15(2)(c)(ii), quoted at para 6) has no counterpart in reg. 16(4) of the 2018 Regulations on my reading of the two texts. CV-C (11 Nov) leaves this OPEN and leaning against: art. 14(3)(c), read with the definition of unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances in art. 3(12) (reg. 16(4)(c) with reg. 2(1) in the UK), is the nearest analogue. Do not say the old defence was abolished.
- **A package, a tour operator, and an express promise.** Kuoni's conditions accepted responsibility for the fault of "our agents or suppliers" (para 2), Kuoni admitted the statutory rule applied (para 9), and the parties agreed the contractual and statutory defences were coextensive (paras 18, 46). The judgment is not authority that a hotel which sells a room directly answers in contract for a contractor's employee. Longmore LJ's general principle that a contracting party "retains liability for his side of the bargain even if he performs it through others" is reported (para 15(5)), not adopted in terms.
- **The words "arrange to provide" did not help Kuoni** (clause 2.2, para 2). The Supreme Court speaks throughout of services Kuoni "contracted to provide" (paras 32, 35). The judgment does not discuss the distinction between arranging and providing.
- **Liability without any failure of oversight.** The claimant disclaimed any case that Kuoni or the hotel failed to supervise or select (para 8). Kuoni answered for the result. The case shows the law reaching the holder without a duty to monitor, which is why it supports K8 and gives K7's P3 nothing.
- **Damages afterwards.** The only remedy in view is compensation. On the remedies objection in CLAIMS_FIXED.md this case sits with the sources that are satisfied by later repair.
- **"Attending" as a contractual service.** Paras 30-32 treat courteous looking-after and helpfulness when asked as services inherent in a package holiday contract, measured against the standard sold ("a four-star hotel", para 31). That is a legal duty to attend, owed by the organiser, in contract. It is not a general duty on hosts, and it is fixed by the standard contracted for.
- The Court of Appeal and trial judgments were not read. Later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports, for packages.** The party that promised the holiday answered for the deliberate wrong of an independent supplier's employee done in the course of a contracted service, in contract and under the Regulations (paras 45, 46, 49), and the Supreme Court refused to make that depend on the supplier's vicarious liability (para 50). The stated reason is practical: one simple rule against the party the traveller contracted with, in place of a claim governed by the law of wherever the hotel stands (para 50). This is K8's contract limb at the highest English level. It narrows K8's allocation sentence in one respect: where K3's grounds pick different parties (the operator promised; the hotel controlled the guest's space and person), package law picks the promisor, and the judgment says nothing of the hotel's own liability.

**K4 (two tiers): narrows the last sentence.** K4 says attending to the particular guest is a customary duty and not a legal one. For package holidays the Supreme Court said, as an example of ancillary services inherent in every package holiday contract, that "the service of looking after and serving holidaymakers courteously" is one (para 30) and that staff "must be helpful to guests when asked for assistance" (para 31). The paper should say that attending is not a duty the general law lays on hosts, while a contract for a stay of a stated standard can make it one.

**K7 (channel from the guest): no support; a caution.** The case supplies answerability and a remedy in damages. It contains nothing on reachability, and it shows liability attaching with no failure to monitor alleged (para 8).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Supreme Court held the tour operator that sold a package holiday liable, in contract and under the 1992 package regulations, to a guest raped by a hotel employee on the holiday (paras 4, 45-46, 49).
- The Supreme Court held that being guided through the hotel by a member of staff was a service within the holiday arrangements the operator had contracted to provide, and that the assault was improper performance of that contract (para 35).
- The Supreme Court held that the operator's liability "does not depend on vicarious liability for the acts of an employee" (para 50), and that importing vicarious liability would make claims against tour operators "unnecessarily complex and expensive" (para 50).
- In the Supreme Court's words, a package holiday contract includes "the service of looking after and serving holidaymakers courteously in matters relating to their holiday experience" (para 30).
- The claimant alleged no failure by the operator or the hotel to supervise or select the employee (para 8).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the hotel was held liable, or held not liable. It was not a party, and the Supreme Court left its vicarious liability undecided (paras 10, 50).
- That the case applies to a hotel booked directly, or outside package travel.
- That the Supreme Court held the operator's duty "non-delegable". The term is not used.
- That the Supreme Court itself stated the general rule that a contracting party answers for those through whom it performs. That sentence is Longmore LJ's, reported at para 15(5).
- That the case was decided under the 2018 Regulations or Directive 2015/2302.
- That the judgment says anything about a channel, a complaint during the stay, or a remedy before the harm.
