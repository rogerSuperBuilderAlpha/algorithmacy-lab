## Summary

Dagger and Lefkowitz survey theories of political obligation, understood as a moral duty to obey the law of one's own state. Part 4 sets out four families of justification: consent, fair play, membership or association, and natural duty. The associative family holds that obligations attach to a person "merely in virtue of" occupying a role in a socially salient relationship, whether or not the role was chosen. Its critics answer that once associativists add conditions of justice, value or exit, the binding force comes from the agent's will or from a natural duty, so that justification "bottoms out" in a principle external to the association. Associativists reply in three ways, and the entry leaves the dispute open. Part 5 reports pluralist theories that combine several principles, and the objection that unsatisfactory principles do not improve by being combined.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- State of the debate: "To those questions many answers have been given, and none now commands widespread assent." (preamble, para. 1) [full-text]
- Obligation and duty: "some philosophers claim to have uncovered differences between obligations and duties, the most important of which is that obligations must be voluntarily undertaken or incurred, but duties need not be (e.g., Brandt 1964; Hart 1958)." The authors set the distinction aside: it "has played no significant role in the debates over the supposed moral responsibility to obey the law." (§2.1) [full-text]
- Particularity: following Simmons, political obligations are taken to be owed "only to the particular political/legal society that claims primary or exclusive jurisdiction over a person"; the debate concerns “those moral requirements [including obligations and duties] which bind an individual to one particular political community, set of institutions, etc.” (Simmons 1979, p. 31, as quoted; §2.3, last para.) [full-text]
- The four families: "philosophical justifications of political obligation nowadays usually take the form of arguments from consent, fair play, membership, or natural duty." (§4, opening para.) [full-text]
- Consent is not denied as a source: "The critics’ claim is not that consent cannot be a source of obligations, for they typically believe that it can. Their claim, instead, is that too few people have either expressly or tacitly given the kind of actual consent that can ground a general obligation to obey the law" (§4.1, para. 1) [full-text]
- A limit on consent: "Most theorists maintain that putative acts of consent are void if it would be wrong to consent to another’s authority." (§4.1, para. 4) [full-text]
- Fair play, in Hart's words as quoted: “when a number of persons conduct any joint enterprise according to rules and thus restrict their liberty, those who have submitted to these restrictions when required have a right to a similar submission from those who have benefited by their submission” (Hart 1955, p. 185, as quoted; §4.2, para. 1). Simmons's objection as reported: "there is a difference between receiving and accepting benefits, and receiving them is not enough to place someone under an obligation." (§4.2, para. 4, citing Simmons 1979, p. 129) [full-text]
- Associative obligations defined by role: "Associative obligations are ones that agents are alleged to have merely in virtue of their occupying a role in some socially salient relationship, such as being a parent, a friend, or a doctor. These roles are at least partly constituted by the various obligations their occupants owe to the other(s) in the relationship; to be X’s parent or Y’s friend just is to have certain duties to X or Y (as well as certain claims against them)." (§4.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Content varies with the practice: "Exactly what those obligations are varies somewhat depending on the relationship in question and the society in which the participants in that relationship are embedded." (§4.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Chosen or not: "Since associative obligations attach to an agent simply in virtue of her occupying a role in a particular relationship, it makes no difference whether she comes to occupy that role voluntarily, as in the case of professional relationships and most friendships, or non-voluntarily, as in the case of sibling relationships or, in many cases, citizenship." (§4.3, para. 2) [full-text]
- The role as specification of a share: "As Samuel Scheffler observes, “membership-dependent reasons are reasons for doing one’s share, as defined by the norms and ideals of the group itself, to help sustain it and contribute to its purposes” (2018, p. 17). Law specifies, perhaps incompletely, what counts as doing one’s share." (§4.3, para. 3) [full-text]
- The constitutive view, in Horton's words as quoted: "My claim is that a polity is, like the family, a relationship into which we are mostly born, and that the obligations which are constitutive of the relationship do not stand in need of moral justification in terms of a set of basic moral principles or some comprehensive moral theory." (Horton 1992, pp. 150–51, as quoted; §4.3, para. 4, block quotation). Dworkin as quoted: “political association, like family and friendship and other forms of association more local and intimate, is in itself pregnant of obligation” (1986, p. 206; §4.3, para. 4). [full-text]
- The objection from unjust groups: "if membership is sufficient to generate an obligation to obey, then the members of unjust and exploitative groups will have an obligation to obey the rules." (§4.3, para. 6, citing Dagger 2000; 2018, p. 89) [full-text]
- The associativists' conditions: Horton requires that a polity provide “the generic good of order and security” and that members identify with it; Scheffler holds that “the value of one’s membership is not independent of the nature of the society or the justice of its institutions,” (2018, p. 14); Renzo offers a “quasi-voluntarist reformulation of the associative model” on which we occupy roles voluntarily as long as “we could have stepped out of them if we had wanted” (2012, p. 109, p. 120). All as quoted. (§4.3, para. 7) [full-text]
- The critics' conclusion, which is K3's structure: "Critics maintain that in offering responses like those just described, associative theorists effectively abandon their attempt to provide a distinctive account of political obligation (Dagger 2018, p. 79–82; Wellman 2000, p. 553; Simmons 2001, pp. 83–84; 2005, pp. 111–115). That is because the moral duty to perform the (conventional) duties that constitute the role of citizen ultimately depends either on an agent’s exercise of will, i.e. consent or accepting the benefits of others’ cooperation, or a natural duty, such as the duty to support just institutions or the duty to maximize aggregate welfare. In all of these cases, citizens’ duty to obey the law ultimately depends on a consideration other than their membership in the polity. The task of morally justifying political obligation bottoms out not in the fact of association but in a moral principle external to it." (§4.3, para. 8) [full-text]
- First reply, disabling against grounding: "Some associative theorists respond that the critics conflate considerations that disable associative obligations with considerations that ground or justify associative obligations (Horton and Windeknecht 2015, p. 914; Scheffler 2018, p. 11)." Freedom to exit may be a necessary condition, "Yet it is the good realized in the relationship that justifies participants’ claims against one another, and the duties to which they correlate, not any consent implicit in their continued participation in it." (§4.3, para. 9) [full-text]
- Second reply, the standards are internal: "considerations of fairness, respect, care, and reliance are part and parcel of our concepts of parent, sibling, friend, and citizen." An appeal to unfairness or neglect "need not involve an appeal to some moral principle external to the relationship. Instead, it may involve an appeal to a principle internal to the relationship; that is, to a moralized conception of what it is to be a sibling or citizen." (§4.3, para. 10) [full-text]
- Third reply, no outside vantage point: "There is no vantage point outside the specific, historical, political communities in which we are inevitably situated, from which we can identify the true bases of our moral obligations"; Fossen as quoted: “Political obligations are practical commitments that come into play through participants’ mutually holding each other to account in political practices,” (2014, p. 231). (§4.3, para. 11) [full-text]
- Natural duties defined against roles: they are duties people have "simply in virtue of their status as moral agents; they need do nothing to acquire them, nor does their bearing such duties depend on their occupying some role in a socially salient relationship." (§4.4, para. 1) [full-text]
- The particularity objection to natural duty: Simmons argues that natural duty accounts cannot "justify the particularity of political obligations"; even granting a duty to support just institutions, "why must we discharge that duty by supporting and complying with the just institutions that comprise the state in which we are citizens or residents?" (§4.4, para. 5, citing Simmons 1979, pp. 31–5; 2005, pp. 166–79). One answer points "to the intensity and frequency of interaction among those who live in close proximity to one another" (§4.4, para. 6, citing Waldron 1993; 1996). [full-text]
- Pluralism: Klosko, Wolff and Knowles "have explicitly called for a pluralistic or multiple-principle approach to political obligation. There is no single answer to the problem of political obligation, as they see it, because the problem has more than one aspect." (§5, para. 2) [full-text]
- The objection to pluralism: sceptics "are likely to say that combining principles, whether in hybrid or pluralist fashion, will not help, for combining a set of principles that are unsatisfactory individually will hardly produce a strong and satisfying theory (Simmons 2007, n. 17)." The entry closes: "Whether a plurality of principles is necessary or even desirable, in sum, remains one of many open questions" (§5, para. 3) [full-text]
- Which principle fits which party: "Attention to the ambiguity in the referent of the term “political” may also support a pluralistic or multi-principle solution to the problem of political obligation; e.g., a membership justification for the duty of citizens to obey the law, and a consent justification for the duty of foreigners to do so (see §5)." (§2.2, para. 3) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A survey of another question.** The entry asks why a citizen should obey the law. The role in view is 'citizen', mostly unchosen, and the party bound is the member, toward the polity or fellow members. A commercial host chose its role and owes its duty to an outsider, the guest. The structural point carries over (duties that constitute a role against a principle that binds one to them), and the entry itself generalises to "a parent, a friend, or a doctor" and to "professional relationships" (§4.3, paras 1-2). The application to hosts is the paper's.
- **The authors are among the critics.** The view at §4.3, para. 8 is attributed to Dagger (2018; 2000), Wellman and Simmons. One of the entry's two authors therefore reports his own side's conclusion. The three replies that follow (paras 9-11) are given at equal length and left unanswered.
- **What I did not read.** Sections 1 and 3, and none of the primary texts cited (Simmons 1979, 2001, 2005; Horton 1992, 2010; Scheffler 2018; Dworkin 1986; Renzo 2012; Dagger 2018). Every quotation of those authors above is 'as quoted' in the entry.
- **Fair play is a fourth ground.** The entry treats the principle of fairness as a main contender (§4.2). K3 lists three grounds and omits it. A commercial host takes the benefits of a practice of travel and custom, so a reviewer may ask why fairness is absent.
- **The disabling reply bites on ground 1.** If voluntariness is only a condition without which a role's value fails, as the first reply holds (§4.3, para. 9), then K3's voluntary assumption would be an enabler and the role's own good would do the grounding. K3 needs an answer, or must say that for a role entered by holding out the distinction makes no practical difference.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K3 (the role specifies; three external grounds bind): supports the structure as one side of an open debate, and narrows the three-ground strategy.**

- *Supports.* The entry gives K3 a published statement of both halves. Roles "are at least partly constituted by the various obligations their occupants owe" (§4.3, para. 1), and "Law specifies, perhaps incompletely, what counts as doing one’s share" (§4.3, para. 3): the role and its rules fix content. Critics then hold that the duty "to perform the (conventional) duties that constitute the role" depends on will or on a natural duty, and that justification "bottoms out not in the fact of association but in a moral principle external to it" (§4.3, para. 8). K3's 'voluntary assumption' and 'invited reliance' fall under "an agent’s exercise of will"; 'dependence' is nearest to "a natural duty".
- *Refutes, if the other side is right.* Horton's constitutive view denies that a role's obligations "stand in need of moral justification in terms of a set of basic moral principles" (§4.3, para. 4), and the three replies at paras 9-11 are live. The entry reports no winner (preamble; §5, para. 3). K3 therefore takes a side.
- *Narrows.* Two objections reach K3's own design. Natural-duty grounds cannot explain why the duty is owed by and to particular parties (§4.4, para. 5), which is ground 3's weakness in another setting. And several grounds that each fall short do not add up (§5, para. 3). K3 escapes the second only if at least one ground suffices by itself in the core case, or if the paper shows how the grounds combine.

**What the paper may say**

- On the associative view, obligations attach to a person "merely in virtue of their occupying a role in some socially salient relationship", and the role is "at least partly constituted" by those obligations (§4.3, para. 1).
- On that view it "makes no difference" whether the role was entered voluntarily, "as in the case of professional relationships", or not (§4.3, para. 2).
- Critics of the associative view (the entry cites Dagger, Wellman and Simmons) hold that the duty to perform the conventional duties of a role depends on an exercise of will or on a natural duty, so that justification "bottoms out not in the fact of association but in a moral principle external to it" (§4.3, para. 8). The paper may say that it follows these critics.
- Associative theorists reply that such critics confuse conditions that disable an obligation with what grounds it, and that the relevant standards are internal to the role (§4.3, paras 9-10). The paper should report the reply where it takes the critics' side.
- Consent is widely accepted as a source of obligation; the dispute in political philosophy is over how many people have given it (§4.1, para. 1).
- Some theorists hold that no single principle grounds political obligation and combine several; sceptics answer that principles which fail singly do not succeed together (§5, paras 2-3).

**What the paper may not say**

- That philosophers agree that roles bind only through external principles. The entry reports a dispute that "remains" open.
- That Simmons, Wellman, Horton, Scheffler, Dworkin or Renzo say anything beyond the words quoted or the summary given in the entry, or that the paper has read them.
- That the entry concerns hosts, firms, commercial roles or duties owed to outsiders. Its subject is the duty to obey the law.
- That the entry endorses K3. Its authors report the critics' view, to which one of them belongs, and the replies.
- That the entry offers voluntary assumption, reliance and dependence as a set. Its four families are consent, fair play, membership and natural duty.
