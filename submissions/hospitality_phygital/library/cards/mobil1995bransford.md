## Summary

A customer was beaten by an attendant at a Mobil Mini Mart petrol station that Mobil owned and had leased to an independent dealer. He sued Mobil on apparent agency, pointing to Mobil's ownership, its products, its trademarks throughout the station, the franchise agreement that required them, and visits by Mobil's representatives. The trial court gave Mobil summary judgment; the district court of appeal reversed; the Supreme Court of Florida quashed that decision and ordered summary judgment for Mobil reinstated. The court held that the use of franchise logos and advertising does not by itself represent that the franchisor controls the business, restated the three elements of apparent agency (representation, reliance, change of position), and found the first missing. It receded from Orlando Executive Park v. Robbins so far as that case suggested that logos alone can create an apparent agency. It treated Mobil's ownership of the land as irrelevant because the lessee had control of the premises, and it noted that a claim dismissed against the apparent agent must be dismissed against the apparent principal. Shaw J dissented: the contracts bound the dealer to standards of customer service that Mobil imposed and could enforce, which made control over customer service a question for a jury. Florida law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Court, parties, claim**

- Supreme Court of Florida, No. 80310, 5 January 1995, rehearing denied 17 February 1995; per curiam, with Grimes CJ, Overton, Kogan and Harding JJ and McDonald, Senior Justice, concurring (at 122; the saved text prints the last name as "MeDONALD", an OCR fault) and "SHAW, J., dissents with an opinion" (at 122). Vote: five to one. [full-text]
- How the case came up: "We have for review Bransford v. Berman, 601 So.2d 1306 (Fla. 4th DCA 1992), based on apparent conflict with the opinion in Orlando Executive Park, Inc. v. Robbins, 433 So.2d 491 (Fla.1983)." (at 120). [full-text]
- The facts and the claim: "In 1990, Jeremy Bransford entered a Mobil Mini Mart gas station in Broward County owned by Mobil Oil Corporation but leased to Alan Berman. While on the premises, Bransford was attacked and beaten by one of Berman’s employees, who allegedly had a history of assaulting customers. Bransford later sued Mobil on the theory that it had effectively established an apparent agency relationship with the leaseholder, Berman." (at 120). [full-text]
- The claimant was a customer of a shop, not a guest of a hotel: he "entered a Mobil Mini Mart with a friend, bought two sub sandwiches, and then exchanged sharp words with the attendant" (Shaw J, at 122). [full-text]
- What he relied on: "that Mobil owned the property, that Mobil products were sold in the station, that Mobil trademarks and logos were used throughout the premises, and that the franchise agreement with Mobil required the use of Mobil symbols and the selling of Mobil products. Moreover, Mobil allegedly sent its representatives to the station to provide various routine franchise support services." (at 120). [full-text]

**The holding on signs**

- "We find Bransford’s allegations legally insufficient to plead a case against Mobil. In today’s world, it is well understood that the mere use of franchise logos and related advertisements does not necessarily indicate that the franchisor has actual or apparent control over any substantial aspect of the franchisee’s business or employment decisions. Nor does the provision of routine contractual support services refute this conclusion." (at 120). [full-text]
- The contract counted for the brand. It said the dealer "is an independent businessman, and nothing in this contract shall be deemed as creating any right in [Mobil] to exercise any control over, or to direct in any respect, the conduct or management of [the] business." (at 120, the court quoting the Retail Dealer Contract with its own brackets). [full-text]
- When a franchisor does answer: "Franchisors may well enter into an agency relationship with a franchisee if, by contract or action or representation, the franchisor has directly or apparently participated in some substantial way in directing or managing acts of the franchisee, beyond the mere fact of providing contractual franchise support activities." (at 120). [full-text]

**The test**

- "In cases of alleged apparent agency, something must have happened to communicate to the plaintiff the idea that the franchisor is exercising substantial control. Our law is well settled that an apparent agency exists only if each of three elements are present: (a) a representation by the purported principal; (b) a reliance on that representation by a third party; and (c) a change in position by the third party in reliance on the representation." (at 121). [full-text]
- Which element failed: "It is the first of these elements that is primarily relevant here." The plaintiff "alleged no genuine factual representation by Mobil, but merely assumed that such a representation is implicit in the prominent use of Mobil symbols and products throughout the station and in the provision of support activities. As noted above, such an assumption is not sustainable in today’s world." (at 121). [full-text]

**The earlier hotel-franchise case, as this court reads it**

- "The only relevant fact in Orlando Executive Park was that the franchisor’s direct participation was substantial despite the fact it did not own the property: The franchisor actually operated several components within the complex in question." (at 121). "That fact alone obviously and directly “represented” to the public that the franchisor was in substantial control of the business, even though the franchisor did not own the premises." (at 121). [full-text]
- "We recognize that Orlando Executive Park suggested that oil company cases somehow are different from other franchising cases and that logos or other trademark symbols alone can create an apparent agency. Because we believe these to be erroneous impressions, we recede from Orlando Executive Park to the extent it is inconsistent with this opinion." (at 121). [full-text]

**The owner out of possession**

- "Indeed, actual ownership of the premises is relevant only to the extent it may indicate some degree of actual or apparent control over the business. Mobil’s ownership here is irrelevant because the record indicates that Mobil had leased the property to another who possessed actual and superseding control over the premises; and there was nothing beyond trademark symbols, Mobil products, and franchise support to indicate any apparent control by Mobil as to this plaintiff." (at 121). [full-text]

**The brand's liability depends on the operator's**

- "We merely note for the instruction of the courts below that the dismissal of any claim against an apparent agent also requires dismissal of the same claim against the apparent principal." (at 121). [full-text]
- "Thus, if the agent cannot be held liable, neither can the principal, because there is nothing to impute." (at 121). [full-text]
- Disposition: the case "is remanded for proceedings consistent with our views here, and with instructions that the trial court’s order of summary judgment in favor of Mobil be" reinstated, and "The decision of the district court is quashed." (at 121-122). [full-text]

**The dissent (Shaw J, at 122-124)**

- "I would uphold the district court’s ruling that the trial court erred in granting Mobil’s motion for summary judgment." (at 122). [full-text]
- More holding out than the majority lists: "Mobil owned the station and prominently displayed its logo, insignia, and color scheme in order to induce customers to patronize the station. Mobil gas and other products were sold there. Employees were required to wear Mobil uniforms and Stetham was wearing a Mobil hat when he assaulted Bransford." (at 122). "Mobil representatives checked on the station’s pricing, appearance, and advertising." (at 123). [full-text]
- The clause the majority cut short. The Retail Dealer Contract's independence clause ends "subject only to Buyer’s performance of the obligations imposed under this contract", and Shaw J writes: "The majority fails to include in its opinion the last portion of this quote, underlined above, which is the key provision." (at 123). [full-text]
- Those obligations included a duty to hear customers. Clause 9, as the dissent quotes it, bound the dealer to "render prompt, fair, courteous, and efficient service to Buyer’s customers" and to "promptly investigate all customer complaints, and make such adjustments which are reasonable and appropriate" (at 123). [full-text]
- The lease recited that Mobil "has built valuable goodwill throughout the country and has fostered confidence in the motoring public in service stations and products bearing Landlord’s trademarks" (at 123, the dissent quoting the Service Station Lease). [full-text]
- The dissent's conclusion on control: "These provisions guaranteeing exemplary customer service were imposed and enforceable by Mobil." and "According to the agreements, Mobil set the standard for employee/customer relations at the Mini Mart." (at 123). [full-text]
- The pleaded reliance: the plaintiff "entered the property believing that it was a MOBIL station and that the property would be operated at a level commensurate with that expected of the MOBIL OIL CORPORATION" (at 122, the dissent quoting para 9 of the amended complaint). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Not a hotel case.** The claimant bought sandwiches at a petrol-station shop. He was not received for the night and nothing in the opinion concerns an innkeeper's duty. The case speaks to K8 as general franchise law on what a brand's signs represent. It is the same court's qualification of a hotel-franchise precedent, but the paper cannot cite it as a decision about hotels.
- **The harm was done by the operator's own employee.** In Crinkley and Hayman third parties assaulted guests and the complaint was poor security. Here the attendant was the assailant, so the underlying claims against the operator were negligent retention and inadequate security (at 121, in the passage the saved text garbles).
- **The holding is qualified.** The court's sentence is that logos do not *necessarily* indicate control ("does not necessarily indicate", at 120). It leaves room for a franchisor that "has directly or apparently participated in some substantial way in directing or managing acts of the franchisee" (at 120). The holding is that logos, branded goods and routine support are not enough alone.
- **Reliance was not decided.** The court rested on the first element. It did not rule on whether a customer who walked into a Mobil station had relied on Mobil.
- **The court's premise is empirical and unsupported.** "In today’s world, it is well understood" (at 120) cites nothing. The Crinkley guest testified that he "did not know the difference between a franchise inn and a company owned inn" (Crinkley, para 52). The two courts assume opposite things about what customers know.
- **Contractual standards did not count as control for the majority.** The dissent shows that Mobil's contracts required prompt service, trained attendants and the investigation of customer complaints, and that Mobil could enforce them. The majority classed what Mobil did as "routine contractual support services" (at 120). A brand can therefore write the service standard, including a complaints duty, into its contract with the operator and still not answer to the customer in Florida.
- **Later treatment not checked.** I did not read Orlando Executive Park v. Robbins or the district court's decision in Bransford v. Berman; what this card says of either is what the Supreme Court of Florida says of it.
- **OCR.** One paragraph at p. 121 is corrupt in the saved copy. A verifier should read that paragraph in a clean copy of the reporter before anyone quotes more of it than the two sentences given above.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): refutes the allocation by holding out where the holding out is the brand alone.** K8 lets the party that held out answer on the ground of reliance. The Supreme Court of Florida holds that a brand's name, logos and products are not a representation of control at all, so the reliance ground has nothing to attach to. Three further points narrow K8. A franchisor's liability on apparent agency is derivative: "if the agent cannot be held liable, neither can the principal" (at 121). An owner that has leased the premises to another "who possessed actual and superseding control" is out (at 121). And the court locates responsibility with control of the premises, which is K8's dependence ground and picks the operator.

**K3 (grounds of the duty): supplies the three-element form of the reliance ground and one more weakness.** Representation, reliance and change of position must each be shown (at 121). For the table of ground by hard case: for the *brand*, voluntary assumption by signage is denied in Florida, since signs are held to assume nothing; for the *owner*, none of the three grounds reaches an owner out of possession; the *operator* in control is the party the dependence ground picks.

**K7 (channel from the guest): a note only.** The dissent records a franchise contract that obliged the operator to "promptly investigate all customer complaints" (at 123). That is evidence that brands write complaint duties into their contracts with operators. The majority did not treat such a clause as giving the customer anything against the brand.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Supreme Court of Florida has held that the use of franchise logos and related advertising does not by itself indicate that the franchisor controls the franchisee's business (at 120).
- In Florida apparent agency requires a representation by the supposed principal, reliance on it by the third party, and a change of position in reliance (at 121).
- The court receded from its earlier decision in Orlando Executive Park v. Robbins so far as that decision suggested that logos or trademarks alone can create an apparent agency (at 121).
- The court treated the franchisor's ownership of the premises as irrelevant because it had leased them to an operator with actual control (at 121).
- In a dictum "for the instruction of the courts below", in a garbled paragraph, the court said that on apparent agency a brand cannot be liable unless the operator is, because there would be nothing to impute (at 121).
- One judge dissented, on the ground that the franchisor's contracts set and could enforce the standard of customer service (at 122-124).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the case concerned a hotel, a guest or an innkeeper's duty. It concerned a customer at a petrol-station shop.
- That franchisors are immune in Florida. The court says a franchisor answers if it has "directly or apparently participated in some substantial way in directing or managing acts of the franchisee" (at 120).
- That the court overruled Orlando Executive Park. It receded from it "to the extent it is inconsistent with this opinion" (at 121).
- Anything about what Orlando Executive Park decided beyond this court's description of it. This reader did not read it.
- That the court found no reliance. It decided on the first element.
- That American courts in general reject brand-only apparent agency. This is one State's supreme court; Crinkley went the other way on its facts.
