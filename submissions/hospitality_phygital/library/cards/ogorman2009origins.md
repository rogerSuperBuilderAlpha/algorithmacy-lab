## Summary

Uses texts and archaeology from Mesopotamia (c. 2000 BC), Pompeii (AD 79) and Middle Eastern trade routes (from c. AD 700) to show that commercial hospitality is at least four thousand years old, already distinct from domestic and civic hospitality, stratified, and subject to legal control.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Early legal duties ran to policing the guest: tavernkeepers were required, "on pain of death, report all customers who were felons" (pdf p. 4, via Driver and Miles) [full-text]
- "hostels and inns in Mesopotamia date back to at least 2000 B.C. and they were controlled by the laws of the time." (pdf p. 11) [full-text]
- "Crossing thresholds of commercial hospitality also guaranteed and provided physical protection, sanctuary and security." (pdf p. 11) [full-text]
- "the sector was already distinct and separate from domestic and civic hospitality" (pdf p. 11) [full-text]
- "one constant and unremitting aspect of the commercial industry and those employed within it was the increasing legal control they were subject to." (pdf p. 11) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- The protection sentence (pdf p. 11) sits in the conclusion, follows a sentence on revenue and status, and cites nothing; the body's evidence is about caravanserais built by rulers and religious donors (pdf pp. 9-10). It cannot carry "commercial thresholds have historically guaranteed protection" as a general claim (PO §2.1 l. 53).
- The Hammurabi rule fits bogen1996innkeeper's crime-control account of the English duties (p. 53): early law made the host answer *to the authorities for* the guest before it made the host answer *to* the guest.
- O'Gorman does not discuss English or American innkeeper law. The "bridge to tradition (d)" that FABLE_REVIEW §4(a) hoped for is a pointer, not a bridge.

## Where it enters the manuscript
