## Summary

Dr Scott negligently told Mr Gregg that a lump under his arm was benign; it was a lymphoma, and treatment was delayed by about nine months. The trial judge found that the delay reduced Mr Gregg's prospects of disease-free survival for ten years (from 42% to 25%), but that a better outcome had never been more likely than not, and dismissed the claim. By three to two the House of Lords dismissed his appeal. Lord Hoffmann held that the progress of the disease had a determinate cause which the claimant had to prove on the balance of probability; recovery for loss of a chance in a case like this would require overruling Hotson and Wilsher and dismantling the limits on Fairchild, and so wholesale a change "should be left to Parliament" (paras 79-80, 85, 90). Baroness Hale held that "damage is the gist of negligence", that redefining personal injury as loss of chance would cause "far more problems in the general run of personal injury claims than the policy benefits are worth", and, "not without regret", that it should not be done (paras 217, 225-226). Lord Phillips found the case an unsuitable vehicle and upheld the conventional approach, leaving open proportionate recovery where an adverse outcome has occurred (para 190). Lord Nicholls and Lord Hope dissented: a significant diminution of a patient's prospects of recovery caused by medical negligence should itself be actionable damage, whether the prospects were above or below 50% (paras 42-44, 121).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The majority: Lord Hoffmann**

- The question: "whether Dr Scott’s negligence caused injury to Mr Gregg" (para 64). [full-text]
- "What these cases show is that ... the law regards the world as in principle bound by laws of causality. Everything has a determinate cause, even if we do not know what it is. ... it was for the plaintiff to prove that it was an act or omission for which the defendant was responsible. ... What we lack is knowledge and the law deals with lack of knowledge by the concept of the burden of proof." (para 79). [full-text]
- "the outcome was not random; it was governed by laws of causality and, in the absence of a special rule as in Fairchild, inability to establish that delay in diagnosis caused the reduction in expectation in life cannot be remedied by treating the outcome as having been somehow indeterminate." (para 80). [full-text]
- Whose conduct the hypothesis turns on: "the law distinguishes between cases in which the outcome depends upon what the claimant himself (McWilliams v Sir William Arrol & Co [1962] 1 WLR 295) or someone for whom the defendant is responsible (Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority [1998] AC 232) would have done, and cases in which it depends upon what some third party would have done. In the first class of cases the claimant must prove on a balance of probability that he or the defendant would have acted so as to produce a favourable outcome. In the latter class, he may recover for loss of the chance that the third party would have so acted. This apparently arbitrary distinction obviously rests on grounds of policy. In addition, most of the cases in which there has been recovery for loss of a chance have involved financial loss" (para 83). [full-text]
- "The rule which the House is asked to adopt is the very rule which it rejected in Wilsher’s case ... Hotson ... too would have to be overruled." (para 85). [full-text]
- "a wholesale adoption of possible rather than probable causation as the criterion of liability would be so radical a change in our law as to amount to a legislative act. It would have enormous consequences for insurance companies and the National Health Service. In company with my noble and learned friends Lord Phillips of Worth Matravers and Baroness Hale of Richmond, I think that any such change should be left to Parliament." (para 90). "I would dismiss the appeal." (para 91). [full-text]
- Rejected 'hook': using a proved injury (the spread of the cancer) as "a “hook” on which to hang a claim for damage which it did not actually cause" was "lacking in principle" (paras 86-87). [full-text]

**The majority: Baroness Hale**

- "It is now hornbook law that damage is the gist of the action in negligence. ... The primary facts of what took place must be proved on the balance of probabilities. It must also be shown on the balance of probabilities that what the defendant negligently did or failed to do caused the claimant’s damage." (para 193). [full-text]
- Conventional recovery remains: "The defendant is liable for any extra pain, suffering, loss of amenity, financial loss and loss of expectation of life which may have resulted from the delay." (para 206). [full-text]
- "Tort law is there to compensate those who have been wronged. Some wrongs are actionable whether or not the claimant has been damaged. But damage is the gist of negligence. So it can never be enough to show that the defendant has been negligent. The question is still whether his negligence has caused actionable damage." (para 217). [full-text]
- "Almost any claim for loss of an outcome could be reformulated as a claim for loss of a chance of that outcome." (para 224). "There is no reason in principle why the change in approach should be limited to medical negligence. ... introducing the latter would cause far more problems in the general run of personal injury claims than the policy benefits are worth." (para 225). [full-text]
- "The complexities of attempting to introduce liability for the loss of a chance of a more favourable outcome in personal injury claims have driven me, not without regret, to conclude that it should not be done." (para 226). [full-text]

**The majority: Lord Phillips**

- "The complications of this case have persuaded me that it is not a suitable vehicle for introducing into the law of clinical negligence the right to recover damages for the loss of a chance of a cure. ... Where medical treatment has resulted in an adverse outcome and negligence has increased the chance of that outcome, there may be a case for permitting a recovery of damages that is proportionate to the increase in the chance of the adverse outcome. That is not a case that has been made out on the present appeal. I would uphold the conventional approach to causation that was applied by Judge Inglis." (para 190). [full-text]

**The dissents**

- Lord Nicholls: "where a patient is suffering from illness or injury and his prospects of recovery are attended with a significant degree of medical uncertainty, and he suffers a significant diminution of his prospects of recovery by reason of medical negligence whether of diagnosis or treatment, that diminution constitutes actionable damage." (para 44); confined "to medical negligence cases where the claimant was already suffering from illness or injury at the time of the negligence and the defendant’s duty related to the amelioration of that very illness or injury" (para 51). "I would therefore allow this appeal." (para 59). [full-text]
- Lord Nicholls on financial loss of a chance: "When a solicitor’s failure to issue a writ in time deprived a claimant of the opportunity to pursue court proceedings damages were not assessed on an all-or-nothing basis" (para 18, on Kitchen v Royal Air Force Association). [full-text]
- Lord Hope: "I would hold therefore that the significant reduction in the prospects of a successful outcome which the negligence caused is a loss for which the appellant is entitled to be compensated." (para 121). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The ratio is narrow in form and wide in reach.** The case is clinical negligence. But Lord Hoffmann's reasons rest on causation in general (paras 79-80) and Baroness Hale's on personal injury generally (para 225: "no reason in principle why the change in approach should be limited to medical negligence"). The red team's lead, 'In personal-injury claims, English law does not compensate a lost chance', is a fair statement of the majority's position for a guest's injury claim.
- **Three hypotheticals, each on the balance of probability.** On Lord Hoffmann's distinction (para 83), what the guest herself would have done (reported) and what the host or its staff would have done (corrected) are both questions the claimant must prove more likely than not; only the conduct of an independent third party can be valued as a chance, and mostly in financial-loss cases.
- **Financial loss is different.** Loss of a chance is recoverable in some financial-loss cases (paras 18-19, 83), so a guest's purely economic loss may stand differently *(my reading; no hospitality case read)*.
- **Contract and statute are outside it.** Gregg is about negligence for personal injury. Remedies that do not depend on proving that a report would have prevented harm, such as repeat performance or price reduction for a service not performed with care (card uk2015consumer, ss. 55-56), are not touched by it *(my inference)*.
- **Lord Phillips left a door open** for proportionate recovery where the adverse outcome has occurred (para 190). It is a dictum in a majority speech, not a holding.
- **Read depth.** Lord Phillips's statistical analysis (paras 127-183) and parts of Lord Hope's and Baroness Hale's speeches were not read; nothing here depends on them.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7 (channel from the guest), conclusion C in the legal register: refutes the inference from a missing channel to liability for the harm, unless causation is proved.** A guest who is injured after a failure that a channel might have let the host correct can recover in negligence only by proving, more likely than not, that she would have reported, that the report would have reached someone who would have acted, and that acting would have prevented the injury (paras 79-80, 83, 193). A reduced chance of correction is not actionable damage in personal injury (paras 90, 225-226). P4's own evidence that many guests do not report (ARGUMENT.md, P4, 'Against') weakens the first link in any given case. The dossier should state C in the legal register, if at all, as a standard whose breach makes the guest's other remedies easier to reach, not as a route to damages for the harm the failure caused.

**K6 (the guest's claim): narrows.** The guest's claim to performance and its contractual remedies do not face the Gregg problem; her claim for injury does.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In *Gregg v Scott* the House of Lords, by a majority, refused to treat the loss of a chance of a better medical outcome as actionable damage in negligence; the claimant must prove on the balance of probability that the negligence caused the adverse outcome (Lord Hoffmann, paras 79-80, 90; Baroness Hale, paras 193, 226; Lord Phillips, para 190). (Narrowed after CV-G: Lord Phillips left proportionate recovery open (para 190); Lord Hope's main ground, in dissent, was that the growth of the tumour was itself an injury.)
- Lord Hoffmann distinguished cases turning on what the claimant or the defendant would have done, which must be proved on the balance of probability, from cases turning on what a third party would have done, where a lost chance may be valued (para 83).
- Baroness Hale saw "no reason in principle why the change in approach should be limited to medical negligence" and declined it for personal injury (paras 225-226).
- Lord Nicholls and Lord Hope dissented (paras 44, 59, 121).

**What the paper may not say**

- That English law gives a guest damages for the lost chance that a report would have prevented her injury.
- That *Gregg* concerned anything but clinical negligence, or that it decided financial-loss or contract claims.
- That Lord Phillips held proportionate recovery available; he said there "may be a case" for it (para 190).
