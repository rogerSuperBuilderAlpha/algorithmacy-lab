## Summary

Two vignette experiments with US respondents. Study 1 is a 2 x 2 design (human vs robot, failure vs success) set at a hotel reception, n = 331. Study 2 uses a restaurant waiter and compares mechanical and analytical robots with a human. Respondents attribute less responsibility to a robot than to an employee, especially after failure; they attribute more responsibility to the firm when the agent is a robot; and they see robot performance as more stable.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Study 1 sample and setting: "data collected from 331 US participants ... randomly assigned to each of the four scenarios"; scenarios "focused on the hospitality sector" (hotel reception, Pepper robot) (pp. 273-274) [full-text]
- Agent responsibility: greater for employee (M = 4.01) than robot (M = 3.47), F = 16.33, p < 0.01; the difference holds only under failure (pp. 275-276) [full-text]
- Firm (hotel) responsibility: greater for robot (M = 4.35) than employee (M = 4.12), F = 4.68, p < 0.05; the agent x outcome interaction is not significant (H4b not supported), though the robot effect is significant only in the failure condition (F = 3.97, p < 0.05) (pp. 275-277) [full-text]
- Study 2: "mechanical robots, but not analytical robots, increase customer's perceptions of firm responsibility compared to human agents" (p. 282) [full-text]
- Interpretation: "robots are perceived to be more representative of a firm than employees" (p. 281) [full-text]
- Managerial advice: "offering human assistance to customers proactively following a robot failure or reactively when customers indicate that they are uncomfortable or stuck with the robot" (p. 283); the challenge is sharpest "when the option of human-operated service is no longer available to customers" (p. 282) [full-text]

## Support verdicts (rr-2026-11)

- v4 §7: "after a service failure guests hold a robot less responsible than they would a frontline employee, and lay the responsibility on the firm instead (Belanche et al. 2020)". SUPPORTED, with two qualifications. The respondents are US vignette participants imagining a hotel, not guests (p. 273). The firm effect is a modest main effect, not a failure-specific interaction (H4b not supported, p. 275). It also disappears for robots presented as analytical (p. 282).
- v4 §7 surrounding claim "What guests seem to need is not a human presence as such, but someone with the power to change the answer": not tested by Belanche. The paper's own remedy, human assistance after robot failure (p. 283), is close to the fourth principle and could be cited for it.

## Critical notes & threats

- "Guests" in v4 should read "respondents" or "customers in hotel scenarios" if a reviewer is strict.
