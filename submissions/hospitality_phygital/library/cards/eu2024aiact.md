## Summary

The primary legal text. Article 86 gives a person affected by a decision a deployer takes on the output of an Annex III high-risk system a right to explanation. Locators below are PDF page numbers of the 144-page Official Journal file (not OJ page numbers). New numbering (rr-2026-11): section 9, jurisdiction boundary condition.

Added in rr-2026-11-k. Section numbering for the added claims is v6's: 4 = Assembled hosts at the touchpoint; 5 = Conclusion, where v6 cites art. 14(4)(e). Articles 14 and 26 set out what human oversight of a high-risk system means and who must supply it. Article 14 is a requirement about the system, which the provider must meet (art. 16(a)): it must be built and handed over so that the natural persons assigned to oversee it can understand and monitor it, resist over-reliance on it, interpret its output, decide in any particular situation not to use it or to "disregard, override or reverse" its output, and stop it. Article 26 lays the corresponding duties on the deployer, the firm that uses the system: to assign oversight to natural persons with "the necessary competence, training and authority", to monitor the system's operation, and to suspend its use and tell the provider and the authority when it has reason to think the system presents a risk. The Regulation enforces these duties through market surveillance authorities and fines (arts 85, 99). The person affected by a decision is given information that the system is in use (art. 26(11)), an explanation of a decision taken (art. 86) and a right to complain to the authority (art. 85). No provision lets that person reach the overseer.

## Critical notes & threats

- Text-only: no interpretation of whether a given hotel system is high-risk beyond what Annex III's words say. Staff-facing systems (4(b)) are the textual hook; guest-facing ones are not named.
- Art. 86(1) excludes Annex III point 2 and requires a decision "by the deployer" with legal or similarly significant effect, so it does not on its face reach every guest-facing refusal.
- Application dates were amended by Reg. (EU) 2026/1744 (see Key facts). The manuscript must not say flatly that the right to explanation 'applies from 2 August 2026' for hotel systems: Art. 86 itself is unamended and in force from that date, but the high-risk classification rules it depends on (Art. 6(2), Annex III) apply from 2 December 2027. Say the right exists for Annex III high-risk systems and give both dates, or avoid a date.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **'Owed to overseers' is not what the text says.** DRAFT_v6 (Conclusion) says the stop-button requirement "is owed to overseers, not guests". Art. 14(4) states what the persons assigned to oversight must be "enabled" to do; the requirement is laid on the system and met by the provider (arts 8(1), 16(a)), and the deployer must assign the persons (art. 26(2)). The Regulation names no one to whom these duties are owed. They are enforced by authorities (arts 85, 99). The accurate statement is that the Act requires an overseer with power to override and to stop, and gives the person affected no route to that overseer.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Authority is required; its content is fixed by art. 14(4).** Art. 26(2) asks for "the necessary competence, training and authority" without defining them. Read with art. 14(4)(d)-(e), the authority is at least the power to decide not to use the system, to disregard, override or reverse its output in a particular situation, and to stop it. That link is my reading of the two articles together; neither cross-refers to the other in terms, though art. 26(3) speaks of "the human oversight measures indicated by the provider".
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **The monitoring signal comes from the system and its instructions, not from the person affected.** The deployer monitors "on the basis of the instructions for use" (art. 26(5)) and keeps the system's logs (art. 26(6)). Nothing requires the deployer to receive, or act on, a report from the person a decision concerns.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Suspension is triggered by risk to health, safety or fundamental rights** (arts 26(5), 79(1)), a product-safety standard. A decision that is merely wrong about one guest does not trigger it.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **'As appropriate and proportionate'.** The overseer's capabilities in art. 14(4) are qualified by those words, and the measures by "commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy and context of use" (art. 14(3)). The Act does not require a person to review each output.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Only art. 14(5) puts a person before the effect**, and only for remote biometric identification, where two natural persons must verify before "action or decision is taken". The existing note of 11 Nov 2026 below records that a check-in system which only verifies identity is outside Annex III point 1(a).
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **High-risk systems only, and not yet.** Arts 14 and 26 bind providers and deployers of high-risk systems. This card already records that no Annex III area names hospitality and that staff-facing systems (point 4(b)) are the textual hook. For those systems the duties apply from 2 December 2027.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Not read:** arts 9-13 and 15 (the other Section 2 requirements), art. 27 (fundamental rights impact assessment), art. 50 (transparency), art. 72 (post-market monitoring), art. 73 (serious incidents), and Regulation (EU) 2019/1020. Later treatment not checked beyond the Omnibus and the EUR-Lex table.

## Where it bears on the argument

Added in rr-2026-11-k (reader R6b).

**K7, premise P3: supports, as enacted law for high-risk AI systems.** P3 says that a duty to see that something is done includes having the means to learn that it is not being done and to correct it while performance is still possible. For high-risk systems the Regulation writes that content into the word 'oversight': the overseer must be able to "duly monitor" the system "in view of detecting and addressing anomalies, dysfunctions and unexpected performance" (art. 14(4)(a)), to "disregard, override or reverse the output" in "any particular situation" (art. 14(4)(d)) and to stop it (art. 14(4)(e)); the deployer must give the task to persons with "competence, training and authority" (art. 26(2)), monitor operation, and suspend use on reason to think there is a risk (art. 26(5)). Selection of a competent supplier does not discharge these duties. That answers, for this body of law, the item on K7's 'refutes or narrows' list about discharge by choosing a competent delegate.

**K7, conclusion C: narrows.** The conclusion needs a channel from the guest to the person with power over the single case. The Regulation creates that person and no channel. The affected person is told the system is in use (art. 26(11)), may ask for an explanation of "the decision taken" (art. 86(1)) and may complain to an authority (art. 85). The deployer's monitoring runs on the instructions for use and the logs (art. 26(5)-(6)). P4, that the guest's report is often the only timely signal, has no counterpart in the text. The duties are public duties enforced by fines, apply to high-risk systems only, and for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027.

**K9 (mediation): supports the description of what can be missing.** K9 says that after reception the duties persist and what can fail is the channel. The Act is an instance: it secures oversight inside the deploying firm and leaves the person outside it with an explanation after the event.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- For high-risk AI systems, the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act requires that high-risk systems be provided so that the persons assigned to oversee them are enabled, as appropriate and proportionate, to decide, "in any particular situation", not to use it or to "disregard, override or reverse" its output, and to stop it (art. 14(4)(d)-(e)).
- The firm that uses such a system must assign that oversight to natural persons "who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support" (art. 26(2)).
- That firm must monitor the system's operation and, where it has reason to consider that use of the system may result in a risk to health, safety or fundamental rights, inform the provider or distributor and the market surveillance authority and suspend its use (arts 26(5), 79(1)).
- The Act gives a person affected by a decision the right to be told that a high-risk system is in use, to obtain an explanation of the system's role in a decision taken (for Annex III systems other than point 2, and decisions with legal or similarly significant adverse effect; subsidiary to other Union law, art. 86(3)), and to complain to a market surveillance authority (arts 26(11), 86(1), 85).
- Breach of a deployer's duties under art. 26 is punishable by administrative fines of up to EUR 15 000 000 or 3 per cent of worldwide annual turnover (art. 99(4)(e)).
- For systems listed in Annex III the duties in arts 14 and 26 apply from 2 December 2027 (arts 85-86 sit in Chapter IX, which applies from 2 August 2026) (Art. 113 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, point (40)(b)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the stop-button requirement is "owed to overseers". The Act states what overseers must be enabled to do and does not say to whom the requirement is owed; it is enforced by public authorities (arts 14(4), 85, 99).
- That the Act gives a guest, or any affected person, a way to reach the overseer, to have a decision reviewed or to have a system stopped. No article does. Art. 27(1)(f) and recital 96 require some deployers, in their fundamental-rights impact assessment, to set out 'complaint mechanisms', without linking them to the overseer.
- That the Act requires a human to approve each automated decision before it takes effect. Only art. 14(5) requires prior human verification, and only for remote biometric identification.
- That these duties bind hotels for guest-facing systems. They bind providers and deployers of high-risk systems, and no Annex III area names hospitality (this card, Key facts, Annex III).
- That the duties are in force now. Chapter III, Sections 2 and 3 apply to Annex III systems from 2 December 2027.
- That the deployer must act on a complaint from the person affected. The duty to monitor is "on the basis of the instructions for use" (art. 26(5)).

**How this bears on Step 6**

- **T17 holds on the text, with one correction.** A face-matching check-in kiosk or room-entry system whose sole purpose is to confirm that the guest is who she claims to be, or to give her access to the service or the premises, is excluded from Annex III point 1(a) (Annex III 1(a); recital 54). The 'minor impact' ground T17 cites is in recital 17, not recital 54.
- **What the paper may say (added).** The AI Act excludes from its high-risk biometric category systems used for biometric verification whose sole purpose is to confirm a person's identity, including "for the sole purpose of having access to a service, unlocking a device or having secure access to premises" (Annex III point 1(a); recital 54), on the ground that such systems "are likely to have a minor impact on fundamental rights" (recital 17).
