## Summary

Rymer kept the Sea House Hotel at Worthing. Under the same roof and licence, with its own door to the street, was a refreshment bar called the Carlton. A local householder who lived within twelve hundred yards had been in the habit of bringing large dogs into the bar; after an exchange of letters in which he asserted a right to do so, he came in from a walk with one dog and was refused a drink. Rymer was indicted as an innkeeper for refusing him refreshment, and a jury at quarter sessions convicted. On a case stated, five judges quashed the conviction on three grounds: the bar was not an inn; the prosecutor was not a traveller; and the innkeeper had reasonable cause to refuse. Kelly CB said that in such a place "no one has a right to insist on being served any more than in any other shop", and Denman J that the object of the law is "merely to secure that travellers shall not, while upon their journeys, be deprived of necessary food and lodging".

- Who proceeded against whom: a criminal prosecution. The defendant was tried "for that he, being a licensed innkeeper, refused to supply the prosecutor with refreshments" (p. 136). [full-text]
- The charge as the court read it: "The indictment charges the defendant that, being an inn-keeper, and under the obligation to receive the prosecutor in his inn, he refused to do so without having any reasonable cause or excuse for such refusal." (p. 139). [full-text]
- The trial direction, which the court did not adopt for a bar or a non-traveller: the jury were told that the defendant "could not select his customers, or reject any from caprice or dislike" (p. 139). [full-text]
- The three questions: "first, whether the place in which the prosecutor claimed to be received and served was an inn; secondly, whether the prosecutor was a traveller; and, thirdly, assuming both these questions answered in the affirmative, whether the defendant’s refusal was without reasonable cause" (p. 139). [full-text]
- A hotel and its bar are different places in law: "The defendant was the proprietor of an hotel, and if the prosecutor had been refused accommodation in the hotel the case might have been different. But he was not." (p. 139). [full-text]
- First ground: "Such a place is not an inn within the meaning of the common-law rule. An inn is a place “instituted for passengers and wayfaring men:” Calye’s Case. A tavern is not within the definition. In such a place as this no one has a right to insist on being served any more than in any other shop." (p. 140). [full-text]
- Second ground: "it is essential to such a prosecution that the prosecutor should be a traveller"; "Here the prosecutor was not a traveller in any sense whatever." (p. 140). [full-text]
- Third ground: "even if both the foregoing questions had been answered otherwise, I think it clear that the defendant had reasonable cause for refusing to receive the prosecutor" (p. 140). [full-text]
- The purpose of the rule: "the object of the law upon the subject of an innkeeper’s liability, is merely to secure that travellers shall not, while upon their journeys, be deprived of necessary food and lodging" (Denman J, pp. 140-141). [full-text]
- A dictum on the guest's entitlements inside an inn: "a guest cannot, under any circumstances, insist on bringing a dog into any room or place in an inn where other guests are" (Manisty J, p. 141); Kelly CB left open whether a guest could ever "have a right to bring a dog into an inn" (p. 140). [full-text]
- Headnote: "Held, that he could not be convicted: first, because the refreshment bar was not an inn; secondly, because the prosecutor was not a traveller; thirdly, because, had it been otherwise, the defendant had reasonable ground for his refusal." (p. 136). [full-text]
- Disposition: "Conviction quashed." (p. 141). The prosecution failed. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Holding and dicta.** Three grounds. Kelly CB says of the first that "on this ground, even if it stood alone, this conviction must be quashed" (p. 140); the second and third are given as further reasons, the third on the assumption that the first two were wrong. The remarks about dogs in inns (pp. 140-141) are dicta. No counsel appeared (p. 139), so the points were not argued.
- **A criminal case.** It shows the public route of enforcement: indictment at quarter sessions for refusing a traveller. It does not decide whether the traveller could also sue. The judges nonetheless speak of what a person "has a right to insist on" (p. 140), which is the language of an entitlement in the person refused.
- **The definition of an inn is quoted from *Calye's Case*.** The library's card for that case has the same phrase at fol. 32b (calye1584case).
- **Date.** The margin on p. 136 gives 20 January and the text on p. 139 gives 25 January. I cannot tell from the report which is the date of judgment.
- **OCR.** The saved text misreads the judges' names ("Kruy, C.B.", "Frexp, J., and Huppisston, B."), the recognizance ("500." for "50l."), a case name ("Burgess vy. Olements") and a date ("8rd of March"). Names and quotations above follow the page images. Line-break hyphens are mended and footnote markers dropped.
- **Later treatment** not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (admission tier): supports the boundary and narrows any wider reading.** The duty to receive attaches to an inn and to travellers. A bar is "a mere shop" (p. 139) even when a hotel proprietor keeps it under the hotel's roof and licence, and there "no one has a right to insist on being served" (p. 140). The duty also yields to reasonable cause (p. 140). For the paper's assembled hosts, the point is that the duty follows the establishment and its function, not the proprietor or the building.
- **K5 (directedness): narrows.** The duty to receive was enforced here by the Crown on indictment. That is the public route K5's defeater points to. The report does not deny a private action, and its language of right (p. 140) is consistent with one; the private action is shown by constantine1944imperial.

**What the paper may say**

- In 1877 an innkeeper could be indicted for refusing a traveller without reasonable cause (pp. 136, 139).
- The court confined the duty to inns and travellers: a refreshment bar attached to a hotel "is not an inn", and there "no one has a right to insist on being served any more than in any other shop" (p. 140).
- The stated object of the rule was "merely to secure that travellers shall not, while upon their journeys, be deprived of necessary food and lodging" (pp. 140-141).
- An innkeeper may refuse for reasonable cause (p. 140).

**What the paper may not say**

- That bars, taverns or restaurants were under a common-law duty to serve all comers. The case holds the opposite for a refreshment bar.
- That the innkeeper's duty was enforced only by indictment, or only by private action. This case shows the first and is silent on the second.
- That the conviction stood. It was quashed.
- That the court was unanimous after argument. No counsel appeared.
