## Summary

Earl D. Thomas, a Black man, sued The Pick Hotels Corporation and others for damages after a hotel that had confirmed a reservation in writing and by telephone refused him and his wife "solely because of their race" when they arrived (665). The trial court dismissed the action as barred by Kansas's two-year limitation period for torts. The Tenth Circuit reversed. It held that a claim on the innkeeper's common-law duty sounds in tort and was out of time, that the Kansas Civil Rights Statute added no new right because it was "merely declaratory of the common law" (666), and that the complaint also stated a claim on an express contract, which had a three-year period and could go forward.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- **Court, date, panel.** United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, No. 5079, decided 14 July 1955; 224 F.2d 664. "Before BRATTON and MURRAH, Circuit Judges, and WALLACE, District Judge." (665). Murrah wrote; the report shows no dissent or concurrence. [full-text]
- **Jurisdiction and law applied.** A federal court sitting in diversity, applying Kansas law: the Kansas statute of limitations, G.S. 1949, 60-306, and the Kansas Civil Rights Statute, G.S. 1949, 21-2424 (665–666). [full-text]
- **Parties and claim.** "Earl D. Thomas, a Negro, sued The Pick Hotels Corporation and others for damages resulting from a denial of hotel accommodations." (665) [full-text]
- **Facts, from the pleadings.** "the defendant hotel agreed first in writing and later by telephone to provide hotel accommodations on a specified date; that when the appellant and his wife presented themselves to the hotel on the reservation date they were refused accommodations solely because of their race" (665). No evidence was heard; the case was decided on a motion to dismiss. [full-text]
- **The three theories pleaded.** Contract, "the common law duty of an innkeeper to accommodate all members of the general public without discrimination", and the Kansas Civil Rights Statute (665). [full-text]
- **The basis of the innkeeper's duty, in the court's words.** "Contracts for hotel accommodations are usually treated as the means for the inducement or establishment of the common law innkeeper-guest relationship, which when established creates a common law relational duty on the part of the innkeeper, the breach of which is redressible in tort." (665–666; the page turns after "on the part") [full-text]
- **Holding 1: the common-law claim is a tort claim and is out of time.** "Treating the claim then as one arising out of the common law duty of an innkeeper to provide accommodations without discrimination, the case clearly sounds in tort, does not arise out of a contract, and is therefore clearly barred by the two-year statute of limitations." (666) [full-text]
- **Holding 2: the statute gives the injured person damages and adds nothing to the common law.** "The Kansas Civil Rights Statute in substance forbids hotel owners or innkeepers from making any distinction on account of race or color and provides that the offending person shall be liable in damages to the person or persons injured thereby." "But the statute is merely declaratory of the common law." (666) [full-text]
- **The duty to receive survives.** "While the common law duty of an innkeeper to provide accommodations may have been dormant or stale in Kansas, it has never been abrogated and undoubtedly existed prior to the adoption of the statute. Indeed the complainant invokes it and relies upon it here." (666) [full-text]
- **Holding 3: the contract claim stands.** "But there is nothing in the common law or the statute to preclude the parties from entering into a valid and enforceable contract for hotel accommodations." "Indeed the statute and the common law sanction the contract by forbidding the innkeeper from making any distinction on account of race or color." (666). "We conclude that the complaint states a claim on an express contract to provide hotel accommodations and a breach of that contract." (666) [full-text]
- **Did the claim succeed?** In part, and only at the pleading stage. The tort and statutory claims failed as time-barred. The contract claim survived dismissal: "The judgment is accordingly reversed." (666). The opinion decides no liability and awards nothing. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A limitation ruling.** The court had to classify the claim to pick the limitation period. Its statements that the innkeeper's duty exists, is relational and is "redressible in tort" are steps in that classification. They were not tested against a defence on the merits.
- **The plaintiff had a reservation.** He was not a traveller arriving unannounced. The court nonetheless treats the refusal as a breach of "the common law duty of an innkeeper to provide accommodations" (666), which is the duty to receive.
- **Relation and contract are kept apart.** On the court's account the contract brings the relationship about and the common law then attaches the duty to the relationship. That is why the same refusal could be pleaded in tort and in contract.
- **"Dormant or stale".** The court's own phrase for the state of the duty in Kansas in 1955 (666). It is evidence that the duty was little used, from a court that still held it to be law.
- **Whose hotel.** The defendant is a hotels corporation sued with "others". The opinion does not say who owned or ran the hotel, and treats "the defendant hotel" and "innkeeper" as one. It gives nothing on the holder among several firms.
- **The authorities are the court's.** Williston, Corpus Juris Secundum, American Jurisprudence, the first Restatement of Torts § 866 and the cases cited at 666 were not read.
- **Later treatment not checked.** The outcome on remand, later Kansas law, and the effect of federal public-accommodations law after 1955 were not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K5 (directedness): supports, thinly.** A refused guest sues the hotel in his own name on the duty to receive. The court accepts that the breach is "redressible in tort" and that the statute makes the offender "liable in damages to the person or persons injured thereby" (666). The remedy on the tort side was lost to time, and on the contract side was only allowed to be tried.
- **K4 (admission tier): supports for one US state in 1955.** The duty "to provide accommodations" is described as common law that "has never been abrogated" (666). The words "to accommodate all members of the general public" are the plaintiff's pleading as the court summarises it (665).
- **K3 (grounds): supports the role as what the duty attaches to.** The duty is "relational" and arises from the innkeeper–guest relationship, which a contract merely induces or establishes (665–666). The opinion names no further ground: nothing on reliance, dependence or holding out.

**What the paper may say** (naming the court, the year and Kansas law):
- In 1955 the Tenth Circuit, applying Kansas law, described the innkeeper's duty to provide accommodation as a common-law duty that "has never been abrogated", though it "may have been dormant or stale" (666).
- The court treated the innkeeper–guest relationship as creating "a common law relational duty on the part of the innkeeper, the breach of which is redressible in tort", with the contract as the means of establishing the relationship (665–666).
- A guest refused on arrival despite a reservation could sue both on that duty and on the contract; the tort claim was out of time and the contract claim was allowed to proceed (666).

**What the paper may not say:**
- That the refused guest won, or recovered damages.
- That the court applied the duty to receive to a walk-in traveller.
- That this is the present law of Kansas or of the United States.
- That the court said in its own voice that the innkeeper must "accommodate all members of the general public"; that phrase summarises the pleading (665).
- That the duty is described as owed to the public and not to the guest: the court's phrase is a "relational duty", and the statute as it describes it gives damages "to the person or persons injured".
