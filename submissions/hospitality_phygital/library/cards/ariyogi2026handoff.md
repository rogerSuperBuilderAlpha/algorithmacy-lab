## Summary

A structured evidence review, published in volume 3 of an Indonesian open-access journal, that maps 19 empirical reports (2019 to July 2026) on AI identity disclosure, human escalation, trust and responsibility attribution in automated hospitality, and builds a conceptual 'executable transparency framework' on them. The framework has four elements: identity and capability information, an accessible route to human help, continuity of information across the transfer, and 'an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery' (p. 84). Five propositions (pp. 84-85) and a table of suggested measures (p. 86) turn it into a research agenda. The authors assign 'recovery ownership at organizational level' (p. 79), separate it from blame placed on a robot or on the employee who receives the complaint (pp. 79, 82), and tell managers to 'identify who can authorize corrective action' (p. 86). The grounds they give are the guest's evaluation of the branded organization (p. 78), attribution findings (p. 82) and recovery effectiveness. The review calls itself an 'access-limited synthesis' (p. 77): 15 of the 19 mapped reports were coded from abstracts or summaries (Table 1, p. 83), and no independent screening was done (p. 80).

**Route to help, handoff and authority**

- Handoff defined: "Handoff means that responsibility for handling a request moves from one agent to another. An effective handoff therefore requires more than the presence of a staff member: someone must receive the problem, obtain enough information, and be able to act." (p. 79). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **No several-firms question.** The article's contrasts are robot versus firm (p. 82) and organization versus the employee who takes the complaint (p. 79). Other firms enter once, as suppliers of "technical components" in a sentence about how the guest evaluates the brand (p. 78). 'Organization', 'firm', 'hotel' and 'branded service organization' are used for one party throughout. Nothing settles which of brand, operator, platform or vendor owns recovery when they differ. (My reading.)
- **The ground is evaluation and effectiveness, not role.** Ownership is justified by what the guest evaluates (p. 78), by where customers place blame (p. 82) and by what makes recovery work; the authors deny that it settles liability (pp. 79, 82, 85). No passage derives ownership from a role, from holding out, from invited reliance or from control of the guest's space. (My reading.)
- **'Should' as design, not claim.** The guest 'should have an intelligible route to help' (p. 82) and managers 'should' identify who can authorize correction (p. 86), but every such feature is offered as a proposed process feature or a testable proposition (pp. 78, 84-86), measured partly through the guest's 'organizational assurance'. The only obligation named is the organization's 'obligation to respond' inside its definition of accountability (p. 79); it says neither to whom the obligation runs nor why. (My reading.)
- **Close wording to the manuscript's third duty.** The handoff definition, "someone must receive the problem, obtain enough information, and be able to act" (p. 79), is very near the manuscript's "reaches someone able to correct it". A reviewer who knows the article will see the overlap. The manuscript's difference lies in the ground (a duty of the host's role, owed to the guest during the stay), the binding party, and timeliness, which the article declines to fix (p. 82).
- **Method and venue.** The text contradicts itself on AI use (p. 80 against p. 87). Fifteen of nineteen mapped reports were coded from abstracts (p. 83), and the authors write that completing the search and appraisal "remains a prerequisite to presenting this manuscript as a comprehensive, publication-ready SLR" (p. 87). The article was received on 1 September and accepted on 13 October 2026 (p. 77). Five of its references are to the same journal (pp. 88-90), and its DOI is unregistered (source_basis). None of this makes its propositions wrong, but a referee may question leaning on it. Cite it for what it proposes, not for evidence.
- **Shared sources with the library.** It cites Sharma and Mattila (2026) (held here as `sharmamattila2025rights`, cited) and Huang and Lo (2025) (`huanglo2025failure`, cited), and leans on Leo and Huh (2020), which is not in the bibliography (references, pp. 88-90).

## Where it bears on the argument

**The v8 gap statement.** It occupies part of the claimed gap. It names a bearer of recovery (the organization), separates that bearer from the employee and the robot, and prescribes a route to someone with authority to correct. It does not derive any of this from the role of host, bind it to one firm among several, or state it as owed to the guest. See manuscript/v8_2026-11/stage2/READ_gapworks.md. (My reading.)

**K7, a channel from the guest.** This is the closest published statement of the channel: an accessible route, information continuity and "an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery" (p. 84). The manuscript cannot claim the route as new. It can claim the ground (a duty of the host's role during the stay), the party bound, and the timeliness requirement. (My reading.)

**K8, holder under delegation.** Ownership stays "at organizational level" while corrective work goes to employees (p. 79). That is a two-level split inside one firm, not an allocation among firms. (My reading.)

**`institutional-not-trainable`, `human-contact-conditional`.** Authority, not presence, decides recovery: a concierge "who receives an escalated complaint but cannot amend a booking" cannot complete it (p. 86). Human involvement is "conditional rather than automatically superior" (heading, p. 82), and severity decides when collaboration helps (p. 82).

**What the paper may say** (third person, Intellect Harvard)

- Ariyogi et al. (2026: 79) assign 'recovery ownership at organizational level', separating it from the employee who performs corrective work.
- They define an effective handoff as one in which 'someone must receive the problem, obtain enough information, and be able to act' (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 79).
- They propose that guests have 'an accessible route to human help' and 'an identifiable actor authorized to complete recovery' (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 84), and that managers 'identify who can authorize corrective action' (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 86).
- They rest organizational ownership on how guests evaluate 'the branded service organization ... even when technical components are supplied elsewhere' (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 78) and on attribution findings, and they deny that it settles liability (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 79, 82, 85).
- They present these features as a research agenda and testable propositions, 'not a validated intervention' (Ariyogi et al. 2026: 78).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the article allocates responsibility among several firms, or chooses the brand over a vendor, platform or operator. It never poses that question.
- That it states a duty owed to the guest, or grounds anything in the role of host, holding out, reliance or control of space.
- That it requires the route to be timely. It declines to set any waiting time (p. 82).
- That it is a systematic review or reports pooled evidence. It calls itself an access-limited, bounded synthesis (pp. 77, 87).
- That its framework is tested. It "has not been tested as an integrated model" (abstract, p. 77).
