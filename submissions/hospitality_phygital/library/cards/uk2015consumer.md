## Summary

The Consumer Rights Act 2015 treats every contract by which a trader supplies a service to a consumer as including a term that the trader must perform the service with reasonable care and skill. A trader is a person acting for business purposes "whether acting personally or through another person acting in the trader's name or on the trader's behalf". If the service does not conform, the consumer may require the trader to perform it again, and may have a price reduction only where repeat performance is impossible or is not given within a reasonable time; damages and other remedies remain. A term cannot exclude the trader's liability under the care-and-skill term, and no term or notice can exclude liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence, which includes breach of the common duty of care under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Who is bound: "“Trader” means a person acting for purposes relating to that person's trade, business, craft or profession, whether acting personally or through another person acting in the trader's name or on the trader's behalf." (s. 2(2)) [full-text]
- Who is protected: "“Consumer” means an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside that individual's trade, business, craft or profession." (s. 2(3)) A business traveller is on the face of this definition not a consumer; the trader bears the burden of proving that an individual was not one (s. 2(4)). [full-text]
- Public operators are within it: "“Business” includes the activities of any government department or local or public authority." (s. 2(7)) [full-text]
- Contracts covered: "This Chapter applies to a contract for a trader to supply a service to a consumer." (s. 48(1)) It excludes "a contract of employment or apprenticeship" (s. 48(2)). Such a contract "is referred to in this Part as a “contract to supply a service”" (s. 48(4)). The Act does not define "service" in the sections read. [full-text]
- The care-and-skill term: "Every contract to supply a service is to be treated as including a term that the trader must perform the service with reasonable care and skill." (s. 49(1)) [full-text]
- The remedies when the service does not conform: "the consumer's rights (and the provisions about them and when they are available) are— (a) the right to require repeat performance (see section 55); (b) the right to a price reduction (see section 56)." (s. 54(3)) Conforming means "the service being performed in accordance with section 49" or conforming to a term under s. 50 (s. 54(2)). [full-text]
- Other remedies survive: sections 54 to 56 "do not prevent the consumer seeking other remedies ... instead of or in addition to a remedy referred to there (but not so as to recover twice for the same loss)" (s. 54(6)), and those include "claiming damages", "seeking specific performance" and "exercising a right to treat the contract as at an end" (s. 54(7)(a), (c), (f)). [full-text]
- Repeat performance: "The right to require repeat performance is a right to require the trader to perform the service again, to the extent necessary to complete its performance in conformity with the contract." (s. 55(1)) "If the consumer requires such repeat performance, the trader— (a) must provide it within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience to the consumer; and (b) must bear any necessary costs incurred in doing so (including in particular the cost of any labour or materials)." (s. 55(2)) [full-text]
- The limit of repeat performance: "The consumer cannot require repeat performance if completing performance of the service in conformity with the contract is impossible." (s. 55(3)) What is a reasonable time or significant inconvenience depends on "the nature of the service" and "the purpose for which the service was to be performed" (s. 55(4)). [full-text]
- The order of remedies: "A consumer who has that right and the right to require repeat performance is only entitled to a price reduction in one of these situations— (a) because of section 55(3) the consumer cannot require repeat performance; or (b) the consumer has required repeat performance, but the trader is in breach of the requirement of section 55(2)(a) to do it within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience to the consumer." (s. 56(3)) The reduction "may, where appropriate, be the full amount of the price" (s. 56(2)). [full-text]
- No contracting out of the care-and-skill term: "A term of a contract to supply services is not binding on the consumer to the extent that it would exclude the trader's liability arising under section 49 (service to be performed with reasonable care and skill)." (s. 57(1)) A term restricting that liability is not binding "if it would prevent the consumer in an appropriate case from recovering the price paid" (s. 57(3)). [full-text]
- Nor by drafting the duty away: "The references in subsections (1) to (3) to excluding or restricting a liability also include preventing an obligation or duty arising or limiting its extent." (s. 57(5)) Nor by burdening the remedy: a term is not binding to the extent that it would "make such a right or remedy or its enforcement subject to a restrictive or onerous condition" (s. 57(4)(b)). [full-text]
- Unfair terms generally: "An unfair term of a consumer contract is not binding on the consumer." (s. 62(1)) "A term is unfair if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations under the contract to the detriment of the consumer." (s. 62(4)) [full-text]
- Negligence causing death or injury: "A trader cannot by a term of a consumer contract or by a consumer notice exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence." (s. 65(1)) [full-text]
- What counts as negligence there: breach of "any obligation to take reasonable care or exercise reasonable skill in the performance of a contract where the obligation arises from an express or implied term of the contract", of "a common law duty to take reasonable care or exercise reasonable skill", or of "the common duty of care imposed by the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957" (s. 65(4)(a)-(c)). "It is immaterial ... whether liability for it arises directly or vicariously." (s. 65(5)(b)) [full-text]
- Limits on s. 65: it does not apply to "any contract so far as it relates to the creation or transfer of an interest in land" (s. 66(1)(b)), nor to an occupier's liability to a person given access "for recreational purposes" where allowing that access "is not within the purposes of the occupier's trade, business, craft or profession" (s. 66(4)). [full-text]
- Commencement: s. 49 and ss. 54 to 57 came into force on 1 October 2015 "for specified purposes" and on 1 October 2016 "in so far as not already in force" (commencement notes to those sections; s. 57(2) and (3) were in force from Royal Assent); ss. 62 and 65 on 1 October 2015. [full-text]
- What the sections read do not contain: any statement in terms that the trader is liable for the want of care of an independent sub-contractor; any duty on the trader to provide a contact point, a complaints channel or a person with authority; any reference to hotels, accommodation or innkeepers. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Delegation rests on a definition and on the common law.** Section 49 says "the trader must perform the service with reasonable care and skill". Whether that makes the trader answerable for a sub-contractor's carelessness is not stated in terms. Two things point that way: the definition of a trader as acting "personally or through another person acting in the trader's name or on the trader's behalf" (s. 2(2)), and the common-law rule that a supplier who arranges for others to perform is not absolved (*Wong Mee Wan*, p. 4; see wong1995kwan). I read no case construing s. 2(2) or s. 49 on this point. The paper should cite the Act for the term and the common law for its reach.
- **Whether a stay is a 'service'.** The sections read do not define "service". A hotel's contract with a private guest looks like "a contract for a trader to supply a service to a consumer" (s. 48(1)), but I read no provision or case saying so, and did not check whether an order under s. 48(5) excludes any service. A guest travelling on business is on the face of s. 2(3) outside the Act.
- **Premises.** The Act's care-and-skill term and s. 5 of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 both bear on a contract to enter and use premises. *Maguire* (2006) holds that the term implied by reason of the right of entry is the common duty of care, entrustment defence included (see maguire2006sefton). The 2015 Act does not mention s. 5 in the sections read, and treats a contractual duty of care and the occupier's common duty of care as separate heads of "negligence" (s. 65(4)(a), (c)). How s. 49 applies to the safety of the premises in which a service is supplied is open on the texts I read.
- **Section 57 and 'arranging only'.** Section 57(5) extends the bar to a term "preventing an obligation or duty arising or limiting its extent". A clause saying that the trader answers for no supplier's care would seem to be caught. A contract that defines the trader's service as arranging a booking is different: it describes the service, and the Act then requires care and skill in arranging. Whether s. 57(5) or s. 62 reaches such a definition is not answered by the sections read.
- **Repeat performance is triggered by the consumer.** The trader's duty under s. 55(2) arises "If the consumer requires such repeat performance". The Act presupposes that the consumer can put the requirement to the trader. It lays no duty on the trader to make that possible.
- **Versions.** Section 49 was saved in its enacted form; I compared it with the revised text in sources/cra2015s49/ and the words are the same. The revised pages carry an editorial notice of two insertions elsewhere in the Act (ss. 12(2A), 37(2A)) not yet applied; neither touches the sections read. A verifier should check the live site before quotation.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8, the contract limb: supports for consumers, with the reach supplied by the common law.** The party that contracts as trader must perform with reasonable care and skill (s. 49(1)), whether "acting personally or through another" (s. 2(2)), and cannot exclude that liability (s. 57(1), (5)). For death or personal injury it cannot exclude liability for negligence at all, including the occupier's common duty of care (s. 65(1), (4)). This removes, for consumer contracts in the United Kingdom, the exemption clause that *Wong Mee Wan* left open (p. 11 there). It leaves the 'arranging only' construction open, and it protects consumers only (s. 2(3)).

**K7, the timing boundary: supports.** K7 says interruptibility belongs to the duty to perform and accountability to the duty to repair, and that the boundary follows from whether performance is still possible. The Act draws that line for services: the consumer's first remedy is to require the trader to complete performance in conformity with the contract (s. 55(1)); money comes only if "completing performance ... is impossible" or the trader fails to redo it "within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience" (ss. 55(3), 56(3)). Damages remain alongside (s. 54(6), (7)).

**K7, the conclusion C: does not enact it.** K7 looks for law "that enacts the conclusion", including "duties on traders to be reachable". These ten sections contain none. The right to repeat performance assumes a consumer who can reach the trader and gives no remedy for being unable to, beyond the fallback to a price reduction when the trader does not perform again in time (s. 56(3)(b)).

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- United Kingdom statute treats every contract by which a trader supplies a service to a consumer as including a term that the trader must perform it with reasonable care and skill (ss. 48(1), 49(1)).
- The Act defines a trader as acting "personally or through another person acting in the trader's name or on the trader's behalf" (s. 2(2)).
- A term excluding the trader's liability under that term does not bind the consumer, and the bar extends to terms "preventing an obligation or duty arising or limiting its extent" (s. 57(1), (5)).
- A trader cannot exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence, which includes breach of the occupier's common duty of care (s. 65(1), (4)(c)).
- Where a service does not conform, the consumer may require repeat performance, and may have a price reduction only if that is impossible or is not provided within a reasonable time (ss. 54(3), 55, 56(3)).

**What it may not say.**
- That the Act says in terms that a trader answers for its sub-contractors. It does not; that reading needs s. 2(2) and the common law.
- That the Act gives a consumer a right to reach the trader or obliges the trader to provide a contact point. The sections read contain no such provision.
- That the Act covers every guest. It covers consumers (s. 2(3)).
- That the Act overrides s. 5 of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 for dangers on premises. The sections read do not address the point.
