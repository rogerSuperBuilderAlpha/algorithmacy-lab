## Summary

A conceptual article, informed by the authors' ELSA work inside a German police-intelligence AI
project (p. 4), that argues contestability research is too system-centred, lifecycle-bound and
designer-oriented. It proposes a non-exhaustive typology of contestation practices in developing
teams, deploying organizations (governing deployed systems, a culture for contestation, rejecting
AI) and external oversight, and reads them against the EU AI Act. It distinguishes
institutionalized from situative, bottom-up ("parrhesiastic") contestation.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Starting point, citing others: "the combination of legal obligations and technical transparency measures may not be sufficient to enable contestability of decisions" (p. 2) [full-text]
- Practice requires competence: "For a practice of contestation to exist, participants would have to be aware of the approaches, procedures and tools by which AI can and should be challenged. They must be competent to contest" (p. 3) [full-text]
- Organizational capability: "Guidelines and frameworks for AI contestability-by-design are meaningless if the actors in the field do not have the necessary capabilities to understand, organize and enact contestability in practice" (p. 9) [full-text]
- Contestation culture "aims at improving the structural capabilities of stakeholders to challenge AI systems" (pp. 8-9) [full-text]
- External oversight "arises explicitly from the detachment from the system and its principal actors", via "second-degree oversight mechanisms such as audits, reviews, and appeals" (p. 10) [full-text]
- Designers versus affected communities: argued, not observed. A designer "might not consider the option to discard the system development"; for affected communities termination is "perfectly conceivable" (p. 11) [full-text]
- Institutionalized versus situative critique, after Weiskopf and Tobias-Miersch: existing conceptualizations "focus mainly on institutionalized procedures" and "may ... marginalize the often informal, parrhesiastic character of contestation practices" (p. 12) [full-text]
- Internal bodies can help: Meta's Oversight Board illustrates "the potential for layered internal oversight systems to strengthen accountability" (p. 8) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- (rr-2026-11) Correction: the abstract-era fact "Designers and affected communities are found to want different things" is wrong in kind. The paper offers no data on what either group wants; the contrast is an argument from Boltanski and Thévenot's orders of justification (p. 11).
- (rr-2026-11) The article sits awkwardly with the competence/standing split. It makes competence a condition of contestation practice (p. 3) and asks for capability-building in organizations (p. 9). Its "capabilities" are structural and organizational, which fits the manuscript's institutional reading, but it does not say skill is irrelevant.
- It does support the move from interface to institution: practices "outside the frame of individual systems, life cycles and fully formalized procedures" (p. 8) and external oversight (p. 10).
- Conceptual and law-oriented (EU AI Act, police use); no hospitality content.
