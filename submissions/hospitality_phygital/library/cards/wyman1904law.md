## Summary

Argues that the common law split businesses into private callings, free to refuse, and public callings bound to serve all on reasonable terms, and that the line followed economic situation: virtual competition against virtual monopoly. The innkeeper is the leading example, because the benighted traveller has nowhere else to go.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- In public businesses "one must serve all that apply without exclusive conditions, provide adequate facilities to meet all the demands of the consumer, exact only reasonable charges for the services that are rendered, and between customers under similar circumstances make no discriminations." (p. 156) [full-text]
- "When the weary traveller reaches the wayside inn in the gathering dusk, if the host turn him away what [shall] he do?" (p. 159; OCR "slhall") [full-text]
- "the traveller is so in the hands of the innkeeper that only an affirmative law can protect him." (p. 159) [full-text]
- The shop is different: "There are shops in plenty and he has time to [choose]." (p. 159; OCR "clhoose") [full-text]
- Thesis: "in the private calling the situation is that of virtual competition, while in the public calling the situation is that of virtual monopoly." (p. 161) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Wyman wrote to use the public-callings law against the trusts; bogen1996innkeeper (p. 52, p. 89 n. 192) argues the monopoly story was constructed for that purpose and that "innkeeping was not a monopoly".
- The dependence intuition (p. 159) is the same as Position A's and as sandovalstrausz2005travelers's traveller vulnerability, without the monopoly claim.
