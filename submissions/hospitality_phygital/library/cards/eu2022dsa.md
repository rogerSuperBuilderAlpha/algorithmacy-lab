## Summary

The Regulation does two things that matter here. Chapter II restates the conditional exemptions from liability that Directive 2000/31 gave to intermediaries: a hosting provider is not liable for information stored at a user's request unless it knew of the illegality and did not act (art. 6(1)). Two exceptions take the exemption away: where the user acts under the provider's authority or control (art. 6(2)), and, for the consumer-law liability of platforms on which consumers conclude distance contracts with traders, where the platform presents the item or enables the transaction in a way that would lead an average consumer to believe the platform itself, or someone under its authority or control, is providing the information, product or service (art. 6(3)). The exemptions say only when a provider cannot be held liable; they are no basis for holding one liable (recital 17). Chapter III lays duties of diligence on providers, kept separate from liability (recital 41). Every provider of an intermediary service must designate a single point of contact through which recipients of the service can communicate with it directly and rapidly, by means that do not rely solely on automated tools (art. 12). Online platforms, other than micro and small enterprises, must run an internal complaint system against their decisions about users' content and accounts, open for six months after the decision, with decisions taken under the supervision of qualified staff and not solely by automated means (art. 20), and must take part in non-binding out-of-court dispute settlement before certified bodies (art. 21). The Regulation applies from 17 February 2024 (art. 93(2)). It regulates the intermediary service and expressly not the accommodation, transport or other service that is intermediated (recital 6; art. 2(2)).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Scope: the intermediary service only**

- Art. 2(2): "This Regulation shall not apply to any service that is not an intermediary service or to any requirements imposed in respect of such a service, irrespective of whether the service is provided through the use of an intermediary service." [full-text]
- Recital 6 names accommodation: "certain providers of intermediary services intermediate in relation to services that may or may not be provided by electronic means, such as remote information technology services, transport, accommodation or delivery services. This Regulation should apply only to intermediary services and not affect requirements set out in Union or national law relating to products or services intermediated through intermediary services, including in situations where the intermediary service constitutes an integral part of another service which is not an intermediary service as recognised in the case-law of the Court of Justice of the European Union." [full-text]
- Art. 2(4)(e) and (f): the Regulation is "without prejudice to" Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 and to "Union law on consumer protection and product safety". [full-text]
- Who is protected. Art. 3(b): "‘recipient of the service’ means any natural or legal person who uses an intermediary service, in particular for the purposes of seeking information or making it accessible". Art. 3(c): "‘consumer’ means any natural person who is acting for purposes which are outside his or her trade, business, craft, or profession". Recital 2: "Business users, consumers and other users are considered to be ‘recipients of the service’ for the purpose of this Regulation." [full-text]
- Who is bound. Art. 3(g)(iii): a hosting service consists "of the storage of information provided by, and at the request of, a recipient of the service". Art. 3(i): "‘online platform’ means a hosting service that, at the request of a recipient of the service, stores and disseminates information to the public", unless that is a minor and purely ancillary feature of another service. [full-text]
- Art. 3(f): "‘trader’ means any natural person, or any legal person irrespective of whether it is privately or publicly owned, who is acting, including through any person acting in his or her name or on his or her behalf, for purposes relating to his or her trade, business, craft or profession". [full-text]

**Art. 6: the hosting exemption and how it is lost**

- Art. 6(1): "Where an information society service is provided that consists of the storage of information provided by a recipient of the service, the service provider shall not be liable for the information stored at the request of a recipient of the service, on condition that the provider: (a) does not have actual knowledge of illegal activity or illegal content and, as regards claims for damages, is not aware of facts or circumstances from which the illegal activity or illegal content is apparent; or (b) upon obtaining such knowledge or awareness, acts expeditiously to remove or to disable access to the illegal content." [full-text]
- Art. 6(2): "Paragraph 1 shall not apply where the recipient of the service is acting under the authority or the control of the provider." [full-text]
- Art. 6(3): "Paragraph 1 shall not apply with respect to the liability under consumer protection law of online platforms that allow consumers to conclude distance contracts with traders, where such an online platform presents the specific item of information or otherwise enables the specific transaction at issue in a way that would lead an average consumer to believe that the information, or the product or service that is the object of the transaction, is provided either by the online platform itself or by a recipient of the service who is acting under its authority or control." [full-text]
- Art. 6(4): the article "shall not affect the possibility for a judicial or administrative authority, in accordance with a Member State's legal system, to require the service provider to terminate or prevent an infringement". [full-text]
- Recital 17, what the exemptions are not: "The rules on liability of providers of intermediary services set out in this Regulation should only establish when the provider of intermediary services concerned cannot be held liable in relation to illegal content provided by the recipients of the service. Those rules should not be understood to provide a positive basis for establishing when a provider can be held liable, which is for the applicable rules of Union or national law to determine." [full-text]
- Recital 18, the active role: the exemptions "should not apply where, instead of confining itself to providing the services neutrally by a merely technical and automatic processing of the information provided by the recipient of the service, the provider of intermediary services plays an active role of such a kind as to give it knowledge of, or control over, that information". [full-text]
- Recital 23, on art. 6(2), with price as the example: "The exemption of liability should not apply where the recipient of the service is acting under the authority or the control of the provider of a hosting service. For example, where the provider of an online platform that allows consumers to conclude distance contracts with traders determines the price of the goods or services offered by the trader, it could be considered that the trader acts under the authority or control of that online platform." [full-text]
- Recital 24, on art. 6(3), the purpose: such platforms "should not be able to benefit from the exemption from liability for hosting service providers established in this Regulation, in so far as those online platforms present the relevant information relating to the transactions at issue in such a way as to lead consumers to believe that that information was provided by those online platforms themselves or by traders acting under their authority or control, and that those online platforms thus have knowledge of or control over the information, even if that may in reality not be the case". [full-text]
- Recital 24, the examples: "Examples of such behaviour could be where an online platform fails to display clearly the identity of the trader, as required by this Regulation, where an online platform withholds the identity or contact details of the trader until after the conclusion of the contract concluded between the trader and the consumer, or where an online platform markets the product or service in its own name rather than in the name of the trader who will supply that product or service." [full-text]
- Recital 24, the standard is objective: "it should be determined objectively, on the basis of all relevant circumstances, whether the presentation could lead an average consumer to believe that the information in question was provided by the online platform itself or by traders acting under its authority or control". [full-text]
- Recital 16, where the exemptions come from: the "framework of conditional exemptions from liability for providers of intermediary services, laid down in Directive 2000/31/EC" is to "be preserved" and "incorporated in this Regulation". [full-text]
- Art. 8: "No general obligation to monitor the information which providers of intermediary services transmit or store, nor actively to seek facts or circumstances indicating illegal activity shall be imposed on those providers." Recital 30 adds that nothing in the Regulation is "a general obligation for providers to take proactive measures in relation to illegal content". [full-text]

**Art. 12: a point of contact for recipients**

- Art. 12(1), whole: "Providers of intermediary services shall designate a single point of contact to enable recipients of the service to communicate directly and rapidly with them, by electronic means and in a user-friendly manner, including by allowing recipients of the service to choose the means of communication, which shall not solely rely on automated tools." [full-text]
- Art. 12(2), whole: "In addition to the obligations provided under Directive 2000/31/EC, providers of intermediary services shall make public the information necessary for the recipients of the service in order to easily identify and communicate with their single points of contact. That information shall be easily accessible, and shall be kept up to date." [full-text]
- Art. 12 sits in Chapter III, Section 1, headed "Provisions applicable to all providers of intermediary services". Section 1 has no exclusion for small firms other than art. 15(2), which exempts micro and small enterprises from the transparency report only. [full-text]
- Recital 43, whole: "Providers of intermediary services should also be required to designate a single point of contact for recipients of services, enabling rapid, direct and efficient communication in particular by easily accessible means such as telephone numbers, email addresses, electronic contact forms, chatbots or instant messaging. It should be explicitly indicated when a recipient of the service communicates with chatbots. Providers of intermediary services should allow recipients of services to choose means of direct and efficient communication which do not solely rely on automated tools. Providers of intermediary services should make all reasonable efforts to guarantee that sufficient human and financial resources are allocated to ensure that this communication is performed in a timely and efficient manner." [full-text]
- The neighbouring contact point for authorities (art. 11) is explained in recital 42 as serving "two-way communications ... relating to matters covered by this Regulation" and "operational purposes". Recital 43 states no such limit of subject for art. 12, and art. 12 itself states none. [full-text]

**Art. 20: internal complaint handling**

- Who and for whom. Art. 20(1): "Providers of online platforms shall provide recipients of the service, including individuals or entities that have submitted a notice, for a period of at least six months following the decision referred to in this paragraph, with access to an effective internal complaint-handling system that enables them to lodge complaints, electronically and free of charge, against the decision taken by the provider of the online platform upon the receipt of a notice or against the following decisions taken by the provider of the online platform on the grounds that the information provided by the recipients constitutes illegal content or is incompatible with its terms and conditions". [full-text]
- The four kinds of decision, art. 20(1)(a)-(d): "decisions whether or not to remove or disable access to or restrict visibility of the information"; "decisions whether or not to suspend or terminate the provision of the service, in whole or in part, to the recipients"; "decisions whether or not to suspend or terminate the recipients’ account"; "decisions whether or not to suspend, terminate or otherwise restrict the ability to monetise information provided by the recipients". [full-text]
- Art. 20(2): the six months "shall start on the day on which the recipient of the service is informed about the decision in accordance with Article 16(5) or Article 17". [full-text]
- Art. 20(3): the systems must be "easy to access, user-friendly and enable and facilitate the submission of sufficiently precise and adequately substantiated complaints". [full-text]
- Art. 20(4): "Providers of online platforms shall handle complaints submitted through their internal complaint-handling system in a timely, non-discriminatory, diligent and non-arbitrary manner." Where the complaint shows the decision was unfounded, or "contains information indicating that the complainant’s conduct does not warrant the measure taken, it shall reverse its decision referred to in paragraph 1 without undue delay". [full-text]
- Art. 20(5): "Providers of online platforms shall inform complainants without undue delay of their reasoned decision in respect of the information to which the complaint relates and of the possibility of out-of-court dispute settlement provided for in Article 21 and other available possibilities for redress." [full-text]
- Art. 20(6), whole: "Providers of online platforms shall ensure that the decisions, referred to in paragraph 5, are taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means." [full-text]
- Recital 58: the systems must "lead to swift, non-discriminatory, non-arbitrary and fair outcomes, and are subject to human review where automated means are used", and "should not set formal requirements, such as referral to specific, relevant legal provisions or elaborate legal explanations". [full-text]
- Art. 19(1): Section 3, which holds arts 20 and 21, "shall not apply to providers of online platforms that qualify as micro or small enterprises as defined in Recommendation 2003/361/EC", unless they are very large online platforms (art. 19(2)). [full-text]
- Art. 17(1) supplies the decision to complain of: hosting providers must give "a clear and specific statement of reasons to any affected recipients of the service" for restrictions "imposed on the ground that the information provided by the recipient of the service is illegal content or incompatible with their terms and conditions", and art. 17(2) fixes the time: "at the latest from the date that the restriction is imposed". Art. 17(3)(c) requires "information on the use made of automated means in taking the decision". [full-text]

**Art. 21: out-of-court dispute settlement**

- Art. 21(1): recipients "addressed by the decisions referred to in Article 20(1) shall be entitled to select any out-of-court dispute settlement body that has been certified in accordance with paragraph 3 of this Article in order to resolve disputes relating to those decisions, including complaints that have not been resolved by means of the internal complaint-handling system". This is "without prejudice to the right of the recipient of the service concerned to initiate, at any stage, proceedings to contest those decisions by the providers of online platforms before a court". [full-text]
- Art. 21(2): "Both parties shall engage, in good faith, with the selected certified out-of-court dispute settlement body with a view to resolving the dispute." and "The certified out-of-court dispute settlement body shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement of the dispute on the parties." [full-text]
- Art. 21(4): the bodies decide "within a reasonable period of time and no later than 90 calendar days after the receipt of the complaint", extendable to "a maximum total duration of 180 days". [full-text]
- Art. 21(5): if the body decides for the recipient, "the provider of the online platform shall bear all the fees charged by the out-of-court dispute settlement body"; "For recipients of the service, the dispute settlement shall be available free of charge or at a nominal fee." [full-text]
- Recital 59: "Recipients of the service should be able to choose between the internal complaint mechanism, an out-of-court dispute settlement and the possibility to initiate, at any stage, judicial proceedings." [full-text]

**Who the trader is: the disclosure duties that recital 24 points to**

- Art. 30(1): platforms allowing consumers to conclude distance contracts with traders must obtain, before a trader uses them, "the name, address, telephone number and email address of the trader" and the other items listed. Art. 30(7): the platform "shall make the information referred to in paragraph 1, points (a), (d) and (e) available on its online platform to the recipients of the service in a clear, easily accessible and comprehensible manner". [full-text]
- Recital 73, no guarantee: providers "which have made the best efforts required by this Regulation" are not to "be understood as guaranteeing the reliability of the information towards consumer or other interested parties". [full-text]
- Art. 29(1): Section 4, which holds art. 30, does not apply to such platforms "that qualify as micro or small enterprises". [full-text]

**Remedies and dates**

- Art. 54, whole: "Recipients of the service shall have the right to seek, in accordance with Union and national law, compensation from providers of intermediary services, in respect of any damage or loss suffered due to an infringement by those providers of their obligations under this Regulation." Recital 121 adds that this is "Without prejudice to the provisions on the exemption from liability". [full-text]
- Art. 53: recipients "shall have the right to lodge a complaint against providers of intermediary services alleging an infringement of this Regulation with the Digital Services Coordinator of the Member State where the recipient of the service is located or established". [full-text]
- Recital 41: "The due diligence obligations are independent from the question of liability of providers of intermediary services which need therefore to be assessed separately." [full-text]
- Art. 93(2): "This Regulation shall apply from 17 February 2024." [full-text]

**What the provisions read do not contain**

- Arts 6, 12, 20 and 21 and the recitals read nowhere use the words hotel, guest or host in the sense of a provider of lodging; 'accommodation' occurs in recital 6 and in recital 12 ("the illegal offer of accommodation services", an example of illegal content). [full-text]
- Art. 12 sets no time for a reply, no duty to reply, no duty to resolve anything, and no requirement that whoever answers have authority over a decision. The word 'human' is in recital 43, not in the article. [full-text]
- Art. 20 gives no complaint against a platform's decisions on any ground other than a notice, illegal content or incompatibility of "the information provided by the recipients" with the terms and conditions. It gives no complaint against the trader, and none about how the intermediated service was performed. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Art. 6(3) removes a shield and builds nothing.** It says when art. 6(1) "shall not apply". Whether the platform is then liable, to whom and for what, is "for the applicable rules of Union or national law to determine" (recital 17). The paper cannot say that a platform that looks like the provider is liable to the guest; it can say that such a platform cannot rely on the hosting exemption against consumer-law liability.
- **Four conditions confine art. 6(3).** The liability must arise "under consumer protection law". The platform must be one of the "online platforms that allow consumers to conclude distance contracts with traders". The person misled is "an average consumer", so a traveller booking in the course of work is outside the definition in art. 3(c), and so is anyone for whom an employer books. And the counterparty must be a 'trader': a private individual letting a room is not one on the definition in art. 3(f), and the text does not say what happens to a platform that hosts both. A claim in tort for injury during a stay is not obviously "liability under consumer protection law"; the text does not settle it.
- **The exemption is from liability "for the information stored".** Art. 6(1) protects a platform against liability for what the host or hotel posted. It is not, on its words, an immunity for the platform's own conduct. How far a guest's claim about a stay counts as liability for stored information is a question the provisions read do not answer.
- **The test differs from the American one in the way K3 cares about.** Crinkley, Hayman and Mobil require that this plaintiff relied. Art. 6(3) asks what "an average consumer" would be led to believe, "objectively" (recital 24), and applies "even if that may in reality not be the case" that the platform has control. It is holding out without proof of individual reliance.
- **Art. 12 is a duty to offer a channel, with the human in the recital.** The operative words are that the means of communication "shall not solely rely on automated tools". Recital 43 lists chatbots among acceptable means, requires that their use be disclosed, and asks for "all reasonable efforts" to allocate "sufficient human and financial resources". No provision read says a person must answer or that the answer must come from someone who can change an outcome.
- **Art. 12 is owed about the platform's service.** The recipient communicates "with them", the provider. A guest with a problem at a property is not given a channel to the property by art. 12, and the Regulation disclaims any effect on requirements relating to the intermediated service (art. 2(2); recital 6).
- **Art. 20 comes after the decision and covers moderation.** The six months run from the day the recipient is informed (art. 20(2)), and the statement of reasons is due "at the latest from the date that the restriction is imposed" (art. 17(2)). On K7's line this is accountability, with a duty to reverse "without undue delay" (art. 20(4)). The decisions covered are those about information the recipient provided, including suspension of the service or the account on that ground. A booking refused by a risk score is not within the words unless it rests on information the recipient provided being illegal or contrary to the terms.
- **Art. 20(6) requires supervision by staff, not a decision by a person.** "under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means" permits automated handling that qualified staff supervise.
- **No general monitoring.** Art. 8 forbids imposing a general duty to monitor on intermediaries. Anyone extending K7's premise P3 (a duty to see that something is done includes the means to learn that it is not) to a booking platform meets this bar in EU law.
- **Small platforms are out of arts 20, 21 and 30** (arts 19, 29), though not out of art. 12.
- **Amendments not established.** I read the text as published in 2022. See source_basis for the one check made. Art. 91 schedules Commission evaluations, with possible proposals for amendment, for November 2025 and November 2027; I did not look for their outcome.
- Enforcement practice, guidance and case law on these articles were not read. Later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports the holding-out ground in one confined form; refutes any claim that platforms answer by default.** Art. 6(3) is a legislative holding-out rule: a platform that presents a service so that an average consumer would think the platform provides it loses its exemption. The recital's examples are the paper's own cases of an assembled host that hides its members: not showing who the trader is, withholding the trader's identity until after the contract, marketing "in its own name" (recital 24). Art. 6(2) and recital 23 are the control ground, with price-setting as the example, which is the criterion the Court of Justice used in Uber and Airbnb Ireland. Against K8: the default is exemption (art. 6(1)); the exceptions remove the exemption and create no liability (recital 17); they reach consumer-law liability to consumers only; and no general duty to monitor may be imposed (art. 8).

**K7 (channel from the guest): supports the conclusion in form for platforms; narrows it on the person, on timing and on subject.** Art. 12 is a statutory duty on every intermediary to keep a channel that is direct, rapid and not solely automated, owed to every user. Art. 20(6) and recital 58 require human oversight of complaint decisions. These are duties on intermediaries, not on traders; they are the nearest statutory form of the "duties on traders to be reachable" that K7 lists as confirming. The limits: the channel is to the platform about the platform's service; nobody need answer; the complaint system works after the decision; and the complaint must concern content or account decisions. Art. 54 gives damages for breach of these duties, which is repair afterwards.

**K3 (grounds of the duty): input to the table of ground by hard case.** For the *booking platform*: reliance is replaced by the average consumer's belief (art. 6(3)); control is tested by authority or control over the trader (art. 6(2)), with price-setting the example; dependence is not a ground the Regulation uses. For the *placed guest* and the business traveller: outside 'consumer', so outside art. 6(3). For the *system vendor*: the provisions read do not address a supplier of systems to a hotel.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- Under the Digital Services Act a platform that hosts traders' offers is not liable for the information they store with it unless it has actual knowledge of the illegality or, for damages claims, is aware of facts or circumstances from which it is apparent, and fails to act expeditiously (art. 6(1)).
- The exemption does not apply where the user acts under the platform's authority or control (art. 6(2)), and the recitals give a platform that determines the trader's price as an example (recital 23).
- For liability under consumer protection law, a platform on which consumers conclude distance contracts with traders loses the exemption where it presents the offer or enables the transaction in a way that would lead an average consumer to believe that the platform itself, or a trader under its authority or control, provides the product or service (art. 6(3)).
- The recitals give as examples a platform that fails to display the trader's identity, withholds it until after the contract, or markets the service in its own name (recital 24).
- The Regulation states that its liability rules do not provide a positive basis for holding a provider liable (recital 17).
- Every provider of an intermediary service must designate a single point of contact that lets users communicate with it directly and rapidly and choose a means of communication that does not rely solely on automated tools (art. 12(1)).
- The recitals add that it should be explicitly indicated when a user is communicating with a chatbot (recital 43).
- Online platforms other than micro and small enterprises must provide an internal complaint system against the decisions listed in art. 20(1)(a)-(d), taken on the stated grounds, for at least six months after the user is informed of the decision (very large platforms not excluded), and must ensure that decisions on complaints are taken under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff and not solely by automated means (arts 19, 20(1), 20(6)).
- Certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies under the Regulation cannot impose a binding settlement (art. 21(2)).
- The Regulation applies from 17 February 2024, with other dates for some provisions and platforms (arts 92, 93(2)) and does not affect legal requirements relating to the accommodation or other service that a platform intermediates (recital 6; art. 2(2)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That art. 6(3) makes a platform liable to a guest. It disapplies an exemption; liability is for other law.
- That art. 6(3) protects every guest. It concerns consumers, traders, distance contracts and consumer protection law.
- That the Act gives a right to speak to a human being. Art. 12 requires a means of communication that does not rely solely on automated tools; recital 43 speaks of human resources and reasonable efforts.
- That art. 12 lets a guest reach the hotel or host, or requires anyone to resolve the guest's problem.
- That art. 20 covers complaints about a stay, a refused booking as such, or a price. It covers the platform's decisions on notices and on information the recipient provided.
- That art. 20 operates before a decision takes effect. It operates for six months after the recipient is informed.
- That art. 20(6) requires a human decision. It requires supervision by qualified staff and forbids decisions taken solely by automated means.
- That the Act treats a booking platform as the provider of the accommodation, or regulates accommodation.
- That these articles are unamended at the date of the paper, unless someone checks a consolidated text.
- That the Regulation as a whole was read for this card.
