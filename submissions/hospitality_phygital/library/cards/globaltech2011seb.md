## Summary

Pentalpha, a Hong Kong maker of appliances, copied SEB's patented 'cool-touch' deep fryer from a model bought abroad that bore no U.S. patent marks, asked a lawyer for a right-to-use opinion without telling him the design was copied, and sold the fryers to retailers who resold them in the United States. The Court held that inducing infringement under 35 U.S.C. § 271(b) requires knowledge that the induced acts infringe, and that "deliberate indifference to a known risk" is not enough. It then imported the criminal-law doctrine of wilful blindness: a defendant who subjectively believes there is a high probability that a fact exists and takes deliberate actions to avoid learning it is treated as knowing it. The Court said that this standard "surpasses recklessness and negligence", found the evidence sufficient, and affirmed. Kennedy J dissented: wilful blindness is not knowledge, and the moral claim that it is equally culpable is contested. United States federal law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The question: "We consider whether a party who “actively induces infringement of a patent” under 35 U. S. C. § 271(b) must know that the induced acts constitute patent infringement." (757) [full-text]
- The facts the Court relied on: Pentalpha "purchased an SEB fryer in Hong Kong and copied all but its cosmetic features"; the model bought abroad "bore no U. S. patent markings"; Pentalpha "retained an attorney to conduct a right-to-use study, but Pentalpha refrained from telling the attorney that its design was copied directly from SEB’s" (758). [full-text]
- First holding: "we now hold that induced infringement under § 271(b) requires knowledge that the induced acts constitute patent infringement." (766) [full-text]
- What is not enough: "we agree that deliberate indifference to a known risk that a patent exists is not the appropriate standard under § 271(b)." (766) [full-text]
- The doctrine and its home: "The doctrine of willful blindness is well established in criminal law. Many criminal statutes require proof that a defendant acted knowingly or willfully, and courts applying the doctrine of willful blindness hold that defendants cannot escape the reach of these statutes by deliberately shielding themselves from clear evidence of critical facts that are strongly suggested by the circumstances." (766) [full-text]
- Its rationale, in two forms: "The traditional rationale for this doctrine is that defendants who behave in this manner are just as culpable as those who have actual knowledge." And: "It is also said that persons who know enough to blind themselves to direct proof of critical facts in effect have actual knowledge of those facts." (766, citing Edwards (1954) and *United States v. Jewell*, CA9 1976) [full-text]
- A duty-holder's ignorance in the older cases, as the Court reports them: *Spurr v. United States* (1899), on a bank officer who certified a check: a wilful violation would occur "if the [bank] officer purposely keeps himself in ignorance of whether the drawer has money in the bank." (767). The dissent quotes the same sentence's continuation: "or is grossly indifferent to his duty in respect to the ascertainment of that fact." (773-774) [full-text]
- An 1882 jury charge, as quoted: the judge rejected the "great misapprehension" that a person may "close his eyes, when he pleases, upon all sources of information, and then excuse his ignorance by saying that he does not see anything." (767 n. 6, quoting *United States v. Houghton*, D.N.J.) [full-text]
- Extension to civil cases: "Given the long history of willful blindness and its wide acceptance in the Federal Judiciary, we can see no reason why the doctrine should not apply in civil lawsuits for induced patent infringement under 35 U. S. C. § 271(b)." (768) [full-text]
- The two requirements: "While the Courts of Appeals articulate the doctrine of willful blindness in slightly different ways, all appear to agree on two basic requirements: (1) The defendant must subjectively believe that there is a high probability that a fact exists and (2) the defendant must take deliberate actions to avoid learning of that fact." (769) [full-text]
- What it does not cover: "We think these requirements give willful blindness an appropriately limited scope that surpasses recklessness and negligence. Under this formulation, a willfully blind defendant is one who takes deliberate actions to avoid confirming a high probability of wrongdoing and who can almost be said to have actually known the critical facts." "By contrast, a reckless defendant is one who merely knows of a substantial and unjustified risk of such wrongdoing ... and a negligent defendant is one who should have known of a similar risk but, in fact, did not" (769-770). [full-text]
- Why the Federal Circuit's test failed: it "permits a finding of knowledge when there is merely a “known risk” that the induced acts are infringing", and "in demanding only “deliberate indifference” to that risk, the Federal Circuit’s test does not require active efforts by an inducer to avoid knowing about the infringing nature of the activities." (770) [full-text]
- The deliberate act was a designed inquiry: "Even more telling is Sham’s decision not to inform the attorney from whom Pentalpha sought a right-to-use opinion that the product to be evaluated was simply a knockoff of SEB’s deep fryer. On the facts of this case, we cannot fathom what motive Sham could have had for withholding this information other than to manufacture a claim of plausible deniability in the event that his company was later accused of patent infringement." (771). Sham was Pentalpha's "chief executive officer and president" (770). [full-text]
- Application to the company: the evidence sufficed for a jury to find "that Pentalpha subjectively believed there was a high probability that SEB’s fryer was patented, that Pentalpha took deliberate steps to avoid knowing that fact, and that it therefore willfully blinded itself to the infringing nature of Sunbeam’s sales." (771) The opinion attributes the CEO's beliefs and acts to the company without discussing how a corporation's knowledge is fixed. [full-text]
- Circuit formulations the Court collects, as quoted: "Ignorance is deliberate if the defendant was presented with facts that put her on notice that criminal activity was particularly likely and yet she intentionally failed to investigate those facts" (769 n. 9, quoting *United States v. Florez*, CA8 2004). [full-text]
- The jury was not instructed on the new standard; the Court declined to remand because the point had not been raised below (770 n. 10). [full-text]
- Dissent, Kennedy J: "Willful blindness is not knowledge; and judges should not broaden a legislative proscription by analogy." (772). On the rationale: "But the moral question is a difficult one." "Perhaps the culpability of willful blindness depends on a person’s reasons for remaining blind." (773, citing Luban, 'Contrived Ignorance', 87 Geo. L. J. 957 (1999), and Hellman (2009)). On evidence: "Facts that support willful blindness are often probative of actual knowledge." (774). On reach: "The Court appears to endorse the willful blindness doctrine here for all federal criminal cases involving knowledge." (774) [full-text]
- The opinion does not mention a duty of care, a duty to inquire as such, hosts, guests or service providers (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Register.** This is a doctrine about a defendant's state of mind, borrowed from criminal law (766-768) and applied to a statutory knowledge requirement for liability in patent law. It does not say what anyone owes anyone. Used for a host's duty of care to a guest it is an analogy, and the paper must say so.
- **The limit is the point.** The Court built the two requirements to exclude recklessness and negligence (769-770) and rejected the lower court's "deliberate indifference to a known risk" (766, 770). A host that never formed a belief that a particular guest's problem was going unheard is outside the doctrine, however careless its design.
- **The inquiry was manipulated, not omitted.** What the Court found "telling" was a positive act: withholding the key fact from the adviser hired to inquire (771). That is closer to designing a channel so that it cannot carry the decisive information than to having no channel. It still presupposed Pentalpha's high-probability belief (771).
- **How much is holding.** The wilful-blindness standard is the ground of affirmance, joined by eight Justices. Kennedy J's dissent reads the majority as endorsing the doctrine for all federal criminal cases (774); whether it is holding outside § 271(b) was not decided here. Later treatment not checked.
- **Corporate knowledge.** The Court treats Pentalpha as wilfully blind through its chief executive's acts (770-771) without any analysis of corporate knowledge. Nothing here supports aggregating what different employees know.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3: governs. It supports the principle in its narrowest form and refutes its extension to negligence.** The highest court in the United States states that "defendants cannot escape the reach of these statutes by deliberately shielding themselves from clear evidence of critical facts" (766): a requirement of knowledge cannot be met by arranging not to know. The rationale is equal culpability, or that such a person "in effect" knows (766). Two conditions confine it: a subjective belief that a fact is highly probable, and deliberate action to avoid confirming it (769). The Court says in terms that the doctrine "surpasses recklessness and negligence" (769). So the case supports R5a's sentence about a host that "removes every route by which a guest could tell it" only where the host already believes it highly probable that a failure is occurring and removes or blocks the route to avoid confirming it. It does not reach a host that designed an unreachable service without such a belief. For that host the case points the other way: the law's name for its state is negligence, which the doctrine excludes.

**What the paper may say**

- In United States federal law, courts applying the doctrine of wilful blindness "hold that defendants cannot escape the reach of these statutes by deliberately shielding themselves from clear evidence of critical facts that are strongly suggested by the circumstances" (*Global-Tech*, 563 U.S. at 766).
- The Supreme Court gives the doctrine two requirements: the defendant "must subjectively believe that there is a high probability that a fact exists" and "must take deliberate actions to avoid learning of that fact" (at 769).
- The Court says these requirements give the doctrine a scope "that surpasses recklessness and negligence", and defines the negligent defendant as "one who should have known of a similar risk but, in fact, did not" (at 769-770).
- The traditional rationale is that the wilfully blind "are just as culpable as those who have actual knowledge" (at 766); Kennedy J, dissenting, called that moral question "a difficult one" (at 773).
- The Court found it "telling" that the company's chief executive withheld the decisive fact from the lawyer engaged to check for patents, and inferred that he meant "to manufacture a claim of plausible deniability" (at 771).
- Applied to a host, the doctrine is an analogy from criminal and patent law, not a rule of the law of hosts.

**What the paper may not say**

- That wilful blindness, in this case or as the Court states it, reaches negligent or reckless ignorance. The Court says the opposite (769-770).
- That the case imposes a duty to inquire, to monitor or to keep a channel open. It concerns whether a defendant knew.
- That the case concerns hosts, guests, tort or a duty of care.
- That the case settles how a corporation's knowledge is attributed or aggregated.
- That the doctrine is accepted without dissent: Kennedy J rejected it (772-775).
