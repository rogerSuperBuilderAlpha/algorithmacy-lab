## Summary

Sreenivasan reviews Gilbert's *Rights and Demands*. He reports her central class of rights, 'demand-rights', which she defines twice over: by the directed obligation owed to the holder, and by the holder's standing to demand performance. Together the two definitions make being owed a duty and having standing to demand it equivalent. He reports her explanation of that standing by joint commitment, grants that standing to demand is a neglected and real aspect of claim-rights, and then argues that it cannot be what a claim-right is. Property rights show that the standing cannot rest on joint commitment; Locke's state of nature shows that people who are owed nothing can have standing to demand. He closes by placing demand inside Hart's will theory as one more 'measure of control'.

## Critical notes & threats

- **Report, not source, for Gilbert.** Everything in the first list is Sreenivasan's account of a closed book. The paper may write 'Gilbert, as Sreenivasan (2019) reports her, holds ...' and may not cite Gilbert (2018) at the bracketed pages. Wenar and Cruft give the second equivalence independently, also at Gilbert's p. 70 (wenar2025rights, §2.2.2, para. 18).
- **What the objections do and do not show.** Both objections attack the claim that standing to demand *is* what a claim-right, or being owed a duty, consists in. The first concerns its ground, the second its sufficiency. Neither gives a case of a competent adult who is owed a duty and lacks standing to demand it; on the contrary, he sees 'no reason to deny' the owner's standing. So the review supports 'contested' for the constitutive question and leaves the ordinary case untouched.
- **A review is short.** It is a review in an online review journal, 3,946 words by count of the saved text including the two notes. Sreenivasan's own theory is in his 2005 and 2010 articles (closed); this card does not carry it beyond the review's words.
- **Cultural variation in standing.** The remark that 'other times and places have understood it differently' is an aside inside an objection, illustrated by an anecdote about strangers in Germany correcting his mother on child rearing. It is not evidence about hospitality codes and should not be cited as such.
- The saved text renders dashes as '--' and uses straight quotation marks; quotations above follow it.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K6 (supports, and sharpens).** The disagreement stated exactly: Gilbert makes being owed a duty equivalent to the holder's standing to demand it (equivalence (3)); Sreenivasan holds that the standing is a real aspect of claim-rights and not their analysis. K6's sentence that the matter 'is contested among theories of directed duty' is safe on this text. K6 can also be put more strongly for the ordinary case, since the critic of the demand analysis himself affirms the right-holder's standing. (Narrowed after CV-D: Sreenivasan affirms the standing to demand of 'a property owner'; its extension to a guest is the card's.)
- **K7, second route (narrows).** If standing to demand can be held by parties who are owed nothing, then an argument from 'the guest has standing to demand' to 'the host owes the guest an addressee' needs more than the standing. The review supplies no premise of that kind. It does supply one usable point: a demand presupposes a right already held and creates none (reported of Gilbert, her p. 62), so a channel for demands adds nothing to what the guest is owed; it serves the exercise.
- **K5 (bears indirectly).** The remark about agents 'incapable of making demands' is the will theory's old difficulty. The classical guest is not incapable; the code forbids him. The review does not discuss a right-holder barred from demanding by a rule of role.
