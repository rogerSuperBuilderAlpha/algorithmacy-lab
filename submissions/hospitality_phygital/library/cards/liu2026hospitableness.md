## Summary

Qualitative study (20 online interviews, participants from 12 countries, mostly postgraduates)
combining unprompted discussion with an AI-generated video of a robot at hotel check-in. It
builds the construct "robotic hospitableness" in three dimensions (robot-mediated,
robot-assisted, robot-personified) and argues that designed robots can produce the
*experiential effects* of hospitableness for guests, while explicitly denying that robots
possess hospitableness as a moral disposition or ethical agency.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The question asked: "We shift the evaluative question from whether guests accept robots to whether guests feel hosted by them, that is, whether the interaction produces the experiential outcomes of hospitableness." (p. 1) [full-text]
- Construct: "a hospitality-oriented perspective asks whether a robot makes guests feel welcomed, cared for, and reassured." (p. 3) [full-text]
- Hospitableness as disposition: "hospitableness is fundamentally unidirectional ...: a host's disposition to care for a guest without expectation of reciprocity." (p. 2) [full-text]
- Method: "online interviews with 20 participants from 12 countries" (p. 4); stimulus is a designed video, so responses "reflect how robotic hospitableness is cognitively and affectively interpreted when projected onto non-human agents, rather than reactions to a specific embodied system." (p. 3) [full-text]
- No moral agency claimed: "This does not imply that robots possess hospitableness as a moral disposition, emotional capacity, or ethical agency in the way human hosts do. Rather, it indicates that robots can be designed to produce the experiential effects of hospitableness ... In this sense, robotic hospitableness is relationally experienced by guests rather than internally possessed by the machine." (p. 9) [full-text]
- Not a test: "The findings therefore reflect perceived and anticipated hospitableness rather than behavioral responses to deployed systems. Future studies should test the framework using physical robots, VR, and field experiments." (p. 9) [full-text]
- Closing: "As robots increasingly occupy frontline roles, the question shifts from whether they can perform tasks to whether they can meaningfully host." (p. 9) [full-text]
- Human staff remain for the hard cases: robot-assisted hospitableness preserves "human touchpoints for complex interactions" (p. 7) [full-text]
- Three dimensions: Robot-Mediated, Robot-Assisted and Robot-Personified Hospitableness (abstract, p. 1; Fig. 4, p. 9) [full-text]
- Single touchpoint (check-in); sample "predominantly postgraduate" (p. 9) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

The full text strengthens the paper's use. Liu et al. say in so many words that robotic hospitableness is "relationally experienced by guests rather than internally possessed by the machine" (p. 9). That is the paper's distinction between seeming hospitable and holding a position that owes something, made by the rival itself. The paper can quote it. Nothing in the article discusses responsibility, accountability, or who answers the guest when the robot's welcome fails; human staff appear only as the fallback for "complex interactions" (p. 7).

#### correction 2026-11-13 (v8 stage 2 reader)

Re-read in full (pp. 1–11, figures viewed as images). Two statements in the critical notes go further than the text.

1. **The card says** "human staff appear only as the fallback for 'complex interactions' (p. 7)". **The text** also makes staff coordinated partners, in one of the three elements of robot-assisted hospitableness: "Service coordination involves seamless collaboration between robots and staff." and "When robots and staff are visibly aligned, guests experience the organization as caring and well organized." (p. 7). The video stimulus includes a "staff collaboration display" (Table 3, p. 4).
2. **The card says** "Nothing in the article discusses responsibility, accountability, or who answers the guest when the robot's welcome fails." Accountability, and who answers when the robot fails, are indeed absent. **But the text** uses duty language once, in the subheading "Effort reduction as host responsibility" (p. 7). It also attributes the robot's conduct to the hotel: "Using appropriate honorifics signals that the robot (and by extension, the establishment) recognizes and respects the guest’s cultural identity." (p. 6). And it frames data safeguards as care owed to the guest: "transparency in data use, guest control over personal information, and revocable consent as core expressions of care" (p. 8).
