## Summary

De Kenessey argues that talk of what one person owes another picks out two relations that philosophers have treated as one. The constraint relation holds when someone has a right of the paradigm kind (promissory, bodily, property, privacy): the obligor has a non-instrumental, agent-relative reason to act, which the right-holder can usually waive, and whose infringement leaves secondary duties to explain and to compensate. The accountability relation holds when someone has warrant to hold another personally accountable: to demand the act, to resent its omission, and to forgive. The first is an input to the question of what the obligor ought to do; the second is an output that depends on the answer. So each can hold without the other. A constraint obligation that is justifiably overridden leaves no warrant to demand performance (§2), and considerations of a person's welfare can ground warrant to demand where no constraint right exists (§3). He uses the distinction to reframe two debates: whether rights have built-in exceptions, and whether all rights can be waived (§4).

- The thesis: "Philosophers have so far treated directed obligation as a single thing, while in fact there are two distinct relations both worthy of the name." (introduction, Early View p. 2) [full-text]
- The accountability relation defined: "The second relation, which I call the accountability relation, is the relation I stand in to you when you have warrant to hold me personally accountable for whether I ϕ by demanding that I ϕ, resenting me if I fail to ϕ without excuse, and deciding whether to forgive me for ϕing." (introduction, p. 2) [full-text]
- In each relation obligation and right are two sides of one thing: he speaks of constraint obligations "and the rights that are the flip side of these obligations constraint rights" (introduction, p. 2) [full-text]
- The ordinary case: "when I have a constraint obligation to you, that usually gives you the warrant to demand that I respect this obligation and resent me if I don’t, thus implying an accountability obligation." (introduction, p. 2) [full-text]
- Input and output: "the constraint relation is a normative input, while the accountability relation is a normative output." (introduction, p. 2) [full-text]
- Why demand is an output: "your demand that I ϕ or resentment at my failure to ϕ cannot be fitting unless I ought to ϕ all things considered." (introduction, p. 2) [full-text]
- The vocabulary is ambiguous throughout: the ordinary phrases, among them 'owed to', 'having a right' and 'having a claim', "are ambiguous between the constraint and accountability senses." (introduction, p. 3) [full-text]
- What survives a permissible infringement: "even if it is permissible for me to infringe your constraint rights, you have the standing to demand an explanation and (in some cases) compensation for my doing so." (p. 3, n. 6) [full-text]
- The features are symptoms, not an analysis: "To delineate these features is not, in my view, to give a theory of what these relations are." (§1, p. 4) [full-text]
- Secondary duties include explaining: breach "places me under new, ‘secondary duties’ to you: an obligation to explain myself and to compensate for my breach" (§1.1, feature 4, p. 5); "they are the moral residue left behind by the infringement of a constraint obligation – whether that infringement was justified or not." (§1.1, p. 5) [full-text]
- Waiver marks the person owed: "The power to waive is one of the most salient features that distinguishes the person to whom a constraint obligation is owed from everyone else." (§1.1, feature 5, p. 5) [full-text]
- Warranted is not the same as advisable: "a speech act or attitude can be fitting (/unfitting) even when there are compelling practical reasons to avoid it (/have it)"; "A hostage might have warrant to demand that her captor release her even if she ought not make this demand, because doing so will only bring her more abuse." (§1.2, feature 6, p. 6) [full-text]
- Third parties may also hold to account: "And so the warrant to hold accountable is not always unique to the person to whom an accountability obligation is owed." (§1.2, p. 6); "even if third parties can hold me accountable for wronging you, only you have the power to forgive me." (§1.2, feature 7, p. 7) [full-text]
- The condition on accountability: "In general, one can be under an accountability obligation to ϕ only if one ought to ϕ all things considered." (§2.1, p. 8) [full-text]
- The argument: "(P1) It can be the case both that X has a constraint obligation to Y not to ϕ and that X ought to ϕ all things considered. (P2) If X ought to ϕ all things considered, then X cannot be under an accountability obligation not to ϕ. (C) Therefore, it can be the case both that X has a constraint obligation to Y not to ϕ and that X does not have an accountability obligation to Y not to ϕ." (§2.1, p. 9) [full-text]
- Two kinds of answerability: "the way you are answerable to the owner for a justified rights infringement is different in kind from the way you would be answerable to them for an unjustified rights violation." (§2.1, p. 9) [full-text]
- What the owner keeps and what the owner lacks: the constraint right "does imply a secondary right to be compensated if this primary right is infringed"; "What they can’t fittingly do is demand that you not break into the cabin in the first place or resent you for doing this." - "I owe the owner an explanation and compensation for any damage I caused." (§2.2, p. 11) [full-text]
- A disagreement with Gilbert: "Here I am disagreeing with Margaret Gilbert, who holds that, at least for promises, the promisee has warrant to demand that the promise is kept even if the promisor ought all things considered to break it (Gilbert, 2018: 135)." (p. 9, n. 15) [full-text]
- The un-overridden case: "When a constraint obligation is not overridden, it typically grounds an accountability obligation." (§3, p. 14) [full-text]
- Accountability without a constraint right: "my mother does not have a constraint right that I call her on her birthday, and yet she still has warrant to hold me personally accountable if I fail to do so without excuse." (§3, p. 14) [full-text]
- The result: "the constraint and accountability relations are extensionally dissociable in both directions: each can obtain in the other’s absence." (§3, p. 18) [full-text]
- On the older debate: "Will theorists thus appear committed to the thesis that all rights are waivable, since they take the power to waive to be constitutive of having a right." (§4, p. 18); the debate between the will and interest theories "would also benefit from clarifying which relation these theories are meant to be theories of." (§4, p. 24) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Does it allow a duty owed to someone who has no standing to demand it? Yes, in one kind of case.** The case is the overridden constraint obligation (§2.1, p. 9): the cabin owner is owed, in the constraint sense, that the hiker stay out, and cannot fittingly demand it, because the hiker ought to break in. The duty is owed and the warrant to demand performance is absent. Even there the person owed keeps 'the standing to demand an explanation and (in some cases) compensation' (p. 3, n. 6) and a secondary right to compensation (p. 9).
- **That case is not the classical guest's.** The guest of Pitt-Rivers' code lacks the liberty to demand because the guest's role forbids it, not because the host is justified in failing to attend. For a duty that is not overridden de Kenessey says only that it 'typically' grounds warrant to demand (§3, p. 14), and gives no example where it does not. The article therefore does not supply 'owed to the guest, and the guest may not demand it' for an unexcused failure.
- **The duty to attend looks like his other kind.** A duty to 'show concern for their needs and wishes' (pittrivers2012law, p. 516) is a reason to promote someone's welfare. In §3 such reasons ground accountability obligations where no constraint right exists (the birthday call, p. 14), and the mark of an accountability obligation is the very warrant to demand that the classical guest lacks. Classified by this article, the customary duty to attend is either not directed at all or directed in a sense that brings standing to demand with it. This application is the card's, not the author's.
- **One passage does fit the guest.** A demand can be warranted while there are compelling practical reasons not to make it (§1.2, p. 6, the hostage). That gives a way to say that the guest's demand would be warranted and that the code gives the guest decisive reason of role not to voice it. The author's example concerns prudence, and a rule of role is a different kind of reason; the extension is the card's.
- **He never separates obligation from right.** In both relations the right is 'the flip side' of the obligation (p. 2). The article is no support for a duty owed to A with no right in A. What it separates is being owed (constraint sense) from warrant to demand.
- **Asserted, not argued.** The secondary duty to explain oneself (§1.1, p. 5; §2.2, p. 11; n. 6) is stated as a familiar feature, with Thomson (1980) and Ripstein (2016) named and not discussed. It arises on infringement. The article offers no account of why explanation is owed and says nothing of a duty to remain reachable.
- **Scope.** Moral obligation between persons; he sets legal property rights aside (p. 8, n. 13). Nothing on organizations, roles or custom. He declines to give a theory of either relation (§1, p. 4).
- **One article, recent.** The two-relation view is this author's proposal. He lists those who 'have noticed this paper’s distinction, or something close to it' as Cornell, Ripstein, Wallace, Martin and Attie-Picker (p. 3, n. 4), and he disagrees with Darwall, Wallace and Gilbert on several points. It is not a settled result.
- **Pages.** Quotation in the paper needs issue pages (913–938). The Early View page plus 912 is the likely conversion and is unverified.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K6 (supports).** It is direct support for keeping 'standing held' apart from any theory of standing to demand: on this view warrant to demand is not part of the constraint right and depends on what the obligor ought to do all things considered (pp. 2, 8–9). It also supports v6's choice to define the guest's position from the duty and to treat accountability as coming after: accountability is 'a normative output' (p. 2). It narrows one thing: the guest's warrant to demand performance lapses where the host is justified in not performing, though the guest may still demand an explanation (p. 3, n. 6; p. 9).
- **K5 (narrows the customary limb).** It shows that being owed and being positioned to demand can come apart, which K5 needs. It shows it for overridden duties only, and its taxonomy puts a welfare-based duty to attend on the accountability side, where standing to demand is definitional. K5's customary limb is not licensed by this article as written.
- **K7, second route (supports a narrower claim).** A published statement that infringing what is owed to someone leaves a duty 'to explain myself' to that person, justified breach or not (§1.1, p. 5), and that the person keeps 'the standing to demand an explanation' (p. 3, n. 6). This is support for answerability after a lapse. It is stated in passing, belongs to the stage K7 assigns to repair, and gives no duty to be reachable while performance is still possible.
