## Summary

Section 29 forbids a service-provider to discriminate against, harass or victimise a person requiring its service, including by not providing it, and imposes a duty to make reasonable adjustments.

Added in rr-2026-11-k. Sections 20 and 21 and Schedule 2 give that duty its content for service-providers. Where a provider's provision, criterion or practice, a physical feature, or the want of an auxiliary aid puts disabled persons at a substantial disadvantage in the provision of the service, the provider must take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage or supply the aid. The duty is owed by reference to "disabled persons generally", so it must be met in advance. A failure to comply "in relation to that person" is discrimination against that disabled person. The provider may not charge the disabled person for complying, and need not take a step that would fundamentally alter the nature of the service. The person wronged sues in the county court (in Scotland, before the sheriff), which may grant any remedy the High Court could grant in tort or on judicial review; damages may include compensation for injured feelings.

## Critical notes & threats

- This is a ground-limited duty not to refuse, not the innkeeper's duty to receive any fit traveller who can pay. The innkeeper's duty is wider in one way (any ground) and narrower in another (travellers, room available).
- Read with uk1956hotel: the hotel proprietor carries both; a bank or shop carries only s. 29.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **This cuts against a sentence in v6.** DRAFT_v6.md (line 38, in 'The host's power and the host's obligation') reads: 'None of these sources makes a negotiated exception something the guest is owed, and the exceptions the law recognized ran in the host's favour'. The first half is about the sources that paragraph lists, and this Act is not among them. The second half is wrong for the present law of Great Britain: for a disabled guest, ss. 20(3) and 21(2) with Sch. 2 para. 2 make a departure from the provider's own "provision, criterion or practice" a legal duty, breach of which is a wrong to "that person", with a county-court claim and damages. That exception runs in the guest's favour. The cap-lifting conditions in the Hotel Proprietors Act 1956 s. 2(3) also run in the guest's favour (uk1956hotel). The sentence is the authors' to change; I flag it and do not reword it.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **It narrows K4's last sentence without overturning it.** K4 says attention to the particular guest is customary and not legal. The adjustment duty is legal, but it is limited to disabled persons, is framed by reference to "disabled persons generally" (Sch. 2 para. 2(2)), requires only what "it is reasonable to have to take", and stops short of altering "the nature of the service" (para. 2(7)). It is a duty to adjust for a class, enforced by the individual. It is not a general legal duty to attend to each guest.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Not specific to hospitality.** The duty binds every "service-provider" (s. 29(7)(a)): hotels, restaurants, banks and shops alike. It cannot be offered as a host's distinctive duty.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **"Service-provider" and who it is in a several-firm arrangement.** S. 29(1) speaks of a person "concerned with the provision of a service to the public". I did not read s. 31 or any case on which of a brand, operator or platform is the service-provider. The Act does not answer K8 on this card's reading.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Terms not read.** "Disability" is defined in s. 6, which this run knows only as quoted in *DMA* para 248. I read no definition of "substantial" and the card does not gloss the word. The Code of Practice's statement that the duty is "anticipatory" is known to this run only as quoted in *DMA* para 289 (from the Court of Appeal in *VC*).
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Extent.** England, Wales and Scotland. Not Northern Ireland.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **The existing s. 29(7)(a) quotation stops before the parenthesis.** The full text of the paragraph is "(a) a service-provider (and see also section 55(7));".
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Later treatment.** Applied in *DMA* (read). Other case law on ss. 20-21 in hotels or restaurants not searched and not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

Added in rr-2026-11-k.

- **K4 (two tiers; attending): corrects v6's statement on exceptions and narrows K4's last sentence.** The law of Great Britain gives a disabled guest a claim to an adjustment of the provider's practice (ss. 20(3), 21(2); Sch. 2 para. 2). K4 anticipated this and named it as the correction to make.
- **K4 (admission): adds a ground-limited duty not to refuse on every service-provider** (s. 29(1)), and a duty not to discriminate in the terms or by ending the service (s. 29(2)). This already stood on the card.
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** Failure to comply "in relation to that person" is discrimination against that person (s. 21(2)); the person sues in the county court (s. 114(1)(a)) and may recover damages, including for injured feelings (s. 119(4)).
- **K7 (channel): a point against the defeater that the law offers only later damages.** The county court may grant "any remedy which could be granted by the High Court" in tort or on judicial review (s. 119(2)), and the Act contemplates interim injunctions (s. 114(6)(a)). The statute does not itself require a contact point or a person with authority. In *DMA* the court treated monitoring as a reasonable step the duty required of a public body (para 291).

**What the paper may say**

- In Great Britain a service-provider owes disabled people a duty to make reasonable adjustments (s. 29(7)(a)), which includes changing "a provision, criterion or practice" that puts them at a substantial disadvantage (s. 20(3)).
- For service-providers the duty is owed by reference to "disabled persons generally" (Sch. 2 para. 2(2)), and failing to comply with it "in relation to that person" is discrimination against that person (s. 21(2)).
- The provider cannot charge the disabled person for the adjustment (s. 20(7)) and need not "fundamentally alter" the nature of the service (Sch. 2 para. 2(7)).
- The disabled person can sue in the county court (s. 114(1)(a)); the court may grant any remedy available in tort or on judicial review (s. 119(2)), and damages "may include compensation for injured feelings" (s. 119(4)).
- So the law of Great Britain makes reasonable adjustment of a host's provision, criterion or practice something a disabled guest is legally owed, within what it is reasonable to have to take and short of altering the nature of the service (ss. 20(3), 21(2); Sch. 2 para. 2(2), (7)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the exceptions the law recognizes all run in the host's favour, or that the law makes no exception something a guest is owed. For disabled guests this Act does.
- That the law requires a host to attend to each guest's particular needs or wishes. The duty is confined to disability and to reasonable steps.
- That the duty is special to hosts. It binds every provider of a service to the public.
- That the Act requires a human point of contact or a right to a hearing. Nothing read says so.
- Anything about how "substantial", "disability" or "service-provider" are defined, or about Northern Ireland.

**What the provisions say, for the question T6 asks** (whether a host can define away a guest's route to help by selling an unstaffed stay)

- The third requirement: "where a disabled person would, but for the provision of an auxiliary aid, be put at a substantial disadvantage in relation to a relevant matter in comparison with persons who are not disabled, to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to provide the auxiliary aid." (s. 20(5)); and "A reference in this section, section 21 or 22 or an applicable Schedule to an auxiliary aid includes a reference to an auxiliary service." (s. 20(11)). [full-text]
- Information: "Where the first or third requirement relates to the provision of information, the steps which it is reasonable for A to have to take include steps for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format." (s. 20(6)). [full-text]
- The first requirement, which reaches a provider's own policies: "where a provision, criterion or practice of A's puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage ... to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage." (s. 20(3)). [full-text]
- For services, all three apply and are anticipatory: "A must comply with the first, second and third requirements." (Sch. 2 para. 2(1)); "the reference in section 20(3), (4) or (5) to a disabled person is to disabled persons generally." (Sch. 2 para. 2(2)); "the relevant matter is the provision of the service, or the exercise of the function, by A." (Sch. 2 para. 2(4)). [full-text]
- Physical features may be met by "a reasonable alternative method of providing the service" (Sch. 2 para. 2(3)), and include features "brought by or on behalf of A, in the course of providing the service ..., on to premises other than those that A occupies" (Sch. 2 para. 2(6)). [full-text]
- The limit: "If A is a service-provider, nothing in this paragraph requires A to take a step which would fundamentally alter— (a) the nature of the service, or (b) the nature of A's trade or profession." (Sch. 2 para. 2(7)). [full-text]
- The cost cannot be passed on: A is "not ... entitled to require a disabled person ... to pay to any extent A's costs of complying with the duty" (s. 20(7)). [full-text]

**How this bears on Step 6 (legal register)**

- **A floor the host's own description of the service does not set, with one exception.** The duty attaches to "the provision of the service" (Sch. 2 para. 2(4)), not to what the provider undertook, and it is owed to "disabled persons generally" in advance (para. 2(2)). A host whose only route to help is a kiosk, an app or an unanswered line, and whose disabled guests are put at a substantial disadvantage by that, must take reasonable steps: an auxiliary service such as a person who can be reached (s. 20(5), (11)), information about how to get help in an accessible format (s. 20(6)), or a change to the kiosk-only practice (s. 20(3)) *(my application of the text to the self-service hotel; no case read)*. The one exit is a step that would "fundamentally alter" the nature of the service or trade (para. 2(7)). Whether giving a disabled guest a reachable person fundamentally alters an unstaffed hotel is the question the self-service host would raise, and the text does not answer it.
- **It answers T6 only for disabled guests, and only to the reasonable.** The duty runs to disabled persons (s. 20(3)-(5)); the steps are those "it is reasonable to have to take". It does not require a channel for every guest, or a person with power over the single case.
- **It is the guest's own right.** Failure "in relation to that person" is discrimination against that person (s. 21(2), above), actionable in the county court (s. 114(1)(a), above). It fits T4: the legal correlate is the guest's remedial right.
- **Who is bound in a several-firm arrangement** remains open on this card (s. 31 not read).

**What the paper may say (added)**

- In Great Britain a provider of services to the public must, in anticipation, take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to provide an auxiliary aid or service where a disabled person would otherwise be put at a substantial disadvantage, and where information is involved those steps include providing it "in an accessible format" (Equality Act 2010, s. 20(5)-(6), (11); Sch. 2 para. 2(1)-(2)).
- The provider need not take a step that would "fundamentally alter" the nature of the service or of its trade (Sch. 2 para. 2(7)).

**What the paper may not say (added)**

- That the Act requires every host to keep a channel to a person for every guest, or a person with power to correct performance.
- That the Act settles whether a reachable person is a reasonable step for an unstaffed hotel, or whether it would fundamentally alter the service.
