## Summary

A conceptual macromarketing article that gives phygital a second dimension. Berthon and colleagues keep phygital for a solution's type, from physical-dominant to digital-dominant, and coin 'accesship' for its mode of acquisition, from access-dominant (temporary, contractual use rights) to ownership-dominant (legal title). Crossing the two gives a 2 × 2 phygital–accesship typology (PAT) with four cells: Streams (digital, accessed), Tokens (digital, owned), Lends (physical, accessed) and Keeps (physical, owned). Each cell is read as a 'sequenced solution' whose value arrives in stages over time. Three psychological mechanisms — materialism, need for touch and psychological ownership — yield six propositions about which cell better supports consumer well-being, with satisfaction as the proxy. The implications address firms, consumers and policymakers in turn. Firms should disclose total cost of use and make cancellation easy; regulators should mandate licence transparency, continuity and termination disclosures and 'meaningful remedies when access is withdrawn' (p. 417). The article names no hospitality setting and no AI. Its one accommodation example is renting a Tuscan villa through Airbnb, used to show that access is easier to acquire than ownership (p. 415).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**What kind of paper, and where it came from**

- Conceptual: "Our research, which is conceptual in nature, offers a framework that explains how hybrid sequenced solutions influence consumer well-being." (p. 408); the propositions "are largely based on logical deduction by extrapolating insights from extant literature as well as sensemaking rather than new empirical evidence" (p. 417). [full-text]
- Research question: "How do the type of solution (i.e., physical, digital) and the mode of its acquisition (i.e., access, ownership) affect consumer well-being?" (p. 409). [full-text]
- Its gap: "A substantial body of scholarship suggests that both digital and physical solutions may harm well-being (e.g., Appel et al., 2020; Belk, 1985; Burroughs & Rindfleisch, 2002), yet the phygital literature has remained largely silent about these implications." (p. 409). [full-text]
- Provenance: "This research was conducted as part of the American Phygital Association Summit 2025, sponsored by the American Institute of Business Experience Design (AIBXD) – New York." The same page names Wided Batat under the heading "Associate Editor" (p. 420). Pierre Berthon (Bentley University) is the corresponding author (p. 408). [full-text]

**Definitions**

- Phygital is the type, accesship the mode: "phygital focuses on the nature (type) of a solution (Lim, 2026a) while accesship focuses on the nature (mode) of acquisition (Pierce et al., 2003)" (p. 408). [full-text]
- Dominance, not purity: "phygital denotes hybrid composition, yet hybrid solutions vary in which (dominant) element delivers the core value at entry, thus we use digital-dominant versus physical-dominant as shorthand for relative dominance" (pp. 408-409). [full-text]
- Phygital, borrowed from Batat: a phygital solution "integrates a combination of physical, human, digital and media content elements, platforms, technologies, and extended realities" (quoted at p. 411 as Batat 2024b, "p. 10"); "In essence, phygital solutions fuse the tangible (physical) and the intangible (digital) to create hybrid consumption experiences (Batat & Hammedi, 2023; Lim, 2026a)." (p. 411). [full-text]
- Access versus ownership: "Ownership typically implies a long-term, even permanent, time horizon and provides both functional and symbolic benefits, whereas access is more temporary and primarily provides functional benefits" and "Ownership also confers a more complete bundle of property rights, including control, customization, transferability, and responsibility for risk, whereas access arrangements restrict rights but reduce maintenance burdens and financial commitments" (p. 410). [full-text]
- Accesship: "We define accesship as the access–ownership structure through which a solution is acquired and governed over time, ranging from temporary, contract-based use rights (e.g., memberships, rentals, subscriptions) at the one end to permanent legal ownership at the other end, with hybrid arrangements in between." (p. 411). [full-text]
- Orthogonality: "Hence, accesship is orthogonal to phygital: a digital good can be owned permanently (e.g., a non-fungible token (NFT) or purchased e-book) and a physical good can be temporarily accessed (e.g., a leased vehicle or rented apparel)." (p. 411). [full-text]
- Table 1 (p. 412) contrasts access and ownership on four dimensions: "Time-bound rights and journey structure", "Responsibility and maintenance", "Transferability and legal rights" and "Cost structure". [full-text]
- Sequenced solutions: "intentionally designed customer journeys that deliver value in staged phases, with later phases contingent on earlier adoption or continued participation" (note 1, p. 420). [full-text]

**The typology (PAT)**

- Axes: "the two rows of this typology represent the type of solution (i.e., digital-dominant versus physical-dominant) while the two columns represent its mode of acquisition (i.e., access-dominant versus ownership-dominant)" (p. 412). [full-text]
- Classification rule: "The four quadrants should be viewed as anchor configurations rather than pure categories. Classification is based on the solution’s dominant core at entry, namely (1) whether legal title transfers to the consumer (ownership-dominant) versus use rights remain temporary and contractual (access-dominant), and (2) whether the core value is delivered primarily through intangible digital elements (digital-dominant) versus tangible physical elements (physical-dominant)." (p. 412). [full-text]
- The four cells, from Table 2 (p. 413): Streams, "Solutions anchored in temporary digital access, where continued participation progressively unlocks additional benefits over time" (music streaming); Tokens, "Solutions anchored in permanent digital ownership, where owning the digital asset enables sequenced digital enrichment over time" (a Bandcamp album); Lends, "Solutions anchored in temporary physical access, where the time-limited use experience is extended through integrated (digital) complements during the access period" (a library vinyl record); Keeps, "Solutions anchored in permanent physical ownership, where (digital) complements enhance the owned object’s core value proposition (e.g., functionality and longevity)" (a turntable). [full-text]

**Well-being: definition, mechanisms, propositions**

- Definition: "Within macromarketing, consumer well-being is conceptualized as (dis)satisfaction arising from aggregate consumption experiences across acquisition, possession, consumption, maintenance, and disposal, and is viewed as multi-dimensional and hierarchical in nature" (p. 410). [full-text]
- Contingency: "These mechanisms yield competing predictions, thus the well-being implications of solution type and acquisition mode are expected to be contingent on which mechanism dominates for a given consumer and context." (p. 414). Table 3, Panel B measures "consumer satisfaction (as a proxy of consumer well-being)" (p. 414). [full-text]
- Propositions on type: "P1. Digital-dominant solutions are more likely to support consumer well-being when materialism is salient." (p. 414); "P2. Physical-dominant solutions are more likely to support consumer well-being when (a) need for touch and (b) psychological ownership are salient." (p. 415); P3: digital-dominant solutions "should exhibit higher levels of acquisition satisfaction, lower levels of possession satisfaction, and little relationship with maintenance or disposal satisfaction" (p. 415). [full-text]
- Propositions on mode: "P4. Access-dominant solutions are more likely to support consumer well-being when materialism is salient." (p. 415); "P5. Ownership-dominant solutions are more likely to support consumer well-being when (a) need for touch and (b) psychological ownership are salient." (p. 415); P6 repeats P3's pattern for access-dominant solutions (p. 416). [full-text]
- Conclusion: "while Streams provide affordability, convenience, and flexibility, they may compromise identity integration and emotional attachment, which are key pathways to consumer well-being" and "Keeps offer tactile gratification and self-extension but also risk reinforcing materialistic values that may diminish well-being over time" (p. 420). [full-text]

**Harms named**

- "Access-dominant models (i.e., Streams, Lends) can expand a firm’s customer base by lowering upfront costs but may also create lock-ins and opaque cost structures." (p. 416). [full-text]
- "Moreover, consumers may face limitations on functionality, interoperability, or even continued access when a service is discontinued or a license is revoked (Calo, 2013)." (p. 417). [full-text]
- "access-heavy formats (i.e., Streams, Lends) can broaden participation, yet introduce the risks of hidden (and often recurring) costs and subscription lock-ins that disproportionately burden low-income or digitally vulnerable users" (p. 417). [full-text]
- "At present, this divergence between psychological versus legal ownership represents a grey zone that may increase consumer vulnerability and potentially lead to consumer exploitation." (p. 419; RQ8). [full-text]

**Safeguards, and who is told to provide them**

- Firms, as strategy: "These two dimensions represent strategic levers that firms can pull to better align solutions with both their internal capabilities and external conditions." (p. 416). [full-text]
- Firms, as design: "firms should invest in personalization, curated journeys, and community features that cultivate psychological ownership and perceived control in the absence of title transfer" (p. 416), "thereby shifting competitive advantage toward onboarding ease, continuity guarantees, and low-friction exit" (p. 416). [full-text]
- Firms, against exploitation: "Hence, firms should disclose the total cost of use, provide low-friction cancellations, and design equitable tiers (e.g., student/low-income pricing) to avoid exploitative practices associated with “roach-motel” subscription flows (i.e., easy to sign up but difficult and frustrating to cancel) and digital market manipulation (Calo, 2013; Sheil et al., 2024)." (p. 416). [full-text]
- Policy, on withdrawn access: "Accordingly, policy revisions are needed to enhance protections for access-based and digital-dominant solutions by mandating license transparency, continuity and termination disclosures, as well as meaningful remedies when access is withdrawn. These measures preserve the well-being pathways tied to perceived control and psychological ownership (i.e., P2 and P5), even when legal ownership is absent." (p. 417). [full-text]
- Policy, on costs, repair and switching: "regulators can require total cost of use disclosures, friction-reduced cancellation flows, and protections against predatory price subscriptions (Sheil et al., 2024)"; "policy could require that Lends solutions, such as ridesharing services, adopt minimum standards for maintenance cycles, utilization reporting, and end-of-life recirculation"; and initiatives "that promote interoperability, portability, and license transparency in order to preserve competition and reduce switching costs" (all p. 417). [full-text]
- Three addressees: "firms must carefully design solutions that balance control and flexibility; consumers must navigate new solutions that may be detrimental to their well-being; and policymakers must grapple with outdated assumptions regarding property, risk, and responsibility in increasingly fluid marketplaces" (p. 420). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Safeguards without a bearer among several firms.** The article addresses its prescriptions to "firms" in general and to "policymakers" and "regulators", and names "consumers" as beneficiaries, singling out "low-income or digitally vulnerable users" (p. 417). Its examples involve more than one firm (the Keeps turntable gains value through "streaming partnerships", p. 413; Tokens are bought "through platforms such as Bandcamp", p. 413), but no passage says which firm owes a safeguard when several supply one solution. The only allocation is two-party and descriptive: providers "typically retain responsibility for upkeep and liability" under access (p. 411). The policy text asks for "meaningful remedies when access is withdrawn" (p. 417) without saying who supplies them or through what channel. (My reading.)
- **No duty language.** The article frames safeguards as strategic levers (p. 416), as a source of "competitive advantage" (p. 416), and as policy "measures" that "preserve the well-being pathways" (p. 417). The register is consumer welfare and anti-exploitation, not directed duties. A reviewer could still read "firms should ... provide low-friction cancellations" (p. 416) as saying who owes what to whom, so any gap sentence citing this article must be worded with care. (My reading.)
- **Friction here favours the consumer.** The article wants exit and cancellation made easier (pp. 416-417): friction is what a firm puts in front of a leaving customer, not something that slows the firm. "Seamless" and "frictionless" never appear. I read this as support for `exit-voice-standing` (leaving made costly by design). It also complicates any blanket claim that phygital scholarship prizes smoothness. Here the article asks for smoothness at the exit, on the consumer's behalf.
- **'Control' is always the consumer's.** Every use of control concerns a property right (p. 410) or the consumer's perceived control (pp. 414-417, 420). None treats a provider's control of a space or system as a ground of responsibility. (My reading.)
- **Thin evidence base for well-being.** Satisfaction stands in for well-being (Table 3, p. 414), and the authors call the work "purely conceptual" (p. 417).
- **The Batat citation.** The article quotes "Batat (2024b)" with "(p. 10)" (p. 411). I found the words on printed p. 1229 of Batat's PH-CX article (Journal of Strategic Marketing 32(8): 1220-1243), its tenth page, in the library's copy at manuscript/revision_2026-11/research/sources/batat2024phcx/user_pdf/fulltext.pdf. The article's labels are the reverse of this library's: its Batat 2024b is PH-CX (here `batat2024phcx`, rendered 2024a), and its 2024a is the Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services metaverse article (p. 421). If v8 needs the definition, cite `batat2024phcx` at p. 1229 directly.
- **Anonymity (the authors' call; not decided here).** Pierre Berthon co-authors both this article and the submission. The article came out of the APA Summit 2025, and the special issue's lead editor was its Associate Editor (p. 420). The coinage 'accesship' and the Streams/Tokens/Lends/Keeps labels are distinctive, so heavy reliance on them could point a reader who attended the summit toward the authors. The 2026-08-09 precedent in manuscript/NOTES.md (an Algorithmacy Lab source marked DO-NOT-CITE as deanonymizing) concerned an unpublished, unretrievable source. This article is published and open access, so that precedent does not settle the question. (My reading.)

## Where it bears on the argument

**The v8 gap statement.** The article partly supports it. It prescribes well-being safeguards to firms and regulators (pp. 416-417), and it says nothing about which of several firms owes them: that half holds. The other half needs narrowing. It names who should act ("firms", "regulators") and who benefits ("consumers"), but it does not frame any safeguard as owed. It also offers nothing on trust or fairness by those names. The full verdict is in manuscript/v8_2026-11/stage2/READ_berthon2026.md. (My reading.)

**K3, grounds of the duty.** The article's Table 1 puts upkeep and liability with the provider under access (pp. 411-412). On its own terms a hotel stay would be physical-dominant and access-dominant, a Lends. That would place upkeep and liability with the provider during a stay. But the article grounds this allocation in the mode of acquisition, cites it as typical (Moeller and Wittkowski), and never discusses a stay. It is not a control ground. (My reading.)

**K7, a channel from the guest.** "Meaningful remedies when access is withdrawn" (p. 417) names the need and leaves the route open. It does not say who provides the remedy, to whom the consumer turns, or how quickly. The paper's further duty, to keep a timely route by which a failure reaches someone able to correct it, answers the question the article leaves open. (My reading.)

**K8, allocation among several firms.** The article says nothing on this. It is evidence for the absence, not for any allocation rule. (My reading.)

**`exit-voice-standing`, `framing-argued-past`, `wellbeing-valence`.** Lock-ins, 'roach-motel' flows, switching costs and portability (pp. 416-417) match leaving made costly by design. The two dimensions as "strategic levers that firms can pull" (p. 416) fit the framing the paper argues past: consequences turn on design choices and consumer traits. Well-being runs from the individual (P1-P6) to "societal welfare" (pp. 417, 419).

**What the paper may say** (third person, Intellect Harvard; only if the authors rule that it may be cited)

- Berthon et al. (2026: 408-409) treat phygital as a solution's type, running from physical-dominant to digital-dominant, and add 'accesship' for its mode of acquisition, running from access-dominant to ownership-dominant.
- They define accesship as 'the access–ownership structure through which a solution is acquired and governed over time' (Berthon et al. 2026: 411).
- Crossing the two dimensions yields four kinds of solution, which they call Streams, Tokens, Lends and Keeps (Berthon et al. 2026: 412-413).
- Berthon et al. (2026: 409) observe that 'the phygital literature has remained largely silent' about the ways digital and physical solutions may harm well-being.
- They note that under access arrangements 'providers typically retain responsibility for upkeep and liability' (Berthon et al. 2026: 411).
- They warn that consumers may lose 'continued access when a service is discontinued or a license is revoked', and call for policy that mandates 'continuity and termination disclosures, as well as meaningful remedies when access is withdrawn' (Berthon et al. 2026: 417).
- They ask firms to 'disclose the total cost of use, provide low-friction cancellations, and design equitable tiers' (Berthon et al. 2026: 416).
- Their recommendations are addressed to firms in general and to policymakers, and they do not say which of several firms supplying one solution should provide a remedy (Berthon et al. 2026: 416-417, 420).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the article discusses hospitality, hotels, hosts or guests. Its one accommodation example compares the effort of renting a villa through Airbnb with buying one (p. 415).
- That it concerns AI or algorithmic systems.
- That it prescribes safeguards for trust or fairness. Neither word appears; "equitable tiers" and "exploitative practices" (p. 416) are the nearest.
- That it holds providers liable in law. The upkeep-and-liability allocation is a typical feature of access arrangements, cited to Moeller and Wittkowski (p. 411).
- That it says nothing about who should act. It names firms, consumers and policymakers (pp. 416-417, 420).
- That its propositions are tested. The work is "purely conceptual" (p. 417).
