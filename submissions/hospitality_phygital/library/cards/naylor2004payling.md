## Summary

A customer of a Rotherham nightclub was badly injured while a door attendant ejected him. The attendant worked for a security contractor whom the club's owner had engaged, and the contractor had no public liability insurance. The trial judge held that the owner was not the attendant's employer and owed no non-delegable duty, but that he was in breach of a duty to check that the contractor was insured. The Court of Appeal allowed the owner's appeal and dismissed the claim. An employer of an independent contractor must take reasonable steps to satisfy himself that the contractor is competent; absent special circumstances he has no free-standing duty to check the contractor's insurance; and the owner had done enough by using attendants licensed under the local council and police scheme, whom he had employed for eighteen months without cause for doubt. English law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: Court of Appeal (Civil Division), 7 May 2004, [2004] EWCA Civ 560, on appeal from Sheffield County Court. Latham LJ (paras 1-25), Neuberger LJ (paras 26-54) and Waller LJ (paras 55-60) each gave a judgment and all would allow the appeal. [full-text]
- Parties and claim: "the respondent sustained severe head injuries in the course of being ejected from Main Street nightclub, Rotherham by a door attendant. The appellant owned and ran the club. The door attendant was employed by Mr Whitehead whom the appellant had engaged to provide security at the club. Mr Whitehead did not hold any public liability insurance policy covering the activities of his employees." It "was accepted that the door attendant had acted negligently." (para 1) The defendant was a commercial hospitality business; the claimant was "a visitor to the club" (para 28). The judgment does not give his age or say whether he paid to enter. [full-text]
- What the trial judge decided on the two doctrines the appeal did not reopen: "The judge, HHJ Murphy, held that the door attendant was not the employee of the appellant, nor did the appellant owe any non-delegable duty to the respondent." (para 1) [full-text]
- The licensing scheme: the club's entertainment licence required the owner to "employ doormen who were licensed pursuant to a scheme operated by the Rotherham Metropolitan Borough Council, and South Yorkshire Police"; licence-holders had to be "of good character" and to have had 21 hours of approved training (para 2). [full-text]
- What the owner knew: he "had been using the services of Mr Whitehead since he first took over the club which was approximately eighteen months before the accident, and that he had no reason to doubt the competence of Mr Whitehead and his employees as a result of anything that had happened during that time." (para 2) [full-text]
- The rule, Latham LJ: "Once the judge had determined that this was not the type of case in which the appellant owed a non-delegable duty, the basic principle is that his obligation is limited to ensuring that the independent contractor who he has employed is competent." (para 19) [full-text]
- The duty and what it does not include: "The duty of care with which we are concerned was the duty to take reasonable steps to ensure the safety of the visitors to the club. He owed no duty to them to be insured, or even to have sufficient assets to meet any liability that might arise." (para 22) [full-text]
- The rule, Neuberger LJ: "An employer will not be liable for the negligence of his independent contractor, unless it can be shown that (a) he negligently selected an incompetent contractor, or was in some way responsible for the negligent way in which the independent contractor carried out the task, or (b) the task involved was unlawful, extra-hazardous, or carried out on the highway, or (c) the duty in respect of which the employer is alleged to be negligent was statutory in origin, or on some other basis non-delegable in nature." (para 32, citing Clerk & Lindsell and Charlesworth & Percy) [full-text]
- Two types of case: "The first is where the employer will not be liable for the torts of his contractors, provided that reasonable care is taken in selecting him for the relevant task, and that the employer is not responsible for the tort, eg by requiring the task to be carried out in a negligent way, or in some other way contributing directly to the negligent act. The second type of case is the well established category of exceptions where the employer cannot avoid liability, for instance where the liability is non-delegable or where the task is unlawful, extra hazardous, or carried out on a public highway." A third, intermediate category "seems to me to be unnecessary". "In the normal type of case, the more hazardous the task for which the contractor is employed, the greater the care one would expect of the employer when selecting the contractor." (para 38) [full-text]
- No free-standing duty as to insurance: "Save in the absence of special circumstances, in my view the law does not cast a free standing duty on an employer to satisfy himself that his independent contractor has insurance cover or would otherwise be good for a claim." [so in the transcript; the sense is 'save in special circumstances'] Checking insurance may be one way to "fulfil his duty to satisfy himself in a sufficient and convenient way that the independent contractor is suitable for the task" (para 34). [full-text]
- Why not: such a duty would, in Waller LJ's words from *Gwilliam* as Neuberger LJ quotes them, "drive a coach and horses through the fundamental position of there being no vicarious liability for the activities of an independent contractor save in the exceptional cases, such as extra hazardous activities" (para 42). Special circumstances "could, in my view, include cases where the employer is himself under a duty (whether statutory or not) to insure himself" (para 43). [full-text]
- The duty that did apply: "his client was under a duty to take reasonable steps to satisfy himself that Mr Whitehead was a competent person to whom to delegate the provision of door supervisors at the club." (para 45, recording counsel's concession, which Neuberger LJ calls right) [full-text]
- Reliance on a public accreditation scheme: "the court should be very slow to conclude that, where such a scheme is operated to license persons to perform a certain type of task, it is not enough for a businessman to check that an independent contractor is so licensed before employing him for such a task." (para 48) "it would require exceptional circumstances before a judge could properly hold that an employer was negligent in selecting that contractor, if he had satisfied himself that the contractor and all those working for him were properly accredited under the scheme." (para 47) [full-text]
- When the scheme would not be enough: "It might, for instance, have become notorious that the scheme was ineffective and discredited. Or Mr Naylor may have had experience of a particular door supervisor who, though still accredited under the scheme, had shown himself to be wholly unsatisfactory and unreliable." Here the owner "had employed Mr Whitehead and his employees as door supervisors for at least the previous eighteen months, and there was no suggestion that they had performed in any way unsatisfactorily in the past." (para 50) [full-text]
- Latham LJ's conclusion: the contractor's men were licensed, "And he had had a significant period of time in which to assess the competence of Mr Whitehead and his employees." "I would accordingly allow this appeal." (para 25) [full-text]
- Section 2(4)(b) of the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, used by analogy: Latham LJ records that in *Gwilliam* Lord Woolf "used Section 2(4) of the Act by way of analogy to determine the scope of the duty where an independent contractor had been employed" (para 9). Waller LJ in *Gwilliam* (his para 40, quoted at para 12 here) read the paragraph as laying "two obligations on the employer of an independent contractor, one is “To act reasonably in entrusting the work to an independent contractor” and the other is to take steps to satisfy himself about the competence etc." [full-text]
- Waller LJ in this case: "the job of acting as a bouncer does not in my view fall within the type of hazardous activity which was under consideration in Gwilliam or for that matter in Bottomley" (para 58); a free-standing insurance duty "can only arise where the employer would be under a duty to insure himself or at the least has actually recognised the need for insurance in relation to the particular activity or is of such standing that he can meet a potential claim" (para 59). "One of course has great sympathy for the claimant in this case, but I too would allow the appeal." (para 60) [full-text]
- Order: "Appeal allowed with costs not to be enforced without leave of the court; claim dismissed" (order, noted as not part of the approved judgment). The claim failed. [full-text]
- The judgment does not say when a duty is non-delegable beyond listing the category among the "well established" exceptions (paras 32, 38), and it calls the exceptions to no liability for contractors "exceptional cases" only in the words quoted from *Gwilliam* (para 42). It does not mention hotels or innkeepers (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The non-delegable point was not argued on appeal.** The county court judge held that the club owner owed the customer no non-delegable duty (para 1). The Court of Appeal took that as given (para 19) and decided the insurance point and breach. So the case is appellate authority that a nightclub owner who reasonably selected a security contractor is not liable in negligence for the contractor's man, and only first-instance authority, unreasoned in the text I read, that no non-delegable duty was owed. It predates *Woodland* (2013).
- **Everything is said of selection.** The duty is to satisfy oneself of competence "before employing him" (para 48); the care grows with the hazard "when selecting the contractor" (para 38). The court states no duty to watch the contractor's men at work, to audit them, or to arrange to hear how they behave. What the owner had learned over eighteen months counted in his favour (paras 2, 25, 50), and experience of an unreliable attendant would have counted against him (para 50). Knowledge acquired matters; a duty to acquire it is not stated.
- **Neuberger LJ's first category has a second limb.** The employer escapes if he selects with care "and ... is not responsible for the tort, eg by requiring the task to be carried out in a negligent way, or in some other way contributing directly to the negligent act" (para 38), and is liable if "in some way responsible for the negligent way in which the independent contractor carried out the task" (para 32). A host that designs the contractor's procedure, or sets a system's rules, may be within that limb. The judgment does not develop it.
- **The text of s. 2(4)(b) as quoted here** (para 9) ends "that the contractor was competent and the work had been properly done", without the statute's second "that". The statute's own wording is on uk1957occupiers.
- ***Gwilliam* and *Bottomley* enter only through this judgment.** I did not read either. What is said of them above is what the Court of Appeal says (paras 7-18, 35-37, 51-52, 55-57).
- **The claimant's position.** He was a customer on licensed premises injured by the very staff put there to keep order. If a duty to customers were ever going to stay with a hospitality business despite a contractor, these were favourable facts, and the claim failed.
- **Later treatment not checked.** *Maguire* (2006) does not cite it (searched the saved text).

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): refutes the unqualified claim for care of the person; confirms the qualified one.** K8 says that for independent contractors the rule varies, "by reasonable entrustment in occupiers' law". *Naylor* is that rule applied to a hospitality business and a customer, and the business escaped (paras 19, 25, 50, 54). The paper cannot say that a commercial host answers in English tort law for the negligence of an outsourced security firm's staff towards a guest. It can say the host answers if it chose the firm without reasonable care, if it was itself responsible for how the task was done, or if the case falls within an exception (para 32).

**K7, premises P2 and P3: limits.** What stays with the holder in the ordinary case is "limited to ensuring that the independent contractor who he has employed is competent" (para 19). That is a duty about choosing. The court ties its discharge to a licence check and to past experience (paras 47-50) and describes no continuing duty to monitor or to be reachable. This is the position K7's 'refutes or narrows' list describes: discharge by choosing a competent delegate.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- The Court of Appeal held that a nightclub owner was not liable to a visitor to the club (para 28) negligently injured by a door attendant supplied by an independent security contractor (paras 1, 25, 54, order).
- The owner's duty was "to take reasonable steps to satisfy himself" that the contractor "was a competent person to whom to delegate the provision of door supervisors" (para 45).
- An employer of an independent contractor is not liable for the contractor's negligence unless he selected negligently, was himself responsible for how the task was done, or the task or duty falls within an exception such as a non-delegable duty (para 32).
- Checking that the contractor's staff held the licence required by the local council and police was enough, given eighteen months without cause for doubt (paras 47-50).
- Experience that a contractor's employee is "wholly unsatisfactory and unreliable" could make continued reliance negligent (para 50).

**What it may not say.**
- That the Court of Appeal decided a nightclub owes its customers no non-delegable duty. The trial judge so held, and the Court of Appeal proceeded on that ruling (paras 1, 19).
- That the owner had a duty to supervise or monitor the door staff. The judgments speak of selection.
- That the owner was bound to make sure the contractor could pay a claim. The court rejected that duty absent special circumstances (paras 34, 43).
- Anything about *Gwilliam* or *Bottomley* beyond what this judgment reports of them.
