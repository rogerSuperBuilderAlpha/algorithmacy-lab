- **The 'contestation' sentence, located (question c).** It closes a paragraph that begins on PDF p. 8 and ends on PDF p. 9: "It is apparent that dynamics in spaces of hospitality are critical to understand. Dikeç (2002), whilst recognizing the importance of boundaries in hospitality, argues that hospitality is about opening those boundaries and giving space to the stranger such that mutual recognition is achieved. Hospitality then becomes concerned with provision of the social, cultural, institutional, ethical and political spaces where participants can learn to engage with and learn from one another. Indeed, hospitality space should facilitate not just recognition but also enable contestation and conflict to occur." The last sentence carries no citation, and the editors do not mark it as Dikeç's. It may be their own extension. Dikeç (2002) could not be read in this run (its open copy on HAL was unreachable), so the paper may attribute the words only to Lynch et al. The sentence concerns 'spaces of hospitality' in the social and political sense, not a route kept by a host.
- **Power after the threshold (question a).** The editors name "hospitality as social control" as one of two themes, "a means of controlling the 'other' or 'stranger'" that "acts as a powerful mediating social control mechanism" (PDF p. 3). They report Robinson and Lynch (2007) on "an exchange paradox in a commercial setting whereby the guest/consumer is enticed to consume hospitality of their own free will but the highly regulated experience may cause a loss of free will", and Sheringham and Daruwalla (2007): "The other, while symbolically elevated, is subject to domination by the host, and to the rules of being a guest" (PDF p. 8). Hospitality involves "the dialectics of social control and resistance embedded in each" (PDF p. 5). Resistance is named; no recognised means for the guest is.
- **Several hosts (question b).** "as shown in Robinson and Lynch (2007), the hospitality relationship often involves micro-hosts such as the initial sitting passenger above whose own micro-host is, for example, the train guard and macro-hosts such as the train company" (PDF p. 8). The point in context is that people are "both hosts and guests simultaneously" (PDF p. 8): hosting is layered and the company is a host. The editorial assigns no duty among the layers. Robinson and Lynch (2007) is closed in this run (record: doi 10.1016/b978-0-08-045093-3.50014-x).
- **Protection and duty, historically.** In Greek, Roman and Enlightenment traditions "hospitality entailed a sacred obligation not just to accommodate the guest, but to protect the stranger who arrived at the door" (PDF p. 2), and hospitality as the management of strangers sits within "discourses regarding duties, obligations and moral virtues involving two key participants: the host and the guest (Telfer 2000)" (PDF p. 4).
- **Commercial hosting contested.** Aramberri (2001) "suggested that the host should 'get lost'", calling locals and tourists "'service providers' and 'customers'" (PDF p. 4); the editors reply that "hospitality remains a powerful term for describing social arrangements amongst strangers both within and beyond the commercial realm" (PDF p. 4). Aramberri is closed in this run.
- **Workers (question f).** Hospitality organisations have shown "labour issues such as poor working conditions" and "the significance of emotional labour (Hochschild 1983)"; emerging themes include "the nature of hosting as work (Veijola 2009) and the labour involved in home hosting" (PDF p. 9); a conference track will take up "employee/worker voice and experience" (PDF p. 13).
- **Telfer as carried.** The quotation of Telfer (2000: 45) ends at "extortionately" (PDF p. 8), as the red team says.
- **Bearing.** The editorial gives the keystone the field's own vocabulary for its problem: the host's power after the threshold is "social control", and the commercial host may be a macro-host above micro-hosts. Its one normative sentence on the guest's side is that hospitality space should "enable contestation and conflict to occur" (PDF p. 9). That supports a guest's voice as part of hospitality, against the S1 reading of voice as consumer feedback, but it names spaces, not a host's duty, and its attribution to Dikeç is unverified. It does not support Step 6 as derived (the channel as the host's means of performing).
