## Summary

A ten-page reference guide to Derrida's writing on hospitality, by a philosopher. Westmoreland sets out the two hospitalities. Conditional hospitality is the only kind the West has known: it deals in rights, duties and obligations, it is tied to law and the sovereign state, and in it the host questions the guest and sets terms. Absolute hospitality, which Derrida identifies with ethics itself, lies outside right, law, debt and duty: the host gives place to an anonymous arrival without asking a name or a return. Westmoreland then follows Derrida's 'formula' in which the host who welcomes without condition becomes the guest's hostage, and ends on the relation between the one law and the many laws. They conflict and neither reduces to the other; the law stands above the laws; yet the law needs the laws in order not to be abstract, the laws draw their inspiration from the law, and the two are 'forever inseparable'.

## Critical notes & threats

**What the source claims a host owes.** Two different things under two names. Under the laws, the host owes what law and custom fix, and may question, limit and refuse. Under the law, the host is to give place to anyone, unasked and unpaid, even at the cost of mastery over the home. Westmoreland insists that the second is not a duty: it is 'without imperative, order, or duty' (p. 3). So on this reading the unconditional demand is no part of what a host is bound to do, and whatever a host is bound to do falls on the conditional side.

**Register.** Philosophical exposition. Westmoreland describes Derrida's concepts; he reports no custom and states no legal rule. The conditional side is said to be 'juridical' (p. 1) as a matter of its history.

**Person, role or organization.** The master of a house, and the sovereign state. No firm, no employee, no paying guest. Derrida's host is defined by ownership of a home (p. 4).

**Evidence and authority.** Derrida's texts in translation, quoted with pages, and secondary writers (de Vries, Naas, Weber, Kamuf). Westmoreland is a guide, and a compressed one: his own sentences often paraphrase passages he cites by page only. Where he and Derrida's words differ in force, the card marks which is which.

**Commercial setting.** None. Any application to a hotel is the paper's own step.

**Care with the quotations.** Westmoreland's block quotation of p. 25 runs from 'absolute hospitality requires that I open up my home' to 'even their names' with no parenthesis after 'the foreigner'. O'Gorman (2007, author manuscript p. 9) and Shryock (2012: S22) both print a parenthesis there ('provided with a family name, with the social status of being a foreigner, etc.'). Westmoreland appears to have dropped it without an ellipsis. On the same page of Derrida he prints one sentence about a 'break' ('should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty', p. 3) and the other two print a different one ('commands a break with hospitality by right, with law or justice as rights'). The reader report, section (b), sets these side by side. None can be settled without the book.

**The Kant sentence.** Westmoreland's summary of Kant (p. 4) agrees with the text I read (card kant1795perpetual) and supplies Nisbet's wording at second hand.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K2 (governor: it bounds what the paper may take from Derrida).** K2 lists 'Derrida on the conditional laws of hospitality' as confirming, and asks the run to find 'how Derrida relates the conditional laws to the unconditional law'. On Westmoreland's account:

1. Rights and duties sit in the conditional laws, and that is where all actual hospitality sits (pp. 1, 8). This supports K2's claim that hospitality is a practice with rules and that a host's duty can be fixed by them.
2. A welcome given 'solely out of duty or by law' is 'not absolute hospitality' and 'remain[s] conditional' (p. 4). This is the critical scholar's objection in Derrida's terms. It does not say that a duty fixed by rules negates hospitality; it says such hospitality is not the unconditional kind.
3. The law and the laws conflict, the law ranks above the laws, and each needs the other (pp. 8-9). A paper that grounds the host's duty in rules is therefore working, in Derrida's terms, wholly within conditional hospitality, and should say so.

**K2's refuter, restated.** The refuter was 'critical hospitality theory holding that a duty fixed by rules negates hospitality'. This source does not hold that. It holds that duty and absolute hospitality exclude each other (p. 3) and that conditional, duty-bearing hospitality is the hospitality we have (p. 1). The refuter narrows to a claim about register: the paper's duty is a duty of conditional hospitality.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- Westmoreland reads Derrida as holding that 'the only hospitality that we have ever encountered in the West has been conditional', and that conditional hospitality 'concerns itself with rights, duties, obligations' (2008: 1).
- On Westmoreland's reading, hospitality shown 'solely out of duty or by law' is 'not absolute hospitality'; 'Such hospitable acts remain conditional' (2008: 4).
- Westmoreland describes the relation between the unconditional law and the conditional laws as a hierarchy in which 'the law of hospitality requires the laws so as to not be abstract' (2008: 8).
- Derrida, as quoted in Westmoreland, writes of 'the laws (plural) of hospitality, in marking limits, power, rights, and duties' (*Of Hospitality*: 77, as quoted in Westmoreland 2008: 8).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That Derrida treats a duty-bound welcome as hospitality in the full or unconditional sense. Westmoreland's Derrida says absolute hospitality 'should break with the law of hospitality as right or duty' (*Of Hospitality*: 25, as quoted at 2008: 3).
- That Derrida, or Westmoreland, says anything about commercial hosts, firms or paying guests.
- That the unconditional law is a duty the host owes. Westmoreland calls it 'without imperative, order, or duty' (2008: 3).
- Any sentence of Derrida's in the paper's own voice on this article's authority. Each needs 'as quoted in'.
