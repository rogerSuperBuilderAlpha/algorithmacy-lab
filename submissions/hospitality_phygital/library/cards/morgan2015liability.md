## Summary

Morgan examines liability for independent contractors in contract and tort after Woodland. A contractual duty of care is usually a duty to see that care is taken by whoever performs; in tort a duty of care is normally only a duty to take care oneself, and the exceptional stricter duty is the so-called "non-delegable duty". He calls the label a misnomer, criticises Woodland's use of a hypothetical-contract analogy, and argues that the real open question is the exclusion of independent contractors from vicarious liability in an age of outsourcing.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The misnomer: "This is known, very misleadingly, as a “non-delegable duty”. No duty of care can be delegated, but its performance may well be (whether to employees, independent contractors, or otherwise)." (AM pdf p. 7) [full-text]
- Delegation remains permitted: "Engagement of independent contractors (and “delegation” of the relevant activity to them) remains perfectly permissible; however the defendant must exceptionaly ensure that they take reasonable care (or rather, since it is impossible to ensure that contractors are never careless, he must answer for any harm negligently caused)." (AM pdf p. 8; "exceptionaly" sic) [full-text]
- Preferred name: "what is more accurately a duty to ensure that care is taken" (AM pdf p. 8). [full-text]
- Contract contrast: "A contractual “duty of care” is usually interpreted as one to see that care is taken (by whoever actually provides the relevant service)." (AM pdf p. 7) [full-text]
- Morgan cites Stevens (2007) for the same point ("arguably a misnomer"), in Neyers et al. (eds), *Emerging Issues in Tort Law* (AM pdf p. 8 n. 55). Stevens not read. [full-text]
- Outsourcing as the larger question: the "increasingly common use of independent contractors (“outsourcing”)" raises "questions about the traditional limitation of vicarious liability to the employment relationship" (AM pdf p. 1). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Morgan's reformulation helps the paper more than the label does: what stays with the principal is a duty *that the work be done properly*, which it answers for if the performer fails (AM pdf p. 8). That is the shape of the paper's claim about a host who hands reception to a system.
- In a contract setting (a paying hotel guest), Morgan's point is that the stricter duty is already the default (AM pdf p. 7). This may make the tort doctrine less necessary for hotels than for schools; it also means the hotel's contract terms can narrow the duty, which the paper should acknowledge. That is this card's inference, not Morgan's statement, and the freedom is limited: Woodland para 25(5) says the possibility of contracting out "is limited by legislation" (Morgan cites para 25(5) at AM pdf p. 7 n. 51), and VERIFY_H_v3 reads UCTA 1977 s 2(1) and CRA 2015 s 65 as barring exclusion of liability for death or personal injury from negligence.
