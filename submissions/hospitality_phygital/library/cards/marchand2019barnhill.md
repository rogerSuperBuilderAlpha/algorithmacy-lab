## Summary

Blue Bell Creameries, an ice-cream maker, suffered a listeria outbreak in 2015; three people died, the company recalled everything it made, and it took a dilutive investment to survive. A stockholder sued derivatively, alleging that two executives had disregarded contamination risks and that the directors had breached their duty of loyalty under *Caremark* by failing to oversee food safety. The Court of Chancery dismissed for failure to plead demand futility. The Supreme Court of Delaware reversed on both claims. On the second it held that the complaint supported an inference that the board "failed to implement any system to monitor Blue Bell’s food safety performance or compliance": no board committee on food safety, no protocol requiring management to report on it, and no discussion of it in the minutes while management was receiving warning after warning. Directors, the Court said, "must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it". The Court stressed that it was not judging how well a system worked after the fact, only whether the board had tried to have one.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court, date and disposition: Supreme Court of the State of Delaware, No. 533, 2018; submitted 24 April 2019, decided 18 June 2019, corrected 19 June 2019; before "STRINE, Chief Justice; VALIHURA, VAUGHN, SEITZ, and TRAYNOR, Justices, constituting the Court en Banc"; "Upon appeal from the Court of Chancery. REVERSED and REMANDED." (caption page) Strine CJ wrote the opinion (slip op. at 1). [full-text]
- The events: "Blue Bell Creameries USA, Inc., one of the country’s largest ice cream manufacturers, suffered a listeria outbreak in early 2015, causing the company to recall all of its products, shut down production at all of its plants, and lay off over a third of its workforce." "Three people died as a result of the listeria outbreak." (slip op. at 1) [full-text]
- The claim: a stockholder's derivative suit alleging that the executives "breached their duties of care and loyalty by knowingly disregarding contamination risks and failing to oversee the safety of Blue Bell’s food-making operations, and that the directors breached their duty of loyalty under Caremark." (slip op. at 1) [full-text]
- Procedural posture: a motion to dismiss "for failure to plead demand futility" (slip op. at 2). "The facts come from the plaintiff’s complaint, documents incorporated by reference into the complaint, and the Court of Chancery’s opinion based on these same documents." (slip op. at 6 n. 13) "At this stage of the case, we are bound to draw all fair inferences in the plaintiff’s favor from the well-pled facts." (slip op. at 13) [full-text]
- The court below: the Court of Chancery reasoned that “[w]hat Plaintiff really attempts to challenge is not the existence of monitoring and reporting controls, but the effectiveness of monitoring and reporting controls in particular instances,” and “[t]his is not a valid theory under . . . Caremark.” (slip op. at 2-3, quoting the decision below) [full-text]
- The holding on oversight: "we hold that the complaint alleges particularized facts that support a reasonable inference that the Blue Bell board failed to implement any system to monitor Blue Bell’s food safety performance or compliance." (slip op. at 4) [full-text]
- The duty: "directors have a duty “to exercise oversight” and to monitor the corporation’s operational viability, legal compliance, and financial performance." "A board’s “utter failure to attempt to assure a reasonable information and reporting system exists” is an act of bad faith in breach of the duty of loyalty." (slip op. at 4, quoting Stone v. Ritter and Caremark) [full-text]
- What was missing: "the complaint alleges that Blue Bell’s board had no committee overseeing food safety, no full board-level process to address food safety issues, and no protocol by which the board was expected to be advised of food safety reports and developments." And "during a crucial period when yellow and red flags about food safety were presented to management, there was no equivalent reporting to the board and the board was not presented with any material information about food safety." (slip op. at 5) [full-text]
- Too late: the complaint alleges that "management turned a blind eye to red and yellow flags that were waved in front of it by regulators and its own tests, and the board—by failing to implement any system to monitor the company’s food safety compliance programs—was unaware of any problems until it was too late." (slip op. at 8-9) [full-text]
- Information stopped below the board: "Despite management’s knowledge of the growing problem, the complaint alleges that this information never made its way to the board, and the board continued to be uninformed about (and thus unaware of) the problem." (slip op. at 12) "In short, the complaint pleads that the Blue Bell board had made no effort at all to implement a board-level system of mandatory reporting of any kind." (slip op. at 13) [full-text]
- During the outbreak: "Instead of holding more frequent emergency board meetings to receive constant updates on the troubling fact that life-threatening bacteria was found in its products, Blue Bell’s board left the company’s response to management." (slip op. at 14-15) [full-text]
- Complaints at lower levels: after the outbreak, former employees “claimed that Company management ignored complaints about factory conditions in [the Texas facility].” (slip op. at 17, quoting the complaint) The Court of Chancery had noted that Blue Bell “distributed a sanitation manual with standard operating and reporting procedures, and promulgated written procedures for processing and reporting consumer complaints.” (slip op. at 22-23, quoting the decision below; repeated at 34 n. 110) [full-text]
- The standard: "Although Caremark claims are difficult to plead and ultimately to prove out" (slip op. at 29), "Under Caremark and Stone v. Ritter, a director must make a good faith effort to oversee the company’s operations." "Failing to make that good faith effort breaches the duty of loyalty and can expose a director to liability." (slip op. at 29) [full-text]
- Caremark, as quoted: “[I]t is important that the board exercise a good faith judgment that the corporation’s information and reporting system is in concept and design adequate to assure the board that appropriate information will come to its attention in a timely manner as a matter of ordinary operations, so that it may satisfy its responsibility.” (slip op. at 29 n. 100, quoting Caremark, 698 A.2d at 970; quoted again at 34 n. 111 from Stone) [full-text]
- Two ways to fail: "Bad faith is established, under Caremark, when “the directors [completely] fail[] to implement any reporting or information system or controls[,] or . . . having implemented such a system or controls, consciously fail[] to monitor or oversee its operations thus disabling themselves from being informed of risks or problems requiring their attention.”" (slip op. at 30, quoting Stone v. Ritter) [full-text]
- The rule in the Court's own words: "In short, to satisfy their duty of loyalty, directors must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it." (slip op. at 30) [full-text]
- Discretion over the design: "directors have great discretion to design context- and industry-specific approaches tailored to their companies’ businesses and resources." (slip op. at 30) Caremark, as quoted: “the duty to act in good faith to be informed cannot be thought to require directors to possess detailed information about all aspects of the operation of the enterprise.” (slip op. at 30 n. 103) [full-text]
- The floor: "But Caremark does have a bottom-line requirement that is important: the board must make a good faith effort—i.e., try—to put in place a reasonable board-level system of monitoring and reporting." (slip op. at 30-31) [full-text]
- Failure of the system is not breach: "our case law gives deference to boards and has dismissed Caremark cases even when illegal or harmful company activities escaped detection, when the plaintiffs have been unable to plead that the board failed to make the required good faith effort to put a reasonable compliance and reporting system in place." (slip op. at 31) "That is, we are not examining the effectiveness of a board-level compliance and reporting system after the fact." (slip op. at 31) [full-text]
- Among the pleaded facts: "no regular process or protocols that required management to keep the board apprised of food safety compliance practices, risks, or reports existed;" (slip op. at 32) and deficiencies "that might have been rectified had any reasonable reporting system that required management to relay food safety information to the board on an ongoing basis been in place." (slip op. at 33) [full-text]
- The inference: "When a plaintiff can plead an inference that a board has undertaken no efforts to make sure it is informed of a compliance issue intrinsically critical to the company’s business operation, then that supports an inference that the board has not made the good faith effort that Caremark requires." (slip op. at 33) [full-text]
- Systems below the board do not answer: "But the fact that Blue Bell nominally complied with FDA regulations does not imply that the board implemented a system to monitor food safety at the board level." "Indeed, these types of routine regulatory requirements, although important, are not typically directed at the board." (slip op. at 34) [full-text]
- Nor does discretionary reporting: the directors argued that management reported to them on “operational issues.” "But if that were the case, then Caremark would be a chimera. At every board meeting of any company, it is likely that management will touch on some operational issue." Caremark "does require that a board make a good faith effort to put in place a reasonable system of monitoring and reporting about the corporation’s central compliance risks. In Blue Bell’s case, food safety was essential and mission critical." (slip op. at 35-36) [full-text]
- How hard the claim is: Stone v. Ritter, as quoted, calls it “possibly the most difficult theory in corporation law upon which a plaintiff might hope to win a judgment.” (slip op. at 29 n. 99) The Court lists cases dismissed although fines were paid or, in one, there was “personal injury and death to GM customers” (slip op. at 31 n. 105). [full-text]
- The close: "If Caremark means anything, it is that a corporate board must make a good faith effort to exercise its duty of care. A failure to make that effort constitutes a breach of the duty of loyalty." The plaintiff "has met his onerous pleading burden and is entitled to discovery to prove out his claim." "We therefore reverse the Court of Chancery’s decision and remand for proceedings consistent with this opinion." (slip op. at 37) [full-text]
- Whose loss: "Less consequentially, but nonetheless important for this litigation, stockholders also suffered losses because, after the operational shutdown, Blue Bell suffered a liquidity crisis that forced it to accept a dilutive private equity investment." (slip op. at 1) The suit is derivative, on the corporation's behalf; no consumer is a party. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Allegations, not findings.** The Court decided only that the complaint could go forward. Every fact above is pleaded or drawn from documents the complaint incorporates (slip op. at 6 n. 13). The paper may say what the Court held the law requires. It may not say that Blue Bell's board was found to have breached its duty.
- **One jurisdiction, one body of law.** This is Delaware corporate law on directors' fiduciary duties. It is not tort, contract or consumer law, and it binds no hotel as host. Its use in the paper is as an analogue: a place where law has said what a duty to oversee requires.
- **To whom the duty runs.** Directors owe the duty to the corporation; a stockholder enforces it derivatively; the remedy is damages to the company. The consumers who died have no claim under *Caremark*. The Court treats their deaths as the gravest consequence (slip op. at 1, 37) and the stockholders' loss as what the suit is about (slip op. at 1). So the case supports the claim that a duty of oversight includes arranging to be informed. It does not support a channel owed to, or usable by, the person harmed.
- **The standard is low and concerns effort.** Breach requires bad faith: an "utter failure" to try, or a conscious failure to monitor (slip op. at 4, 30). A system that existed and failed is no breach, and the Court declines to examine effectiveness after the fact (slip op. at 31). K7 asks that the channel work for as long as performance is possible. The case requires only that the holder try to have a reporting system.
- **Time appears, in Caremark's words.** The system must bring information to the board "in a timely manner" (slip op. at 29 n. 100), and the pleaded failure is that the board learned "too late" (slip op. at 9). That is the nearest legal statement found in this run to P3's timing clause. It is a clause about when the overseer learns, not about a deadline for correction.
- **Where the signal came from.** The warnings in this case came from regulators and the company's own tests (slip op. at 8-12), not from customers. The opinion mentions consumer-complaint procedures once, in a quotation of the court below (slip op. at 22-23; 34 n. 110), and employees' ignored complaints once (slip op. at 17). It says nothing about whether consumers' reports must reach the board.
- **Reading the case as about level.** The Court's test is "board-level" throughout (slip op. at 5, 13, 31, 33, 36). Procedures that stop at management did not show that the overseer had arranged to be informed (slip op. at 34). The analogy to K7's 'a person with power' is mine: the opinion requires that information reach the body charged with oversight, and does not speak of power over a single case.
- **Not read.** *Caremark* and *Stone v. Ritter*, the decision below, later Delaware cases, and the outcome on remand. The reporter citation is unverified.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3 (a duty to see that something is done includes the means to learn that it is not being done, and to correct it in time): supports the learning half as law in one jurisdiction, and narrows it.** A court states in terms that a party charged with oversight "must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it" (slip op. at 30), and adopts the formulation that the system must bring "appropriate information ... to its attention in a timely manner as a matter of ordinary operations, so that it may satisfy its responsibility" (slip op. at 29 n. 100). The last clause links learning to performing the duty, which is P3's link. The narrowing is fourfold: the duty is to try, in good faith (slip op. at 30-31); a system's failure is not breach (slip op. at 31); the duty is owed to the corporation; and the law responds with damages after the event.

**K7, P2 (a holder that performs through others keeps a duty of oversight): supports by analogy.** Directors who leave operations to management keep a duty "to exercise oversight" (slip op. at 4), and a board that "left the company’s response to management" during a crisis is described as part of the failure (slip op. at 14-15). The duty kept is to be informed, not to guarantee the work.

**The tort lawyer's objection (stage 0): confirmed.** The case gives liability afterwards and no hearing to those harmed.

**What the paper may say**

- In Delaware corporate law, directors "must make a good faith effort to implement an oversight system and then monitor it" (*Marchand v. Barnhill*, Del. 2019, slip op. at 30).
- The Delaware Supreme Court adopts *Caremark*'s statement that a board must judge in good faith that its reporting system is "adequate to assure the board that appropriate information will come to its attention in a timely manner" (slip op. at 29 n. 100, quoting *Caremark*).
- On the facts alleged, warnings about food safety reached management and "never made its way to the board", which "was unaware of any problems until it was too late" (slip op. at 9, 12); the Court held that these allegations, if proved, would show a breach.
- The Court held that procedures and reports at management level did not show that the board had a system for informing itself (slip op. at 34-36).
- The standard is good-faith effort: the Court was "not examining the effectiveness of a board-level compliance and reporting system after the fact" (slip op. at 31).
- The paper may offer the case as an analogue from another body of law, in which a duty to oversee is held to include arranging to learn in time.

**What the paper may not say**

- That the case concerns hotels, hosts, guests, tort or consumer law, or any jurisdiction but Delaware.
- That the Court found the directors liable. It reversed a dismissal on the pleadings.
- That the duty is owed to consumers, or that consumers can enforce it.
- That the law requires an oversight system to work, or to include a channel from customers.
- That *Caremark* or *Stone v. Ritter* says anything except as quoted in this opinion.
- That the case requires correction in time. It requires an effort to be informed in time.
