## Summary

A chapter of comparative philology. Benveniste takes the Latin word for guest and host, *hospes*, apart into *\*hosti-pet-s* and studies each half. The second half, *-pet-/pot-*, first meant identity ('himself') and came to mean 'master' because the head of a group is the one who personifies it. The first half, *hostis*, did not at first mean enemy or stranger. Its family of words (*hostire*, *hostimentum*, *hostia*) all carry the sense 'compensate, equalize', and a *hostis* was a stranger recognized as holding equal rights under a compact: 'he who stands in a compensatory relationship'. Hospitality is on this account an institution of reciprocal obligation, a weakened form of the gift exchange Mauss described, with a Greek counterpart in *xenía*, a pact with 'precise obligations' that passes to descendants. When clan relations gave way to the *civitas*, *hostis* turned into 'enemy' and Latin coined *hospes*, 'the incarnation of hospitality'. The chapter ends with a parallel family from the root *\*mei-* ('exchange'), which yields *mūnus* and, in Iranian, another word for guest.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The thesis on *hostis*, from the chapter's summary: "The primitive notion conveyed by hostis is that of equality by compensation: a hostis is one who repays my gift with a counter-gift. Thus, like its Gothic counterpart, gasts, Latin hostis at one period denoted the guest. The classical meaning “enemy” must have developed when reciprocal relations between clans were succeeded by the exclusive relations of civitas to civitas" (Abstract, point 2) [full-text]
- The thesis on *hospes*: Latin coined *\*hosti-pet-*, "which may perhaps be interpreted as arising from an abstract noun hosti “hospitality” and consequently meaning “he who predominantly personifies hospitality, the one who is hospitality itself.”" (Abstract, point 3) [full-text]
- The compound: "hospes goes back to *hosti-pet-s. The second component alternates with pot-, which signifies “master,” so that the literal sense of hospes is “the guest-master.” This is a rather peculiar designation." (para. 2) [full-text]
- How 'himself' becomes 'master': "there must be a circle of persons subordinated to a central personage who assumes the personality and complete identity of the group to such an extent that he is its summation: in his own person he is its incarnation" (para. 22) [full-text]
- The master of the house represents, he does not command: "The role of the person so named is not to give orders but to assume a representation which gives him authority over the family as a whole with which he is identified." (para. 23, of *\*dem-pot(i)-*) [full-text]
- *Hostis* is a stranger with recognized rights under a compact: "A hostis is not a stranger in general." He is "“the stranger insofar as he is recognized as enjoying equal rights to those of the Roman citizens.” This recognition of rights implies a certain relation of reciprocity and supposes an agreement or compact." (para. 30) [full-text]
- The foundation of hospitality: "From this point of view hostis will signify “he who stands in a compensatory relationship” and this is precisely the foundation of the institution of hospitality." (para. 30) [full-text]
- The obligation: "The notion of “hospitality” is illuminated by reference to potlach, of which it is a weakened form. It is founded on the idea that a man is bound to another (hostis always involves the notion of reciprocity) by the obligation to compensate a gift or service from which he has benefited." (para. 31; 'potlach' as printed) [full-text]
- The Greek institution: *xénos* "indicates relations of the same type between men bound by a pact which implies precise obligations that also devolve on their descendants"; *xenía*, "placed under the protection of Zeus Xenios, consists of the exchange of gifts between the contracting parties, who declare their intention of binding their descendants by this pact" (para. 32) [full-text]
- In Thrace "this system of obligation was still preserved in its full force" (para. 32) [full-text]
- The institution lapsed at Rome: "In historical times the custom had lost its force in the Roman world: it presupposes a type of relationship which was no longer compatible with the established regime. When an ancient society becomes a nation, the relations between man and man, clan and clan, are abolished. All that persists is the distinction between what is inside and outside the civitas." (para. 33) [full-text]
- Names for the two roles, and a denial: "In Greek, the guest (the one received) is the xénos and he who receives is the xenodókhos". "The one who receives is not the “master” of his guest. As we have seen, -pot- did not have originally the meaning of “master.”" (para. 34) [full-text]
- "We thus understand better the literal sense of *ghosti-pets, hospes as the incarnation of hospitality." (para. 35) [full-text]
- "Thus the history of hostis recapitulates the change brought about in Roman institutions." "But xénos did not evolve the sense of “enemy” as did hostis in Latin." (para. 36) [full-text]
- A parallel in *mūnus*, an office that obliges a return: "If mūnus is a gift carrying the obligation of an exchange, immūnis is he who does not fulfill his obligation to make due return." (para. 38) [full-text]
- Rights under the pact, from the meeting of Glaucus and Diomedes (*Iliad* 6): "This situation gives each of the contracting parties rights of greater force than the common national interest. These rights are in principle hereditary, but should be periodically renewed by means of gifts and exchanges so that they remain personal" (para. 45) [full-text]
- The Iranian word for guest gives the same result: "Once again we end up by defining the “guest” by the notion of mutuality and the bonds of reciprocity." (para. 50) [full-text]
- The chapter's close: these terms all come back to "institutions of welcoming and reciprocity, thanks to which the men of a given people find hospitality in another, and whereby societies enter into alliances and exchanges" (para. 53) [full-text]
- Print pages at second hand: Bulley (2015, author manuscript p. 8) cites "Benveniste 1973: 71" for the *hosti-pet* chain and "Benveniste 1973: 74" for *potior*, "‘to have power over something, have something at one’s disposal’", wording that matches para. 24 of the online text. His edition is the London (Faber) printing [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**What the source claims about what a host owes.** Nothing about protection or provision. The obligation Benveniste finds in the vocabulary is reciprocity: whoever has received a gift or service is bound to compensate it (para. 31), and the partners to *xenía* exchange gifts and bind their descendants (para. 32). Both parties owe and both hold rights 'of greater force than the common national interest' (para. 45). The chapter never says what a host must do for a guest under his roof.

**Register.** A custom described, as an institution reconstructed from words. Benveniste reports what the terms presuppose. He makes no moral claim and cites no law of hospitality, though he uses legal words throughout ('pact', 'compact', 'contracting parties', 'rights').

**Person, role or organization.** Persons standing for groups. The pact binds men and their descendants, and is made by 'Kings as well as private people' (para. 32). The half of *hospes* that means 'master' names the person who personifies a household or clan (paras 22-23). So the vocabulary treats the one who receives as the embodiment of a group, which is suggestive for a paper about hosts that are firms. It is no more than suggestive: Benveniste is describing kin groups and the semantics of *potis*, and he does not discuss corporate bodies.

**Evidence and authority.** Comparative philology: Festus's glosses, Plautus, the Twelve Tables, Herodotus, Homer, Vedic and Iranian forms, with Mauss on the gift as the interpretive model. The reconstruction is a specialist's argument about prehistory. Later philologists may dispute the etymology; I read no assessment of it.

**Commercial setting.** Absent. The chapter stands in a section called 'Giving and Taking', before the section on purchase, and treats hospitality as gift exchange. Benveniste adds that the institution 'had lost its force in the Roman world' in historical times (para. 33). The paper cannot use him for commercial hosts or for the period of Roman inns. Schmitz (1875: 621, read in this run) describes Roman private hospitality as legally defined and in force; the two may be describing different layers of the institution, and the paper should not merge them.

**A check on the etymology of power.** Hospitality scholars who follow Derrida read *hospes* as the 'master' of the guest and derive the host's power from it (Bulley 2015, author manuscript p. 8, citing these pages). Benveniste says the opposite in terms: 'The one who receives is not the “master” of his guest' (para. 34). His *hospes* is the one who personifies hospitality. v6's section on the host's power should not cite this chapter for mastery over the guest.

**Locators.** Paragraph numbers are my count of blocks in the saved extraction and will not match any printed edition. Before quoting, take the page from the 1973 printing or the 2016 HAU reissue. Bulley's citations suggest the chapter begins at p. 71 of the 1973 London printing; I did not check that.

**Edition.** The task named the HAU edition. I could verify its record and not open its text, so the card rests on the Center for Hellenic Studies edition of the same translation, which its publisher says was 'revised and updated'. Wording may differ slightly from the 1973 and 2016 printings.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K2 (supports the premise that hospitality is a practice with roles and rules; background only).** The oldest layer of the vocabulary names two parties to a pact with 'precise obligations' (para. 32) and distinct names for the one received and the one who receives (para. 34). That supports K2's first sentence at the level of words. It does not support K2's conclusion that what the host owes attaches to the role and can bind a firm.

**K1 (narrows).** K1 says that to host is to take responsibility for the guest. In Benveniste's reconstruction to be *hostis* is to stand in a relation of compensation, and what binds is the duty to return a benefit. Protection appears only as the god's protection of the pact ('under the protection of Zeus Xenios', para. 32). A reader who takes K1's duty of care to be the original core of hospitality will not find it here.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- Benveniste traces Latin *hospes* to *\*hosti-pet-s* and argues that *hostis* first named not an enemy but 'he who stands in a compensatory relationship' (ch. 7, para. 30).
- On his account hospitality 'is founded on the idea that a man is bound to another ... by the obligation to compensate a gift or service from which he has benefited' (ch. 7, para. 31).
- The Greek *xénos*, he writes, names 'men bound by a pact which implies precise obligations that also devolve on their descendants' (ch. 7, para. 32).
- Benveniste holds, of the original sense of *-pot-* in *hospes* and *atithi-pati*, that the one who receives 'is not the “master” of his guest' (ch. 7, para. 34).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That Benveniste finds a host's duty to protect, shelter or provide in the vocabulary. The obligation he finds is to reciprocate.
- That the etymology shows the host's mastery or sovereignty over the guest. He denies it.
- That the institution he reconstructs governed inns or any paid hospitality, or that it was in force in historical Rome.
- Any page number, until a printed edition is checked.
