## Summary

A position paper on hospitality as a university subject. Morrison and O'Gorman review how the subject grew from hospitality management into a wider 'hospitality studies' drawing on the social sciences, and argue that the two schools feed each other. Their one worked illustration is historical: O'Gorman's own translation of chapter 53 of St Benedict's Rule (c. 530), on the reception of guests (Figure 1), read hermeneutically and then recast as a 'taxonomy of hospitality principles' in the language of modern managers (Figure 2). Figure 2 lists 33 principles under five heads: business, guest, hospitality provision, staffing and management. Among the business principles is the sentence that matters here, that businesses 'have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests'. The body of the article never discusses any single principle. It presents the figure and moves on to its conclusion about curricula.

## Critical notes & threats

**What the source claims about what a host or a hospitality business owes.** One sentence: businesses are responsible for guests' health, safety and security (Figure 2). Around it sit principles on respect, welcome for all, provision for need and for special needs, and timeliness.

**Register.** Managerial principle. The verbs are those of a code for managers ('have a responsibility', 'must', 'should', 'is to blame'). The authority offered is a religious rule for a monastic house, which is itself a normative code for a community, read through modern commentaries. The article does not present the principle as a custom observed in commerce, as a moral argument, or as law.

**Holder.** An organization, by name: 'Businesses', and 'the business, its management and staff'. The taxonomy then assigns parts to roles inside it: managers are to 'be seen by the guests as the host', the server is 'seen by the guest as representing the business as a whole', and management answers for staff who lack skills or equipment. This is the nearest thing in the hospitality literature I read to the paper's holder: the firm holds the responsibility and its people appear to the guest as the firm.

**Stated, not argued.** The body gives the figure two sentences (AM p. 7) and discusses no principle. The method section says the principles 'emerged' and were 'translated into more modern business terminology' (AM p. 6); it does not show the step from any verse to any principle. For the safety principle the step cannot be reconstructed from the article: none of the 24 verses in Figure 1 mentions the health, safety or security of guests. The authors say the principles came from 'both the Rule and contemporaneous monastic practice' and from commentaries (AM p. 6), so a basis may exist outside the article. That sentence about what cannot be reconstructed is my reading of Figures 1 and 2 side by side, not a statement the authors make.

**Commercial setting.** The article offers the taxonomy as relevant to 'modern professional hospitality managers' (AM p. 7) and claims 'continuity of hospitality management practices over the last 1500 years' (AM p. 7). It studies no commercial host. The source is a house that received guests without charge as a religious duty.

**Role or motive.** Both, undistinguished. The same list that makes the business responsible and defines staff roles also requires staff to be 'genuinely disposed to providing service'. The article does not ask whether the responsibility would hold if the disposition were absent.

**Trigger and allocation.** No principle says when the responsibility begins or ends. 'All guests are welcome' and food 'available at all times for guests as they arrive' concern provision, not the moment a duty attaches. The taxonomy knows one business; it does not consider a brand, an operator, a platform or a supplier.

**Version and what can be cited.** The repository labels the file an accepted author manuscript. The copy is unpaginated. The paper can cite the article by its range (27(2): 214-221) and pinpoint by figure ('Figure 2'); it cannot give a journal page for the figure or for any sentence until someone opens the version of record. The text layer of the saved PDF renders 'fi' as 'Þ' and 'fl' as 'ß' in places and drops some hyphens ('inter relationship', 'co exist'); quotations above restore the ligatures and are otherwise as printed, slips included.

**Self-citation.** The illustration rests on O'Gorman's own earlier work (O'Gorman 2006, 2007; O'Gorman and MacPhee 2006, AM p. 5) and his own translation. No second reader's interpretation is reported.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K1 (supports, in the managerial register).** A hospitality-management journal article states that the business is responsible for guests' health, safety and security (Figure 2). That is a statement of the host's responsibility by hospitality scholars. It is weaker than K1 needs in two ways: it is a prescription in a managers' taxonomy, not a description of a practice, and the authors' own definition of hospitality (AM p. 5) names no duty at all.

**K2 (narrows; defeats the gap statement as worded).** K2 says the literature 'has not supplied ... a holder and a trigger for a host that is an organization'. This article supplies a holder: 'Businesses'. It also says the guest takes the server to represent 'the business as a whole' and takes the manager to be 'the host', which anticipates part of the paper's account of who appears as host. It does not argue from role as against motive, does not name a trigger, and does not divide the duty among firms. On those three the gap stands for this source.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- Morrison and O'Gorman list, among the 'business principles' of hospitality, that 'Businesses have a responsibility for the health, safety and security of the guests' (2008: Figure 2).
- They derive their principles from the Rule and contemporaneous monastic practice, read with commentaries, and restate them in 'modern business terminology' (2008; AM p. 6).
- In their taxonomy the person serving 'is seen by the guest as representing the business as a whole', and managers 'have to be seen by the guests as the host' (2008: Figure 2).
- They state the business's responsibility and do not argue it: the principle appears in a figure and the text does not discuss it (Figure 2; AM p. 7).
- Their taxonomy does not say when the responsibility begins or which of several firms holds it (Figure 2, read whole).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That hospitality scholars have never named a holder for an organizational host. This source names one.
- That Morrison and O'Gorman argue the business's responsibility from the host's role, or separate it from motive. The same figure asks for staff who are 'genuinely disposed'.
- That the principle reports commercial practice, hospitality law or a finding about hotels. Its stated source is a monastic rule.
- That St Benedict's Rule requires hosts to keep guests safe, on this article's authority. The chapter as translated in Figure 1 has no such verse.
- Any journal page number for these passages.
