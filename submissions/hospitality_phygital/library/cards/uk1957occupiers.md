Outline numbering (rr-2026-11-h): §2.3 (what is not specific to hospitality). Section numbering (rr-2026-11-k, v6): 2 The host's power and the host's obligation; 3 Delegation and standing.

## Summary

Section 2 sets the "common duty of care" that every occupier of premises owes to every lawful visitor, permits the occupier to extend, restrict or exclude it by agreement "in so far as he is free to", and limits the occupier's answerability for an independent contractor's faulty work.

Added in rr-2026-11-k. Section 5 deals with people who come onto premises under a contract with the person occupying or controlling them, which is the position of a paying hotel guest. So far as the occupier's duty about dangers from the state of the premises, or from things done or left undone on them, rests on a term implied into that contract, the duty "shall be the common duty of care". The section leaves contracts of carriage, hire of vehicles and bailment alone.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- "An occupier of premises owes the same duty, the “common duty of care”, to all his visitors, except in so far as he is free to and does extend, restrict, modify or exclude his duty to any visitor or visitors by agreement or otherwise." (s. 2(1)) [full-text]
- "The common duty of care is a duty to take such care as in all the circumstances of the case is reasonable to see that the visitor will be reasonably safe in using the premises for the purposes for which he is invited or permitted by the occupier to be there." (s. 2(2)) [full-text]
- Contractors: where damage is due to "the faulty execution of any work of construction, maintenance or repair by an independent contractor employed by the occupier, the occupier is not to be treated without more as answerable for the danger if in all the circumstances he had acted reasonably in entrusting the work to an independent contractor and had taken such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought in order to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work had been properly done" (s. 2(4)(b)) [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. The contractor rule is an example of a general test: "In determining whether the occupier of premises has discharged the common duty of care to a visitor, regard is to be had to all the circumstances, so that (for example)—" (s. 2(4), opening words). [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. Risks the visitor accepts: "The common duty of care does not impose on an occupier any obligation to a visitor in respect of risks willingly accepted as his by the visitor" (s. 2(5)). [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. Entrants under contract: "Where persons enter or use, or bring or send goods to, any premises in exercise of a right conferred by contract with a person occupying or having control of the premises, the duty he owes them in respect of dangers due to the state of the premises or to things done or omitted to be done on them, in so far as the duty depends on a term to be implied in the contract by reason of its conferring that right, shall be the common duty of care." (s. 5(1)) [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. Structures: "The foregoing subsection shall apply to fixed and moveable structures as it applies to premises." (s. 5(2)) [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. What s. 5 leaves alone: "This section does not affect the obligations imposed on a person by or by virtue of any contract for the hire of, or for the carriage for reward of persons or goods in, any vehicle, vessel, aircraft or other means of transport, or by or by virtue of any contract of bailment." (s. 5(3)) [full-text]
- Added rr-2026-11-k. Commencement: "This section does not apply to contracts entered into before the commencement of this Act." (s. 5(4)) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- The duty to keep visitors reasonably safe binds shops, banks and hospitals as occupiers as much as hotels. Safety alone does not distinguish hospitality (R2.4).
- s. 2(4)(b) lets an occupier discharge the duty by reasonable choice of contractor **and by taking such steps as he reasonably ought to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and the work properly done**: here the law does not make the safety duty non-delegable, but the defence itself requires oversight of the delegate. H2 should weigh this against the innkeeper's answerability for servants (calye1584case, fol. 33a).
- s. 2(1) allows the duty to be varied "by agreement" only "in so far as he is free to". Other statutes limit that freedom: UCTA 1977 s 2(1) bars excluding or restricting, by contract term or notice, liability "for death or personal injury resulting from negligence", and CRA 2015 s 65(1) bars the same in consumer contracts, s 65(4)(c) naming "the common duty of care imposed by the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957". For a hotel guest the safety duty cannot be contracted out of for personal injury. (UCTA and CRA read by the verifier from Wayback captures, VERIFY_H_v3 §A; not saved in sources/.)
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Section 5(1) and *MacLenan v Segar*.** In 1917 McCardie J held that a paying guest's contract carried an implied warranty that the premises were as safe as reasonable care and skill "on the part of any one" could make them, so that the innkeeper answered for an independent contractor (maclenan1917segar, pp. 332-333). S. 5(1) says that a duty which "depends on a term to be implied in the contract by reason of its conferring that right, shall be the common duty of care". On its face that puts the paying guest's implied term at the level of s. 2, where s. 2(4)(b) applies. This is my reading of the words. I read no case or commentary on whether *MacLenan*'s warranty survives, and the paper should not assert either answer without one.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **What s. 5(1) does not reach, on its words.** It governs only a duty that "depends on a term to be implied in the contract by reason of its conferring that right" to enter. It does not speak to express terms, to other implied terms (the Consumer Rights Act 2015 s. 49 term that a service be performed with reasonable care and skill was read by the scout, not by me), to bailment or carriage (s. 5(3)), or to dangers that do not arise from the state of the premises or things done or omitted on them. It applies only where the contract is "with a person occupying or having control of the premises"; a guest whose contract is with some other firm falls outside it.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Section 2(4)(b) is narrower than "choose a competent contractor".** It covers "work of construction, maintenance or repair". It protects the occupier only if he acted reasonably in entrusting the work and also took "such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought" to satisfy himself both that the contractor was competent and "that the work had been properly done". The words "(if any)" allow that in some cases no step is needed. The provision is an example ("for example") of the s. 2(2) test, not a self-standing immunity. How courts have applied it to hotels was not checked.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Terms not read.** "Occupier" and "visitor" are not defined in ss. 2 or 5; I did not read s. 1.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Later treatment.** *Everett v Comojo* [2011] EWCA Civ 13 para 33 (read) treats the s. 2 duty as the model for a nightclub's duty about third parties' acts. Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

Added in rr-2026-11-k.

- **K4 (reception tier): supports the bare duty, denies that it is special to hosts.** Every occupier owes every visitor reasonable care for the visitor's safety in using the premises (s. 2(1)-(2)).
- **K8 (holder): confirms the 'reasonable entrustment' limb and narrows the contract limb.** For faulty construction, maintenance or repair by an independent contractor the occupier is not answerable "without more" if he chose reasonably and checked as he reasonably ought (s. 2(4)(b)). K8 says the defence is not available where the duty is contractual. S. 5(1) says the opposite for one kind of contractual duty: a term about the safety of premises implied from the right to enter is "the common duty of care", so the same defence applies. K8's contract limb holds, on these words, only for express terms and for implied terms of another kind.
- **K7 (channel): cuts both ways on P2 and P3.** The Act lets a holder escape for a contractor's fault, which is the defeater K7 names. But it does so only where the holder took reasonable steps to satisfy himself "that the work had been properly done" (s. 2(4)(b)). The statute thus builds a duty to check performance into the defence. It does not require continuing monitoring, it allows that no step may be needed, and it says nothing of a channel from the visitor.

**What the paper may say**

- In England and Wales every occupier owes every visitor a "common duty of care" to see that the visitor "will be reasonably safe in using the premises" (s. 2(1)-(2)); a hotel owes it as any occupier does.
- Where a danger is due to an independent contractor's faulty construction, maintenance or repair, the occupier "is not to be treated without more as answerable" if he acted reasonably in entrusting the work and "had taken such steps (if any) as he reasonably ought in order to satisfy himself that the contractor was competent and that the work had been properly done" (s. 2(4)(b)).
- For a person who enters under a contract with the occupier, the duty that rests on a term implied from the right to enter "shall be the common duty of care" (s. 5(1)).
- So in occupiers' law the holder who delegates repair work keeps a duty to check, and loses liability only if that checking was reasonable.

**What the paper may not say**

- That an occupier is never liable for an independent contractor, or always is. The statute makes it turn on reasonable entrustment and reasonable checking.
- That a hotel's duty for the safety of its premises is non-delegable because the guest pays. S. 5(1) points the other way for the implied term; whether *MacLenan* survives was not established.
- That s. 2(4)(b) covers services generally, such as security, cleaning or software. Its words are "construction, maintenance or repair".
- That the Act requires an occupier to monitor a contractor continuously or to provide a means for visitors to report.
- That the safety duty distinguishes hospitality from other trades.
