## Summary

Hindriks asks why organised collectives violate their obligations when they do not mean to. His answer is the 'control gap': a shortfall in moral control, that is, in how receptive and how reactive an agent is to moral considerations. An agent with such a gap is prone to wrongdoing and so exposes others to unacceptable risk. He argues from this to a 'duty of control', a second-order obligation to prevent or close control gaps, which he also states as an obligation to see to it that the agent's risk levels are morally acceptable. Most of the paper then corrects the literature on 'responsibility gaps'. Where every member's responsibility for a corporate wrong is defeated there is a 'responsibility void'; a void does not show that the collective is to blame, since the event may be blameless or blame may fall on those who set up the circumstances ('enablers'). Voids are symptoms of control gaps, and the excuses members can offer are evidence a collective can use, after the fact or in advance, to find and close its gaps.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The duty named: "A corporate control gap is a deficiency in the moral control of a collective agent." "Corporate control gaps form obstacles for the members to properly fulfill their tasks. Because of this, collective agents have ‘a duty of control’ to prevent or close control gaps." (p. 2) [full-text]
- Who can bear it: "As I use the term, ‘corporate’ stands for ‘organized collective.’ Any organized collective can bear corporate responsibility, irrespective of whether it is incorporated." (p. 2, n. 3) [full-text]
- Moral control is recognising and reacting: a moral agent "is able to bring moral considerations to bear both on her decisions and on her actions"; in Fischer and Ravizza's terms "she can recognize moral considerations and react to them." (p. 3) [full-text]
- Control gap defined: "[CG] A moral agent exhibits a control gap exactly if the causal control she actually has falls short of the moral control she should have." "The moral control an agent should have, is the amount that is needed to function properly as a moral agent." (p. 3) [full-text]
- A gap is inability, not unwillingness: "An agent that exhibits a control gap is unable to respond to moral considerations as it should. In contrast, an agent with a bad quality of will or a level of moral concern that is too low does not want to do so." (p. 3, n. 6) [full-text]
- How a collective is receptive and reactive: organised collectives "employ a collective decision procedure along with a division of labor. They have systems of roles that distribute a variety of tasks and responsibilities to their members (Tuomela, 1995)." "the collective decision procedure of a collective agent determines how receptive it is to moral reasons, while its system of roles settles how reactive it is to them" (p. 4) [full-text]
- Collective moral agency is contested: "As I see it, organized collectives can but need not be moral agents"; "Others take this to be impossible (Quinton 1976; S. Miller 2001; Narveson 2002; Rönnegard and Velasquez 2017)." (p. 4, n. 8) Hindriks also gives "an additional, pragmatic reason for assuming this", namely to engage the responsibility-gap literature (p. 4). [full-text]
- The duty stated: "The duty of control, I propose, is the obligation to secure a proper level of moral control. It requires agents to prevent or close control gaps. This in turn is a matter of resolving a deficiency regarding how receptive or reactive they are to moral considerations." (p. 4) [full-text]
- The argument in three steps: "The first step of my argument for this claim is that control gaps increase the risk that agents pose to others. Because of this, such gaps are morally undesirable. Secondly, agents with control gaps are prone to violate their obligations. This reveals that such gaps are morally bad. Third, agents should be in a position to fulfill their moral obligations and maintain the risks they pose to others at a morally acceptable level. Thus, it is wrong for an agent to exhibit a control gap." (p. 4) [full-text]
- The principle under the third step: "Bob Goodin points out that ‘a curable compulsion excuses failure only for as long as it would take to cure.’ (2012, 18) More generally, if an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it." (p. 4) The reference is to Goodin (2012), 'Excused by the unwillingness of others?', *Analysis* 72(1): 18–24 (reference list, p. 17). [full-text]
- Second-order: "I propose that the duty of control is a second-order obligation. It requires the agent to meet the prerequisites for fulfilling first-order obligations." (pp. 4-5) [full-text]
- The gap is systematic: "As obligations are reasons, such a gap constitutes an obstacle for fulfilling them. Furthermore, this is not an incidental but a systematic problem. It follows that collective agents with control gaps are not properly prepared to fulfill their ordinary or first-order obligations." (p. 5) [full-text]
- What fulfilling it takes: collective agents with control gaps "should improve the ways in which they manage the risks to which they expose others, including but not limited to safety and financial risks. Doing so requires them to assess such risks as well as the deficiencies that give rise to them. Thus, the duty of control is in effect the obligation to see to it that the risk levels of the agent are morally acceptable (Hindriks and Veluwenkamp 2023)." (p. 5) [full-text]
- Outsiders in the roles: "Furthermore, an agent need not be a member to fulfill these roles. An external consultant can enact a corporate decision. And a staffing agency can enable it." (p. 10) [full-text]
- An outside supplier that fails: "Imagine the schedulers did arrange for backups by hiring a temp agency. But that agency does not deliver. In that case, this agency is to blame, even if only indirectly." (p. 10, n. 24) [full-text]
- The hospital: when many nurses and doctors fall ill in the annual flu season, "the schedulers should have anticipated the situation and take preventive measures. They could, for instance, have arranged for backups and postponed minor surgeries." (p. 10) If the scheduling department is understaffed and uses outdated software, "The hospital should have better equipped the scheduling department. Because it had failed to do so, it is responsible for the control gap." (p. 11) [full-text]
- The limit of the duty: in "an unforeseen and unprecedented pandemic" the schedulers "could not reasonably have been expected to take suitable precautions for the situation. However, this also applies to the hospital as such. Hence, it is excused as well." (p. 10) And where operators' responsibilities are defeated by "random factors for which enablers bear no responsibility, such as a brand-new machine that malfunctions", "the agent was not obligated to prepare for those factors." (p. 14) [full-text]
- Maintaining procedures: "irrespective of what happened in the past, the current enablers of the organized collectives are now responsible for maintaining the decision procedure." The next paragraph begins: "If they should have spotted and fixed the flaw, they are indirectly responsible for the wrong decision that was made" (p. 11) [full-text]
- Enablers: "The enablers are responsible for facilitating the operators. They need to make sure that the conditions under which the operators work are adequate." (p. 12) [full-text]
- Finding the gap: "The duty of control is the obligation to prevent or close control gaps. To fulfill this duty, a collective agent has to identify the deficiency from which it suffers." (p. 13) [full-text]
- Two ways to look: "The top-down approach considers the way in which the collective agent is organized. It investigates the structure and culture of the collective agent and assesses the risks it poses to others." (p. 14) On the bottom-up approach, members' defeaters "are diagnostic of the shortcomings that a collective agent displays. They reveal that the circumstances in which members are expected to perform their tasks are inadequate." (p. 14) [full-text]
- Latent gaps: "control gaps can be latent: the risks they pose to others need not materialize." (p. 14) [full-text]
- Before the event: "The obvious way to apply the bottom-up approach is after the fact, when responsibilities have in fact been defeated. However, it can also play a heuristic role before an accident has occurred. When applied in a forward-looking manner, the thing to do is to anticipate extenuating circumstances." (pp. 14-15) [full-text]
- A member's excuse is not the collective's: where a member's broken arm means "the collective agent cannot achieve one of its goals", "it cannot plausibly invoke the excuse of the member as an extenuating circumstance." (p. 15) [full-text]
- Replacement and safeguards: "As another example, consider a nurse who falls ill. This excuses him from fulfilling his tasks. But the hospital is responsible for finding a replacement." Of a surgeon too stressed to operate: "the hospital should also have policies in place that ensure that she does not proceed and perform the surgery." (p. 15) [full-text]
- What closing the gap achieves: "the moral control of the collective agent will have increased. Furthermore, the risks it poses to others will have been decreased to a level that is morally acceptable. Thus, the agent has fulfilled its duty of control." (p. 15) [full-text]
- The conclusion: "The obvious way to try and fulfill this duty of control is for the collective agent to identify the control gap, investigate the underlying deficiency and assess the risks to which it gives rise. Subsequently, it can use the information it uncovers to close the gap." (p. 16) "Thus, there can be a shortfall in moral control, but not in moral responsibility. Furthermore, many of the moral failures of collective agents can be explained in terms of another moral failure, that to prevent or close a control gap." (p. 16) [full-text]
- The earlier statement of the idea: Hindriks and Veluwenkamp (2023), 'The risks of autonomous machines: From responsibility gaps to control gaps', *Synthese* 201(1), 21, argued for closing control gaps in autonomous machines; "Here I apply these ideas to collective agents." (p. 2; reference list, p. 17) [full-text]
- The article does not use 'complaint', 'customer', 'client', 'stakeholder', 'victim', 'feedback' or 'report', and 'channel' occurs only in "the English Channel" (p. 4); those exposed to risk appear only as "others" and, in the hospital case, "patients" (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). ## Critical notes & threats

- **The argument is short and risk-based.** The case for the duty occupies about a page (pp. 4-5). Its premises are that control gaps raise the risk of wrongdoing and that an agent who ought to do something "ought to put itself in the position that it can do it" (p. 4). The second premise carries the weight and is supported by one quotation from Goodin (2012) and one example. A philosopher of obligation may ask for more. The principle resembles the transmission of 'ought' from an end to its necessary means; the scout lists one article on that principle (Kiesewetter 2015, record only, unread), and Hindriks's reference list does not include it.
- **The duty is not said to be owed to anyone.** Hindriks speaks of risks "to others". He does not say that the duty of control is owed to those others, or that they may demand its performance. K7 runs from a duty owed to the guest; the directedness would have to come from the first-order duty.
- **Information comes from inside.** The collective learns of its gaps by examining its own structure and culture and its members' excuses (pp. 13-15). Nothing in the article has it learn from the people its conduct affects. The hospital's patients are those harmed, never those who report.
- **Timing is general.** The duty is "to prevent or close" gaps, and the bottom-up method can be used "before an accident has occurred" (pp. 14-15). There is no claim that the duty runs for as long as a particular performance is still possible. The Goodin quotation ties excuse to the time a cure would take (p. 4), which is the nearest thing to a timing clause.
- **Outside firms.** Hindriks allows that an outside consultant or agency can fill a role (p. 10), and when a hired agency fails he says "this agency is to blame" (n. 24). He does not say there whether the hospital remains responsible; on p. 15 he says the hospital "is responsible for finding a replacement" for an absent nurse. The article therefore does not settle what a collective owes when a contractor fails.
- **Moral agency of firms is assumed, with a pragmatic reason given** (p. 4). Authors he cites deny it (n. 8).
- **Slips in the published text.** Note 27 ends "(@)"; p. 14 reads "prone to do violate"; p. 10 reads "should have anticipated the situation and take preventive measures". Quotations above reproduce the text.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3 (a duty to see that something is done includes the means to learn that it is not being done and to correct it in time): supports the general principle; the paper's step remains.** Hindriks gives a published argument that an agent bound by first-order obligations is thereby bound to secure "the prerequisites for fulfilling" them (pp. 4-5), and he names the two prerequisites as receptivity and reactivity (p. 4): being able to recognise what bears on one's conduct and being able to respond. P3's 'means to learn' and 'means to correct' are instances of the two. He adds that fulfilling the duty requires the agent to "assess" its risks and "identify the deficiency" (pp. 5, 13). The paper may cite him for the major premise. Three things in P3 go beyond him: that the learning must include a route from the person owed the duty, that the duty is timed to the window in which performance is still possible, and that correction needs a person with power over the single case.

**K7, P2 (a holder that performs through others keeps a duty to see the work done): supports for members, is unclear for contractors.** A collective cannot plead a member's excuse (p. 15) and must find a replacement (p. 15); it is responsible for under-equipping those it relies on (p. 11). For an outside agency the text says only that the agency is to blame (n. 24).

**K8 and K2 (a firm as holder): supports, with the dispute noted.** "Any organized collective can bear corporate responsibility" (n. 3); its system of roles distributes tasks to members (p. 4). The limit is in the word 'organized': Hindriks's agent has a collective decision procedure. A brand, an operator and a platform with no common procedure are not one such agent on his account.

**What the paper may say**

- Hindriks argues that collective agents have a 'duty of control': "the obligation to secure a proper level of moral control", which "requires agents to prevent or close control gaps" (p. 4).
- He calls it "a second-order obligation" that "requires the agent to meet the prerequisites for fulfilling first-order obligations" (pp. 4-5), and rests it on the principle that "if an agent ought to do something, it ought to put itself in the position that it can do it" (p. 4).
- On his account moral control has two parts, receptivity and reactivity to moral considerations, and in a collective the decision procedure settles the first and the system of roles the second (p. 4).
- He holds that a collective cannot invoke a member's excuse as its own, and that a hospital whose nurse falls ill "is responsible for finding a replacement" (p. 15).
- He holds that any organised collective can bear corporate responsibility, incorporated or not (p. 2, n. 3), while noting that others deny collectives moral agency (p. 4, n. 8).
- The paper may present P3 as an application of this duty to the case where the capacity that is missing is the capacity to hear from the person owed the duty; the application is the paper's.

**What the paper may not say**

- That Hindriks derives a duty to keep a channel from the people affected, or mentions complaints, customers or guests. He does not.
- That his duty of control is owed to the people at risk, or gives them a claim.
- That he ties the duty to the time in which performance is still possible.
- That he settles what a firm owes when an outside contractor fails. His one remark blames the contractor (n. 24).
- That he establishes that firms are moral agents. He argues it elsewhere and here partly assumes it.
- That Goodin (2012) says anything beyond the one sentence Hindriks quotes; the paper has not read it.
