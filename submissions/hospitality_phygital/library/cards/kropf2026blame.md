## Summary

From the abstract: care robots cannot bear moral responsibility (no consciousness, intention or
reason-responsiveness) but can hold role responsibility from their social function in caregiving,
which makes them targets of "thin" praise and blame and narrows responsibility gaps without
displacing human accountability.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Thesis: robots "can still hold role responsibility based on their social function in caregiving contexts." [abstract]
- Grounds it in role, not agency: "These robots are appropriate targets for (thin) praise and blame, not because they are moral agents, but because they operate within roles tied to morally relevant duties." [abstract]
- Claims compatibility with human accountability: recognizing role responsibility "helps narrow existing responsibility gaps in care environments without displacing human accountability." [abstract]
- Structure per the abstract: meaning of role responsibility; how it differs from moral responsibility; application to care robots; objections [abstract]

## Critical notes & threats

- This is the strongest challenge to the *Transfer* move. If a system can occupy a role "tied to morally relevant duties", then delegating the welcome to a system might hand the role to something after all. The paper must answer it, not cite it in passing (`must-engage`).
- The abstract says role responsibility rests on "social function"; the manuscript's reply (that a welcome's role is constituted by obligation owed to the arrival and by authority to make an exception, which a system cannot hold) cannot be checked against Kropf et al.'s actual definition until the full text is read. Which account of role responsibility they use (Hart's or another) is unknown from the abstract.
- "Without displacing human accountability" may in fact support the paper: the robot's thin role responsibility sits beside, not instead of, the institution's. Confirm in the full text before using it either way.
