## Summary

A philosophical argument, built on Vincent's (after Hart's) account of role responsibility, that
AI-based care robots can hold role responsibility (duties that attach to an assigned, scoped
role) and are apt targets of "thin" blame (attributability), but cannot bear moral
responsibility, accountability ("thick" blame) or answerability, all of which stay with human
actors. Role responsibility narrows the responsibility gap without closing it.

## Critical notes & threats

**Settled: Kropf et al. support, rather than oppose, "role functions can move while human accountability stays".** The full text separates two things the earlier card ran together. A robot can take on a role, scoped to what it can do, and carry that role's duties (pp. 3–6). Accountability and answerability do not come with it: they are "inconceivable" for the robot (p. 9) and lie "with the moral – and therefore also human – actors" (p. 10), and shifting them onto the machine lets humans "evade moral responsibility" (p. 12). On the accountability question Kropf et al. are an ally. They are a rival on one narrower point only. The revision plan's *Transfer* move says ownership "moves only with the role" and that a host who hands the answering to a system "leaves none". On Kropf et al.'s account the role can move to a machine and answerability stays behind with humans anyway. That separates role from answerability, which the paper can use. It also means "ownership moves only with the role" cannot cite them. `must-engage` stays, because the paragraph needs to state this distinction accurately, not because Kropf et al. oppose the conclusion.

The abstract phrase "without displacing human accountability" appears only in the abstract. The body says the same in other words (pp. 9, 10, 12). It does not specify *which* human party is accountable beyond "programmers, the manufacturers (or responsible persons), or other human beings" (p. 9), and it calls that answer "unsatisfactory for the people concerned". That last clause is close to the paper's own worry: accountability that exists in principle but does not reach the person affected.
