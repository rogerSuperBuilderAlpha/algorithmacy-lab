## Summary

Habib sorts theories of promissory obligation into four types: normative power, conventionalist, expectation and joint commitment. Expectation theories locate the wrong of breaking a promise in the betrayal of expectations the promise was made to produce. They come in three kinds, distinguished by what they require of the promisee: assurance (Scanlon), reliance (Thomson) and trust (Friederich and Southwood). Habib reproduces Scanlon's Principle F, states that reliance views demand actual loss, and notes that all three kinds use a condition of uptake by the promisee. He then lists the objections: that the approach collapses promising into advising and warning, that it cannot say why these expectations bind when others do not, that it is circular, and that it fails where the promisee has no expectation. Section 6.2 connects reliance to the law of contract.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- What marks promissory obligation: "Unlike paradigmatic moral duties, the duty not to harm for example, promissory obligations are not owed equally to everyone, but rather only those we have promised. Further, promissory obligations are voluntary; we don’t have to make promises, but we must keep them when we do." (introduction, para. 4) [full-text]
- The taxonomy: "On this standard taxonomy the main types of promissory theory are: Normative power views, Conventionalist views, Expectation views, and Joint Commitment views" (introduction, last para.) [full-text]
- Expectation theory stated: "Theories of this sort claim that promises are designed to invoke expectations of performance in the promisee, and that the betrayal of those expectations is the wrong in breaking a promise, and thus the source of the promissory obligation." (§3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Three kinds: "We can distinguish (along with Friederich & Southwood [2009, 2011], among others) between three sorts of expectational theories on the basis of what they propose is required on the part of the promisee to incur the obligation. The three candidates are assurance, reliance and trust." (§3, para. 3) [full-text]
- Assurance: "Assurance views require that the promisee seek and care for the promisor’s assurance of performance. In other words, the promisee must want to be assured of the fact of the promisor’s promissory fulfillment. The paradigm of this sort of view is Scanlon’s (1990, 1998)" (§3.1, para. 1) [full-text]
- Scanlon's derivation, as Habib reports it: "Scanlon claims that promissory obligations derive from another sort of more basic moral obligations, specifically obligations not to “unfairly manipulate” others. One has a moral duty to keep one’s promises because making a promise will lead others to believe that you will do what you promise." (§3.1, para. 2) [full-text]
- Principle F, as Habib reproduces it: "Principle F: If (1) A voluntarily and intentionally leads B to expect that A will do X (unless B consents to A’s not doing so); (2) A knows that B wants to be assured of this; (3) A acts with the aim of providing this assurance, and has good reason to believe that he or she has done so; (4) B knows that A has the intentions and beliefs just described; (5) A intends for B to know this, and knows that B does know it; (6) B knows that A has this knowledge and intent, then, in the absence of special justification, A must do X unless B consents to X’s not being done. (Scanlon 1998: 304)" (§3.1, para. 3) [full-text]
- Why it binds, on the report: "Principle F is reasonable (i.e., a real moral principle with normative force) because the reasons potential promisees have not to be deceived outweigh the reasons potential promisors have to deceive." (§3.1, para. 4) [full-text]
- No loss needed on the assurance view: "On Scanlon’s view, it is enough that the promisee come to reasonably believe in the promisor’s fulfillment for a breach to be a wrong. The promisee need not suffer a loss, other than by epistemic disappointment, to be wronged." (§3.1, para. 5) [full-text]
- Reliance demands loss: "A more demanding requirement is that a promisee must in fact suffer from some loss or harm in order for a wrong to have occurred, and this is what another expectational tradition, reliance views, propose. The paradigm of a contemporary reliance view is Thomson’s (1990)." (§3.2, para. 1) [full-text]
- Invitation to rely: "For Thomson, a promise is an invitation to rely on the promisor’s performance, where that means expect and plan around that future performance, and not merely come to believe or expect it." (§3.2, para. 2) [full-text]
- The legal counterpart: "Something like a reliance understanding of promises plausibly underlies the private law doctrine of “consideration”, which indemnifies promisees for costs incurred as a result of such reliance" (§3.2, para. 3). And: "as Lon Fuller and William Perdue pointed out in an influential 1936/37 article “The Reliance Interest in Contract Damages”, the damages awarded by courts to those who have had a promise or contract broken is best understood as being proportional to the harm the plaintiff suffered in relying on the promise." (§6.2, para. 4) [full-text]
- Trust: "The third sort of expectational view, trust views, explain promises as invitations to trust, where that concept is subtly different and somewhat more demanding than expectation or reliance." Obligations form "on the basis of the acceptance, or “uptake”, in the jargon, of these invitations to trust." (§3.3, paras 1-2) [full-text]
- Uptake is common to all three: "All three sorts of expectation theory employ a condition of promissory uptake, as mentioned above." (§3.3, para. 3) The paradigm promisee "is aware of the promise, acknowledges and confirms the promissory arrangement, welcomes or at least doesn’t spurn the promised act and expects and/or relies on its execution or the subsequent state of affairs. In the literature these are called “uptake” conditions (the term was coined by Thomson [1990: 301])." (§5.1, para. 1) Expectational theories generally have "the more demanding ones" (§5.1, para. 2). [full-text]
- Objection, collapse: "by making promises out to be merely expectation-producing mechanisms, expectationalists collapse the distinction between promising and other things, like advising, warning and threatening (see Raz 1972; Peetz 1977; see also Árdal 1979, in response)." (§3.3, para. 4) [full-text]
- Objection, why these expectations: "the expectationalist can’t explain why promissory expectations produce obligations, in a way that other expectations don’t (see Raz 1972; Owens 2006)." Mason accepts the collapse, "claiming that promises are just one sort of inducement to trust, and the harm of breaking a promise is exactly the harm of misleading that might be performed by lying or otherwise deceiving (Mason 2005)." (§3.3, para. 5) [full-text]
- Objection, circularity: "So it seems that the trust of my promisee is both the cause and the effect of my promise, and this seems an unacceptable circle." If trust grounds the obligation, "then prior to the promisee coming to trust the promisor, no such obligation exists." (§3.3, para. 6, citing Robins 1976; Prichard 1949; Warnock 1971) A theory that does without a convention, "like Scanlon does", loses the other route to explaining the trust: "These objections are pressed against Scanlon’s theory by N. Kolodny and R. J. Wallace (2003)." (§3.3, para. 7) [full-text]
- Objection, missing expectations: "Scanlon discusses another sort of case, the Profligate Pal (Scanlon 1998: 312) where the promisee fails to have the standard expectations because the promisor (the profligate pal) has made and broken too many promises in the past. In such cases expectationalists must either admit that there is no obligation to keep the promise, which seems very counterintuitive, or come up with some reason for the obligation apart from the fact that the promise created expectations in the promisee." (§3.3, para. 8) [full-text]
- Promisees who cannot rely: Leonard argues that small children and people with severe cognitive limitations "are intuitively owed fidelity, but expectational promissory theories can’t accommodate this judgment, since the promisees don’t have the required uptake, i.e., they fail to form expectations or reliance." (§5.1, para. 5) [full-text]
- A hybrid answer: Kolodny and Wallace propose that "the appeal to the convention would explain the moral obligation, while the appeal to expectations would account for the directed wrongness of promise-breaking." (§2.3, para. 4) [full-text]
- Obligation without agreement: "Darwall assumes that transactions can engender obligations without an explicit “agreement”. As an example of this he cites accepting an invitation." (§4.2, para. 2) [full-text]
- An inconsistency in the entry: §3, para. 2 lists "T. M. Scanlon (1990, 1999: Ch. 7)", while §3.1 cites "Scanlon 1998: 304" and the bibliography gives "–––, 1998, What We Owe To Each Other, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press." The bibliography gives the article as "Scanlon, Thomas, 1990, “Promises and Practices”, Philosophy & Public Affairs, 19(3): 199–226." [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The entry is about promises.** Every account it reports explains why a promise binds. K3's second ground is wider: a host that holds out to travellers at large invites reliance without promising anything to a named person. The entry gives three pointers beyond promise and stops there: Scanlon's "more basic" obligations not to manipulate (§3.1, para. 2), Mason's view that a promise is "one sort of inducement to trust" (§3.3, para. 5), and Darwall's transactions without agreement (§4.2, para. 2). It does not reproduce Scanlon's principles for expectations created without a promise, so the paper cannot take them from here.
- **Principle F is a second-hand text.** Habib is the only open source I found that gives it in full with a page (see the second-hand note in WS_P2_R2.md). Jonker's open dissertation gives an avowed paraphrase ("Roughly put"). The wording is therefore unconfirmed against the book or against a second quoting source.
- **Principle F is demanding.** Its six clauses require mutual knowledge between A and B of A's aim to assure. That fits a booking or a check-in between identified parties. It does not obviously fit a sign, a brand or a listing addressed to nobody in particular; the entry does not discuss such cases.
- **'Reliance' has a narrow sense here.** On the reliance view the promisee "must in fact suffer from some loss or harm" (§3.2, para. 1). The paper's 'invited reliance' would inherit that requirement if it took Thomson as its source, and would escape it only by taking the assurance view, which needs the guest to want and receive assurance.
- **Unread parts.** I did not read sections 1, 2.1-2.2, 5.2-5.4, 6.1 or 6.3, nor Scanlon, Thomson, Kolodny and Wallace, Raz or Owens.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K3, ground 2 (invited reliance): supports the ground's existence and narrows its reach.**

- *Supports.* The entry shows that a family of published accounts grounds an obligation in expectations one party has intentionally raised in another, and gives the paper its two nearest formulations: Principle F (§3.1, para. 3) and Thomson's "invitation to rely" (§3.2, para. 2). It also ties the ground to law through the reliance interest in contract damages (§6.2, para. 4), which is the legal counterpart K3 asks for at the level of a report.
- *Narrows.* Each account needs something from the other party: a wish to be assured and knowledge of the assurer's aim (assurance), actual loss from planning on performance (reliance), or accepted trust (§3.1-§3.3; uptake, §3.3, para. 3). A guest who expected nothing, like the friend of the Profligate Pal (§3.3, para. 8), or who could not form the expectation (§5.1, para. 5), falls outside. So ground 2 binds a host to the guests who relied and in proportion to what they were led to expect. It cannot by itself supply a duty owed to every guest received. This matches the weakness CLAIMS_FIXED records from *Crinkley*.
- *Threat.* The circularity objection (§3.3, paras 6-7) says that an expectation is reasonable only if the obligation already exists. Applied to hosts, a traveller's reliance on being kept safe is reasonable because hosts are bound to keep guests safe, so reliance cannot be the whole ground. Kolodny and Wallace's answer brings a practice back in (§2.3, para. 4), which for the paper would mean the role does more than specify. K3 should state how it avoids this.

**What the paper may say**

- One family of theories locates the wrong of breaking a promise in the betrayal of expectations that the promise was made to produce (§3, para. 1).
- These theories differ in what they require of the person to whom the promise is made: assurance, reliance or trust (§3, para. 3).
- On Scanlon's Principle of Fidelity, a person who "voluntarily and intentionally leads" another to expect an act, knowing the other wants to be assured of it and aiming to give that assurance, must do it unless the other consents otherwise (Scanlon 1998: 304, as quoted in Habib 2022: §3.1).
- On Thomson's view a promise is "an invitation to rely", and a wrong requires that the person who relied in fact suffer loss (as reported in Habib 2022: §3.2, paras 1-2).
- All three kinds of expectation theory require uptake by the other party (§3.3, para. 3).
- Critics object that such theories are circular and that they fail where the other party has no expectation (§3.3, paras 6-8).
- Courts measure some contract damages by the loss a plaintiff suffered in relying on a promise, as Fuller and Perdue argued (as reported in Habib 2022: §6.2, para. 4).

**What the paper may not say**

- That Scanlon or Thomson holds that a business which invites the public's reliance is thereby bound. The entry reports their accounts of promising only.
- That Principle F is quoted from Scanlon. It is quoted from Habib's reproduction.
- That Scanlon has principles covering expectations created without a promise, on this entry's authority. The entry does not state them.
- That invited reliance binds a host to guests who did not rely or did not know of the invitation. Every account reported needs uptake.
- That the reliance ground is uncontested or sufficient. The entry lists four standing objections.
- That the entry mentions hosts, hospitality, holding out or apparent agency. It does not.
