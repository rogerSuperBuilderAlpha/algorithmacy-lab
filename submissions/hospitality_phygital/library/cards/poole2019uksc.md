## Summary

A mother and her two sons were placed by Poole Borough Council in a house next to a family the council knew to be persistently anti-social. The neighbours harassed and assaulted them for several years. The sons sued the council in negligence for failing, in its social services functions under ss. 17 and 47 of the Children Act 1989, to protect them by removing them into care. The claim was struck out. Lord Reed, for a unanimous Court, held that public authorities are subject to the same general principles of negligence as private persons, except as legislation requires; that they owe no duty of care merely because they have statutory powers or duties; and that a duty to protect from harm caused by others arises, as for private persons, in special circumstances such as an assumption of responsibility (para 65). The council's investigating and monitoring of the family "did not involve the provision of a service to them on which they or their mother could be expected to rely", and it had not taken the children into its care (para 81). No assumption of responsibility was pleaded (paras 79-83, 85-89), and the alleged breach, removal of the children from a mother who was not at fault, had no possible basis (para 90). A claim based on the council's housing functions had been dropped before the appeal (paras 8, 10, 77).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The claim and what was not before the Court**

- The placement: "In May 2006 the claimants and their mother ... were placed by the council in a house on an estate in Poole, adjacent to another family who to the council’s knowledge had persistently engaged in anti-social behaviour." (para 3). [full-text]
- Two limbs were pleaded: one "in the exercise of its housing functions"; the second "in relation to the exercise of its functions under sections 17 and 47 of the 1989 Act" (para 8). Only the second was appealed (para 10). "It is also necessary to remember that there is no claim against the council based on its exercise of its functions under housing legislation." (para 77). [full-text]
- "It is accepted that the provisions of the 1989 Act which impose duties on local authorities do not create a statutory cause of action." (para 25). [full-text]

**The general rule (para 65)**

- "It follows (1) that public authorities may owe a duty of care in circumstances where the principles applicable to private individuals would impose such a duty, unless such a duty would be inconsistent with, and is therefore excluded by, the legislation from which their powers or duties are derived; (2) that public authorities do not owe a duty of care at common law merely because they have statutory powers or duties, even if, by exercising their statutory functions, they could prevent a person from suffering harm; and (3) that public authorities can come under a common law duty to protect from harm in circumstances where the principles applicable to private individuals or bodies would impose such a duty, as for example where the authority has created the source of danger or has assumed a responsibility to protect the claimant from harm, unless the imposition of such a duty would be inconsistent with the relevant legislation." (para 65). [full-text]
- The basic premise, from Robinson: "public authorities are generally subject to the same general principles of the law of negligence as private individuals and bodies, except to the extent that legislation requires a departure from those principles." (para 64). [full-text]
- Harming versus failing to protect: the Court draws "a distinction between causing harm (making things worse) and failing to confer a benefit (not making things better), rather than the more traditional distinction between acts and omissions" (para 28); of the two categories, "The present case falls into the latter category." (para 74). [full-text]
- The four situations, quoted from Tofaris and Steel as cited in Robinson: "a person A is not under a duty to take care to prevent harm occurring to person B through a source of danger not created by A unless (i) A has assumed a responsibility to protect B from that danger, (ii) A has done something which prevents another from protecting B from that danger, (iii) A has a special level of control over that source of danger, or (iv) A’s status creates an obligation to protect B from that danger." (para 76). [full-text]
- Landlords: "there is a consistent line of authority holding that landlords (including local authorities) do not owe a duty of care to those affected by their tenants’ anti-social behaviour" (para 77, reporting Lord Brown in Mitchell). [full-text]

**Assumption of responsibility**

- Service to the public: "a public body which offers a service to the public often assumes a responsibility to those using the service. The assumption of responsibility is an undertaking that reasonable care will be taken, either express or more commonly implied, usually from the reasonable foreseeability of reliance on the exercise of such care. Thus, whether operated privately or under statutory powers, a hospital undertakes to exercise reasonable care in the medical treatment of its patients. The same is true, mutatis mutandis, of an education authority accepting pupils into its schools." (para 80). [full-text]
- Para 81 in full: "In the present case, on the other hand, the council’s investigating and monitoring the claimants’ position did not involve the provision of a service to them on which they or their mother could be expected to rely. It may have been reasonably foreseeable that their mother would be anxious that the council should act so as to protect the family from their neighbours, in particular by re-housing them, but anxiety does not amount to reliance. Nor could it be said that the claimants and their mother had entrusted their safety to the council, or that the council had accepted that responsibility. Nor had the council taken the claimants into its care, and thereby assumed responsibility for their welfare. The position is not, therefore, the same as in Barrett v Enfield. In short, the nature of the statutory functions relied on in the particulars of claim did not in itself entail that the council assumed or undertook a responsibility towards the claimants to perform those functions with reasonable care." [full-text]
- Statutory schemes can generate it: "the dicta should not be understood as meaning that an assumption of responsibility can never arise out of the performance of statutory functions." (para 72); "Clearly the operation of a statutory scheme does not automatically generate an assumption of responsibility, but it may have that effect if the defendant’s conduct pursuant to the scheme meets the criteria set out in such cases as Hedley Byrne and Spring v Guardian Assurance plc." (para 73). [full-text]
- Behaviour can generate it: "It is of course possible, even where no such assumption can be inferred from the nature of the function itself, that it can nevertheless be inferred from the manner in which the public authority has behaved towards the claimant in a particular case." (para 82). A council email speaking of "our duty of care towards you and your family" did not suffice: "a duty of care cannot be brought into being solely by a statement that it exists" (para 82). [full-text]
- Custody and care: in Barrett "the effect of taking the child into care was that the local authority assumed responsibility for his care" (para 47, reporting Lord Hutton); Lord Slynn's analogies were a school that accepts a pupil and "a prison which had a prisoner in its custody, and consequently assumed responsibility for his physical wellbeing" (para 47). [full-text]
- Contract with a third party: Lord Browne-Wilkinson accepted in X v Bedfordshire "that in general a professional duty of care is owed irrespective of contract and can arise even where the professional “assumes to act for the plaintiff” pursuant to a contract with a third party, as in Henderson v Merrett Syndicates Ltd ... and White v Jones" (para 42, Lord Reed's report). [full-text]
- Entrustment: assumption of responsibility "can also apply where ... the claimant entrusts the defendant with the conduct of his affairs, in general or in particular. Such situations can arise where the defendant undertakes the performance of some task or the provision of some service for the claimant with an undertaking that reasonable care will be taken." (para 88). [full-text]

**Outcome**

- "I would therefore conclude, like the Court of Appeal but for different reasons, that the particulars of claim do not set out an arguable claim that the council owed the claimants a duty of care." (para 83). The vicarious claim failed because nothing suggested the social workers had assumed responsibility (paras 86-89). The breach alleged had "no possible basis" (paras 90-91). "I would dismiss the appeal." (para 92). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The red team's para 81 is about monitoring, not placement.** REDTEAM_TORT.md (T14) writes: "Placing a family in housing 'did not involve the provision of a service to them on which they ... could be expected to rely'". Para 81's subject is "the council’s investigating and monitoring the claimants’ position" under the 1989 Act. The housing limb, the one about placement, was not before the Court (paras 8, 10, 77). The quotation is accurate; its attachment to placement is not.
- **Failing to protect, not causing harm.** Poole concerns a duty to protect from harm done by third parties (para 74). It says nothing about the duty of an occupier for the state of premises, which arises from causing or allowing a danger and does not turn on assumption of responsibility (para 28 frames the distinction). An operator's occupier's duty to a placed guest is not touched by Poole *(my inference)*.
- **It concerns the placing body, not the operator.** The defendant was the authority with statutory functions. Nothing in the judgment concerns a private body that houses people under contract with an authority. Paras 42, 72-73 and 80 show that a provider acting under a statutory scheme or a third-party contract may assume responsibility to the person it serves, by ordinary principles.
- **Strike-out.** The decision is on pleadings (paras 82, 89, 91). It holds no facts.
- **Social services, children, anti-social neighbours.** The functions were investigation and monitoring under the 1989 Act. The case does not decide what duty a council owes someone it has placed in temporary accommodation under housing legislation.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation), the placed guest: supports T14's narrowing of the placing body, and leaves the operator to ordinary principles.** On Poole, a body that places a guest in accommodation owes no common law duty to protect the guest merely because it holds statutory powers or duties to house or support (para 65(2)). A duty to protect arises if it created the danger or assumed responsibility, for instance by taking the person into its care (paras 65(3), 47, 81). The dossier's row on the placed guest should treat the placing body's duty as public law unless such facts appear. For the operator, Poole points the other way from T14's gloss: a body that offers a service "often assumes a responsibility to those using the service", "whether operated privately or under statutory powers" (para 80), and a provider can assume responsibility while acting under a contract with a third party (para 42) or a statutory scheme (para 73). That supports, by analogy, an operator's duty of care to the guest it accommodates under a contract with the placing body *(my inference; the case does not decide it)*.

**K3 (grounds of the duty): supports.** Assumption of responsibility, reliance and entrustment are the grounds of a duty to protect (paras 67-69, 80, 88). Statutory function alone is not (para 65(2)).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Supreme Court held that "public authorities do not owe a duty of care at common law merely because they have statutory powers or duties, even if, by exercising their statutory functions, they could prevent a person from suffering harm" (para 65(2)).
- Public authorities may owe a duty to protect from harm where private-law principles would impose one, "as for example where the authority has created the source of danger or has assumed a responsibility to protect the claimant from harm" (para 65(3)).
- In Poole the council's investigating and monitoring of a family it had placed next to anti-social neighbours "did not involve the provision of a service to them on which they or their mother could be expected to rely", and it had not taken the children into its care (para 81).
- A public body "which offers a service to the public often assumes a responsibility to those using the service", "whether operated privately or under statutory powers" (para 80). (Narrowed after CV-G: in para 80 'whether operated privately or under statutory powers' is said of a hospital. Para 77 doubts, in a dictum, that placing a family creates the danger.)

**What the paper may not say**

- That Poole held that placing a family in housing creates no duty. The housing claim was not before the Court (paras 10, 77); para 81 concerns investigating and monitoring.
- That Poole concerns hotels, temporary accommodation operators or occupiers' liability.
- That a private operator housing a placed guest owes that guest no duty of care. Poole does not decide it.
- That Poole decides any fact. It is a strike-out decision on pleadings.
