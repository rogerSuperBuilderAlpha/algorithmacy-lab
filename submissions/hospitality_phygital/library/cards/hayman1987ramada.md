## Summary

A flight-attendant trainee was assaulted at a franchised Ramada Inn where her employer had housed her. She sued the franchisor, Ramada Inn, Inc., alone, for negligent security. The trial court gave the franchisor summary judgment and the North Carolina Court of Appeals affirmed, two judges to one. The majority treated the claim as one of vicarious liability for the franchisee and rejected each route to it. There was no actual agency, because the licence agreement left the franchisor no control over day-to-day operation and none over security. There was no apparent agency, for two reasons: the franchisor had made no false representation, since its licence required the operator to identify itself as owner and operator in advertising, on stationery and on a sign at the front desk; and the guest had not relied on any representation, since she was there by her employer's arrangement. The indemnity and insurance clauses of the licence could not be used against the franchisor. The operator, Turnpike Properties, had never been served and was not a party. Cozort J dissented: nothing in the record showed that the disclosure the licence required had ever been made, and reliance was an open question of fact. North Carolina law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Court, parties, claim**

- North Carolina Court of Appeals, No. 8721SC45, heard 4 June 1987, filed 7 July 1987; opinion by Becton J, "Judge Martin concurs", "Judge COZORT dissents" (at 280); the appeal was from summary judgment for the defendant entered in Forsyth County Superior Court (head matter, at 274). Vote: two to one. [full-text]
- The claim: the plaintiff sued "to recover damages for personal injuries sustained on 10 April 1983 when she was allegedly assaulted on the premises of the Ramada Inn on Akron Drive in Winston-Salem"; the complaint, "filed 2 April 1986, charged that defendant was negligent in failing to provide adequate security for its patrons and failing to inform her of the crime rate in the motel’s vicinity and on its grounds" (at 275). [full-text]
- How the court framed it: "This appeal primarily involves the vicarious liability of a franchisor for the negligent acts or omissions of its franchisee, and includes questions specifically relating to actual control by the franchisor, apparent agency, and equitable estoppel." (at 275). The court adds that "The Complaint does not allege vicarious liability, nor is it clear from the record whether the issue was raised in the court below." (at 276). [full-text]
- The claimant was a guest, placed by her employer: "At the time she was assaulted, plaintiff was a flight attendant trainee for Piedmont Airlines. She and her classmates were housed at the Akron Drive Ramada Inn during their training period pursuant to a long-standing arrangement between the airline and the motel. The facility was chosen by Piedmont for housing airline personnel because of its proximity to the airport and the special room rates offered by the motel to Piedmont." (at 275). [full-text]
- Who owned and ran the motel, on the franchisor's affidavits: it "was owned by Turnpike, not by the defendant"; it "was operated by Turnpike under the name Ramada Inn pursuant to a license agreement with the defendant"; and the defendant "had no control over, or authority to direct, the provision of security or other aspects of the facility’s daily operation" (at 275-276). [full-text]
- What the guest swore: "the motel was identified on signs and advertisements as “Ramada Inn,” and that during the several weeks she stayed there, she never saw any sign or other indication about the premises, or was otherwise made aware, that anyone other than Ramada Inn, Inc. owned, operated or bore responsibility for the facility." (at 276). [full-text]

**Actual agency: the test and the holding**

- The test, drawn from other States' franchise cases: liability "depends upon the existence of an agency relationship, which is determined by the nature and extent of control and supervision retained and exercised by the franchisor over the methods or details of conducting the day-to-day operation" (at 277). The court takes its definition of agency from Restatement (Second) of Agency § 1 as quoted in an earlier decision of its own (at 277). [full-text]
- The holding: "we find no evidence that defendant retained or exercised the kind of detailed control over the daily operation of the Akron Drive Ramada Inn that would establish a principal-agent relationship" (at 277-278). [full-text]
- What the licence was for: "The general purpose of the contract is the maintenance of uniform service within, and public good will toward, the Ramada Inn system. Otherwise, Turnpike operates the facility on its own behalf." (at 278). [full-text]
- Security: "Apart from the imposition of a general duty upon Turnpike to maintain its accommodations “in a clean, attractive, safe and orderly manner,” the twenty-page contract imposes no standards nor makes any other provision with respect to security of the premises." (at 278). [full-text]
- Staff: the franchisor "neither retained authority over, nor established standards for, the hiring, firing, supervision, or discipline of personnel or myriad other details of the day-to-day operation" (at 278). [full-text]
- Inspection is not control: "although defendant retained the right to conduct regular inspections of the accommodations to insure compliance with the contract and rules of operation, defendant’s actual control is limited to a right to terminate the franchise agreement and collect damages for any noncompliance by Turnpike" (at 278). [full-text]
- Conclusion on this head: "no actual agency relationship existed that would justify holding defendant responsible for Turnpike’s security arrangements" (at 278). [full-text]

**Apparent agency: the test and the holding**

- The doctrine named: "The legal theory under which an agency relationship may be deemed to exist for purposes of vicarious liability in the absence of an actual agency is known alternatively as “apparent agency” or “agency by estoppel”" (at 278). [full-text]
- The rule, quoted by the court from its earlier decisions in Fike and Daniel Boone Complex: "Where a person by words or conduct represents or permits it to be represented that another person is his agent, he will be estopped to deny the agency as against third persons who have dealt, on the faith of such representation, with the person so held out as agent, even if no agency existed in fact." (at 278). [full-text]
- It reaches companies: "The same rule applies to a corporation which holds out or permits a person (or another corporation) to be held out as its agent." (at 279). [full-text]
- What the guest argued the name represented: that the motel "is part of a national chain of “Ramada Inns” with a high standard of quality and reliability, that the motel is not locally or independently owned, and that defendant is responsible for its operation" (at 279). [full-text]
- First ground, no misrepresentation: "First, we fail to ascertain any false representation or concealment of material fact by defendants regarding its relationship to the facility. To the contrary, defendant required Turnpike, pursuant to the license agreement, to identify itself as owner and operator of the facility and to expressly indicate its licensee relationship to defendant in all advertising, business stationery, and a “clearly visible sign” to be displayed “prominently at the front desk.”" (at 279). [full-text]
- The court did not require proof that the sign was there. It said that "no evidence suggests that Turnpike failed to comply with this requirement or, more significantly, that defendant was aware of or acquiesced in any such noncompliance" (at 279), setting aside the guest's own evidence that she had seen no such indication. [full-text]
- Second ground, no reliance: "Second, plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that she relied or acted upon any representation of defendant. The uncontradicted evidence shows that she was a guest at the facility pursuant to arrangements made by her employer. There is no allegation in the Complaint or other evidence in the record that she would have chosen to stay elsewhere or done anything differently had she known that the facility was not owned and operated by defendant." (at 279). [full-text]
- The court's words for the employer's part are "housed", "chosen by Piedmont" and "pursuant to arrangements made by her employer" (at 275, 279). The words "required" and "necessity" do not occur in the opinion; they are Crinkley's gloss (see Critical notes). [full-text]

**Contract between the firms, and the missing operator**

- The licence required the operator to insure the franchisor and to indemnify it for this kind of claim; the court refused to read that as an acceptance of responsibility, because such an indemnity "concerns only the two parties thereto" (at 280). [full-text]
- The operator was never before the court: "Service of process was accomplished upon the registered agent of Ramada Inn, Inc., and may not be deemed to constitute service upon Turnpike, a separate corporate entity. Under these circumstances, Turnpike was clearly never made a party to this action." (at 280). The guest had argued that suing "Ramada Inn, Inc." reached the operator under its trade name; the court called the argument "without merit" (at 280). [full-text]

**The dissent (Cozort J, at 281-282)**

- "I simply believe that summary judgment was granted too soon; the facts on the issue of apparent agency have not been developed at this point in the case." (at 281). [full-text]
- On disclosure: "The majority’s reliance on the license agreement as factual disposition of this issue is, in my opinion, misplaced. There is no evidence that the requirement of the agreement that Turnpike identify itself as owner/operator was ever followed." (at 281). The two company affidavits "are silent on this issue" (at 281). [full-text]
- On reliance: "It is an issue of material fact not yet resolved." and "I vote to reverse." (at 282). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Two grounds, and v6 reports one.** The draft says the appeals court rejected the claim "where the guest's employer, not the guest, had chosen the motel", citing Crinkley's footnote. Hayman's first ground is separate and goes further: a franchisor that requires its operator by contract to disclose itself has made no false representation, whether or not the disclosure reached the guest. That is escape by notice, and by a notice the court did not ask anyone to prove.
- **Crinkley's footnote and the order it cites do not match.** Crinkley writes "rev. granted in part" for 320 N.C. 631. The text at that page says a petition for discretionary review "as to additional issues" was "denied". The docket number carries an 'A' and the petition speaks of 'additional' issues, which is consistent with an appeal that was already before the Supreme Court on the dissent, but the order does not say so and I found no later opinion. What became of the case after 3 September 1987 is not established here. Later treatment is otherwise not checked.
- **Crinkley's gloss is stronger than Hayman's words.** Hayman says the employer chose the motel and that the guest alleged no alternative she would have taken. It does not say she was "required" to stay or speak of "necessity".
- **Posture.** Summary judgment, on affidavits. The majority held that the guest had forecast too little evidence; the dissent would have let the facts be developed. The case decides what a franchisor must have done for the question to reach a jury, in one State, in 1987.
- **Vicarious only.** The court never asks whether the franchisor owed the guest a duty of its own. The whole analysis is whether the franchisee's negligence can be imputed. The innkeeper's duty of security is assumed to be the operator's ("Turnpike’s security arrangements", at 278).
- **Standards without control.** The licence set standards for "construction, furnishing, and advertising" (at 278) and reserved inspection and termination. The court treated that as protecting the brand's goodwill and not as control. A brand can therefore fix how the hotel looks and is sold, the things a guest sees, without becoming answerable for how it is run.
- **The guest who sues the name on the sign.** The operator was not a party, and the opinion's dates are an assault on 10 April 1983 and a complaint filed 2 April 1986. The court does not say whether a claim against the operator was still open; I draw no conclusion about limitation.
- The Restatement is cited only as the court quotes it (Second Restatement of Agency § 1, through Colony Associates, at 277).

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): refutes the allocation by holding out, in the State whose law Crinkley applied.** K8 proposes that among several firms the one that held out answers to the guest on the ground of reliance. Hayman shows the party whose name was on the sign escaping, and shows the two ways out: a contractual requirement that the operator disclose itself, and the absence of the guest's own choice. It also shows that what stays with a franchisor under the control test is only what it controls day to day; standards, inspection and a power to terminate were not enough. Set beside Crinkley, the pair says that in North Carolina a brand answers for its operator's negligence only if a jury can find a representation that the property was the brand's and reliance by this guest, and that a court may find both missing as a matter of law.

**K3 (grounds of the duty): confirms two of the stated weaknesses.** The claim file says assumption can be disclaimed by notice and that reliance must be actual. Hayman is the authority for both in a hotel case: the licence's disclosure clause defeated the representation (at 279), and an employer-placed guest could show no reliance (at 279). For the table of ground by hard case: for the *brand*, reliance fails where the guest did not choose and assumption fails where the brand has told the operator to disclose; for the *placed guest*, the reliance ground picks nobody; dependence points to the operator, Turnpike, which the court treated as the party responsible for security and which the guest had not sued.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- A year before Crinkley, the North Carolina Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for a hotel franchisor sued by a guest assaulted at a franchised motel, by two judges to one (at 275, 280-282).
- The court held that the franchisor's right to inspect and to terminate the licence was not the day-to-day control that makes an operator its agent (at 278).
- The court found no misrepresentation because the licence required the operator to identify itself as owner and operator in its advertising and on a sign at the front desk (at 279).
- The court found no reliance because the guest was at the motel by her employer's arrangement and alleged nothing she would have done differently (at 279).
- The dissenting judge pointed out that nothing in the record showed the required disclosure had ever been made (at 281).
- The operator that owned and ran the motel was not a party, since the guest had sued and served only the franchisor (at 280).

**What the paper may not say**

- That Hayman turned only on the employer's choice of motel. It gave two grounds, and the first was the absence of any false representation.
- That the guest was "required" to stay there, or that the court spoke of "necessity". Those are Crinkley's words.
- That a sign was displayed at the front desk. The court relied on the licence clause requiring one; the guest swore she saw none.
- That the Supreme Court of North Carolina affirmed, reversed or reviewed the decision. The only text read is an order denying discretionary review of additional issues.
- That North Carolina law today follows Hayman or Crinkley. Neither card reads anything later than 1988.
- That the court held the franchisor owed guests no duty. It decided only that the operator's negligence could not be imputed to it on this record.
- That Hayman is general United States law. It is one intermediate State court.
