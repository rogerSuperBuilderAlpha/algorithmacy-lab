## Summary

Laboratory experiment, 3 (no voice, postdecision voice, predecision voice) × 3 (no, irrelevant,
relevant information), with male undergraduates on a goal-setting task. In the postdecision
condition the experimenter said the goal "would not be changed regardless of what the subject
said" and then kept it. Both voice conditions raised procedural *and* outcome fairness over no
voice; predecision voice raised both more. Perceived control mediated part, not all, of the
effect. The authors warn that the effect makes voice open to abuse.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Sample: "One hundred eighty male undergraduate students participated"; data from one were incomplete, leaving 179 (p. 954) [full-text]
- Postdecision condition: "the experimenter told the subjects that he had already decided on a goal of 10 schedules and that this goal would not be changed regardless of what the subject said." ... "After listening to the subjects' views, the experimenter reiterated the work goal of 10 schedules." (p. 954) [full-text]
- Measures: procedural fairness = "how fair was the way the goal was set"; outcome fairness = "how fair the assigned goal was" (p. 954) [full-text]
- Result: "The voice conditions produced higher ratings of procedural and outcome fairness than did the no-voice condition" and "the predecision voice condition produced higher ratings of procedural and outcome fairness than did the postdecision voice condition" (p. 955) [full-text]
- Postdecision vs no voice tested directly: "for both fairness variables, all pairwise comparisons among the voice conditions were significant" (Scheffé, note 3, p. 955) [full-text]
- Cell means (5-point scales): procedural fairness no voice 2.43, postdecision 3.15, predecision 3.70; outcome fairness 2.11, 2.72, 3.31 (Table 1, p. 956) [full-text]
- "fairness judgments are enhanced by the opportunity to voice opinions even when there is no chance of influencing the decision." (p. 957) [full-text]
- Postdecision subjects still reported more control than no-voice subjects, "even though they were told explicitly that their input could not affect the goal" (p. 956) [full-text]
- Frustration effect as a possibility only: "it is certainly conceivable that postdecision voice might lead to lower procedural justice judgments than does no voice at all," citing Folger's studies where "repeated unfavorable outcomes or communications from others focus attention on possible biases" (p. 953) [full-text]
- The warning: "The subjectivity of control and fairness in voice effects also shows a potential for the abuse of voice-based enhancement of procedural fairness judgments." ... people "might be allowed or even encouraged to express their views to the decision maker—when, in reality, the decision maker has no intention of considering the input. Such 'false consciousness' of control could lead the individuals in question to believe that the decision-making procedure was fair even though, by objective criteria, it is patently unfair." (p. 958, citing Cohen 1985, 1988) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**Settled: the v4 sentence is supported.** The postdecision condition is the "explicitly unchanged" condition (p. 954). Both procedural and outcome fairness rose over no voice (p. 955, note 3; Table 1, p. 956). The abstract-only doubt about whether *both* measures rose is resolved in the sentence's favour.

**The next sentence is contradicted by this source.** v4 continues: "The effect has a limit, and it runs the other way. An invitation to speak that was never going to bear on anything lowers those judgments instead of raising them, and the same research warns that a sense of fairness can be manufactured this way." In Lind et al. the invitation that was "never going to bear on anything" is the postdecision condition, and it *raised* judgments (pp. 955–957). Lind et al. mention the lowering (frustration) effect only as "conceivable", from Folger's studies of repeated unfavourable outcomes and visible bias (p. 953), and they did not find it. The "manufactured" warning is in the paper (p. 958), but it points the other way from v4's "limit": the danger is that hollow voice *raises* perceived fairness. That is why `claim-contradicted` is set.
