## Summary

Grounded-theory study of critical incidents in which customers asked frontline employees for something outside the usual job. Classifies the requests and the factors that lead employees to comply or refuse.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- "Some requests are unexpected and require employees to go beyond their usual job duties." (abstract) [abstract]
- Four request types: deficiencies in "physical resources, knowledge, financial, and time" (abstract) [abstract]
- Employee assessments were "positive compliance factors (motivations and ability) or deterrents to compliance, including policy or legal, potential risk, and lack of resources" (abstract) [abstract]
- Claims to be "the first effort to categorize customer special requests and employee responses to them" (abstract) [abstract]

## Critical notes & threats

- v4 sentence (section 6): "Frontline staff have always weighed and accommodated requests that fall outside the standard case (Beatty et al. 2016)." SUPPORT: YES at abstract level: employees assess requests and decide whether to comply, with policy as a deterrent. "Have always" is the authors' extension; the study is a snapshot of critical incidents.
- v4 sentence (section 2): "A guest can ask a question, turn down an offer, demand an explanation, challenge a decision, or ask to be treated as an exception to the usual rule (Lynch 2017; Beatty et al. 2016)." SUPPORT: PARTIAL. Beatty documents the exception request and employee compliance, not a guest's standing to ask. Customers' special requests are treated as a managerial problem. The other items in the list (turn down, demand explanation, challenge) are not in the abstract.
- Bib note records this as the rival account of the exception (compliance decision against policy), turned into support; the abstract confirms the rival reading: policy and risk as deterrents.
