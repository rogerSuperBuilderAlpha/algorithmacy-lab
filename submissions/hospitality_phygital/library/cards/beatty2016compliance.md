## Summary

Grounded-theory and critical-incident study of "customer special requests" (requests outside the
frontline employee's normal duties) at two firms in the south-eastern United States, an
oil-change chain and a pest-control company: 18 depth interviews, then 133 incidents from 99
oil-change technicians and 261 from 184 pest-control technicians, all male. Requests are
classified by the customer's deficiency (physical resources, knowledge, financial, time);
employee assessments split into compliance motivators (desire to help, ease) and deterrents
(policy/legal, lack of resources, risk). No hotel or hospitality firm is in the sample.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Definition: "We define a customer special request as a customer request that falls outside of the frontline employee's normal or expected job duties." (p. 159) [full-text]
- Customers cannot see the boundary: "customers often do not know whether the request falls inside or outside of the employee's or the company's typical parameters." (p. 159) [full-text]
- Requests are prompted, not volunteered: such behaviours "are not voluntarily performed by the employee; instead, they are prompted by a customer's request and an employee's decision." (p. 159) [full-text]
- Settings: an oil-change provider (190+ locations, 12 states) and a top-10 pest-control firm (pp. 160–161); data from "two large firms in the Southeastern United States" (p. 160) [full-text]
- Discretion with no script: "no policies or procedures exist for most customer special requests at the companies studied due to the wide range of request possibilities. Management at each company talked about employees making their own judgment calls." (p. 161) [full-text]
- Most common deterrent: requests "in violation of company policy or possibly illegal" (57.4% oil change, 68.4% pest control of noncompliance incidents) (p. 167) [full-text]
- "When the company empowers its employees, it trusts them to evaluate the customer's request adequately and to use their best judgment ... The employee must make that call." (p. 169) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

The full text confirms the abstract-level reading and adds two facts the paper should know. First, the sample is oil-change and pest-control technicians, not hospitality staff (pp. 160–161). Second, the exception decision sits with the frontline employee, usually without the supervisor (pp. 170–171). That second point is useful for §6: in the human baseline, discretion over the exception is held at the point of contact.

Support verdicts:

- v4 §6 (line 59): "Frontline staff have always weighed and accommodated requests that fall outside the standard case (Beatty et al. 2016)." **PARTLY.** Weighing and accommodating out-of-scope requests: supported (definition p. 159; judgment calls p. 161; compliance in 79 of 133 and 144 of 261 incidents, p. 160). "Have always": the authors' extension; the study is a recall snapshot. "Frontline staff" in a hospitality paper: the sample is auto-service and pest-control technicians (pp. 160–161). Say "frontline service staff" or add a hospitality source.
- v4 §2 (line 14): "A guest can ask a question, turn down an offer, demand an explanation, challenge a decision, or ask to be treated as an exception to the usual rule (Lynch 2017; Beatty et al. 2016)." **PARTLY.** Beatty supports only the last item, asking for an exception (p. 159). The study treats such requests as customer "deficiencies" for management to anticipate (pp. 164, 169), not as a guest's standing; customers are not even sure where the boundary is (p. 159). Turning down, demanding an explanation and challenging are not in the paper.
- Bib note reading (rival account of the exception as a compliance decision against policy): **SUPPORTED** (policy/legal the leading deterrent, p. 167).

**For R3.9 (rr-2026-11-h, H4).** Beatty et al. show the shape of bounded frontline authority: the employee holds the judgment on the single case (pp. 161, 169), company policy is the commonest reason that judgment says no (p. 167), the customer cannot see where the bound lies (pp. 159, 169), and supervisors are rarely consulted (note 4, p. 171). Discretion is real and the rule above it is not the employee's to change. That is the paper's case/rule distinction (PO §4) in a non-hospitality sample; cite it for the structure, and carry the hospitality instance with Golubovskaya et al. (2017, p. 1297) or Hewagama et al. (2019, abstract).
