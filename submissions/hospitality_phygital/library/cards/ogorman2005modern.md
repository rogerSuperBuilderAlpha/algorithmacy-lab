## Summary

A survey of hospitality in Greek, Roman and biblical texts, offered as a summary of findings from O'Gorman's continuing research. For Greece and Rome he takes hospitality in four settings: mythology, the household, public (inter-state) hospitality and commercial hospitality. In the first three he finds a sacred and reciprocal institution with duties on both sides: to receive the stranger, to protect him, to represent him at law, and to return the favour. The commercial inns get two short passages and no duties. He then reads the Old and New Testaments for the same themes and distils 'five dimensions of hospitality': honourable tradition, fundamental to human existence, stratified, diversified, and central to human endeavour. Under 'stratified' he says that hospitality was codified as private, civic or business, that reciprocity became 'legally defined', and that particular persons came to hold 'formal and defined responsibilities for hospitality'.

## Critical notes & threats

**What the source claims a host owes.** In Greek and Roman guest-friendship: to receive the guest-friend into the house, to assist him, to protect him and to represent him in court (pp. 142-145). In scripture: to welcome and care for strangers (p. 147). In his summary: hospitality meets 'basic human needs (food, drink, shelter and security)' (p. 148), and in civic and business hospitality certain persons are responsible 'for the protection of the guest/stranger' (p. 149).

**Register.** Custom and ancient law, described. O'Gorman reports what texts say hosts did and were bound to do, calls the relations 'formal ones, with legal obligations on both sides' (p. 144), and records that breach was 'both an impiety and a temporal crime' (p. 148). He makes no moral argument of his own. The last pages slide towards a claim about human nature (p. 150) and about the standing of hospitality management as a profession (p. 148), neither of which the evidence reaches.

**Person, role or organization.** Persons bound by a tie, and one role. The duties attach to a man who stands in a relation of hospitality with a particular stranger, a relation made by gift, token or decree and passed to descendants (pp. 143, 145-146). The *proxenos* is the exception: an office whose holder receives and represents visitors from a foreign state (p. 144). The summary generalizes this to 'persons responsible for formal hospitality' and 'particular individuals ... recognised as having formal and defined responsibilities' (p. 149). No organization is named as holder. The state appears as a party to public hospitality and as builder of inns (pp. 144-146), not as a host with duties to a guest.

**The duty is reciprocal, and prudential at root.** Its first ground in this article is self-protection: 'one protects the stranger in order to be protected from him' (p. 142), 'the protection of others in order to be protected from others' (p. 148). The Greek and Roman tradition 'stressed formal reciprocal obligations' and favoured worthy recipients over needy ones (p. 148). A paper that wants the host's duty to rest on the guest's dependence will find reciprocity here instead.

**Commercial setting: the duty is not shown there.** This is the card's main caution. Every duty O'Gorman documents sits in private or public guest-friendship. His two passages on commerce (pp. 144-145 for Greece, p. 146 for Rome) name the types of establishment and their customers and state no duty of the keeper. On his account the inns had a reputation for attracting people 'too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality' and were known for 'violence and danger' (p. 146). The claim that business hospitality 'retains the key foundations' of private hospitality, and that its managers answer for 'the protection of the guest/stranger', appears only in the summary bullets (p. 149), with no text cited for the commercial case. That last observation is mine: O'Gorman does not flag the gap.

**Evidence and authority.** Ancient authors cited by work and section, mostly without quotation, and modern handbooks. The Roman duties come from Schmitz's 1875 dictionary entry almost word for word (p. 145; compare Schmitz 1875: 621, which I read: the obligations 'were to receive in his house his hospes when travelling ..., and to protect, and, in case of need, to represent him as his patron in the courts of justice'). The etymology comes from a desk dictionary. O'Gorman writes 'Reese (1993)' for Reece (p. 142). The paper should treat the article as a hospitality scholar's digest and cite primary or specialist sources for any ancient particular.

**Version.** Journal pages can be cited; the file is the typeset article. One sentence on p. 147 breaks a line at 'well-/being', so I have not quoted that word.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K1 (supports for private and civic hospitality; narrows for commercial hosts).** A history written inside hospitality studies states the host's duties to receive and protect (pp. 143-145, 148). K1's third refuter asks for sources that confine the duty to private or gratuitous hospitality. O'Gorman does not deny the duty of commercial hosts, but his evidence confines it in fact: the duty is documented for guest-friends, and the inns appear as the resort of those without such ties. 'Histories of hospitality state the host's duty to protect' is supported; 'histories show that commercial hosts bore it' is not, on this article.

**K2 (supports the premise; narrows the gap statement slightly).** The article treats hospitality as a codified practice with duties on both sides and distinct standing for host and guest (pp. 144-145, 149), which supports K2's first sentence. It also says that in civic and business hospitality 'particular individuals' held 'formal and defined responsibilities' (p. 149). That is a duty described as attaching to an office. It names no organization, gives no trigger beyond the making of the tie (gift, token, decree: pp. 143, 145-146), and offers no argument from role as against motive.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- O'Gorman's survey of Greek and Roman texts finds that ties of hospitality carried duties to receive and to protect the guest and to represent him in court (2005: 144-145).
- He records that Plato's *Laws* treat the relations of host and stranger as 'formal ones, with legal obligations on both sides' (2005: 144).
- He concludes that hospitality was codified as 'private, civic or business' and that in the civic and business forms particular persons held 'formal and defined responsibilities for hospitality' (2005: 149).
- On his account commercial inns in Rome had a reputation for attracting those 'too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality', and 'a reputation for bedbugs, discomfort, violence and danger' (2005: 146).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That O'Gorman shows ancient commercial hosts owed guests protection. He states it of business hospitality in a summary bullet (p. 149) and documents it only for guest-friendship.
- That the duty he describes rested on the guest's need or dependence. He stresses reciprocity and self-protection (pp. 142, 148).
- That he assigns the duty to an organization or argues it from role.
- Any ancient particular on his authority alone; the Roman passage is Schmitz (1875).
