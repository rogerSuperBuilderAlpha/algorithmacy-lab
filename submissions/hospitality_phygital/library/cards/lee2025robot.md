## Summary

A cross-sectional retrospective survey in Macau. Lee and colleagues reached respondents through WeChat by judgmental sampling and screened them with one question, whether they had experienced a robot service failure; 330 of 461 questionnaires were usable. Respondents rated the remembered failure (controllability, severity, reliability), their satisfaction with the robot service, their intention to switch to human-staffed service, their trust stance towards technology and a zone-of-tolerance gap. PLS-SEM found no direct effect of the failure on switching intention; the failure's dimensions lowered satisfaction, and satisfaction was related to switching intention. The authors read this as loss aversion and status-quo bias: people stay with the robot. 'Robot service' covers telephone self-service, hotel check-in and financial recommendations, and the authors call respondents 'tourists' without screening for travel.

## Critical notes & threats

- **Not a hospitality sample.** Respondents are WeChat contacts in Macau who said yes to one screening question (pp. 7-8). The robots range from telephone self-service to financial advice (p. 8). 'Tourists' is the authors' label.
- **The wrong outcome for K9.** The items ask about switching to human-staffed service (Table 1, p. 8), that is, choosing a different mode or provider next time. They do not ask whom the respondent turned to during the failure, or whether a person fixed it.
- **Reporting faults.** The sign of the satisfaction-to-switching path differs between text (p. 9) and table (p. 13); the text's H2a coefficient is garbled (p. 9); H1 is reported non-significant at t = 1.959 (p. 13); several sentences are garbled (the abstract reads "Quantitative approach through is adopted.", p. 1). The correction notice fixes none of these.
- **Design.** Retrospective self-report, cross-sectional, judgmental and young sample; switching intention is poorly explained (R-squared 0.196).

## Where it bears on the argument

**K9, empirical side: narrows (rival).** In this sample a remembered robot failure did not directly raise intention to switch to human-staffed service (p. 9), and mean intention was low (Table 2, p. 10). A reading of K9 on which guests, when automation fails, turn to people finds no support here. The study cannot speak to recourse within a stay, because it never measured it.

**K7, P4, first half: consistent, not evidence.** The authors' call to find "silent customers with some sort of grievance" (p. 17) fits the worry that guests do not report. It is a recommendation, not a finding.

**K7, P4, second half: no evidence.** The recommendation that staff be able to "remedy service deficiencies" after a robot failure (p. 17) is the authors' advice, not a result.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In a survey of 330 people in Macau recalling a failure by a service robot of any kind, recruited by judgmental sampling through WeChat (pp. 7-8), Lee et al. (2025: 9, 13) report no significant direct effect of the failure on intention to switch to human-staffed service (β = 0.136, t = 1.959, a borderline value).
- Lee et al. (2025: 17) nonetheless recommend that service staff stand behind robot services and be able to remedy failures and follow up.

**What it may not say**

- That hotel guests or tourists as such were studied (pp. 7-8).
- That guests do not seek staff when a robot fails; the study did not measure it.
- The direction or size of the satisfaction-to-switching effect without noting that text and table disagree (pp. 9, 13).
