## Summary

The UK Supreme Court held that an education authority owed a pupil a "non-delegable" duty of care during a swimming lesson run by an independent contractor in school hours. Lord Sumption, for a unanimous court, separates vicarious liability (liability for another's tort) from a non-delegable duty (a personal duty to see that care is taken by whoever performs the work). He sets out five defining features of the second category: a vulnerable or dependent claimant; an antecedent relationship placing the claimant in the defendant's custody or care, from which an assumed positive duty to protect can be imputed; no control by the claimant over how the defendant performs; delegation of an integral part of the assumed function, with the custody that goes with it; and negligence in that very function. Lady Hale adds that the institution is liable because it "has undertaken" the care, and that choosing competent people does not discharge the undertaking.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The core distinction between work and duty: "The work required to perform such a duty may well be delegable, and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant’s. Its delegation makes no difference to his legal responsibility for the proper performance of a duty which is in law his own." (para 7) [full-text]
- The three "critical characteristics" of the relevant category: the duty arises "because of an antecedent relationship between the defendant and the claimant"; it is "a positive or affirmative duty to protect a particular class of persons against a particular class of risks"; and it is "by virtue of that relationship personal to the defendant." (para 7) [full-text]
- What a non-delegable duty is: cases "in which the ordinary principle is displaced and the duty extends beyond being careful, to procuring the careful performance of work delegated to others." (para 5) [full-text]
- The ground is an assumption of responsibility: the defendant must be taken "not just to have assumed a positive duty, but to have assumed responsibility for the exercise of due care by any one to whom he may delegate its performance." (para 11) [full-text]
- A positive duty is not automatically personal: "It does not, however, follow from the mere existence of a positive duty that it is personal to the defendant so as to make it non-delegable." The relevant factors are "the vulnerability of the claimant, the existence of a relationship between the claimant and the defendant by virtue of which the latter has a degree of protective custody over him, and the delegation of that custody to another person." (para 12) [full-text]
- The five defining features (para 23 (1)-(5)), including: "There is an antecedent relationship between the claimant and the defendant, independent of the negligent act or omission itself, (i) which places the claimant in the actual custody, charge or care of the defendant, and (ii) from which it is possible to impute to the defendant the assumption of a positive duty to protect the claimant from harm" (para 23(2)); "The claimant has no control over how the defendant chooses to perform those obligations, i.e. whether personally or through employees or through third parties." (para 23(3)) [full-text]
- Control is not the ground: "Where a non-delegable duty arises, the defendant is liable not because he has control but in spite of the fact that he may have none. The essential element in my view is not control of the environment in which the claimant is injured, but control over the claimant for the purpose of performing a function for which the defendant has assumed responsibility." (para 24) [full-text]
- Arranging is not performing: no liability where "on analysis their own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance" (para 25(3)); the Ministry of Defence was not liable for a contracted hospital because there was "no delegation of any function which the Ministry had assumed personal responsibility to carry out" (para 24). [full-text]
- Authority and answerability tied together: schools "can do only if they are allowed authority over them. That authority confers on them a significant degree of control. When the school’s own control is delegated to someone else for the purpose of performing part of the school’s own educational function, it is wholly reasonable that the school should be answerable for the careful exercise of its control by the delegate." (para 25(1)) [full-text]
- Lady Hale: "The reason why the hospital or school is liable is that the hospital has undertaken to care for the patient, and the school has undertaken to teach the pupil, and that responsibility is not discharged simply by choosing apparently competent people to do it." (para 34) [full-text]
- Outsourcing as the context: Lady Hale notes that large organisations "may well outsource their responsibilities to much poorer and un- or under-insured contractors" (para 42). [full-text]
- Contract and tort: a contracting party "will normally be taken to contract that the work will be done carefully by whomever he may get to do it" (para 7, citing Photo Production v Securicor, Lord Diplock). [full-text]
- The judgment does not mention inns, innkeepers or hotels (searched the full text 2026-11-10). Bailees for reward are mentioned (para 7, Morris v C.W. Martin). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Scope.** The second category is built on vulnerable claimants in custody: patients, children, prisoners, care-home residents (para 23(1)). An adult hotel guest is not obviously "especially vulnerable", and Lord Sumption warns against letting the exception "eat up the rule" (para 22). The paper can use the *structure* (performance delegable, assumed duty retained, ground in undertaking not control). It should not claim that English law already treats a hotel's duty to guests as non-delegable in this sense. That would need innkeeper or occupier authority (H1's tradition).
- **Against Kong.** Kong grounds the deployer's retained answerability in "guidance control over the act of delegation" (kong2026delegation, p. 4). Woodland says the non-delegable duty attaches "not because he has control but in spite of the fact that he may have none" (para 24), and grounds it in an assumed undertaking towards a person in one's charge (paras 11, 23(2)). The two are not strictly opposed: Kong speaks of control over the *act of delegating*, Woodland of control over the *environment*. But Woodland locates the ground in the relationship with the claimant, which Kong's account does not need.
- **Contested doctrine.** Morgan (2015) calls the label "very misleadingly" applied and criticises the contract analogy (morgan2015liability). Beuermann (2018, Medical Law Review, abstract only, not carded) disputes that hospitals owe such a duty at all.
- **Jurisdiction.** English law; the Australian cases it relies on (paras 18-21) were later narrowed in Australia.
- Quotation marks inside the judgment use typographic apostrophes; quotes above keep them.
