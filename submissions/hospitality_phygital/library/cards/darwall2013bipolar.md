## Summary

Darwall separates moral obligation "period" from bipolar (directed) obligation: an obligation owed by
an obligor *to* an obligee. A bipolar obligation entails a correlative claim right, and it gives the
obligee an individual, discretionary authority — a "standing" — to address demands to the obligor and
hold him personally answerable: to insist, complain, seek apology or compensation, forgive. The chapter
ties together the three things the paper's standing needs: address to someone (second-personal),
correlativity with a duty (Hohfeld, via Thompson), and claiming as the right holder's own act
(Feinberg). It also draws the legal analogy itself: civil standing belongs to the wronged party,
criminal prosecution to "the people".

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- "Bipolar obligations always involve a relation between two “poles”: an agent who is obligated (the obligor) and an individual, group, etc., to whom she is obligated, tied, or bound (the obligee)." (p. 20) [full-text]
- "a promisee has a distinctive normative standing or authority in relation to the promiser that third parties do not have." (p. 20) [full-text]
- Moral obligation is second-personal because of "its conceptual connection to moral responsibility or accountability, which entails a standing to address (and be addressed by) legitimate claims and demands." (p. 21) [full-text]
- "bipolar obligations always involve an assumed individual authority or standing that the obligee has with respect to the obligor that others do not have." (p. 27) [full-text]
- On Hohfeld: "X doesn’t simply have a duty not to do A; X has this duty to Y. And this bipolar duty entails a claim right that Y has against X that X not do A. Claim rights and bipolar obligations are, in the jargon, conceptually “correlative.”" (p. 28) [full-text]
- Directed structures can be "sung in various keys": "moral, conventional, customary, legal, or “ludic”" (p. 28, quoting Thompson) [full-text]
- Darwall on Feinberg: "Feinberg emphasizes that the right holder’s standing or authority to demand or claim her rights enters into the very idea that she has a claim right." He then quotes Feinberg (1980: 151): "It is claiming ... that gives rights their special moral significance" (p. 30) [full-text]
- "An apology is, by definition, addressed to someone who receives it and who has the authority to accept it or not. If a victim comes upon an unaddressed admission of guilt and expression of sincere regret in her victimizer’s diary, she has not discovered an apology." (p. 31) [full-text]
- Legal analogue: the "legal authority or standing to bring cases" in civil law is "uniquely up to a(n alleged) victim"; criminal prosecution is "up to “the people” and their representatives." (p. 31) [full-text]
- "bipolar moral obligations entail a distinctive discretionary second-personal authority that obligees have to make claims and demands of obligors and hold them personally responsible." (pp. 31-32) [full-text]
- The obligee's individual authority "cannot exist by itself": it presupposes a representative authority shared with third parties (p. 27) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Why the chapter and not the 2006 book.** The paper's standing is directed: a claim addressed to the party in the position of host. That is Darwall's bipolar obligation, not his moral obligation "period" (owed as a "representative person"), which is the book's main object. The chapter is also the one place Darwall joins second-personal address to Hohfeldian correlativity (p. 28) and to legal standing (p. 31). The book (Harvard UP, 2006; Crossref dates its digital record 2009, DOI 10.4159/9780674253636) was not read in full this run.
- **Earlier printing.** OpenAlex also lists "Bipolar Obligation" at 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199653492.003.0011 (2012). That is probably an earlier publication of this essay; a verifier should confirm before the authors choose which to cite. The page loci here are the 2013 pages.
- **Threat 1, conferral.** For Darwall the obligee's authority is moral and held by persons as such; it is not conferred. The paper says standing "is conferred". The bridge is p. 28: the same directed structure can be set "in various keys", including conventional and legal. The paper should say it is working in the conventional/legal key of commercial hospitality, where the institution confers the standing.
- **Threat 2, the reachability condition is the paper's, not Darwall's.** Darwall's obligee keeps her authority even if the obligor cannot be reached; what fails without an addressee is the *act* — the apology in the diary is no apology (p. 31). The paper's "standing exists only while a counterparty is reachable" is stronger. Either state it as the authors' own claim, or reword so that what lapses is the exercisable standing (the claim that can be addressed and received), while the entitlement stays with the institution.
- **Competence.** Darwall uses "second-personal competence" elsewhere (Korsgaard's 2007 commentary, via Consensus abstract, describes it). The paper's slogan "competence is learned, standing is conferred" uses competence differently (skill with a system). A referee who knows Darwall may notice; one clause in §6 can pre-empt it.
