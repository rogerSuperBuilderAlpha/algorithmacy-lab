## Summary

An adult NHS dental patient sued the owner of the practice for the alleged negligence of three self-employed associate dentists who had treated her there. On a preliminary issue the Court of Appeal held that the owner owed her a non-delegable duty of care for the treatment she received at his practice: she was "a patient of his Practice", he was named on her treatment plan as the provider, and all five *Woodland* features were met. The court then held, though it did not need to, that he was not vicariously liable for the associates, because after *Barclays* their relationship with him was not akin to employment. English law. The merits of the negligence claim had not been tried.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: Court of Appeal (Civil Division), handed down 4 February 2022, heard 13 January 2022, [2022] EWCA Civ 107. Bean LJ gave the judgment; Nicola Davies LJ: "I agree." (para 92); Simler LJ: "I also agree." (para 93). [full-text]
- Parties and claim: the claimant "received dental treatment on a number of occasions between August 2009 and December 2015" at a practice of which the defendant "was then the owner and sole principal dentist". She "was not treated by the Defendant personally but by six different dentists", and alleged negligence by four; three "were self-employed Associate Dentists" (para 1). The defendant ran a dental business under an NHS contract worth "£498,877" in 2009 (para 27). [full-text]
- An adult paying at the desk: "Mrs Hughes was born on 21 October 1956", and chose the practice "on the recommendation of her daughter" (para 9). "She paid at the reception desk at the end of each appointment." (para 10) The payment was "the NHS Charge element of her treatment" (para 56(iv), quoting the trial judge). Patients "were free to elect to have future treatment at another dental practice of their choosing" (para 6). [full-text]
- Stage of the case: a preliminary issue on liability for the associates; "the merits of the claim have yet to be tried" (para 2). [full-text]
- The framework: "Woodland is now the leading case on non-delegable duties of care", with "five cumulative factors" (para 50). [full-text]
- Holding on non-delegable duty: "the judge was clearly right to hold that Dr Rattan was under a non-delegable duty of care to the Claimant in respect of the treatment she received at the Practice. She was a patient of his Practice, not just in layman’s language but as a matter of law." (para 68) [full-text]
- First reason, the holding out on paper: the treatment plan the claimant signed "named Dr Rattan as the Provider of the treatment and stated that “the dentist named on this form is providing you with a course of treatment”. No other dentist was named on the form". "Nothing is said about whether the whole course of treatment will be provided by one individual, nor whether he or she will be an employee of the Practice or an independent contractor." (para 69) [full-text]
- Second reason, whose customers they were: the associate agreements described patients as "patients of the Practice" and bound each associate by restrictive covenants against treating "anyone who has been a “patient of the Practice” in the preceding 12 months, whether or not the individual Associate Dentist had ever treated, spoken to or even met that patient" (para 70). The goodwill "belongs to the Practice Owner" (para 41, quoting clause 39). [full-text]
- *Woodland* feature 1, vulnerability: "“patient” must include anyone receiving treatment from a dentist." "But the sentence cannot be rewritten as though the Claimant had to be within a subset of especially vulnerable patients in order to qualify." Bean LJ leaves open whether the term covers patients "merely advised in consultation" (para 71(1)). [full-text]
- Feature 2, care and control: the relationship arose "at the latest on each occasion when the Claimant signed the Personal Dental Treatment Plan", and "placed the Claimant in the actual care of the Defendant, not because he was a dentist himself but because he was the owner of the Practice. It would have done likewise if the Practice had been run by a company or owned by a partnership." The duty "was a positive or affirmative one to protect the patient from injury" and "involved an element of control over the patient" (para 71(2)). [full-text]
- Feature 3, no control over how the holder performs: the claimant "could express a preference as to which Associate Dentist she would like to see her, but no more than that." Her freedom to refuse treatment or go elsewhere is no answer: "The fact that a prisoner or immigration detainee cannot decide to seek treatment elsewhere does not mean that any patient who can do so is not owed the non-delegable duty of care." (para 71(3)) [full-text]
- The rejected defence, arranging only: counsel for the owner argued that the practice "did not assume a duty to the Respondent to provide dental treatment but merely a duty to make arrangements for the dental treatment to be provided by an associate" (para 60, quoting his skeleton), relying on *Armes* para 37 (para 61). The claimant's counsel put that case as making the practice "little more than a booking agency" (para 66(c)). The court rejected it (paras 68-72). [full-text]
- Delegation does not move the duty: Bean LJ repeats *Woodland* para 7, "The work required to perform such a duty may well be delegable and usually is. But the duty itself remains the defendant’s." (para 71(2)) [full-text]
- Disposition on the first ground: "I would therefore dismiss the appeal against the judge’s finding that Dr Rattan was liable for any negligent acts or omissions of the Associate Dentists in treating Mrs Hughes, by virtue of a non-delegable duty of care. This makes it strictly unnecessary to decide the second ground, but as this is in the nature of a test case we were asked to deal with both issues in any event." (para 72) [full-text]
- Vicarious liability, not needed for the result: under *Cox* alone Bean LJ "would have upheld the judge’s finding", since the associates "were not conducting recognisably independent businesses of their own", where "“Recognisable” in this context seems to me to mean recognisable to someone with no knowledge of the contractual arrangements between the tortfeasor and the Defendant" (para 86). But "following Barclays the critical question now appears to have reverted to being whether the alleged tortfeasor’s relationship with the defendant can properly be described as being “akin” (or “analogous”) to employment, with the focus being on the contractual arrangements between tortfeasor and defendant", and "the Barclays test for vicarious liability is not met in the present case" (para 88). The two weightiest facts: the associates "were free to work at the Practice for as many or as few hours as they wished" and "to work for other practice owners" (para 89(1)-(2)). Bean LJ differs from the judge "though with some hesitation" (para 91). [full-text]
- Result: the owner's appeal was dismissed (para 91). The claimant succeeded on the preliminary issue through the non-delegable duty. [full-text]
- Oversight, in the owner's contract with the health body and not in the duty to the patient. The NHS contract forbade the owner to engage a dentist unless "the Contractor has checked that the practitioner meets the requirements in clause 178" and "has taken reasonable steps to satisfy himself" that the person "has the clinical experience and training necessary to enable him to properly perform dental services"; it required "a practice based quality assurance system" covering every dentist who performed services (para 25). He could not sub-contract clinical matters without taking reasonable steps to satisfy himself that the person "is qualified and competent to provide the service" and held adequate insurance (para 26). He "must take all reasonable steps to ensure that its personnel and contractors comply" with the contract (para 21, quoting clause 2.11). [full-text]
- The trial judge's use of those terms, quoted by Bean LJ: the owner "agreed to a series of obligations in relation to patients of the Practice, including keeping records, providing patient information and proving a complaints procedure" [so in the transcript] and "Whilst he could choose to deliver the services by sub-contractors or via associates, the Defendant was subject to a series of requirements in relation to their selection, training and oversight" (para 55(iii)-(iv)). [full-text]
- Complaints ran through the practice: each associate agreed "to inform the Practice Owner of any complaints, claims or NHS investigations against him/her" (para 31(iii)) and to follow "the Practice complaints procedure" and keep "the Practice Owner informed of complaints made" (para 33). [full-text]
- A complaint that changed performance: the claimant "had raised concerns via her daughter about the number of different dentists by whom she had been seen. Her request to be seen by the same dentist for each appointment was then adhered to." (para 13) [full-text]
- Insurance and indemnity: each associate "personally held professional indemnity cover" (para 3, agreed fact 8.1), and the owner "is contractually entitled to an indemnity from each of the Associate Dentists" (para 4). [full-text]
- The judgment does not mention hotels, guests, customers at large or occupiers (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **It turns on the word 'patient'.** Lord Sumption's first feature names "a patient or a child" (para 50, quoting *Woodland* para 23(1)). Bean LJ holds that any person receiving dental treatment is a patient and refuses to add a threshold of special vulnerability (para 71(1)). He does not hold that a competent adult customer of any service satisfies the first feature. A hotel guest is no patient, and the paper cannot move from this case to guests without an argument that a guest is "especially vulnerable or dependent on the protection of the defendant against the risk of injury" in *Woodland*'s sense.
- **What made the owner the holder.** The reasons are documentary and commercial: his name as "Provider" on the form the customer signed (para 69), the description of customers as the practice's (para 70), his ownership of the goodwill (para 41). The associates' clinical freedom (para 15) did not matter to the duty. This is close to K3's reliance ground and to K8's 'the one that held out', but the court does not put it as reliance: the judge found only that the claimant "considered that she was a patient of the Practice" (para 14), and the Court of Appeal reasons from the documents.
- **The duty is liability, not supervision.** Nowhere does the court say the owner had to monitor the associates in order to discharge his duty to the patient. He is liable for their negligence whether or not he did. The requirements of "selection, training and oversight" were terms of his contract with the Primary Care Trust; the judge used them as evidence that the service was his (para 55), and the claimant was not a party to that contract (the owner's point at para 63).
- **Vicarious liability went the other way on the same facts.** The same associates were independent enough to defeat vicarious liability (paras 88-91). The two doctrines can give opposite answers for one set of workers; the paper should not run them together.
- **Later treatment not checked.** I read no later case. The trial judge had been referred to two county court decisions reaching the same result for dental patients (para 57); the Court of Appeal was not shown them and I did not read them.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): supports, narrowly.** K8 asks for "any non-delegable-duty case with adult paying customers". This is the nearest, for an adult NHS patient who paid the NHS charge (paras 9-10, 56): an adult who chose the business, paid the charge at its desk and was free to go elsewhere (paras 6, 9, 10, 71(3)), owed a non-delegable duty by the owner of the business for work done by self-employed professionals (paras 68, 72). The owner need not be the one with the skill; a company would be in the same position (para 71(2)). The support is narrow because the claimant qualified as a patient (para 71(1)).

**K8, allocation among firms: supports the test of who undertook to provide.** The court refused to treat the business as a "booking agency" that merely arranged treatment (paras 60, 66, 68-70). It looked at who was named as provider and whose customers the patients were.

**K7, premise P3: does not support as law; offers an example.** The non-delegable duty carries no oversight content in this judgment. The example is the NHS contract, which made the owner the single contracting provider and required him to check competence, run quality assurance and keep a complaints procedure (paras 25-26, 55). That is a regulator's design in which answerability and a complaints channel sit with the same party. It is not a duty owed to the patient.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- The Court of Appeal held that the owner of a dental practice owed an adult NHS patient a non-delegable duty of care for treatment given at his practice by self-employed associates (paras 68, 72).
- The patient's freedom to go elsewhere did not defeat the duty (para 71(3)).
- The court relied on the owner being named as provider on the treatment plan and on the associates' covenants not to treat "patients of the Practice" (paras 69-70).
- The same court held the owner not vicariously liable for the same associates (paras 88-91).
- The owner's contract with the health body required him to check his associates' competence, to run a quality assurance system and, as the trial judge summarised it, to provide a complaints procedure (paras 25, 55(iii)).

**What it may not say.**
- That English law owes hotel guests or customers generally a non-delegable duty. The court applied *Woodland*'s category of "patient" (para 71(1)).
- That the court dispensed with *Woodland*'s first feature. It held the feature met.
- That the non-delegable duty required the owner to supervise the associates, or that the patient could enforce the contract terms on oversight and complaints.
- That the claimant proved negligence. The merits were untried (para 2).
