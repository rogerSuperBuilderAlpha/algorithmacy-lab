## Summary

A conceptual FAccT paper. It argues that the "many hands" frame, which treats an algorithmic system as the product of one organisation's many people, no longer fits systems assembled from services that other firms control. In an algorithmic supply chain, production, deployment and use are split among actors linked by data flows; responsibility is distributed, nobody controls the whole chain, each actor sees only a step or two (the "accountability horizon"), and providers write service terms that keep control while shedding accountability. It treats accountability as a mechanism (actor gives account to a forum). It reports that the CJEU, in data-protection law, has held "the factual situation outweighs contractual or other arrangements" (arXiv p. 8), and notes that challenging providers' claims "would involve litigation or regulatory investigation"; it states no rule that allocation must follow factual control. Its own normative sentence is more general: "Accountability should thus be allocated to actors across supply chains based on a proper understanding of their technological and political economic dynamics", and this "requires processes and criteria" that the paper does not supply (arXiv p. 8, §4.1.2).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Restates the many-hands problem and moves past it: "The ‘many hands’ problem holds that accountability is difficult where many people have together contributed to activity or outcome, as it may be impossible to allocate responsibility to any one of them" (arXiv p. 1) [full-text]
- The shift from organisation to chain: systems "often now involve a group of organisations arranged together in a data-driven supply chain, each retaining control over component systems they provide as services to others" (arXiv p. 2) [full-text]
- No single controller: "Responsibility for the workings and outcomes of supply chains is thus distributed among several actors who may not be straightforward to identify nor consistent across instances. Even when some actors are influential, there is therefore typically no one actor in overall control of a supply chain." (arXiv p. 7, §4.1) [full-text]
- Allocation must target the right actor: "directing governance and accountability mechanisms at, or allocating accountability to, the wrong actors in supply chains risks undermining the stated goals of these mechanisms" (arXiv p. 7, §4.1) [full-text]
- The authors' own allocation sentence: "Accountability should thus be allocated to actors across supply chains based on a proper understanding of their technological and political economic dynamics. This requires processes and criteria for identifying the distribution of responsibility across supply chains and allocating accountability to those actors, for which activities, accounting to whom, and with what possible consequences." (arXiv p. 8, §4.1.2) (added by Fable, rr-2026-11-h) [full-text]
- Graded, partial responsibility: "Not every actor in a supply chain will be responsible for the outcome ... Neither will actors who are in some way responsible be equally responsible ... Nor will they be responsible for the whole supply chain – different actors control different aspects of it." (arXiv p. 8, §4.1.2) [full-text]
- Legal roles versus factual control: under data protection law "AI service customers are likely data controllers (the dominant party, ultimately responsible for compliance and accountability), while providers may be data processors"; yet "the CJEU has consistently held that the factual situation outweighs contractual or other arrangements" (arXiv p. 8, §4.1.1) [full-text]
- On the AI Act (proposal version): it "would potentially make actors several steps downstream from production responsible" for production requirements, while "the actor who developed and controls that technology ... would face no obligations" (arXiv p. 8, §4.1.1) [full-text]
- The accountability horizon: "the point beyond which an actor cannot ‘see’, which depends on the actor and the chain" (arXiv p. 8, §4.2); "in many cases, therefore, no actor will have sufficient knowledge of or control over both production and deployment to be able to reliably assess or mitigate the impacts and risks" (arXiv p. 9, §4.2.2) [full-text]
- Terms of service as a shedding device: "Providers disclaim legal accountability for things that happen through use of their services" (arXiv p. 10, §4.3.1), extending Nissenbaum's "vacuum" of accountability (arXiv p. 10) [full-text]
- Accountability defined as a mechanism: "an institutional arrangement whereby an actor provides accounts to a forum, who deliberates on those accounts and may impose consequences" (arXiv p. 2, §2); the authors adopt that sense: "We treat accountability as a mechanism, whereby actors are held accountable for technologies they are responsible for" (arXiv p. 2, §2) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Not about hospitality, and not about the first-order duty to a guest. It is about answerability for an AI system's outcomes. It can carry the multi-actor *structure* the paper must face (R2.1e, R2.2d) and the vendor's place in it; it cannot ground what a host owes.
- It describes the problem and declines to solve it: "We do not offer concrete technical or organisational proposals" (arXiv p. 2; the sentence crosses the column break, not the page). Cite it for the direction of allocation (by the actual distribution of responsibility, p. 8, §4.1.2), not for criteria or a rule.
- Its AI Act analysis concerns the 2021-22 proposal ("proposed AI Act", arXiv p. 8). The adopted Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 uses "provider" and "deployer" (see `eu2024aiact`). Do not quote its AI Act passage as describing current law.
- Locators are arXiv v2 pages.
