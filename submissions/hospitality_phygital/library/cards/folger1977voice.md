## Summary

The canonical voice experiment, and the source section 7 leans on more heavily than any other.
Sixth-grade boys performed a card-sorting task across ten work periods while a manager — supposedly
another boy, actually the experimenter — decided each time how to split a monetary reward. The design
crossed three factors: outcome (inequity, with unequal pay favouring the manager, versus equity);
procedure (voice, where the worker states the pay he considers fair, versus mute); and payment
sequence over time (constant versus improve, with proportionately more going to the worker each
period).

- On **outcome** fairness the effect is an interaction, not a main effect: a pay sequence that improved after voice was rated *less* fair than the same improving sequence without voice, while a constant sequence was rated *more* fair with voice than without — chiefly in the inequity conditions [abstract]

## Critical notes & threats

- **THE MANUSCRIPT'S CLAIM APPEARS TO BE WRONG, AND THIS IS THE LOAD-BEARING CITATION IN SECTION 7.**
  Section 7 says Folger found that voice reduced experienced injustice *independently of whether the
  outcome improved*. Independence is precisely what the paper does not report. On outcome fairness
  Folger reports a voice x improvement interaction, and in the improve condition the direction
  reverses: improvement preceded by voice was judged less fair than the same improvement without it.
  This is the finding the later literature calls the frustration effect, and it is the seed of
  Folger's own Referent Cognitions Theory.
- **Provenance of that correction, stated plainly: it is not full-text verified.** It rests on two
  independent searches that returned consistent, detailed, abstract-shaped statements of the design
  and results, corroborated by the pattern in Folger's 1979 follow-up, where the fair process effect
  is explicitly conditional. Under this arm's own rule — search summaries are leads, never sources —
  that is enough to stop using the current sentence and not enough to publish a replacement. The full
  text has to be read before submission.
- The paper's underlying point survives the correction and arguably improves. What Folger shows is
  that an improved outcome does not absorb or substitute for voice; voice restructures how the
  outcome is then evaluated, and the process satisfaction is a separate effect on its own measure.
  That is a sharper warrant for the anti-seamlessness argument than independence would have been,
  because it says the two are entangled rather than merely additive.
- A referee checking one citation in section 7 will check this one, and procedural-justice
  researchers know this paper. Citing it for a main effect it does not report is the single most
  damaging error available to us.
