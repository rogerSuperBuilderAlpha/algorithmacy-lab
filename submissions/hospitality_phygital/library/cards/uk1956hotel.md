## Summary

Section 1 of the Act deems a "hotel", and only a hotel, to be an inn, and moves the common-law duties, liabilities and rights of an innkeeper to the hotel's proprietor and to no other person. It defines a hotel by a holding-out: food, drink and, if required, a bed offered without special contract to any traveller who presents, appears able and willing to pay, and is fit to be received.

Added in rr-2026-11-k. Section 2 cuts the innkeeper's strict liability for travellers' property down. It applies only to a traveller for whom a bed was engaged and only around the night of the stay (s. 2(1)); it does not cover vehicles or live animals (s. 2(2)); and it is capped at fifty pounds an article and one hundred pounds in all (s. 2(3)). The cap does not apply where the property was lost through the default, neglect or wilful act of the proprietor or a servant, where it was deposited for safe custody, or where the guest offered it for deposit and was refused, or wished to offer it and "through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so". The proprietor keeps the cap only if the statutory notice was displayed at reception or the main entrance. The Act does not extend to Northern Ireland.

## Critical notes & threats

- The Act does not restate the content of the duties; it carries over whatever the common law attached to an innkeeper "as such". Content (receive, custody of goods) has to come from the cases and from scholarship (calye1584case, bogen1996innkeeper, sandovalstrausz2005travelers).
- Section 1(3) is a definition of "hotel", not a rule stating when the duty is owed. Read as activation (my reading, and the drafter's), it has four conditions, three on the guest's side (traveller presenting, able and willing to pay, fit state); the duty itself still comes from the common law that s. 1(1) carries over. It is conditional hospitality in statutory form, which suits the paper and cuts against "simply because they walked in the door" (pitch l. 21).
- Holder is "the proprietor", a single legal person. It answers R2.1c in law but restates R2.1e: in an owner/brand/operator structure, who is "the proprietor" is a question the Act does not settle. I found no case on that question in this run (not searched; H3's remit).
- Section 2 was not read for this card. VERIFY_H_v3 read s. 2(3) from a Wayback capture and reports that it caps the proprietor's strict liability for guests' goods unless the loss came through the default of the proprietor or a servant, or the goods were deposited for safe custody. Do not cite s. 2 from this card. (Superseded in rr-2026-11-k: s. 2 has now been read in full and is quoted above; it may be cited from this card.)
- England, Wales and Scotland only. Restaurants without beds and short-term rentals that do not hold out to "any traveller" fall outside.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **What s. 2 leaves of the strict duty.** The innkeeper's liability without fault survives, as the Schedule's notice says, but only for overnight guests, not for vehicles or animals, and only up to £50 an article and £100 in all. Above the cap the guest must bring the case within s. 2(3)(a), (b) or (c). A paper that says the innkeeper's strict liability "stays" must add the cap.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Whose default lifts the cap.** S. 2(3)(a) and (c) speak of "the proprietor or some servant of his". The Act does not mention independent contractors or agents. Whether the default of an outsourced front desk, or of a machine, counts as the proprietor's default under para (c) is not answered by the text, and I read no case on it. *Al-Najar* [2019] EWHC 1593 (QB) para 184 mentions one case, *Anande v Firoka*, in which the limits were set aside; I did not read it.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Para (c) is about deposit of goods.** It is the only place in the Act where a guest's inability to reach the proprietor or a servant has a legal consequence, and the consequence is the loss of a cap on damages. It gives the guest no right to be attended to.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **A hotel without a desk is contemplated.** The proviso provides for the case "where there is no reception office or desk" (s. 2(3)). The Act does not require one.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Drafting slips in the revised text.** legislation.gov.uk prints "proprieter" in s. 2(1) and "liablility" in s. 2(2). I have not seen the Queen's Printer copy and cannot say whether the slips are in the original.
- Added rr-2026-11-k. **Later treatment** not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

Added in rr-2026-11-k.

- **K4 (two tiers): supports both, for hotels in Great Britain.** S. 1 carries over the innkeeper's common-law duties, the duty to receive among them, to the proprietor of a hotel and to no one else, on the conditions in s. 1(3). S. 2 states the present limits of the goods duty.
- **K8 (holder, goods): supports with a limit.** The strict liability for guests' goods stays with the proprietor "even though it was not due to any fault of the proprietor or staff" (Schedule), which fits K8's first bullet. It is capped (s. 2(3)), and K8 should say so. The duties "shall not attach to any other person" (s. 1(1)), so a brand or platform that is not the proprietor does not hold them under this Act.
- **K7 (channel): a statutory analogue, for goods only.** The proprietor loses the cap where the guest "wished so to offer the property in question but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so" (s. 2(3)(c)). The law here makes the holder pay for being unreachable. The remedy is damages afterwards, and the duty concerned is custody of goods.
- **K5 (directedness): consistent.** The liabilities run to "any guest of his" (ss. 1(2), 2(2)) and "any one guest" (s. 2(3)).

**What the paper may say**

- In Great Britain an innkeeper's duties now attach to "the proprietor" of a hotel and "shall not attach to any other person" (s. 1(1)).
- The proprietor is liable for a guest's property "even though it was not due to any fault of the proprietor or staff of the hotel" (Schedule), but only where a bed was engaged (s. 2(1)) and only up to fifty pounds an article and one hundred pounds in all (s. 2(3)).
- The cap falls away where the loss came through "the default, neglect or wilful act of the proprietor or some servant of his" (s. 2(3)(a)), where the property was deposited for safe custody (s. 2(3)(b)), or where the guest wished to deposit it "but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so" (s. 2(3)(c)).
- The Act provides for a hotel with "no reception office or desk" (s. 2(3), proviso).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the innkeeper's liability for goods is unlimited today.
- That the Act gives a guest a right to reach a member of staff, or requires a reception desk.
- That para (c) covers the default of a contractor or a system. The text says "the proprietor or a servant of his" and no case was read.
- That the Act applies to restaurants, bars or lettings that do not meet s. 1(3), or to Northern Ireland.
- That the Act states the duty to receive in terms. It carries the common-law duties over without restating them.
