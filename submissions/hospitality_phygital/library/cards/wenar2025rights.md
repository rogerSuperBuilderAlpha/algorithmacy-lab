## Summary

Wenar and Cruft separate the form of rights from their function. On form they report Hohfeld's four incidents (privilege, claim, power, immunity) as widely accepted, and define the claim by its correlative: a claim is held when and only when another party owes the holder a duty. On function they report a dispute that is centuries old and unresolved. Will theorists hold that a right gives its holder control over another's duty; interest theorists hold that it furthers the holder's interests; demand theorists hold that it is what the holder may demand; and several newer accounts split the difference or deny that rights have one function. A last subsection reports the newer question of what makes a duty 'directed', and records in single sentences two departures from the standard picture: wronging without a directed duty (Cornell) and directed duties without rights (Martin).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Categories of right by ground, which is the paper's three registers in the entry's words: "Moral rights are grounded in moral reasons, legal rights derive from the laws of the society, customary rights exist by local convention." (§1, list item 'Why') [full-text]
- Form is settled, function is not: "The Hohfeldian analysis of the form of rights is widely accepted, although there are scholarly quarrels about its details. Which theory gives the best account of the distinctive function of rights has been much more contentious" (§2, para. 2) [full-text]
- The claim defined by its correlative: "A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ." (§2.1.2, displayed definition after para. 1) [full-text]
- Directedness is what marks the claim: "every claim-right correlates to a duty in (at least) one duty-bearer. What is distinctive about the claim-right is that a duty-bearer’s duty is “directed at” or “owed to” the right-holder." (§2.1.2, para. 2) [full-text]
- Demanding, waiving and enforcing are not in that definition. Powers are a separate incident: "Ordering, promising, waiving, abandoning, consenting, selling, and sentencing are all examples of acts by which a right-holder exercises a power to change his own Hohfeldian incidents or those of another." (§2.1.3, para. 4) [full-text]
- The same separation inside one right: "You have several powers with respect to your claim—you may waive the claim (granting others permission to touch the computer), annul the claim (abandoning the computer as your property), or transfer the claim (making the computer into someone else’s property)." (§2.1.6, para. 4) [full-text]
- The privilege is the absence of a duty: "A has a privilege to φ if and only if A has no duty not to φ." (§2.1.1, displayed definition) [full-text]
- The dispute over function: "Which theory offers the better account of the functions of rights has been the subject of spirited dispute, literally for ages." (§2.2.2, para. 1) [full-text]
- Will theory, as reported: "a will theorist asserts that the function of a right is to give its holder control over another’s duty", and "In Hohfeldian terms, will theorists assert that every right includes a Hohfeldian power over a claim." (§2.2.2, para. 2) [full-text]
- Interest theory, as reported: "Interest theorists maintain that the function of a right is to further the right-holder’s interests." (§2.2.2, para. 3) [full-text]
- What the will theory cannot hold: "Within the will theory, there can be no such thing as an unwaivable right: a right over which its holder has no power." (§2.2.2, para. 6); "Within the will theory, it is impossible for incompetents like infants, animals, and comatose adults to have rights." (§2.2.2, para. 7) [full-text]
- What the interest theory allows: "It readily accommodates both unwaivable rights (the possession of which may be good for their holders) and the rights of incompetents (who have interests that rights can protect)." (§2.2.2, para. 8) [full-text]
- Demand theories, as reported. Feinberg (1973: 58–59), quoted in the entry: "A right is something a man can stand on, something that can be demanded or insisted upon without embarrassment or shame." (§2.2.2, block quotation after para. 15) [full-text]
- Darwall (2006: 18), quoted in the entry, on what a claim-right includes: "includes a second-personal authority to resist, complain, remonstrate, and perhaps use coercive measures of other kinds, including, perhaps, to gain compensation if the right is violated." (§2.2.2, block quotation after para. 16) [full-text]
- How demand theories differ from the will theory: "Like the will theory, these demand theories center on the agency of the right-holder. They do not turn on the right-holder’s power over the duty of another, so they do not share the will theory’s difficulty with unwaivable rights." Their cost: "Demand theories also share the will-theory’s challenges in explaining the rights of parties incapable of demanding, and in explaining privilege-rights." (§2.2.2, para. 17) [full-text]
- Gilbert, as reported: "M. Gilbert (2018) develops not a demand theory of rights, but a theory of a special class of rights that she calls “demand-rights”. In her formulation, “X has a demand-right against Y if and only if X has the standing to demand of Y that Y φ” (2018: 70)." (§2.2.2, para. 18) [full-text]
- Sreenivasan's hybrid, as reported: "here the function of the claim-right is to endow the right-holder with the amount of control over another’s duty that advances her interests." (§2.2.2, para. 19) [full-text]
- Some conclude the dispute is misconceived: "the debate itself rests on the mistaken premise that there is a single concept of a right for which these theories provide rival analyses (Van Duffel 2012b; Hayward 2013; Valentini 2023)." (§2.2.2, para. 14) [full-text]
- The standard account of the claim: "On the standard account what is distinctive about the claim-right is that a duty-bearer’s duty is “directed at” or “owed to” the right-holder." (§2.2.3, para. 1) [full-text]
- Undirected duties exist: "After all, not all duties are directed to specific others. For example, the moral duty of affluent people to give some of their wealth to charity is not owed to any specific person." (§2.2.3, para. 2) [full-text]
- What breach of a directed duty adds: "the violation of a directed duty is more than just a wrong: it is a wronging of the being to whom the duty is owed (Thompson 2004). And unlike a mere wrong, the wronging of some being calls, ceteris paribus, for apology and compensation." (§2.2.3, para. 2) [full-text]
- The two recorded departures: "Cornell (2015) argues against the commonly accepted thesis that one can wrong only those to whom one has directed duties", and "For the related claim that some directed duties—those central to personal relationships—do not correlate with rights, see A. Martin 2021." (§2.2.3, para. 4) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What an encyclopaedia entry can carry.** It carries the existence and the shape of a debate, and the standard definitions. It does not carry any first-order claim about what rights are. Every position above belongs to the theorist named, and the wording of Feinberg and Darwall is theirs as quoted by Wenar and Cruft: cite as 'Feinberg (1973: 58–59), quoted in Wenar and Cruft (2025: §2.2.2)', never as Feinberg read.
- **It collides with K5's customary limb.** The displayed definition (§2.1.2) makes 'B owes A a duty' and 'A has a claim' one relation. A duty to attend that is 'owed to the guest' is therefore, on the account the entry calls standard (§2.2.3, para. 1), a claim of the guest, and Pitt-Rivers' sentence that the guest 'has no right other than to respect' (pittrivers2012law, p. 514) cannot stand beside it unglossed. The entry records one dissent from correlativity on the duty side, Martin (2021), in a single clause and without discussion (§2.2.3, para. 4). Martin is closed and unread; the entry cannot be cited for her argument.
- **What the entry does not say.** It nowhere says that a claim can be held without a liberty or standing to demand it. That follows only by inference from the definitions: the claim is defined by the other party's duty (§2.1.2), and waiving, annulling and transferring are powers listed separately (§2.1.3, §2.1.6). The inference is this card's, and the demand theories the entry reports deny its conclusion.
- **Feinberg's formula cuts against the classical guest.** By the test quoted at §2.2.2, a right can be 'demanded or insisted upon without embarrassment or shame'. Pitt-Rivers' guest who 'makes claims or demands' usurps the host's place (pittrivers2012law, p. 515). On a demand theory the classical guest therefore holds no right to attention. The comparison is this card's.
- **An internal slip.** §2, para. 2 says the function debate is taken up 'in section 3'; it is taken up in §2.2. Cite by subsection number.
- The entry is revised in place. The archive edition (Summer 2025) is the fixed text; the saved file is the live page of 11 Nov 2026 and matches the revision date of 7 June 2025.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K5 (narrows).** Supports the legal limb's form: where the law lays a duty on the host that is owed to the guest, the guest holds the correlative claim (§2.1.2). Narrows the customary limb: 'owed to the guest, with no right in the guest' is not available on the standard account, and the entry offers only a citation for the minority view (§2.2.3, para. 4).
- **K6 (supports).** 'Contested' is confirmed for the question of what a claim does for its holder, and specifically for whether control or demand is part of it: will theory yes (a power over the duty, §2.2.2, para. 2), demand theories yes (authority to demand, paras 15–17), interest theory no (paras 3, 8), hybrid in part (para. 19). The paper may say this much on the entry's authority and no more.
- **Three registers.** §1 names moral, legal and customary rights as differing by ground. The paper may cite that list for the distinction; the entry says nothing about hospitality.
