## Summary

Hohfeld argues that "right" is used loosely for four different legal relations and sets out the
scheme of jural opposites and correlatives: right/duty, privilege/no-right, power/liability,
immunity/disability. A right "in the strictest sense" is a claim, and its invariable correlative is a
duty held by a determinate other party. The article is the source of the vocabulary
(claim-right, correlativity) that Darwall's bipolar obligation borrows (Darwall 2013, p. 28).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- "The strictly fundamental legal relations are, after all, sui generis" — hence the scheme of "opposites" and "correlatives" (p. 30) [full-text]
- "That clue lies in the correlative “duty,” for it is certain that even those who use the word and the conception “right” in the broadest possible way are accustomed to thinking of “duty” as the invariable correlative." (p. 31) [full-text]
- "if X has a right against Y that he shall stay off the former's land, the correlative (and equivalent) is that Y is under a duty toward X to stay off the place. If, as seems desirable, we should seek a synonym for the term “right” in this limited and proper meaning, perhaps the word “claim” would prove the best." (p. 32) [full-text]
- "a duty is the invariable correlative of that legal relation which is most properly called a right or claim." (p. 33) [full-text]
- Correlatives table: right–duty, privilege–no-right, power–liability, immunity–disability (p. 30) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What it supports.** The paper's claim that a guest's standing is the correlate of the host's obligation (REVISION_PLAN §3 thesis) is Hohfeld's correlativity applied to hospitality: no claim without a duty-bearer, and the claim is held "against Y" (p. 32), a determinate party.
- **What it does not support, and why it is a governor.** Correlativity is a logical relation between positions: X's claim exists if and only if Y's duty does. It says nothing about whether Y can be reached or answers. So Hohfeld implies the guest's *claim* survives delegation as long as the institution's duty does — which fits E.1 (the obligation stays with the institution). The reachability condition (an addressee who can receive the claim) must come from Darwall's second-personal address (darwall2013bipolar, p. 31), not from Hohfeld. Citing Hohfeld for "standing exists only while a counterparty is reachable" would misattribute.
- Legal, not moral, relations. Darwall (2013, p. 28) shows the structure transfers to other "keys", which is the warrant for using it for commercial hospitality.
- Do not confuse with the 1917 continuation (YLJ 26: 710), where Hohfeld treats rights in personam and in rem. A copy of the 1917 article was downloaded by mistake from openyls.law.yale.edu and deleted unused.
- Bibliography form: Hohfeld, Wesley Newcomb (1913), 'Some fundamental legal conceptions as applied in judicial reasoning', *Yale Law Journal*, 23:1, pp. 16–59. Costs about 25 words.
