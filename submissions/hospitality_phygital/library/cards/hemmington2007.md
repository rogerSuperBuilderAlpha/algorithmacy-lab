## Summary

A conceptual article arguing that scholars and the trade have misdescribed commercial hospitality as a service and should define it as behaviour and experience. Hemmington sets 'service' beside 'hospitality' on thirteen dimensions (Figure 1) and draws out five for the commercial setting: the host-guest relationship, generosity, theatre and performance, 'lots of little surprises', and safety and security. Under the last he quotes Telfer on the guest's safety as the host's traditional first responsibility, observes that the industry tends to overlook it, and prescribes that security in hospitality 'should' be the guest's security before the security of the firm's goods. He concludes that hospitality businesses 'must behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience'. The argument is addressed to managers and its stated reward is competitive advantage.

## Critical notes & threats

**What hosts are responsible for, in the commercial setting.** Two things, and they should be kept apart. First, 'the experience': businesses 'must behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience' (AV p. 16). The object of the responsibility is the guest's experience, staged and host-led, and not the guest's safety or welfare. Second, the guest's security: Hemmington says security 'should' centre on the guest and not on the firm's goods (AV p. 15). He does not write that the business is responsible for the guest's safety; the word 'responsibility' in that passage belongs to Telfer's sentence about tradition.

**Register.** Managerial prescription. Every operative sentence is a 'should' or a 'must' addressed to hospitality businesses, and the stated ground is competitive advantage (AV pp. 16-17). The one statement in another register is Telfer's, which Hemmington quotes as a report of tradition ('traditionally') and then describes as overlooked by the industry. So the article is evidence that a hospitality scholar thinks commercial hosts ought to put the guest's security first, and evidence, on his say-so, that the trade does not treat this as binding.

**Holder.** An organization: 'hospitality businesses', 'Hospitality organisations', 'operators', 'the hotel'. Staff appear as 'performers and hosts' (AV p. 14) and as 'cast' to be 'selected and developed' (AV pp. 12-13). The business is to behave 'like' hosts: a simile. Hemmington does not say the business is the host or explain what would make it one.

**Evidence and authority.** None of its own. The security dimension rests on citations in a figure footnote and one sentence of text (AV pp. 10, 15): Telfer for the tradition, Hepple et al. and King for the concept, and four hotel studies for guests' concern with security. I read none of those here. The Telfer quotation carries no page.

**Commercial setting.** The article's whole subject. It accepts that businesses must make a return (AV p. 11) and looks for ways to keep the economic relation out of the guest's sight. It reports, and leaves open, the doubt whether 'true hospitality' can be provided commercially (AV p. 8).

**A point against the paper's later argument.** Hemmington carries the domestic rule against guests' dictating into commerce ('the same principle should apply', AV p. 14) and notes that 'not complaining' is a domestic rule 'at variance' with the commercial relation (AV p. 9). The first cuts against a guest's standing to demand; the second concedes that commercial guests may complain. The paper should not cite the host-led passage without the other.

**Version.** Locators are author-version pages. The paper can cite the article by its range (27(6): 747-755); a quotation needs the version of record for a journal page. The author version has slips ('Laskley', 'Braitwaite', 'in commercial environment') that the published text may have corrected.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K1 (supports, as prescription; narrows the register).** A hospitality scholar writing about the commercial setting makes safety and security one of five dimensions of commercial hospitality and tells businesses to take 'responsibility for the experience' (AV pp. 15-16). The support is for 'scholars say commercial hosts ought to', and not for 'the practice expects'. On the practice Hemmington says the opposite: the guest's safety is 'often overlooked' and 'insufficiently recognized by the industry' (AV p. 15). He also relays K1's first refuter, a canonical definition with no duty in it (Brotherton and Wood's voluntary exchange, AV pp. 5-6).

**K2 (narrows the gap statement; does not argue from role).** Hemmington addresses the duty to 'hospitality businesses' (AV p. 16), so an organization is named as the party who must act. He gives no reason why the business bears it other than advantage, no trigger, and no allocation among firms. He works with the host-guest relation as a role pair distinct from manager-customer (AV p. 9, Figure 1), which is consistent with K2, and he secures performance through selecting staff (AV pp. 12-13), which is the motive-and-person route the paper argues past.

**Telfer at second hand.** This article is the only text I found that carries Telfer's sentence on safety (AV p. 15). It gives no page, and no second source confirms the wording. See the reader report, section (b).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- Hemmington makes 'safety and security' one of five dimensions of hospitality as a commercial experience (2007; AV p. 16).
- Hemmington holds that hospitality businesses 'must behave like hosts taking responsibility for the experience' (2007; AV p. 16).
- Hemmington argues that security in hospitality 'should ... focus on guest security', which he sees as dominant over the security of the hotel's resources and procedures, though financial security is not to be ignored (2007; AV pp. 15-16).
- Hemmington observes that the guest's safety 'is often overlooked and is perhaps insufficiently recognized by the industry' (2007; AV p. 15).
- Telfer, as quoted in Hemmington, writes that 'traditionally the most important responsibility of all was the guest's safety' (2007; AV p. 15; no Telfer page).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That Hemmington says businesses are responsible for guests' safety or welfare. His phrase is responsibility 'for the experience'.
- That he describes a duty the trade recognizes. He says the trade overlooks it.
- That he argues from the host's role, names when the responsibility begins, or says which firm holds it.
- That Telfer's sentence is hers verbatim on Hemmington's authority alone, or that it concerns commercial hosts. It begins 'traditionally' and has no page.
- Any journal page number for these passages.
