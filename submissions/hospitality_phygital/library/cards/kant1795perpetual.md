## Summary

The third of the three 'definitive articles' that Kant proposes for a perpetual peace between states. It limits 'the rights of men, as citizens of the world' to 'the conditions of universal hospitality' and then defines hospitality narrowly. Hospitality is a matter of right, not of philanthropy. It is the claim of a stranger who arrives on another's territory not to be treated with hostility. The owner may send the stranger away, provided this does not cause the stranger's death. The stranger cannot claim to be treated as a guest; that would take a special compact. What the stranger has is a right of visit, grounded in the common possession of the earth's surface, and it reaches no further than permission to attempt intercourse with the inhabitants. Kant then turns the standard on Europe: the 'inhospitable behaviour' he condemns at length is that of the 'commercial states' towards the peoples they visit.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The article: "III.—“The rights of men, as citizens of the world, shall be limited to the conditions of universal hospitality.”" (p. 137; AA 8:357.20-21) [full-text]
- Right, not philanthropy, and the definition: "We are speaking here, as in the previous articles, not of philanthropy, but of right; and in this sphere hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility. The latter may send him away again, if this can be done without causing his death; but, so long as he conducts himself peaceably, he must not be treated as an enemy." (p. 137; AA 8:357.22-358.05) [full-text]
- Not a guest's right: "It is not a right to be treated as a guest to which the stranger can lay claim—a special friendly compact on his behalf would be required to make him for a given time an actual inmate—but he has a right of visitation." (pp. 137-138; AA 8:358.05-08) [full-text]
- The German of that sentence: "Es ist kein Gastrecht, worauf dieser Anspruch machen kann (wozu ein besonderer wohlthätiger Vertrag erfordert werden würde, ihn auf eine gewisse Zeit zum Hausgenossen zu machen), sondern ein Besuchsrecht, welches allen Menschen zusteht, sich zur Gesellschaft anzubieten" (AA 8:358.05-08, Bonn Kant-Korpus) [full-text]
- Against whom, in the German: hospitality is "das Recht eines Fremdlings, seiner Ankunft auf dem Boden eines andern wegen von diesem nicht feindselig behandelt zu werden" (AA 8:358.01-02): the right of a stranger not to be treated with hostility by another on account of arriving on that other's ground (my gloss of the German; Campbell Smith renders the other as the territory's 'owner') [full-text]
- The ground of the right: "This right to present themselves to society belongs to all mankind in virtue of our common right of possession on the surface of the earth" (p. 138; the translation carries the translator's note 133 after 'right') [full-text]
- Its limit: "This right to hospitality, however—that is to say, the privilege of strangers arriving on foreign soil—does not amount to more than what is implied in a permission to make an attempt at intercourse with the original inhabitants." (p. 139; AA 8:358.23-25) [full-text]
- Who violates it, in Kant's examples: "The inhospitality of the inhabitants of certain sea coasts" who plunder ships and enslave the shipwrecked, and of desert tribes who rob those who approach, "is thus contrary to the law of nature" (pp. 138-139) [full-text]
- The visitors can be the wrongdoers: "Let us look now, for the sake of comparison, at the inhospitable behaviour of the civilised nations, especially the commercial states of our continent." (p. 139) [full-text]
- 'Guests' used once, of European traders: China and Japan, "which had made an attempt at receiving guests of this kind, have now taken a prudent step" in restricting them (pp. 140-141) [full-text]
- Where the article sits: the heading over all three definitive articles is "SECOND SECTION CONTAINING THE DEFINITIVE ARTICLES OF PERPETUAL PEACE BETWEEN STATES" (contents page, giving p. 117) [full-text]
- The register of the whole: "Hence the idea of a cosmopolitan right is no fantastical, high-flown notion of right, but a complement of the unwritten code of law—constitutional as well as international law—necessary for the public rights of mankind in general and thus for the realisation of perpetual peace." (p. 142) [full-text]
- A second English rendering, at second hand: Westmoreland (2008: 4) quotes H. B. Nisbet's translation (Cambridge University Press 1991, p. 105, as Westmoreland cites it) for the visitor's right "not to be treated with hostility when he arrives on someone else’s territory" [full-text]
- Kyslan (2026: 77) names the two rights in German, "Besuchsrecht (right to visit)" and Gastrecht, and locates the article at "8: 357–360" (2026: 78) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**What the right is.** A stranger's claim not to be treated as an enemy on arrival, so long as the stranger behaves peaceably (p. 137). Its content is negative: no hostility, and no turning away where turning away means death. It includes no claim to be admitted, lodged, fed or protected.

**Against whom it runs.** Against whoever holds the ground the stranger arrives on: 'its owner' in the translation, 'eines andern' in the German. Kant's violators are coastal peoples, desert tribes and, on the other side, European 'commercial states' (pp. 138-139). The article is one of three for a peace 'between states' and speaks of the rights of persons 'as citizens of the world'. No householder, innkeeper or trader in lodging appears. To say the right runs 'against states' is a fair summary of the examples and the setting; the first paragraph itself says 'owner' and 'inhabitants'.

**Right of visit or guest's right.** A right of visit, expressly. Kant names the guest's right in order to deny it: 'It is not a right to be treated as a guest' (pp. 137-138); 'kein Gastrecht ... sondern ein Besuchsrecht' (AA 8:358.05-07). Being a guest in the full sense, 'an actual inmate' for a time ('Hausgenosse', member of the household), requires 'a special friendly compact'. Kant says nothing about what such a compact contains or what a host then owes.

**Who bears a duty.** The correlative of the stranger's right is the owner's duty not to treat the stranger with hostility. Kant also conditions the right on the stranger's conduct ('so long as he conducts himself peaceably') and spends two of the article's four paragraphs on the wrongs of visitors. Kyslan reads this as a duty on both sides (2026: 79).

**Hospitality studies overstates Kant.** O'Gorman's summary in a hospitality journal has Kant argue 'that individuals had a universal right to shelter in any country' for a limited time (2007, author version pp. 9-10; see card ogorman2007phenomenon). The article gives no right to shelter. The paper should cite the text and not that summary.

**The translation.** Campbell Smith's 'right of visitation' and 'actual inmate' are 1903 English for Besuchsrecht and Hausgenosse. 'Its owner' is firmer than the German 'eines andern' (another). Where wording matters the paper should quote the German with the Akademie locator or check a modern translation; I did not read one.

**Locators.** Printed pages come from the page markers in Project Gutenberg's HTML transcription of the 1917 impression. Akademie line numbers were read from the Bonn Kant-Korpus pages for 8:357 and 8:358.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K2 (governor; confirms the check K2 set).** K2 warned that Kant's right 'may be a right of visit against states and expressly not a guest's right', and said that if so it 'supports only the general point that hospitality has been theorized as right'. The text bears this out in each part (pp. 137-139). The design critique recalled it correctly.

**A lead for K3 and K4, no more.** Kant makes guest status depend on a 'special friendly compact' (pp. 137-138). That is a trigger of a kind: the guest's standing begins with an agreement, not with arrival. He does not develop it, and it concerns a household guest. The paper could note the distinction; it cannot rest the trigger on it.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- Kant treats hospitality as a matter 'not of philanthropy, but of right' (Perpetual Peace, Third Definitive Article, trans. Campbell Smith: 137; AA 8:357).
- For Kant that right is the stranger's claim, on arriving on another's territory, 'to be treated by its owner without hostility' (p. 137; AA 8:358).
- Kant denies that the stranger has 'a right to be treated as a guest'; the stranger has 'a right of visitation', and guest status would need 'a special friendly compact' (pp. 137-138; AA 8:358).
- The right extends no further than 'a permission to make an attempt at intercourse with the original inhabitants' (p. 139).

**What the paper may not say.**

- That Kant gives a guest any right against a host, or a host any duty to receive, shelter, feed or protect.
- That Kant's right of hospitality supports the commercial host's duty, its content, its trigger or its holder. He does not discuss a host who has received anyone, and he does not discuss commerce in lodging. His only 'commercial' parties are the trading states whose conduct as visitors he condemns.
- That Kant's hospitality is a moral ideal of welcome. He sets it against philanthropy.
- That Kant grants a right to shelter or to stay. The owner 'may send him away again' short of causing his death.
