## Summary

In the third book, on private wrongs, Blackstone lists among contracts "implied by reason and construction of law" the undertaking of everyone who takes up a public employment to perform it properly. Two of his examples are the innkeeper's. A "common inn-keeper" is under an implied contract "to secure his guest's goods in his inn", and an innkeeper "or other victualler" who "hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers" makes "an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way", on which a refused traveller has an action on the case for damages (3 Comm. 164). In the fourth book, on public wrongs, he places the same refusal among common nuisances: inns "may be indicted, suppressed, and the inn-keepers fined, if they refuse to entertain a traveler without a very sufficient cause" (4 Comm. 168). The passages concern admission and goods. They say nothing of the guest's person.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- **What kind of text.** A treatise on English law, first edition 1765–1769 by Avalon's source statement; not a judgment and not a statute. Its statements are an author's account of the law of England in the 1760s. [full-text]
- **The class to which the innkeeper's duties belong.** Of the second class of implied contracts, those that arise "from natural reason, and the just construction of law": "Which class extends to all presumptive undertakings or assumpsits; which, though never perhaps actually made, yet constantly arise from this general implication and intendment of the courts of judicature, that every man hath engaged to perform what his duty or justice requires." (3 Comm. 161) [full-text]
- **The supposition on which the duty rests.** "The last class of contracts implied by reason and construction of law arises upon this supposition, that every one who undertakes any office, employment, trust, or duty, contracts with those who employ or entrust him, to perform it with integrity, diligence, and skill. And, if by his want of either of those qualities any injury accrues to individuals, they have therefore their remedy in damages by a special action on the case." (3 Comm. 163; Avalon prints "THE last class" in capitals) [full-text]
- **Goods.** "There is also in law always an implied contract with a common inn-keeper, to secure his guest's goods in his inn; with a common carrier or bargemaster, to be answerable for the goods he carries; with a common farrier, that he shoes a horse well, without laming him; with a common taylor, or other workman, that he performs his business in a workmanlike manner: in which if they fail, an action on the case lies to recover damages for such breach of their general undertaking" (3 Comm. 164). Blackstone's note cites "11 Rep. 54. 1 Saund. 324." [full-text]
- **The duty attaches to the common calling.** "But if I employ a person to transact any of these concerns, whose common profession and [business] it is not, the law implies no such general undertaking; but in order to charge him with damages, a special agreement is required." (3 Comm. 164; Avalon prints "hufinefs", a transcription slip for "bufinefs") [full-text]
- **Admission, the private action.** "Also if an inn-keeper, or other victualler, hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers, it is an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way; and upon this universal assumpsit an action on the case will lie against him for damages, if he without good reason refuses to admit a traveler" (3 Comm. 164). Blackstone's note cites "1 Ventr. 333." Avalon prints "travellers" and then "traveler" in the one sentence. [full-text]
- **Common nuisances are public wrongs.** "common nusances are such inconvenient or troublesome offences, as annoy the whole community in general, and not merely some particular person; and therefore are indictable only, and not actionable; as it would be unreasonable to multiply suits, by giving every man a separate right of action, for what damnifies him in common only with the rest of his fellow subjects" (4 Comm. 167) [full-text]
- **Admission, the public route.** "All disorderly inns or ale-houses, bawdy-houses, gaming-houses, stage-plays unlicenced, booths and stages for rope-dancers, mountebanks, and the like, are public nusances, and may upon indictment be suppressed and fined" and "Inns, in particular, being intended for the lodging and receipt of travelers, may be indicted, suppressed, and the inn-keepers fined, if they refuse to entertain a traveler without a very sufficient cause: for thus to frustrate the end of their institution is held to be disorderly behaviour" (4 Comm. 168). Blackstone's notes cite "1 Hawk. P. C. 198. 225." for the first sentence and "1 Hal. P. C. 225." for the second. [full-text]
- **A comparison Blackstone adds.** "Thus too the hospitable laws of Norway punish, in the severest degree, such inn-keepers as refuse to furnish accommodations at a just and reasonable price" (4 Comm. 168) [full-text]
- **Limits stated.** The private action lies only where the refusal is "without good reason" (3 Comm. 164); the indictment only where it is "without a very sufficient cause" (4 Comm. 168). Neither passage says what a good reason is. [full-text]
- **What is absent.** Neither passage mentions the guest's bodily safety, the innkeeper's servants, or any condition about payment, room or the traveller's fitness. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Two routes, and how they fit.** In book 3 the refused traveller sues, "upon this universal assumpsit", in implied contract (164). In book 4 the refusal is a common nuisance, indictable (168). The sentence that common nuisances "are indictable only, and not actionable" (167) concerns suits for a common nuisance, not an action in assumpsit, so it does not contradict the traveller's action. Blackstone also cross-refers to his earlier treatment ("Vol. III. pag. 216.", note m) and states that nuisances "which when injurious to a private man are actionable, are, when detrimental to the public, punishable by public prosecution" (167). Vol. III p. 216 was not read.
- **Two grounds, one per route.** The private action rests on the innkeeper's own act: he "hangs out a sign and opens his house", which the law reads as an "implied engagement". The public offence rests on the purpose of inns: to refuse is "to frustrate the end of their institution". The first is a holding-out ground; the second is not.
- **The engagement is implied by law.** Blackstone says of this class that the undertaking was "never perhaps actually made" (161). The innkeeper's "assumpsit" is the law's construction of his conduct. It supports the view that holding out is how the role is taken up and that the law then supplies the content. It does not show that the innkeeper promised anything.
- **"Or other victualler".** The sign passage is not confined to inns. Later English cases confine the duty to receive to inns (the scout reads *R v Rymer* that way; not read by me). Blackstone cannot be cited for "inns only".
- **Transcription.** Avalon's text is an unsigned HTML transcription with visible slips ("hufinefs"; a footnote mark printed "g" where the note list has "s"; "q1 Hal. P. C. 225"). I saw no facsimile and compared no later edition. The burdick1911origin card quotes the sign passage from Burdick as "3 Bl Comm 165" with "offers his house"; Avalon's first-edition text has it on p. 164 with "opens his house". A quotation should name the edition and be checked against a page image.
- **Blackstone's authorities were not read.** The reports and treatises in his notes ("1 Ventr. 333.", "11 Rep. 54. 1 Saund. 324.", "1 Hawk. P. C. 198. 225.", "1 Hal. P. C. 225.") stand behind the passages and may be cited only through him.
- **England, eighteenth century.** No authority for United States law or for the present day.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K5 (directedness): supports for admission and for goods; shows the public route beside it.** The traveller who is refused and the guest whose goods are lost each have "an action on the case" for damages (3 Comm. 164), and "individuals" injured by a failure in a public employment "have therefore their remedy in damages" (163). The same refusal was also indictable (4 Comm. 168). On Blackstone's account the duty to receive was both owed to the traveller and enforced by indictment. That contradicts, for England in the 1760s, any claim that the duty was public only.
- **K3 (grounds): supports voluntary assumption by holding out, as the law's construction; narrows by adding a ground outside the list.** The sign and the open house are the trigger and the "universal assumpsit" is the law's reading of them (164). The duty attaches to those whose "common profession" it is (164), which fits the claim that the role specifies content, trigger and holder. Book 4 grounds the public duty in "the end of their institution" (168), a purpose of inns that is none of K3's three grounds.
- **K4 (two tiers): supports admission and the goods limb of reception; silent on the person; wider than inns on its face.** Admission is conditional only in the phrases "without good reason" and "without a very sufficient cause". K4's conditions (capacity, fitness, ability to pay) need other sources.

**What the paper may say** (as Blackstone's account of English law in the 1760s):
- Blackstone wrote that an innkeeper who "hangs out a sign and opens his house for travellers" makes "an implied engagement to entertain all persons who travel that way", on which a traveller refused "without good reason" could bring an action on the case for damages (3 Comm. 164).
- He classed that engagement among contracts implied by law, which arise because "every one who undertakes any office, employment, trust, or duty" is taken to contract to perform it (3 Comm. 161, 163).
- He wrote that the law implies a contract with a common innkeeper "to secure his guest's goods in his inn" (3 Comm. 164).
- He also wrote that inns "may be indicted, suppressed, and the inn-keepers fined, if they refuse to entertain a traveler without a very sufficient cause" (4 Comm. 168).

**What the paper may not say:**
- That Blackstone speaks of the innkeeper's duty to keep the guest safe. He does not, in these passages.
- That Blackstone limits the duty to receive to inns, or lists its conditions.
- That Blackstone explains how the action and the indictment fit together.
- That the innkeeper made a promise in fact.
- "offers his house", or page 165 (the wording and page on the burdick1911origin card, from Burdick). The Avalon first-edition text has "opens his house" on p. 164.
- Anything from Blackstone as present law, or as the law of any United States jurisdiction.
