## Summary

Critical discourse analysis of scholarship, journalism and Airbnb marketing. It shows how scales (home, neighbourhood, city, nation, "anywhere", temporal and digital) are used "to assert power, assign moral responsibility, and make claims to belonging". The platform's digital scale lets Airbnb shed legal responsibility onto hosts and municipalities.

## rr-2026-11-k: bearing on the keystone (R9)

*Added by a Claude (Opus 5.5) subagent, reader R9, 11 Nov 2026, after re-reading the saved version of record whole (manuscript/rewrite_2026-11/journal/corpus/germannmolz2018/, pp. 229-251, reference list scanned). Locators are printed journal pages.*

- **The guest's voice on a platform is a rating after the stay, and it runs both ways (questions a, g).** "After a hospitality encounter, hosts and guests go onto the platform to rate and leave references for one another. Were you a responsive host? Were you a courteous guest? The system encourages hosts and guests to monitor one another in order to safeguard the community from bad actors." (p. 236) "the reputation system is also a marketing system" (p. 237). The arrangement makes hosts and guests relate "through a mode of social surveillance" (p. 237). The article mentions no route by which a guest can complain, be heard or leave during a stay.
- **What host and guest owe each other.** "What do they owe each other?" Germann Molz asks, and reports Chesky's answer that payment "puts both parties on their best behaviour and makes the whole process more reliable" (p. 236, quoting Botsman and Rogers). Her own reading: "the moral responsibility of hospitality is not necessarily about caring for one another's physical and emotional needs, but about safeguarding reputations and securitizing the hospitality network" (p. 237).
- **Several hosts: platform and household (question b).** Dredge, as reported: "Moral responsibility is relational, distributed and multi-lateral. It comprises a range of actors", and the collaborative economy's "liquid organization" means "'responsibilities can be shifted elsewhere or even avoided'" (p. 233). Airbnb's lawyers argued that "'any penalty should be on the hosts renting out their homes, not on the company'" (p. 244, quoting Mock 2017), since Airbnb "cannot control how homeowners conduct their business" (p. 244, Germann Molz's report of the argument). Hosting without the host: "'digitized tourists staying in empty homes and dealing with the standardised instructions left by "local managers"'" (p. 244, quoting Roelofsen and Minca), so that "homeowners can host their guests without ever meeting them" (p. 244).
- **Workers (question f).** The host is "a service provider performing manual and emotional labor to produce the hospitality experience for the guest" (p. 236); hosts "are owed none of the protections afforded by employment laws" (p. 243). She takes the 'hostessing society' from Veijola and Jokinen (2008: 176): "'The whole world is not only travelling ... but the whole world is also hosting'" (p. 231; the source's emphasis is not reproduced), and ends: "we seem to have arrived in the 'hostessing society' where the whole world is hosting" (p. 245).
- **Bearing (S6).** For a household host on a platform, the article gives the guest two addressees: the host in person, or the platform's reputation system, which is mutual surveillance and marketing and is run by a firm that disclaims control over hosts (pp. 236-237, 244). It therefore supports the red team's S6: Step 6's 'telephone number that the proprietor answers' meets the household host in person, and the platform's route is not a route to correction. The article is a discourse analysis (p. 235); it shows how responsibility is framed, not what guests do.
