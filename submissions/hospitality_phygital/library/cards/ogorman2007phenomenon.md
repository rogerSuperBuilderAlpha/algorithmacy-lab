## Summary

A review of what philosophers have said about hospitality, written to ask whether hospitality studies has a philosophical foundation. O'Gorman finds that it does not. He takes the material in three parts. On the individual host he sets Telfer's account of hospitable motives beside Derrida's distinction between the one unconditional 'law of hospitality' and the many conditional 'laws'. On hospitality between peoples and states he reports Derrida, Rosello and Ben Jelloun on immigration. On language he reports Derrida on the foreigner made to speak the host's tongue. He concludes that these writers assume one hospitality relationship where there are several, since it 'exists in dissimilar contexts: domestic, civic or commercial, each with their own different sets of laws', and that a coherent philosophy of hospitality remains 'an enigma'.

## Critical notes & threats

**What the article itself claims a host owes.** Very little in its own voice. It reports others. Its own claims are that hospitality in practice is rule-governed and that the rules differ by context, the commercial context included (AM pp. 8, 21-22), and that host and guest 'are subject to transcendent laws of hospitality' (AM p. 17). It names no content for the commercial host's duty.

**Register.** Exposition of philosophy for a management readership. The duties it mentions are Telfer's (a felt 'sense of duty' as one hospitable motive; guests 'the host is obliged to offer hospitality to', in O'Gorman's paraphrase) and Derrida's ('rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional'). Its own closing sentence returns to hospitality as 'a gift', which sits uneasily with duty.

**Person, role or organization.** Persons and states. Telfer's host is a householder. Derrida's is the master of a house or a nation. No firm appears as host. O'Gorman's point that the commercial context has 'its own different sets of laws' is where a commercial host's duty would go, and he leaves the place empty.

**Telfer through this carrier.** Five passages of *Food for Thought* with pages, all on private hospitality. Two matter for K2. Page 82 puts a 'sense of duty' among the hospitable motives, on condition that there is 'also concern for the guests themselves'. Page 101 calls hospitality an 'ideal' that 'presents itself as joyful rather than onerous'. Together they show Telfer placing duty inside hospitableness, as one of its motives, while describing the whole as an ideal and a virtue. O'Gorman's own gloss ('a distinction ... between hospitality for pleasure and hospitality that is born out of a sense of duty') is his and is not in the words he quotes.

**Derrida through this carrier.** Page 77 locates 'rights and duties' in the conditional laws. Page 25 has the law of absolute hospitality command 'a break with hospitality by right'. Pages 59-60 say the difference between guest and parasite needs 'a law'. O'Gorman reads the first as showing that hospitality in practice is 'normally governed by a set of man made rules'.

**Reliability of the citations.** The page numbers for *Of Hospitality* (25, 59f, 77) and for Telfer (82, 101) agree between the 2006 and 2007 versions. The author-date keys do not always: the 2007 text gives 'Derrida 2000a:13' for a sentence the 2006 version cites to Caputo (2002: 111), and 'Derrida 2000b:6' for a line the 2006 version cites to 'Hostipitality' p. 6. The key '2000:21' for the passage on language matches no single entry in the reference list. The passages keyed '2000b:16' and '2000:21' should not be attributed to *Of Hospitality* on this article's authority.

**Kant.** The sentence on Kant (AM pp. 9-10) gives him 'a universal right to shelter'. Kant's text gives a right of visit and no right to shelter (see card kant1795perpetual). The paper should not cite Kant through this article.

**Evidence and authority.** A literature review; no data. O'Gorman's criticism that Telfer argues by 'simple assertions' (AM p. 7) is itself asserted.

**Version.** Locators are author-manuscript pages. For a journal page the version of record (pp. 189-202) must be opened.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K2 (supports the premise; carries the two sources K2 names; narrows one contrast).** The article supports K2's first sentence in a hospitality journal: hospitality is 'governed by a set of man made rules dependent on the context: domestic, civic or commercial' (AM p. 8). It carries Derrida's sentence that rights and duties belong to the conditional laws (AM p. 8) and his sentence that absolute hospitality breaks with hospitality by right (AM p. 9), so both the confirming and the refuting passage K2 asked about are reachable here. And it carries Telfer p. 82, where duty is a hospitable motive. v6 sets 'externally imposed' obligation against hospitableness on Blain and Lashley's authority; on Telfer's words as quoted here that contrast is not hers.

**K2's gap statement (stands for this source).** O'Gorman observes that philosophers of hospitality have given 'even less recognition' to its commercial context (AM pp. 21-22). He does not supply a holder, a trigger or an allocation. This is a published statement, from inside the field, that the commercial case was unaddressed by its philosophical sources in 2007.

**K1 (weak support).** One paraphrase: Telfer's first class of guests are those 'the host is obliged to offer hospitality to' (AM p. 6). It concerns whom a private host must invite, not what a host owes a guest received.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority.**

- O'Gorman holds that hospitality in practice 'is normally governed by a set of man made rules dependent on the context: domestic, civic or commercial' (2007; AM p. 8).
- O'Gorman finds that Telfer and Derrida give little recognition to the fact that 'the hospitality relationship exists in dissimilar contexts: domestic, civic or commercial, each with their own different sets of laws' (2007; AM pp. 21-22).
- Telfer, as quoted in O'Gorman, counts among hospitable motives a 'sense of duty where there is also concern for the guests themselves' (Telfer 1996: 82, as quoted in O'Gorman 2007; AM p. 6).
- Derrida, as quoted in O'Gorman, places in the laws of hospitality 'those rights and duties that are always conditioned and conditional' (*Of Hospitality*: 77, as quoted in O'Gorman 2007; AM p. 8).

**What the paper may not say.**

- Anything about Telfer or Derrida in its own voice on this article's authority. Every such statement needs 'as quoted in'.
- That Telfer opposes duty to hospitableness. The gloss to that effect is O'Gorman's; the quotation he gives includes duty among hospitable motives.
- That O'Gorman states what a commercial host owes, or assigns a duty to a firm.
- Anything about Kant on this article's authority.
- Any journal page number for these passages.
