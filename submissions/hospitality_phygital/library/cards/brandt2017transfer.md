## Summary

Brandt argues that gamete providers cannot transfer to intending parents the responsibilities they acquire toward their biological offspring; at most they can delegate them. He first separates the two in ordinary language. A transfer leaves the person first bound with no trace of the responsibility. A delegation leaves that person with an obligation to ensure that the responsibility is fulfilled and to make good a delegate's failure, whether or not anyone is to blame. He then shows that the examples others offer of parents transferring their duties (nannies, teachers, doctors, boarding schools) are delegations. Finally he argues, through a library book and a bicycle prank, that responsibilities arising from an agreement with the person owed, or from freely putting an unconsenting person at risk, cannot be transferred by an arrangement with a third party. Several people can each be fully responsible for the same outcome, and the delegate's promise gives the delegator recourse against the delegate without reducing what the delegator owes.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The thesis: "In this paper I reject the claim that the responsibilities acquired by gamete providers can be transferred to their biological children’s intending parents." (abstract, ms p. 1) [full-text]
- The sense of 'responsibility': "I use ‘responsibility’ to designate the obligation(s) an individual acquires to work towards bringing about a certain state of affairs"; it is close to "the care-taking sense of responsibility, which is the duty to stand behind something, make it good, or make right one’s failure to do so." (ms p. 2) [full-text]
- The two ways of assigning: "Delegating and transferring are two ways of assigning responsibilities to a third party that are or were possessed by another individual." (ms p. 2) [full-text]
- The example: Mary is in charge of the newsletter; "The student government meets in order to review the different executive portfolios, and everyone agrees that it would be better for the internal academic officer (Peter) to take over production of the weekly newsletter"; Peter then asks a volunteer, Charlie, to do one issue's page layout (ms p. 3). The newsletter "was transferred to Peter, and the job of creating the page layout for the October issue was delegated to Charlie." (ms p. 3) [full-text]
- After a transfer nothing remains: "we do not think that Mary has any special residual obligation to ensure that the weekly newsletter gets produced." (ms p. 3) Brandt qualifies this: "Mary has no special responsibility beyond that which we might think any board member has to ensure the newsletter gets published." (n. 5, ms p. 16) [full-text]
- After a delegation, a duty about choosing: "we think that Peter might be to blame if Charlie fails to produce the page layout —for example, if there was good reason to distrust Charlie’s commitment to the task and more competent volunteers were available." (ms p. 3) [full-text]
- After a delegation, a duty to step in and to repair: "However, regardless of whether Peter is to blame, if Charlie does not complete the task, then we do think that Peter has an obligation to either complete the page layout himself or find someone else to do it. Furthermore, Peter seems obliged to ‘make right’ any negative consequences that arise from Charlie’s poor performance." (ms p. 3) [full-text]
- The general statement: "When responsibilities are transferred, the person initially bound by them no longer has any trace of them. By contrast, when a responsibility is delegated, the individual initially bound by the responsibility still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled." (ms p. 3) [full-text]
- Blame and obligation come apart: "so long as the delegator had good reason to think the delegatee would complete the assigned task, she is not blameworthy if things go awry." (ms pp. 3-4) "However, the delegator still retains the obligation to ensure that the task gets completed, and/or to make up for whatever negative consequences arise due to the delegatee’s failure to fulfil her responsibilities, even in the absence of blame." (ms p. 4) [full-text]
- Both halves named: a gamete provider could "have an ongoing obligation to both ensure that the delegated responsibilities are fulfilled, and make right any shortfalls of the delegatee(s)." (ms p. 4) [full-text]
- The view rejected, first form: "the view that gamete providers can completely discharge their responsibilities by finding suitable parents for their biological offspring" (ms p. 4, citing Archard at n. 6). [full-text]
- The view rejected, as Brandt quotes O'Neill: “begetters and bearers have at various times delegated or transferred some or all of their tasks to wet nurses, relations, tutors, servants, foster homes, and schools including boarding schools. Provided that they take reasonable steps to ensure that their children will be adequately reared, they do not breach but transfer their parental obligations or some part of those obligations” (ms pp. 4-5; Brandt marks "emphasis added" and cites O'Neill 1979 at n. 11 as 'pp. 28'). [full-text]
- The view rejected, general form: "that transferring parental responsibilities is largely unproblematic so long as the transferer takes due care when selecting others to fulfill the responsibilities." (ms p. 5) [full-text]
- The nanny who leaves early: "It is quite clear that the parent cannot simply leave her child unattended, but must either return home or arrange for someone else to care for the child. The claim that the responsibility to look after the child had been transferred to the nanny for the entire evening is no excuse for leaving the child unattended." In the example the nanny "calls to say that she has decided to leave early." (ms p. 5) [full-text]
- The failing doctor or teacher: "If it becomes clear that a doctor or a teacher is failing in their responsibility to meet a child’s medical or educational needs, the child’s parent(s) have the responsibility to find other people to fulfill the child’s needs, or fulfill them themselves if possible." (ms p. 5) [full-text]
- Transfers do occur: "it seems perfectly permissible for an emergency care physician to transfer her responsibility for the care of patients to an equally capable colleague by swapping shifts, even if she could abide by the original schedule." (ms p. 6) [full-text]
- The ground Brandt takes from others, 'Principle R': when free actions put individuals who have not consented at risk of harm, "some degree of responsibility arises to take steps to reduce the likelihood of those harms occurring, and to reduce the severity of those harms if they do occur." (ms p. 7) [full-text]
- The library book: Sam asks her brother James to return her book, he ruins it, and the library charges Sam. "Since Sam entered into an agreement with the library to be responsible for the book, the library can rightfully seek compensation from her. The fact that she engaged another person to help her fulfil this responsibility seems to have no effect on the responsibility she has to return the book in good condition." (ms p. 10) [full-text]
- Why no transfer: "To permit this kind of transfer would amount to permitting the unilateral alteration of a promise, and this seems deeply problematic. In this example, Sam can only delegate to James the responsibility of returning the library book, not transfer it." (ms p. 10) [full-text]
- The bicycle prank: "It is implausible to suppose that an agreement I enter into with a third party (Bob) can absolve me of my responsibility to help my colleague out of the state that I have helped put her in." (ms p. 11) The note adds: "it is implausible than any agreement I enter with anyone but Michelle can relieve me of my obligation to ensure (by my own actions alone, or with the aid of someone else) that Michelle is made whole." (n. 32, ms p. 18) [full-text]
- Reasonable steps do not discharge: "this example also shows that I do not fulfil my responsibility by merely taking reasonable steps to ensure that Michelle’s needs will be fulfilled. Even if I had good reason to trust Bob, I still have responsibilities towards Michelle." Brandt sets this against Archard, who "claims that by taking reasonable steps to ensure that future children will be adequately reared, gamete providers fulfill their responsibilities to the children they help create." (n. 34, ms p. 18) [full-text]
- Several holders at once: "It is perfectly possible for multiple individuals to be fully responsible for ensuring that the same responsibility is fulfilled." (ms p. 12) [full-text]
- The delegate's promise runs to the delegator: "Bob’s promise to take care of any harm that befell Michelle requires that he compensate me for any costs that I incur while fulfilling my obligation to her, even though the promise does not diminish my responsibility to Michelle." (ms p. 12) "This second responsibility that Bob has does not alter my responsibility to Michelle, but it does give me recourse against Bob if he fails to step up and I am required to come to Michelle’s aid." (ms p. 13) [full-text]
- The conclusion of the argument: "the responsibility to minimize the harms likely to befall someone that individuals acquire when their free actions put an unconsenting person at risk cannot be transferred away." (ms p. 13) [full-text]
- Delegation can end the obligation when the delegate performs: "suppose that instead of refusing to help Michelle, I instead have another friend give Michelle a lift to her meeting. Here it seems that I no longer have an obligation to Michelle. Furthermore Michelle does not seem to be wronged in anyway by having a third person do all the ‘work’" (ms p. 13) [full-text]
- The distinction restated: "in the case of delegation, some responsibility is retained by the delegator, while in the case of transfer, responsibility is completely alienated by the transferor." (ms p. 14) [full-text]
- Content left open: "it is unclear what such responsibilities would entail." On one reading gamete providers "need only ensure that their offspring have a minimally decent life, and thus in societies with robust social safety nets it is unlikely that they will ever have to act on their responsibilities." (ms p. 14) [full-text]
- Learning of failure appears once, as an optional policy: "Finally, we may think that policy ought to provide a mechanism for gamete providers to act on their responsibilities if they so wish, but not force them to do so. Anonymously or otherwise, gamete providers could be provided with a mechanism for finding out how their offspring are faring" (ms p. 15) [full-text]
- The article nowhere uses 'monitor', 'oversight', 'oversee' or 'check'. 'Supervision' occurs once, as a parental task that parents outsource: "the commonplace outsourcing of various parental responsibilities, such as education and supervision" (ms p. 1); it is never used of a delegator's watching a delegate (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The argument is by cases.** Brandt appeals to "an ordinary-language description" (ms p. 3) and to what "we think" about Peter, Mary, a nanny, a library book and a prank. He gives no theory of why delegation leaves a residue. A referee can accept the cases and deny that a firm's outsourcing resembles them.
- **What decides transfer against delegation is not stated as a rule.** Mary's transfer happens because "everyone agrees" in the body that assigns portfolios (ms p. 3); the physician's because a colleague takes the shift (ms p. 6). The library case fails as a transfer because the library, the party owed, did not agree: a transfer would be "the unilateral alteration of a promise" (ms p. 10). The reading that a transfer needs the assent of the party owed, or of the body that assigns the role, fits all three cases. Brandt does not say it in those words, and the paper should present it as its reading.
- **The correction half only.** The delegator must act when the delegate "does not complete the task" (ms p. 3) or when "it becomes clear" that the delegate is failing (ms p. 5). In the nanny case the delegate herself telephones (ms p. 5). Brandt asserts no duty to check, to monitor or to keep a line open, and where he mentions "a mechanism for finding out" he offers it as something policy might provide for those who "so wish" (ms p. 15).
- **Blame is conditioned on reasonable choice.** A delegator with "good reason" to trust the delegate "is not blameworthy if things go awry" (ms pp. 3-4). The retained obligation is forward-looking: to see the thing done and to make good. A paper that wants the holder blamed, or liable for damages, gets less from Brandt than one that wants the holder still bound to perform.
- **The domain is far from hospitality.** The responsibilities are parental and the ground is causal ('Principle R'), with one case from agreement (the library). Brandt says nothing of firms, employees, contractors or automated systems.
- **Version.** I read the accepted manuscript. The published pages (665-678) were not seen; quotations should be checked against the journal before any enters the paper with a journal page.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P2 (a holder that performs through others keeps a duty to see that the work is done with care): supports, in the moral register, for responsibilities that are delegated and not transferred.** Brandt states the premise almost in the paper's words: the delegator "still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled" (ms p. 3). He also supplies the reason a host's outsourcing counts as delegation where the duty arises from the host's agreement with the guest: an arrangement with a third party cannot alter what was promised to the party owed (ms p. 10). And he denies the rival view by name: reasonable steps in choosing do not discharge the responsibility (n. 34, ms p. 18; ms p. 5). He narrows P2 in one respect: where the role itself is reassigned with the assent of those entitled to reassign it, nothing stays (ms p. 3; ms p. 6).

**K7, P3 (the duty includes the means to learn of failure and to correct it in time): supports the correcting half, is silent on the learning half.** The retained obligation is to "complete the page layout himself or find someone else to do it" (ms p. 3) and, for the parent, to "return home or arrange for someone else" while the child still needs care (ms p. 5). That is correction while performance is still possible. Nothing in the article requires the delegator to arrange to find out. P3's learning half remains the paper's step so far as this source goes.

**K8 (what stays with the holder; several firms): supports.** For the contract limb: the party that agreed with the person owed remains bound whoever it engages (ms p. 10). For several parties: more than one can be "fully responsible" for the same outcome (ms p. 12), and a delegate's promise to the delegator gives the delegator "recourse" without changing what the delegator owes (ms pp. 12-13). That matches K8's suppliers "who answer to the host".

**K3: indirect.** The library case rests the obligation on an agreement (voluntary assumption) and the prank case on having put another at risk. Neither is a role.

**What the paper may say**

- Brandt distinguishes transferring a responsibility from delegating it: after a transfer the person first bound "no longer has any trace" of it; after a delegation that person "still has the obligation to ensure that the responsibility gets fulfilled" (accepted ms p. 3).
- On Brandt's account the delegator must complete the task or find someone else if the delegate fails, and must make good the consequences, "even in the absence of blame" (ms pp. 3-4).
- Brandt argues that a person who has agreed with another to be responsible for something cannot shift that responsibility to a third party, because this "would amount to permitting the unilateral alteration of a promise" (ms p. 10).
- Brandt rejects the view that a responsibility is discharged by taking reasonable care in choosing who will fulfil it (ms p. 5; n. 34, ms p. 18).
- Brandt holds that several parties can each be fully responsible for seeing the same thing done, and that a delegate's promise gives the delegator recourse against the delegate (ms pp. 12-13).
- Brandt's cases are parents, a student committee, a borrowed book and a prank; the extension to a commercial host is the paper's.

**What the paper may not say**

- That Brandt holds a delegator must monitor the delegate or keep the means of learning that the delegate has failed. He does not say it.
- That Brandt holds responsibilities can never be transferred. He gives cases of transfer (ms pp. 3, 6) and denies it for responsibilities arising from agreement with the party owed or from putting an unconsenting person at risk (ms pp. 10, 13).
- That Brandt states the rule 'transfer requires the consent of the party owed'. That is a reading of his cases.
- That Brandt addresses firms, contractors, vicarious liability, non-delegable duties or automated systems.
- That the delegator is to blame for the delegate's failure. Brandt separates blame from the retained obligation (ms pp. 3-4).
- Any journal page number for a quotation. Only the manuscript was read.
