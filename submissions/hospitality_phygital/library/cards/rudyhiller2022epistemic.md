## Summary

Rudy-Hiller sets out the epistemic condition (EC) for moral responsibility: what an agent must be aware of (action, moral significance, consequences, alternatives) and in what way (occurrently or dispositionally). Most of the entry concerns ignorance. Ignorance excuses unless it is itself culpable; on the tracing view, culpability for ignorance derives from a 'benighting act' (Holly Smith's term), an act or omission that left the agent ignorant, such as failing to read a booklet or ask a colleague, and that breached a 'procedural epistemic obligation' to inform oneself (Rosen's term). Volitionists (Zimmerman, Rosen, Levy) argue that the benighting act must itself be done in full awareness, so that original blame lies only in akratic acts; this regress threatens most ordinary blame. Four replies are reported: weakened internalism (dispositional awareness suffices), epistemic-vice accounts (FitzPatrick, Montmarquet: ignorance from vicious failures of inquiry is culpable without awareness), quality of will (Arpaly, Harman, Talbert), and capacitarianism (Sher, Clarke and others: an agent is blameworthy if she should and could have known). The entry closes by suggesting that the EC may differ with the kind of responsibility in question. Every agent in it is an individual.

- Culpable ignorance does not excuse, on the majority view, as the author reports it: "it’s widely—although not universally (cf. Holly Smith 2017; Ross 1939: 163–4)—accepted that culpable ignorance fails to exculpate (at least completely)" (§2 ¶2). Blame for acting from culpable ignorance is "derivative (or indirect) rather than original (or direct)" (§2 ¶2, citing Zimmerman 1997, Rosen 2004). [full-text]
- The benighting act, reported from Holly Smith (1983): "what Holly Smith (1983) calls a “benighting act”: an action or omission the consequence of which is the lack of a true belief about the impermissibility of one’s conduct. In other words, by performing a benighting act the agent brings about her own ignorance about the wrongness of her subsequent action." The author's examples are omissions: "discarding (without reading it first) the operational booklet that was given to him when he was hired at the fitness center, not asking a colleague to show him around before touching anything, not reading the conspicuous “Caution: activates treadmill” sign placed above the button" (§2 ¶3). [full-text]
- The duty to inform oneself, reported from Rosen (2004): each such omission is wrong "since John was under a ‘procedural epistemic obligation’ (Rosen 2004: 301) to inform himself about the functioning of the place. Wrongness, however, isn’t the same as blameworthiness" (§2 ¶4). [full-text]
- The volitionist regress, reported (Zimmerman 1997, 2008, 2017; Rosen 2004; Levy 2011): culpability for a benighting act "requires exactly the same as culpability for any other act" (§2 ¶4), so "the agent’s ignorance must be at some point the causal upshot of an action or omission performed in full awareness of all the relevant facts" (§2 ¶5); hence "“the only possible locus of original responsibility is an akratic act” (Rosen 2004: 307)" (§2 ¶6). The four theses are listed at §2 ¶9. [full-text]
- The nearest the entry comes to the principle that one cannot escape by not attending, reported as the dispositionalists' argument: "if the occurrentist position were right, one could shield oneself from blame simply by avoid thinking about the moral status of one’s action, and that’s implausible (Guerrero 2007: 74; Timpe 2011: 23; Robichaud 2014: 150; Levy 2014: 31)" (§1.2 ¶7; 'avoid' so in the text). [full-text]
- Acting under known risk, reported: Guerrero holds that an agent unsure whether her act is permissible "can be blameworthy for carrying it out even in the absence of clear-eyed akrasia and even if her ignorance (which is manifested here as lack of certainty) is blameless", on "an independently plausible moral principle that urges restraint in the face of uncertainty"; Nelkin and Rickless hold that "being aware of doing something that poses a risk of forgetting relevant information can anchor responsibility for unwitting wrongdoing" (§3.1 ¶5). [full-text]
- The epistemic-vice account, reported from FitzPatrick (2008): "ignorance is culpable when it results from the violation of epistemic obligations regarding which the agent could reasonably have been expected to comply given her cognitive capacities and the opportunities provided by her social context, and when such violation is due to the agent’s epistemic vices such as “overconfidence, arrogance, dismissiveness, laziness, dogmatism, incuriosity, self-indulgence, contempt, and so on” (2008: 609)" (§3.2 ¶3). His example is Mr. Potter, "a powerful businessman who engages in ruthless business practices" (§3.2 ¶3). Levy's objection: it is not reasonable to expect such an agent to mend his ways when "he has no internal reason" to (§3.2 ¶4). [full-text]
- Montmarquet, as quoted: "if an agent is culpably ignorant of some fact, there will always have been some possible act or omission, but for which this ignorance would not have existed" (1995: 42; §3.2 ¶5). [full-text]
- Capacitarianism, reported, with negligence allowed: "agents satisfy the epistemic requirements on responsibility either if they are aware of the relevant factual and moral considerations or if they should and could be aware of them given the available evidence, the opportunity to adequately process it, and their cognitive capacities" (§3.4 ¶1, after Sher 2009: 87). Its limit: agents are excused "whenever isn’t reasonable to expect them to know better due to an unfavorable epistemic situation" (§3.4 ¶5; so in the text). [full-text]
- The author's distinction between norms of awareness and duties of inquiry: "while norms of awareness directly demand awareness of relevant considerations, duties of inquiry impose obligations to undertake information-gathering actions that contribute to the occurrence of such awareness (Rosen 2004: 302; 2008: 600–1; H. Smith 2014; Wieland 2017a: 6–8). In other words, while norms of awareness are concerned with agents’ states of mind, duties of inquiry are concerned with agents’ activities." (§3.4 ¶7). Murray's 'norms of vigilance' are placed between the two (note 12). [full-text]
- The overlap with legal negligence, reported from Sher: some unwitting-wrongdoing cases, "especially those involving failures to foresee the risk of harm—overlap with the moral and legal category of negligence" (note 11). The note's examples include "a soldier on duty falls asleep leaving the compound unguarded" and a ferry pilot who "fails to steer the ferry clear of some rocks" (note 11). [full-text]
- Affected ignorance appears only in a note: Moody-Adams (1994) "defends the contrary view that moral ignorance is usually “affected” and therefore doesn’t exculpate" (note 9). The bibliography lists Wieland, "Willful Ignorance", *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice* 20(1) (2017c), and Pleasants, "Institutional Wrongdoing and Moral Perception" (2008); neither is discussed in the body. ## Critical notes & threats

- **Reporting, not establishing.** Nearly every substantive sentence reports a named philosopher. The author places his own 2017 paper among the capacitarians in the third person (§3.4 ¶1, ¶5, ¶8). His own assertions are framing ones (§1.1 ¶1; §3.4 ¶7; §4 ¶3). The paper may cite the entry for the map and for what it reports, with the attribution kept.
- **Blame, not duty.** The entry asks when an agent is blameworthy for acting from ignorance. A 'procedural epistemic obligation' enters only as what makes a benighting act wrong (§2 ¶4), and "Wrongness ... isn’t the same as blameworthiness" (§2 ¶4). Nothing says what such an obligation requires of a role-holder or how far it extends.
- **The decisive question is open.** Whether a benighting act done without awareness can ground blame is the dispute the entry maps (§2 ¶5-¶9; §3). On the volitionist view, a host that never considered the risk of an unreachable guest would be excused; on the epistemic-vice and capacitarian views it might not be. The entry adjudicates neither.
- **Individuals only.** John, Mary, Alessandra, Mr. Potter, a soldier, a ferry pilot. Mr. Potter is a businessman, but the entry treats him as a person with vices, not as an organisation (§3.2 ¶3). Organisational or designed ignorance is not discussed.
- **Typographical state.** The saved text has slips ("avoid thinking", "whenever isn’t reasonable", "her wife’s tea"). Quotations above reproduce them.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3: governs the philosophical half; supports a narrow form and supplies the vocabulary.** The tracing account gives P3 a philosophical form: an agent who breaches an obligation to inform itself cannot plead the resulting ignorance (§2 ¶3-¶4), and the dispositionalists reject a view on which one "could shield oneself from blame" by not thinking (§1.2 ¶7). In the entry's terms P3 is a duty of inquiry, an obligation "to undertake information-gathering actions" (§3.4 ¶7), and not a norm of awareness. That classification helps the paper. It also limits it: the entry reports such duties as presupposed by the tracing account (Rosen; H. Smith 2014) and does not say where they come from or what they require. P3's content (a channel from the guest) remains the paper's. The entry also records a live rival: on the volitionist view ignorance excuses unless a benighting act was done in awareness (§2 ¶5-¶6), so a host without a particular suspicion would escape blame.

**What the paper may say**

- Philosophers commonly hold that culpable ignorance does not excuse, though not all agree (Rudy-Hiller 2022: §2, reporting Holly Smith and others).
- On the tracing account, ignorance is culpable when it results from a 'benighting act', "an action or omission the consequence of which is the lack of a true belief about the impermissibility of one’s conduct" (Holly Smith 1983, as reported in Rudy-Hiller 2022: §2), such as failing to read instructions or to ask (§2).
- Rosen calls the obligation breached by such an omission a "‘procedural epistemic obligation’" to inform oneself (Rosen 2004: 301, as quoted in Rudy-Hiller 2022: §2).
- Several philosophers reject accounts on which "one could shield oneself from blame simply by" not thinking about what one is doing (Rudy-Hiller 2022: §1.2, reporting Guerrero, Timpe, Robichaud and Levy).
- Capacitarians hold that an agent can be blameworthy if she "should and could" have been aware of the relevant considerations (Rudy-Hiller 2022: §3.4); volitionists deny it and require that culpable ignorance trace back to an act done in full awareness (§2).
- Rudy-Hiller distinguishes norms of awareness, which concern "states of mind", from duties of inquiry, which concern "activities" (§3.4).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the entry, or the literature it maps, holds that an agent cannot meet a knowledge-conditioned duty by arranging not to know. Nothing in it is put in those terms. The nearest is the reported argument against occurrentism (§1.2 ¶7).
- That the entry treats organisations, firms, hosts, wilful blindness or law. It does not (searched, 11 Nov 2026).
- That the literature agrees that negligent ignorance is culpable. Volitionists deny it (§2), and the question is the subject of the whole entry.
- Any quotation of Holly Smith, Rosen, Zimmerman, FitzPatrick, Montmarquet, Sher or Guerrero without 'as quoted in' or 'as reported in' Rudy-Hiller.
