## Summary

An appeal about a local authority's housing allocation scheme. The appellant argued that the authority's failure to monitor the scheme's effect on disabled households breached the public sector equality duty (Equality Act 2010, s. 149), relying on R (DXK) and R (DMA). The judge below found a s. 149 breach in the failure to monitor but refused a remedy (para 4). The Court of Appeal held there was no breach and dismissed the appeal (paras 77, 82, 84). In doing so it explained what DXK and DMA decided.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The court and outcome: Lewis LJ gave the judgment; 'LADY JUSTICE WHIPPLE: 85. I agree.' 'SIR GEOFFREY VOS, MASTER OF THE ROLLS: 86. I also agree.' The respondent was right that 'there was no breach of section 149 of the 2010 Act in this case' (para 82). [full-text]
- R (DMA) as read: Knowles J held that the failure 'to collect data, and to use that data to ensure that the contractors provided accommodation to failed asylum-seekers within the relevant timescale, meant that the Secretary of State was not in a position to ensure that she was acting lawfully and in a way which complied with her duty under section 4(2) of the 1999 Act' (para 80). [full-text]
- The reading: 'The decisions in DXK and DMA were both concerned with the situation that arose where the Secretary of State had a duty to provide accommodation to a group of people (failed asylum-seekers) and discharged that duty through independent contractors. The Secretary of State needed to have a system in place that ensured that the contractors did provide the accommodation within the relevant timescale ...' (para 81). [full-text]
- The limit: 'The courts were recognising that the necessary information had to be obtained to enable the Secretary of State to discharge the functions under section 4(2) of the 1999 Act to provide accommodation. The courts were not deciding that there was some free standing duty to monitor, derived from section 149 of the 2010 Act' (para 81). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Public law, and the reading concerns s. 149 (the equality duty), not tort or contract.
- It does not overrule or narrow R (DMA) on its own facts; it confines what DMA says about monitoring to a duty performed through contractors.
- The appeal itself concerned a local authority's allocation policy, not hospitality.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7, by analogy in public law: supports the structure.** A duty-holder that performs through contractors needs the information and the system that let it discharge its own duty; there is no free-standing duty to monitor derived from s. 149. That is the paper's claim that the further duty is a necessary means of performing a duty the host holds, not a separate duty of oversight.

**What the paper may say**

- The Court of Appeal has read R (DMA) as requiring a system to ensure that contractors performed a duty the Secretary of State held, and not as creating a free-standing duty to monitor under the Equality Act (RR v Enfield 2025: paras 80-81).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the case concerns hosts, hotels or private law.
- That it narrows R (DMA)'s declaration on its facts.
