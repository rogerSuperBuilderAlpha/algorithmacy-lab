## Summary

A child in local-authority care was abused by two sets of foster parents with whom the authority had placed her. The authority had not been negligent in choosing or supervising them. The Supreme Court held, unanimously, that the authority owed no non-delegable duty to ensure that care was taken in the foster home: under the Child Care Act 1980 its duty was to arrange foster care and then to approve, inspect, supervise and remove, and placing the child was the performance of that duty. By four to one the court then held the authority vicariously liable for the foster parents' torts, because the foster parents were integral to the authority's child-care service and did not carry on an independent business. Lord Hughes dissented on vicarious liability. English law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: UK Supreme Court, judgment given 18 October 2017, heard 8 and 9 February 2017, [2017] UKSC 60, on appeal from [2015] EWCA Civ 1139. Lord Reed, "with whom Lady Hale, Lord Kerr and Lord Clarke agree"; Lord Hughes "dissenting" (headings before paras 1 and 75). [full-text]
- Parties and claim: the claimant was taken into care "when she was aged seven" and was abused in two foster homes between 1985 and 1988 (para 2). She sued the local authority, a public body acting under statute. She was a child placed by the state and no one's paying customer. [full-text]
- The premise of the case: "The local authority were not negligent in the selection or supervision of the foster parents." (para 1) "there was no case that the local authority had failed to exercise reasonable care in the selection of the foster parents or in the supervision and monitoring of the placements." (para 21) [full-text]
- Order of the doctrines: "Liability in tort normally depends on the breach of a duty owed by the defendant to the claimant. The only true exception to that principle under the common law is vicarious liability". There is no rationale for vicarious liability "where he is directly liable for the harm caused by the third party", so the defendant's own duties come first (para 30). [full-text]
- What a non-delegable duty is: "duties not merely to take personal care in performing a given function but to ensure that care is taken. The expression thus refers to a higher standard of care than the ordinary duty of care. Duties involving this higher standard of care are described as non-delegable because they cannot be discharged merely by the exercise of reasonable care in the selection of a third party to whom the function in question is delegated." (para 31) [full-text]
- The category is exceptional: "Tortious liabilities based not on personal fault but on a duty to ensure that care is taken are exceptional, and have to be kept within reasonable limits." The "well-known examples" are employers (a safe system of work), hospitals (patients) and schools (pupils) (para 32). [full-text]
- What stays with the holder under such a duty: "the defendant may delegate the performance of the function, but he remains under a duty to ensure that the function is performed and that, in doing so, care is taken to protect the claimant from harm." (para 34, summarising *Woodland* para 23) [full-text]
- The limit Lord Reed takes from *Woodland* para 25: "in the absence of negligence of their own, for example in the selection of contractors, [the defendants] will not be liable for the negligence of independent contractors where on analysis their own duty is not to perform the relevant function but only to arrange for its performance" (para 34). [full-text]
- The *Woodland* criteria are not a statute: they "may need to be re-considered, and possibly refined, in particular contexts", but no separate fair-just-and-reasonable stage is routinely needed (para 36). [full-text]
- The test: "The critical question ... is whether the function of providing the child with day-to-day care, in the course of which the abuse occurred, was one which the local authority were themselves under a duty to perform with care for the safety of the child, or was one which they were merely bound to arrange to have performed, subject to a duty to take care in making and supervising those arrangements." (para 37) [full-text]
- Statute can create the duty: "It is a familiar aspect of the legislation governing safety at work, for example, that duties are laid on employers which they cannot escape by employing competent contractors. But everything turns on the particular statute." In *Myton v Woods*, as Lord Reed describes it, an education authority that had "no statutory duty to transport children, but only to arrange and pay for it" was not liable for a taxi firm's negligence (para 38). [full-text]
- Why the duty here was to arrange: section 21 of the 1980 Act required the authority to "discharge" its duty by boarding the child out. "The implication of the word “discharge” is that the placement of the child constitutes the performance of the local authority’s duty to provide accommodation and maintenance. It follows that the local authority do not delegate performance of that duty to the persons with whom the child is placed." The authority's duty "is not to perform the function in the course of which the claimant was abused (namely, the provision of daily care), but rather to arrange for, and then monitor, its performance." It is not "absolved of all responsibility" (para 47). [full-text]
- What the arranger's continuing responsibility consists of: it "is discharged in relation to the boarding-out of children by means of prior approval of the households in which they are placed, and subsequent inspection, supervision and removal if appropriate, in accordance with the relevant regulations. The objective of section 22, and of the regulations made under it, is to ensure that potential problems arising during a foster placement are avoided if possible by means of prior approval of the households involved, and that any problems subsequently arising are identified and addressed once they have become capable of observation by means of inspection and supervision. The statutory regime does not impose on the local authority any other responsibility for the day-to-day care of the child or for ensuring that no harm comes to the child in the course of that care." (para 48) [full-text]
- The machinery of that supervision, from the 1955 Regulations as the court sets them out: removal "forthwith" if a visitor thought the child endangered (reg. 5, para 8); written reports by visitors (reg. 9, para 8); a visit "within one month after the commencement of the placement" and then at least every one to three months (reg. 21, para 9); reviews at least every six months "by persons who did not usually act as visitors" (reg. 22, para 9); and an undertaking by the foster parents "that they would inform the local authority immediately of any serious occurrence affecting the child" and "would at all times permit any person authorised by the local authority to see the child" (reg. 20, para 9). [full-text]
- A channel from the child, as to decisions: section 18(1) required the authority, "in reaching any decision as to a child in their care", "so far as practicable to ascertain the wishes and feelings of the child regarding the decision and give due consideration to them" (para 4, the court's paraphrase). [full-text]
- Non-delegable duty refused: "the proposition that a local authority is under a duty to ensure that reasonable care is taken for the safety of children in care, while they are in the care and control of foster parents, is too broad, and that the responsibility with which it fixes local authorities is too demanding." (para 49) Reasons include the analogy with parents, who owe "a duty to take reasonable care" and no duty to "ensure that reasonable care is taken by anyone else to whom the safety of the children may be entrusted" (para 41), and the risk of "a form of state insurance for the actions of the child’s family members" (para 45). [full-text]
- The two doctrines are distinct: to deny a non-delegable duty because vicarious liability is absent "is to conflate two distinct legal doctrines with different incidents and different rationales" (para 50). [full-text]
- A non-delegable duty can be breached by a deliberate wrong; the examples are bailment cases. From *Port Swettenham Authority v T W Wu*, as quoted: "When, a bailee puts goods which have been bailed to him in the care of his servants for safe custody, there can be no doubt that the bailee is responsible if the goods are lost through any failure of those servants to take proper care of the goods" (para 51). [full-text]
- Vicarious liability imposed. The foster parents "cannot be regarded as carrying on an independent business of their own" (para 59); they "provided care to the child as an integral part of the local authority’s organisation of its child care services" (para 60); placement "creates a relationship of authority and trust between the foster parents and the children, in circumstances where close control cannot be exercised by the local authority, and so renders the children particularly vulnerable to abuse" (para 61); and the authority "exercised powers of approval, inspection, supervision and removal without any parallel in ordinary family life" and so "a significant degree of control over both what the foster parents did and how they did it" (para 62). "It is not necessary for there to be micro-management, or any high degree of control, in order for vicarious liability to be imposed." (para 65) [full-text]
- Liability and supervision: if abuse by foster parents is widespread, "exposure, and the risk of liability, might encourage more adequate vetting and supervision", a suggestion Lord Reed calls "empirically untested" (para 69). [full-text]
- Disposition: "I would allow the appeal, and hold that the local authority are vicariously liable for the torts committed by the foster parents in this case." (para 74) The claim succeeded on vicarious liability and failed on non-delegable duty. [full-text]
- Lord Hughes, agreeing on non-delegable duty: "Liability under a non-delegable duty is, in effect, a liability to guarantee that others provide all reasonable care and, it may well follow, abstain from deliberate tortious behaviour." (para 75) [full-text]
- Lord Hughes, dissenting on vicarious liability: foster parents "look, if the business model is to be used, a great deal more like independent contractors, carefully selected and supervised as many a panel of such contractors is" (para 76); what the authority does "is to take responsibility for making decisions about where the children shall live, and then monitoring the progress with a view to changing the arrangements if they do not benefit the children" (para 87); "Vicarious liability is strict liability, imposed on a party which has been in no sense at fault." (para 91) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Two duties, and oversight belongs to the lesser one.** Lord Reed sets a duty "to perform with care" against a duty "to arrange to have performed, subject to a duty to take care in making and supervising those arrangements" (para 37). Under the first, careful selection is no answer (para 31) and, on Lord Hughes's gloss, the holder guarantees the delegate's care (para 75); the court gives that duty no content of monitoring, because liability does not turn on what the holder knew or did. Under the second the holder must approve, inspect, supervise and remove (para 48), and is liable only for its own want of care. The paper's P3 reads oversight into "a duty to see that something is done". In this judgment the language of seeing-to-it describes the strict duty, and the language of monitoring describes the fault-based one.
- **The monitoring duty came from a statute.** Paras 47-48 construe sections 21 and 22 of the Child Care Act 1980 and the Boarding-Out of Children Regulations 1955. Lord Reed does not derive monitoring from the concept of a duty; he reads it off the regulations, and says "everything turns on the particular statute" (para 38). It cannot be cited as a general common-law principle that whoever arranges must monitor.
- **The monitoring duty was met and the harm still happened.** The authority supervised without negligence (paras 1, 21) and the child was abused in two homes. The duty is one of reasonable care in supervising, and the regime aims at problems "once they have become capable of observation" (para 48). It is no duty to detect everything.
- **The channel was an inspector's, not the child's.** The regulations sent visitors and reviewers into the home and bound the foster parents to report (paras 8-9). The only provision the court mentions that runs from the child is section 18(1), on ascertaining the child's wishes about a decision (para 4). The judgment does not say the child's own report was the means of learning.
- **Distance from hospitality.** A child, compulsory care, a public authority, no contract, no payment. The vicarious-liability holding turned on recruitment, training and control of the foster parents by the authority (paras 59-62); *Barclays* later read it as a case about a relationship that was not an independent business (see barclays2020uksc, para 23 there).
- **Later treatment, as far as read.** *Barclays* [2020] UKSC 13, paras 23-24, treats *Armes* as consistent with the employee/independent-contractor distinction. *Hughes v Rattan* [2022] EWCA Civ 107, paras 61-63, records the para 37 test being argued for a practice owner and, in the result, not applied in his favour. Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): narrows, and supplies the test.** A non-delegable duty of care is exceptional (para 32). It attaches where the holder's own duty is to perform the function with care, and it does not attach where the holder's duty is to arrange and supervise (paras 34, 37, 47). For K8 the question for each host, brand or platform becomes Lord Reed's question: was this party bound to perform the function, or to arrange for it? The case also supports K8's goods limb by analogy, through the bailment cases it treats as non-delegable duties (para 51), though it says nothing of innkeepers.

**K7, premise P2: supports in a divided form.** Something stays with the holder in both branches: under the strict duty, "a duty to ensure that the function is performed and that, in doing so, care is taken" (para 34); under the lesser one, "a duty to take care in making and supervising those arrangements" (para 37).

**K7, premise P3: supports in one statutory setting, and limits.** The court describes a continuing responsibility made of prior approval, inspection, supervision and removal, whose object is that problems "are identified and addressed once they have become capable of observation" (para 48). That is P3's structure: means to learn, and a power to correct while the placement lasts. But the court finds it in regulations, attaches it to the arranger and not to the holder of the strict duty, and treats it as a duty of reasonable care that was discharged although the abuse went undetected (paras 1, 21, 48). The judgment gives no support to the proposition that a duty to see that care is taken, as such, includes a duty to have means of learning that it is not.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- A non-delegable duty is a duty "to ensure that care is taken", which "cannot be discharged merely by the exercise of reasonable care in the selection of a third party" (para 31).
- The Supreme Court calls liabilities of that kind "exceptional" (para 32).
- The dividing question is whether the defendant was bound to perform a function with care or "merely bound to arrange to have performed, subject to a duty to take care in making and supervising those arrangements" (para 37).
- Where a statute made a public authority an arranger, the Supreme Court described its continuing responsibility as prior approval and "subsequent inspection, supervision and removal", aimed at identifying and addressing problems "once they have become capable of observation" (para 48).
- A body that recruits, trains, pays and supervises carers who run no independent business may be vicariously liable for them without day-to-day control (paras 59-62, 65).

**What it may not say.**
- That English law requires a duty-holder who delegates to monitor the delegate. The monitoring duty in *Armes* was statutory and belonged to an arranger (paras 38, 47-48).
- That a non-delegable duty is a duty of oversight. It is liability for the delegate's want of care whatever the holder's oversight (paras 31, 75).
- That monitoring must succeed. The authority here monitored without negligence and the abuse was not prevented (paras 1, 21).
- That the case supports a channel from the person protected. The mechanisms were visits, reviews and reports by the carers (paras 8-9).
- That *Armes* extends non-delegable duties. It refused one (para 49).
