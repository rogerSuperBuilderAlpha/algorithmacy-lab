## Summary

Cyanamid held a patent for absorbable surgical sutures and sued Ethicon, the dominant supplier of catgut sutures, to stop it launching a rival product before the patent action was tried. The trial judge granted an interlocutory (interim) injunction. The Court of Appeal discharged it, holding that a claimant must first show a prima facie case on the evidence. The House of Lords restored the injunction. Lord Diplock, with whom the other four agreed, held that there is no such rule: the court need only be satisfied that there is "a serious question to be tried" and should then consider the balance of convenience. The governing consideration in that balance is whether each side could be adequately compensated in damages for what it would lose in the period before trial. If the claimant could be, and the defendant could pay, no interim injunction should normally be granted. If not, the court asks the same of the defendant under the claimant's undertaking in damages, and where doubt remains weighs the uncompensatable harm to each, keeping the status quo where things are even. English law; a commercial dispute between two companies.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The case**

- House of Lords, 5 February 1975. Lord Diplock gave the only reasoned speech; Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Cross of Chelsea, Lord Salmon and Lord Edmund-Davies agreed (pp. 6-7). [full-text]
- "On 5th March, 1973, Cyanamid started a quia timet action against Ethicon for an injunction to restrain the threatened infringement of their patent by supplying sutures made of XLG to surgeons in the United Kingdom." (p. 1) The action was brought before any infringement, to prevent one. [full-text]
- "The grant of an interlocutory injunction is a remedy that is both temporary and discretionary." (p. 2) [full-text]
- Disposition: "I would allow the appeal and restore the order of Graham J." (p. 6) [full-text]

**Why a court acts before the right is decided**

- The situation: the decision "has to be taken at a time when ex hypothesi the existence of the right or the violation of it, or both, is uncertain and will remain uncertain until final judgment is given in the action." (p. 3) [full-text]
- The origin of the remedy: "It was to mitigate the risk of injustice to the plaintiff during the period before that uncertainty could be resolved that the practice arose of granting him relief by way of interlocutory injunction" (p. 3). [full-text]
- Its price: since the middle of the nineteenth century the relief "has been made subject to his undertaking to pay damages to the defendant for any loss sustained by reason of the injunction if it should be held at the trial that the plaintiff had not been entitled to restrain the defendant from doing what he was threatening to do." (p. 3) [full-text]
- The object: "The object of the interlocutory injunction is to protect the plaintiff against injury by violation of his right for which he could not be adequately compensated in damages recoverable in the action if the uncertainty were resolved in his favour at the trial; but the plaintiff's need for such protection must be weighed against the corresponding need of the defendant to be protected against injury resulting from his having been prevented from exercising his own legal rights for which he could not be adequately compensated under the plaintiff's undertaking in damages if the uncertainty were resolved in the defendant's favour at the trial." (p. 3) [full-text]
- "The Court must weigh one need against another" (p. 3). [full-text]

**The threshold**

- "The court no doubt must be satisfied that the claim is not frivolous or vexatious; in other words, that there is a serious question to be tried." (p. 4) [full-text]
- The court does not try the merits at this stage: "It is no part of the court's function at this stage of the litigation to try to resolve conflicts of evidence on affidavit as to facts on which the claims of either party may ultimately depend nor to decide difficult questions of law which call for detailed argument and mature considerations." (p. 4) [full-text]

**Adequacy of damages**

- The first question: "As to that, the governing principle is that the court should first consider whether if the plaintiff were to succeed at the trial in establishing his right to a permanent injunction he would be adequately compensated by an award of damages for the loss he would have sustained as a result of the defendant's continuing to do what was sought to be enjoined between the time of the application and the time of the trial." (p. 5) [full-text]
- The limit: "If damages in the measure recoverable at common law would be adequate remedy and the defendant would be in a financial position to pay them, no interlocutory injunction should normally be granted, however strong the plaintiff's claim appeared to be at that stage." (p. 5) [full-text]
- The second question, in my summary: if damages would not be adequate for the claimant, the court asks whether the defendant, should it win at trial, would be adequately compensated under the claimant's undertaking for being restrained in the meantime; if so, and the claimant can pay, that is no ground to refuse (p. 5). [full-text]
- When the balance arises: "It is where there is doubt as to the adequacy of the respective remedies in damages available to either party or to both, that the question of balance of convenience arises." (p. 5) [full-text]
- "Where other factors appear to be evenly balanced it is a counsel of prudence to take such measures as are calculated to preserve the status quo." (p. 5) [full-text]
- The weight of what money cannot mend: "The extent to which the disadvantages to each party would be incapable of being compensated in damages in the event of his succeeding at the trial is always a significant factor in assessing where the balance of convenience lies" (p. 5). [full-text]
- Strength of case comes in last and rarely: it may tip the balance only "if the extent of the uncompensatable disadvantage to each party would not differ widely" (p. 5). [full-text]

**Why damages were not enough here**

- Ethicon's product was "not yet on the market; so they had no business which would be brought to a stop by the injunction" (p. 6), and "no factories would be closed and no work-people would be thrown out of work." (p. 6) [full-text]
- Cyanamid's loss could not be put right later: once doctors and patients were used to the rival product, "it might well be commercially impracticable for Cyanamid to deprive the public of it by insisting on a permanent injunction at the trial" (p. 6). [full-text]
- The speeches do not mention consumers, hotels, guests or any duty of a business to its customers (whole text read). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A rule for courts, between litigants.** The case tells a judge when to restrain a defendant until trial. It lays no duty on anyone to set up a means of being stopped. It presupposes a claimant who has reached a court and applied; the guest at a hotel door at midnight has not. The principle travels only as reasoning: intervention before the event is justified by the inadequacy of repair after it.
- **The limit is half the principle.** "no interlocutory injunction should normally be granted" where damages are adequate and the defendant can pay (p. 5). For most failures of a hotel stay, English law would say damages are adequate. The paper's own cases for acting first (shelter for the night, safety, access) must therefore be argued as losses money cannot mend, and the case supplies no list of such losses.
- **The balance is two-sided.** The court weighs the defendant's uncompensatable loss from being stopped against the claimant's (p. 3). A host argument that borrows this case must allow that stopping a system has costs to the host that count.
- **The claimant pays for being wrong.** Interim relief is "subject to his undertaking to pay damages to the defendant" (p. 3). Nothing in K7 corresponds to that.
- **Commercial facts.** The harm was to market share under a patent. The judgment has nothing to say about personal safety or shelter.
- **Interlocutory relief only.** The speech does not discuss final injunctions or specific performance, and I read nothing on them.
- **Locators are transcript pages.** A printed citation needs the Appeal Cases page, which I could not see.
- Later treatment not checked. The note at the head of the BAILII page summarising "American Cyanamid principles" is an editorial gloss and is not quoted here.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7's timing line: supports the principle from the law of remedies, and states the objection in the same passage.** K7 holds that the channel belongs to the period in which performance is still possible, and repair to the period after. Lord Diplock gives the English courts' version: they act early "to protect the plaintiff against injury by violation of his right for which he could not be adequately compensated in damages" (p. 3). That is authority for the form of the argument (earlier intervention is owed where later money cannot make the loss good). It is equally authority for the tort lawyer's objection recorded in CLAIMS_FIXED.md, that the law is ordinarily "satisfied by damages afterwards": where damages are adequate the court does not intervene, "however strong the plaintiff's claim" (p. 5). K7's conclusion goes further than the case. It asks the holder to keep a channel open for every guest in advance; the court's remedy is granted case by case, on application, after weighing both sides.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In English law the object of an interim injunction is to protect a claimant against injury "for which he could not be adequately compensated in damages" if he wins at trial (*American Cyanamid Co. v Ethicon Ltd* [1975] AC 396, Lord Diplock; transcript p. 3).
- Where damages would be an adequate remedy and the defendant could pay them, "no interlocutory injunction should normally be granted, however strong the plaintiff's claim appeared to be at that stage" (transcript p. 5).
- The court weighs the claimant's need for protection against the defendant's need to be protected from loss caused by being wrongly restrained (transcript p. 3).
- Where other factors are evenly balanced, "it is a counsel of prudence to take such measures as are calculated to preserve the status quo" (transcript p. 5).

**What the paper may not say**

- That English law requires a business to let a customer stop a decision before it takes effect. The case concerns a court's power to restrain a defendant until trial.
- That the case shows damages are an inadequate remedy for a failed hotel stay. It says nothing about such a case, and its default points the other way.
- That the courts prefer prevention to compensation. The first question is whether compensation would be adequate, and if it would, no injunction should normally be granted (p. 5).
- That the principle is owed to the claimant alone. The defendant's uncompensatable loss is weighed too (p. 3).
