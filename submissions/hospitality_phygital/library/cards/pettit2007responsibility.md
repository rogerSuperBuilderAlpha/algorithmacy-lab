## Summary

Pettit argues that a group agent can be fit to be held responsible in its own right, even where individual members bear diminished responsibility: 'No incorporated agency without incorporated responsibility'. Fitness to be held responsible needs three conditions: the agent faces a value-relevant choice, can judge the value of the options, and has control to choose on that judgment. Groups that act on shared intentions under a constitution form agents, and the discursive dilemma shows their attitudes cannot be a function of members' attitudes, so they are autonomous. They can form value judgments because members can put evaluative propositions to them. They have control in the way a higher-level cause 'programs' for an effect that lower-level causes implement: the group arranges that some member will perform and that others will step in if the performers fail. Holding the group responsible matters because enactor responsibility may fall short, and because without it people could incorporate to escape responsibility. A merely unincorporated collection is not an agent and cannot be held responsible as a group, though holding loose groupings responsible may have a developmental point.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The slogan: "No incorporated agency without incorporated responsibility, and this, even when individual responsibility is diminished." (p. 172) [full-text]
- Which groups: "Examples include companies, parties, churches, and universities but also partnerships, voluntary associations, and town meetings." (p. 172) [full-text]
- Accountable versus responsible: holding responsible "is distinct from just holding an agent accountable—identifying that person as the one who carries the can, the one who sits at the desk where the buck stops." "The grounds on which someone can be held accountable are much less demanding than the grounds on which they can be held responsible." (p. 173) [full-text]
- Blame is directed: "my assumption is that blaming involves adopting or identifying with the stance of a creditor: someone to whom at least an apology is owed." (p. 174) [full-text]
- The three conditions: value relevance, value judgment ("the understanding and access to evidence required for being able to make judgments about the relative value of such options") and value sensitivity ("the control necessary for being able to choose between options on the basis of judgments about their value"). (p. 175) [full-text]
- Liability as a consequence: corporate bodies "are fit to be held responsible in the same way as individual agents, and this entails that it may therefore be appropriate to make them criminally liable for some things done in their name; they may display a guilty mind, a mens rea, as in intentional malice, malice with foresight, negligence, or recklessness." (p. 176) [full-text]
- Design fault: "it would seem to be a serious design fault, at least from the viewpoint of society as a whole, to allow any group agents to avoid making evaluations of this kind." (p. 187) [full-text]
- How a group controls what members do: "It will do this, by maintaining a constitution for the formation and enactment of its attitudes, arranging things so that some individual or individuals are identified as the agents to perform a required task, and other individuals are identified as agents to ensure that should the performers fail, there will be others to take their place as backups." (p. 192) [full-text]
- What the group answers for: "the group agent will be fit to be held responsible for ensuring, or more or less ensuring, that one or more of its members will perform in the relevant manner." "The group agent as a whole will have responsibility as the source of that deed: the ultimate, reason-sensitive planner at its origin." (p. 192) "But the corporate entity will also have to answer for what it ensures will be done, drawing on the resources provided by such members." (p. 192) [full-text]
- Three kinds of individual responsibility: as designers, members and enactors; enactors include "negative enactors", "those who could have blocked or inhibited or at least challenged the initiatives of the positive enactors and omitted to do so." (pp. 193-194) [full-text]
- The unincorporated collection: members may each have partial excuses, "But neither can the members be held collectively, as distinct from individually, responsible, that is, responsible as a collection or group. For the collection that they constitute, being unincorporated, is not an agent. It fails the first condition for fitness to be held responsible." (p. 195) [full-text]
- What the corporate agent answers for: "We can hold the corporate entity responsible for the harm that it arranges to have done, given the decisions it licenses and the constitution by which it channels those decisions." (p. 196) The regime "would hold the corporate agent responsible for having organized things so that such harm was likely or inevitable." (p. 197) [full-text]
- External complainants: in the workers' cooperative example, the spouses who object to the decision "can only blame the corporate group as a whole." (p. 198) Note 37: "There is a problem of no hands, we might say, rather than the more familiar problem of many hands." (p. 198, n. 37) [full-text]
- Obligations, once, reported: Green's lesson "that the greater powers of institutional agents may occasion more serious obligations in spheres such as that of global justice" (p. 198, n. 38). [full-text]
- Loose groupings: "It may not be strictly appropriate to hold such loose groupings responsible, since various of the conditions necessary for fitness to be held responsible will be missing. But holding it responsible may actually prompt the grouping to incorporate and organize against the condemned behavior." (p. 201) [full-text]
- 'Responsibilizable' entities: we ascribe responsibility "not just to agents that are fit to be held responsible but also to entities that are capable of being made fit to be held responsible." (p. 201) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Responsibility, not duty.** Pettit asks when a group is fit to be held responsible, that is, blamed or praised (pp. 173-175). He does not argue that a firm bears duties, though the conditions he defends are those of a moral agent, and he says firms may display negligence (p. 176). Use him for the firm as a bearer of responsibility; the step to the firm as a bearer of duties is the paper's own *(my inference)*.
- **A firm, not an assemblage.** The argument requires a constitution under which the group forms and enacts attitudes (pp. 179-180, 192). A brand, an operator and a platform that host together under contracts, with no common constitution, are on his account an unincorporated collection, which "is not an agent" (p. 195) *(application mine)*. Pettit offers only a developmental reason for holding such groupings responsible (pp. 200-201).
- **Backups are inside the group.** The control argument has the group arrange that "should the performers fail, there will be others to take their place as backups" (p. 192). Those backups are members. The article does not discuss outside contractors.
- **Blame as a creditor's stance** (p. 174) makes the person wronged the one to whom an apology is owed; Pettit does not develop this into a theory of directed duties.
- **Accountability is a lesser thing** in Pettit's usage: the one "who carries the can" (p. 173). That usage differs from Fox's forum-based accountability used elsewhere in the dossier.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (a firm as holder): supports for responsibility.** An incorporated firm is fit to be held responsible in its own right (pp. 172, 192), and is answerable for "having organized things so that such harm was likely or inevitable" (p. 197). That fits P3's claim that a firm answers for its own design, alongside Hormio. The step from responsibility to duty is not in the text.

**K7, P3: a partial analogue.** A group's control over what its members do consists partly in arranging backups for when performers fail (p. 192). That is an internal fall-back, the firm's own way of making sure performance happens. Pettit does not mention any route from those affected.

**Red team S9 (several firms that are not one agent): cuts against any allocation to the group as such.** A collection of firms without a common constitution is not an agent and cannot be held responsible as a group (p. 195). The article gives no rule for allocating responsibility among several firms. It leaves each incorporated firm answerable for its own organisation (pp. 196-197). An S9 repair that assigns the routing duty to one firm needs another ground *(my inference)*.

**What the paper may say**

- Pettit argues that an organised group, a company included, can be fit to be held responsible in its own right, even where its members' responsibility is diminished (pp. 171-172, 192).
- On his account a corporate agent answers for "having organized things so that such harm was likely or inevitable" (p. 197), and its control over performance includes arranging backups for when performers fail (p. 192).
- He holds that an unincorporated collection is not an agent and cannot be held responsible as a group (p. 195).

**What the paper may not say**

- That Pettit argues firms bear duties, or says anything about duties owed to customers or guests.
- That he gives a rule for allocating responsibility among several firms.
- That he treats a contractual assemblage of firms as a group agent.
