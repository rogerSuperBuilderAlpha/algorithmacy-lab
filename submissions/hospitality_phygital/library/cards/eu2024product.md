## Summary

The new Product Liability Directive replaces Directive 85/374/EEC with effect from 9 December 2026. It makes economic operators strictly liable to natural persons for damage caused by defective products. 'Product' now includes software (art. 4(1)); recital 13 names "AI systems" among examples and says software is a product "irrespective of the mode of its supply or usage", including software-as-a-service. A digital service integrated into or inter-connected with a product so that the product cannot perform without it is a 'related service' and counts as a component (art. 4(3)-(4)). The claimant may be "any natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product" (art. 5(1)). Compensable damage is death or personal injury (including medically recognised psychological harm), damage to property not used exclusively for professional purposes, and destruction or corruption of non-professional data (art. 6(1)). A product is defective where it "does not provide the safety that a person is entitled to expect" (art. 7(1)). The manufacturer is liable, and so is the manufacturer of a defective component integrated within its control (art. 8(1)); several liable operators are jointly and severally liable (art. 12(1)), and liability cannot be excluded by contract (art. 15). The Directive applies "to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026" (art. 2(1)).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**From when and for whom**

- "This Directive shall apply to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026." (art. 2(1)). Directive 85/374/EEC is repealed "with effect from 9 December 2026. However, it shall continue to apply with regard to products placed on the market or put into service before that date." (art. 21). Transposition deadline 9 December 2026 (art. 22(1)). [full-text]
- "Member States shall ensure that any natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product (the ‘injured person’) is entitled to compensation in accordance with this Directive." (art. 5(1)). [full-text]
- Objective: "a high level of protection of consumers and other natural persons" (art. 1, second paragraph). [full-text]

**Software and AI as products**

- "‘product’ means all movables, even if integrated into, or inter-connected with, another movable or an immovable; it includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials and software;" (art. 4(1)). [full-text]
- Recital 13: "Software, such as operating systems, firmware, computer programs, applications or AI systems, is increasingly common on the market and plays an increasingly important role for product safety." It continues: "it should be clarified in this Directive that software is a product for the purposes of applying no-fault liability, irrespective of the mode of its supply or usage, and therefore irrespective of whether the software is stored on a device, accessed through a communication network or cloud technologies, or supplied through a software-as-a-service model. Information is not, however, to be considered a product ... A developer or producer of software, including AI system providers within the meaning of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 ..., should be treated as a manufacturer." [full-text]
- Recital 6: "no-fault liability for defective products should apply to all movables, including software, including when they are integrated into other movables or installed in immovables." [full-text]
- "‘related service’ means a digital service that is integrated into, or inter-connected with, a product in such a way that its absence would prevent the product from performing one or more of its functions;" (art. 4(3)); a 'component' includes a related service (art. 4(4)). [full-text]
- Recital 17: "While this Directive should not apply to services as such, it is necessary to extend no-fault liability to such integrated or inter-connected digital services as they determine the safety of the product just as much as physical or digital components." [full-text]
- 'manufacturer' includes any person who "(a) develops, manufactures or produces a product; (b) has a product designed or manufactured, or who, by putting their name, trademark or other distinguishing features on that product, presents themselves as its manufacturer; or (c) develops, manufactures or produces a product for their own use;" (art. 4(10)). [full-text]
- "‘putting into service’ means the first use of a product in the Union in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge, in circumstances in which that product has not been placed on the market prior to its first use;" (art. 4(9)). [full-text]

**What damage, what defect, who pays**

- "The right to compensation pursuant to Article 5 shall apply in respect of only the following types of damage: (a) death or personal injury, including medically recognised damage to psychological health; (b) damage to, or destruction of, any property, except: (i) the defective product itself; ... (iii) property used exclusively for professional purposes; (c) destruction or corruption of data that are not used for professional purposes." (art. 6(1)). Non-material losses resulting from that damage are covered "in so far as they can be compensated for under national law" (art. 6(2)). [full-text]
- Recital 24: "Types of damage other than those provided for in this Directive, such as pure economic loss, privacy infringements or discrimination, should not by themselves trigger liability under this Directive." [full-text]
- "A product shall be considered defective where it does not provide the safety that a person is entitled to expect or that is required under Union or national law." (art. 7(1)); circumstances include "the effect on the product of any ability to continue to learn or acquire new features after it is placed on the market or put into service" (art. 7(2)(c)) and "in the case of a product whose very purpose is to prevent damage, any failure of the product to fulfil that purpose" (art. 7(2)(i)). [full-text]
- "Member States shall ensure that the following economic operators are liable for damage in accordance with this Directive: (a) the manufacturer of a defective product; (b) the manufacturer of a defective component, where that component was integrated into, or inter-connected with, a product within the manufacturer’s control and caused that product to be defective" (art. 8(1)). [full-text]
- Several operators: "where two or more economic operators are liable for the same damage pursuant to this Directive, they can be held liable jointly and severally." (art. 12(1)). Third parties: liability "is not reduced or disallowed where the damage is caused both by the defectiveness of a product and by an act or omission of a third party" (art. 13(1)); it "may be reduced or disallowed where the damage is caused both by the defectiveness of the product and by the fault of the injured person" (art. 13(2)). [full-text]
- "Member States shall ensure that the liability of an economic operator pursuant to this Directive is not, in relation to the injured person, limited or excluded by a contractual provision or by national law." (art. 15). [full-text]
- Proof: the claimant must prove "the defectiveness of the product, the damage suffered and the causal link between that defectiveness and that damage" (art. 10(1)); defectiveness is presumed where "the damage was caused by an obvious malfunction of the product during reasonably foreseeable use or under ordinary circumstances" (art. 10(2)(c)). [full-text]
- Software updates: an operator is not exempted where the defect is due to "software, including software updates or upgrades" or "a lack of software updates or upgrades necessary to maintain safety", provided it is within the manufacturer's control (art. 11(2)(b)-(c)). [full-text]
- What it leaves alone: the Directive "does not affect ... any right which an injured person has under national rules concerning contractual liability or concerning non-contractual liability on grounds other than the defectiveness of a product" (art. 2(4)(b)). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A rival to the system-vendor row, for physical harm.** For a guest-facing smart lock, alarm, lift control or check-in system placed on the EU market after 9 December 2026 that injures a guest through a safety defect, the guest may sue the manufacturer directly and without proving fault (arts 2(1), 4(1), 5(1), 6(1)(a), 8(1)(a)). The vendor then answers to the guest, not only to the holder. The red team's T13 holds on the text.
- **Safety, not performance.** Defect means a want of "the safety that a person is entitled to expect" (art. 7(1)). A system that wrongly refuses entry, loses a booking or cannot be overridden fails in performance; unless that failure causes death, injury, property damage or data loss of the kinds in art. 6(1), the Directive does not reach it (recital 24 excludes pure economic loss). For those failures the dossier's allocation (vendor to holder, holder to guest) is not displaced by this instrument *(my inference)*.
- **Services as such are out.** Recital 17 says the Directive "should not apply to services as such". A hotel's own performance is not a product; only software and integrated digital services are reached.
- **The hotel group may itself be a manufacturer.** A person who "develops, manufactures or produces a product for their own use" is a manufacturer (art. 4(10)(c)), and putting into service covers first use "in the course of a commercial activity" without prior placing on the market (art. 4(9)). A brand or hotel group that develops its own guest-facing software and puts it into service in the Union after 9 December 2026 may on that wording be strictly liable for its safety defects *(my inference from arts 4(9)-(10), 8(1)(a); no case)*.
- **Several holders.** Joint and several liability (art. 12(1)) and no reduction for third-party acts (art. 13(1)) match T10's picture of concurrent holders with contribution handled by national law ("Without prejudice to national law concerning rights of contribution or recourse", art. 12(1)).
- **Not yet in force for any product.** It applies only to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 (art. 2(1)); older products stay under Directive 85/374/EEC (art. 21), which I did not read.
- **EU law only.** Whether the Consumer Protection Act 1987, Part I, treats software as a product in the United Kingdom is not answered here (lead 7 in REDTEAM_TORT.md).
- Recitals 26-49, 51 onward and the correlation table not read.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): refutes the system-vendor row for physical harm in the EU; leaves it standing for performance failures.** The dossier's row says the vendor "Answers to the holder, and the holder answers to the guest". For damage of the kinds in art. 6(1) caused by a defective product placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026, the manufacturer answers to "any natural person" injured (art. 5(1)), jointly and severally with other liable operators (art. 12(1)), and cannot contract out (art. 15). Step 5 should say so and confine its row to performance failures and to loss outside art. 6(1).

**K9 (what mediation changes): narrows.** Mediation by software adds a strictly liable addressee for the guest's physical harm. It adds none for refusal or delay unless they cause damage of the listed kinds.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- Under Directive (EU) 2024/2853 software is a product (art. 4(1)), and recital 13 names "AI systems" among the examples.
- Any natural person who suffers damage caused by a defective product is entitled to compensation (art. 5(1)), for death or personal injury, damage to property not used exclusively for professional purposes, and destruction or corruption of non-professional data (art. 6(1)), from the manufacturer (art. 8(1)(a)).
- The Directive applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 (art. 2(1)).
- It does not apply to "services as such" (recital 17), and pure economic loss does not by itself trigger liability under it (recital 24).
- A product is defective where it "does not provide the safety that a person is entitled to expect or that is required under Union or national law" (art. 7(1)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That a guest can sue a system vendor under this Directive for a refused entry, a lost booking or any other performance failure that causes no damage listed in art. 6(1).
- That it applies to systems already in hotels before 9 December 2026, or that it is in force in Great Britain.
- That a hotel's service is a product.
- That the vendor's liability displaces the hotel's. The Directive leaves national contractual and fault-based liability unaffected (art. 2(4)(b)).
