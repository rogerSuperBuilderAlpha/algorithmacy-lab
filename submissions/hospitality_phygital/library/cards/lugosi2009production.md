## Summary

An ethnography of an under-capitalised bar in an English town patronised by LGBT consumers. Lugosi
argues that hospitable space has ideological, normative and situational dimensions, and shifts the
focus from host-guest transactions to how consumers' perceptions, actions and interactions produce
hospitality. Patrons collected glasses, policed the bar, ran events, invented house drinks and excluded
disruptive customers; they spoke of an obligation to support the venue.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Conditional hospitality and the guest's duties, as Lugosi summarises the philosophical literature: "Participation or inclusion in hospitality is always conditional: within hospitality transactions hosts have duties to ensure the wellbeing of their guests, while guests have obligations to respect the rules of the host and to reciprocate" (section "Hospitality") [full-text]
- Both parties are bound: "both are subjugated to the hospitality transaction and to the creation of a hospitable space" (section "Hospitality") (added by Fable, rr-2026-11-h) [full-text]
- Roles are negotiated: "those involved in transactions negotiate their roles alongside their dominance or subservience." (section "Hospitality") [full-text]
- Shift of focus: the paper examines "how consumers' perceptions, actions and interactions shape the production of hospitality" (abstract) [full-text]
- Norms of patronage: "discourses of hospitality can only experienced as a series of conditional offerings, rules, roles and obligations" (sic; section "Normative dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]
- Guests' obligations to a collective: "Patronage in this context reveals an asymmetric hospitality, in which transactions and obligations are not between individuals but between individuals and an imagined community." (section "Normative dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]
- Guest labour: consumer participation "included collecting glasses, ushering people in from the garden during the evenings, cleaning ashtrays, opening and closing doors ... and serving drinks"; customers "acted as door staff during events and often policed the bar" (section "Normative dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]
- Guests' requests changing the offer: the house cocktails "were invented by some of customers and staff"; when the regulars who drank them stopped coming, "requests for these drinks also stopped and this manifestation of hospitality disappeared" (section "Situational dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]
- Management permits, guests make: "Management contributed to these ludic moments by allowing these behaviours to take place, but such allowance only created opportunities." (section "Situational dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]
- Guests exclude guests: exclusion was aimed at "disruptive, inebriated customers, who challenged the social order of the bar" (section "Situational dimensions of hospitable space") [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Supplies what the Fable review found missing from R1.2: interaction that is "more than functional" and
  creates value, with guests as co-producers. It is a bar, which also answers R3.8.
- It does not show a guest pressing a claim against a host with authority. The negotiation is over
  roles and space among patrons and permissive managers. For the paper's "address" thesis it is
  evidence that guests shape outcomes where management leaves room, not that they hold standing.
- A single, unusual venue (an under-resourced LGBT bar, data from the author's earlier ethnography).
  Lugosi places the obligations "not between individuals but between individuals and an imagined
  community" (section "Normative dimensions of hospitable space"); his conclusion says guests "may
  therefore have obligations, not just to the hosts/commercial providers, but to other guests/consumers
  and the imagined community to which they belong" (section "Conclusion"). So they run past the
  operator; the source does not say they exclude it.
- Accepted manuscript, not version of record; page numbers missing.
