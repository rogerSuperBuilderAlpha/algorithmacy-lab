## Summary

Gardner asks what tort law is for and answers that corrective justice is a necessary part of the answer and cannot be the whole of it. Tort law contains a legal norm of corrective justice (wrongdoers must repair the losses they wrongfully occasion), which helps to make a counterpart moral norm determinate, and that moral norm itself needs a rationale. The rationale he offers is the continuity thesis. When an obligation goes unperformed, the reasons that made its action obligatory do not lapse. They can no longer be met by performance, so they call for the next-best conformity still available, and they justify a new, secondary obligation to provide it. Reparation is that next-best conformity in the ordinary case. The thesis explains why the duty to repair arises, why it arises even after a justified breach, and why repair never equals performance. It applies in ordinary morality as well as in law.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

All statements below except the last three are paraphrases of the author's eprint, with typescript pages. They are not Gardner's wording.

- After a breach the original norm seems to lose its use as guidance, because conformity with it is no longer possible (typescript p. 34). [full-text]
- His example, taken from MacCormick: a parent promises the children a trip to the beach today and, with good reason, breaks the promise. Without any new promise the parent is bound to take them at the next suitable opportunity. Perfect performance is no longer possible; an imperfect one is; the nearer to the promised performance the better. Something of the first obligation carries through into the second (typescript pp. 34-35). [full-text]
- The later obligation is not the earlier one persisting. Obligations are individuated by the action they require. Where a new action becomes obligatory only because another obligatory action was not taken, there is a new obligation grounded in the breach of the old one, which is what lawyers mean by primary and secondary obligations (typescript pp. 35-36). [full-text]
- An obligation is either performed or not performed. The reasons that make its action obligatory can each be conformed to more or less fully (typescript p. 37). [full-text]
- Before breach, the fact of having an obligation is also a reason for further actions that help one perform it, by enabling or facilitating performance. His example is keeping change ready for a bus fare payable in advance. He says these further actions are not themselves obligatory (typescript pp. 37-38). A footnote records the thought that an act both necessary and sufficient for doing what one is obliged to do may be derivatively obligatory, and sets the question aside by stipulating that the acts in his example are not necessary (typescript p. 38, n. 53). [full-text]
- At breach the primary obligation ends. An obligation to pay in advance cannot be performed once the journey is made, and the fact of having had it can no longer serve as a reason for anything still open to the agent (typescript p. 38). An obligation to pay in arrears nevertheless exists, because the reasons why there was an obligation to pay in advance can still be met in part (typescript p. 39). [full-text]
- The general statement. Once the time for performing a primary obligation is past, so that it can no longer be performed, the reasons that made the action obligatory often can still be served. Unmet by performance, they call for the closest thing to full satisfaction that remains available, and, other things equal, they justify a secondary obligation to do that next-best thing (typescript pp. 39-40). [full-text]
- The thesis named. The secondary obligation exists to serve, so far as it still can be served, the reasons for the primary obligation that was not performed when performance was due. Gardner names this explanation the continuity thesis (typescript p. 40). [full-text]
- Applied to repair: paying for losses one wrongfully occasioned is normally the best conformity still available with the reasons one had not to occasion them (typescript pp. 40-41, restated at p. 45). [full-text]
- Repair is never equal to performance. Falling back to the next best always leaves some reason unmet, so breach followed by correction is not the rational equivalent of performing; he rejects the doctrine of efficient breach on this ground (typescript p. 42 and n. 56). [full-text]
- What cannot be repaired grounds regret, not further correction. Expressing regret or apologising is not correction under the thesis, and the law cannot do it on the wrongdoer's behalf (typescript pp. 42-44; p. 43, n. 57). [full-text]
- Corrective justice is made rational by the remaining possibility of restoring things; regret by its impossibility (typescript p. 45). [full-text]
- How close is close enough. A primary obligation to do something at one time, left undone, yields a secondary obligation to come as near as one now can to having done it then, with nearness fixed by the reasons for the original obligation: almost doing it a little later, doing it later still, or in the end doing something that shares something with it (typescript p. 46). The obligation does not depend on reading a fallback clause into an agreement (typescript p. 47). [full-text]
- A justified breach leaves the secondary obligation in place (typescript pp. 50-53). [full-text]
- Positive obligations can often still be performed late, as with a delivery or building works, which lets courts order more than money where time was not of the essence. Courts still prefer money awards: payment is easier to supervise, an uncooperative defendant is easier to bypass, and parties who have fallen out are not made to work together (typescript p. 53). [full-text]
- Rights. In tort the primary obligation is justified by the interest of the person wronged, so it is rights-based; the secondary obligation shares its rationale, so the person wronged also holds a right to reparative damages. He puts this as a principle that a primary right brings a secondary right on its violation (typescript pp. 54-55). [full-text]
- Limits he states. The thesis does not explain general damages, which answer to irreparable results (typescript p. 56). The continuity is in the reasons for the two obligations, not in the obligation itself; he criticises Weinrib for treating the two as one obligation (typescript pp. 58-59). Obligations of corrective justice exist in ordinary morality without law, which only makes them more determinate (typescript p. 60). [full-text]
- Two side points. An injustice done by anyone is in principle everyone's business, so conformity with corrective norms is of agent-neutral concern (typescript p. 14). Corrective duties can be performed on the wrongdoer's behalf, for instance by an insurer (typescript pp. 12-13 and n. 25). [full-text]
- Version-of-record wording, from an open text. Ripstein's encyclopaedia entry quotes Gardner as saying that corrective justice regulates "the reversal of wrongful transactions on the ground of their wrongfulness" and cites 2011: 22 (ripstein2026theories, §4.3, para. 3). The same words stand at typescript p. 26. [full-text]
- Version-of-record pages, from an open text. Herstein cites "Gardner 2011: 28–37, 45–46" for remedial rights grounded in violated rights (herstein2023legal, §8.6, para. 2). [full-text]
- Gardner's own later restatement, from a different author eprint that carries no request against quotation ('Damages without duty', 2019, saved at sources/gardner2019damages/, read whole, not carded): "the reasons for the remedial duty to come into existence are the same reasons that were not conformed to when the primary duty went unperformed. That is called ‘the continuity thesis’." And a limit: "In cases of irreparable wrong the continuity thesis does not apply." (eprint p. 12 and n. 31; version of record: University of Toronto Law Journal 69(4), 412-420, doi 10.3138/utlj.2019-05-22, closed) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Do not quote or cite from this card.** The eprint's cover asks that it not be quoted or cited and that the definitive version be used. The typescript pages here are for a verifier. The paper needs the Springer version of record, which is closed. Two open texts fix where to look in it: Ripstein gives p. 22 for a sentence that stands at typescript p. 26, and Herstein gives pp. 28-37 and 45-46 for what is, by its content, section 5 and the sixth doubt (typescript pp. 33-45 and 54-55). On those two fixes the general statement of the thesis (typescript pp. 39-40) should fall near pp. 32-34 of the journal. That estimate is this card's and is unverified.
- **The line is drawn per obligation, at the time performance was due.** Gardner's boundary between primary and secondary is the passing of the time for performance, obligation by obligation (typescript pp. 38-40, 46). A duty that runs for the length of a stay is, on his way of counting, a series of things due at times. A room not ready when promised is already a breach, and putting it right an hour later is secondary, next-best conformity for that lapse, while the duty goes on being performable for the rest of the stay. K7's sentence that interruptibility belongs to the duty to perform 'before and during' and accountability to the duty to repair 'after' is therefore too clean. On Gardner's account repair begins during the stay.
- **The thesis is about the duty-bearer's reasons.** It explains why the party in breach must do the next-best thing. It does not say that the party owed must be given a way to be heard. Ripstein makes the same point as a criticism: the account focuses on wrongdoers and their reasons, and reference to the wronged party is not essential to it (ripstein2026theories, §4.3, para. 4). Gardner brings rights in only for tort's primary obligations, by way of an interest theory (typescript pp. 54-55).
- **Explanation and apology fall outside it.** What the thesis grounds is reparation. Expressions of emotion, 'e.g. by confessing or apologizing', are not correction on this view (typescript p. 43, n. 57); the thesis is silent on explanation, not expressly exclusive of it. The paper cannot cite the continuity thesis for a duty to answer the guest after the fact.
- **What it gives K7's premise P3, and what it withholds.** Before breach, the obligation gives its holder reason to take the steps that enable performance, and Gardner says those steps are not themselves obligatory (typescript pp. 37-38). The one opening is the footnote on acts 'both necessary and sufficient' for doing what one is obliged to do (p. 38, n. 53), a derivation he raises and sets aside. A duty to keep a channel follows on this account only if the channel is necessary to performing the duty, which is K7's empirical premise P4.
- **A legal objection.** Smith denies that any legal duty to pay damages exists before a court's award; see ripstein2026theories (§4.4, para. 3). Gardner's 2019 reply defends the duty and relies on the thesis. The dispute is about the law of damages. The thesis as a claim about moral obligation is not touched by it.
- **A mismatch to watch.** Ripstein illustrates Gardner's view with a promise to meet for lunch at noon and arriving at 12:15. That example is not in the 2011 eprint, whose examples are the beach trip and the bus fare. A lunch promise appears in the 2019 reply, used for a different point. Do not attribute the lunch example to the 2011 article without the version of record.
- The eprint has two sections numbered 6 and several typographical slips, and its cover warns that it may lack the final edits.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7 (supports the timing boundary; narrows the mapping).** Supports: the line between performing and repairing is whether performance is still possible, and what follows breach is a distinct duty with the same rationale (typescript pp. 38-40). Supports a further point the paper can use: the next-best conformity is measured by nearness to performance and never equals it (typescript pp. 35, 40, 42, 46), so (the card's inference, not Gardner's) the sooner a holder learns of a lapse the closer the conformity still open to it; closeness is fixed by the reasons (typescript p. 46). Narrows: the boundary falls within the stay, lapse by lapse, and not at its end; the thesis yields no duty to be reachable and no duty to explain; and its author denies that enabling steps are obligatory as such.
- **K7, premise P3 (the paper's own step).** No published argument for P3 was found in this text. The nearest material is at typescript pp. 37-38 and n. 53, which gives reasons for enabling steps and leaves open whether necessary ones are owed.
- **K5 (supports, for the legal duties).** A primary right brings a secondary right to repair in the same person (typescript pp. 54-55), which fits a guest who is owed the duty and who sues on its breach.
