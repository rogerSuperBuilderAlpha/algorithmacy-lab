## Summary

Five people whose asylum claims had been refused, and who were destitute, applied to the Home Secretary for accommodation under section 4(2) of the Immigration and Asylum Act 1999. In each case she accepted that she owed a duty to provide it. She performs that duty through regional contractors. The claimants then waited between 45 and 151 days for a room, and one severely disabled claimant about nine months for suitable accommodation, while charities and friends kept them off the street. They sought judicial review. Robin Knowles J held that the duty is to provide accommodation within a reasonable time measured in a context where a breach of Article 3 is imminent; that the duty stays with the Secretary of State whatever her contracts say; that contractual key performance indicators and later financial penalties do not secure performance "at the time and in the individual case"; and that she had no means of knowing the true position because she did not capture data and monitor properly. He declared her in breach of the section 4(2) duty to the claimants, in continuing breach of that duty and of section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998 by failing properly to monitor, and in breach of the public sector equality duty by failing to monitor provision to disabled people. He awarded £1,000 to each claimant who claimed damages.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court, date, judge: High Court, Queen's Bench Division, Administrative Court, Robin Knowles J; heard 20-23 July 2020 with later written submissions; judgment 14 December 2020; England and Wales (cover sheet). Two applications for judicial review heard together (para 5). [full-text]
- Who sued whom, for what: five refused asylum seekers against the Secretary of State for the Home Department, challenging the performance of an accepted duty: "Each claimant asked the Secretary of State to accept a duty to provide accommodation or arrange for the provision of accommodation. The Secretary of State properly accepted the duty in each case, by reference to section 4(2) of the Immigration and Asylum Act 1999" (para 3). [full-text]
- The subject: "The issues in these proceedings concern the performance of that duty; the actual provision of the accommodation. At the same time they provide a particular focus on the role of monitoring (including collection and capture of data, and evaluation)." (para 4). [full-text]
- The delays: accommodation came 60, 105 and 151 days after the decision for three claimants and not at all in 45 days for a fourth (para 35); for AA the matter was resolved "more than 9 months after the section 4(2) decision" (para 47). [full-text]
- Basis of the duty: statute and the Human Rights Act. The duty engages "when the provision of accommodation is necessary for the purpose of avoiding a breach of the Article 3 prohibition on inhuman or degrading treatment" (para 21); the Secretary of State accepted that "she is under a duty to provide such support" and "must do so within a reasonable period" (para 178). [full-text]
- Performance through contractors: "If her decision is to accept a duty to provide accommodation or arrange for the provision of accommodation to an individual, the Secretary of State uses contractors to perform that duty." (para 99). [full-text]
- The duty stays with the holder: "The Secretary of State is of course free to do this, but the duty remains hers. This point needs to be made specifically because in a letter dated 22 January 2020 from the Government Legal Department to the claimants’ solicitors it is stated, wrongly, that “The SSHD is not responsible for a housing provider’s performance”." (para 100). [full-text]
- The providers do not hold it: "Regardless of the contract, the Secretary of State has her legal duty; the providers do not." (para 241). [full-text]
- Contract remedies come too late: the contract's own statement of requirements says the KPIs "are not aimed at providing a day-to-day management tool, but are the means by which the Provider may provide compensation to the Authority for losses which it suffers as a result of failures in service performance" (para 117, quoting Schedule 2 para 1.2.8.5). [full-text]
- Penalty is not performance: "Enforcement by way of later financial penalty provides incentive to perform. However, it is only enforcement that actually remedies a detected breach that brings about the accommodation required in a particular case." (para 144). [full-text]
- The holder did not know: "Through her officials, the Secretary of State did not in practice have the means to the true position. This is because there were no proper arrangements for data capture and monitoring." (para 173). [full-text]
- Tolerating a failing system is a decision: "To decline to improve a system that is failing to meet the requirements of a duty, when that system can be improved, is equivalent to a decision not to perform a duty" (para 204). [full-text]
- Contract monitoring is not duty monitoring: "monitoring for compliance with a contractual KPI is not monitoring for compliance with a Minister’s legal duty" (para 209). [full-text]
- The court's question: "The question for the Court is whether the Secretary of State is complying with her duty, not whether the contractors are complying with their contractual duties to her." (para 230). [full-text]
- A system that needs litigation to work: "A system that reaches the point of depending on applications for judicial review to make it work may require particular scrutiny." (para 231). [full-text]
- The failure identified: "the failure to capture data properly and, using that data, to monitor properly, so that the Secretary of State can know whether she is acting lawfully and in accordance with her duty, and can act immediately if there is a sign that either is not the case" (para 236). [full-text]
- Why it matters: "Without proper monitoring the system is without a key means by which to identify and correct failure and to inform change to enable it to meet its purpose, to be found in section 4(2)." (para 238). [full-text]
- The holding on monitoring, tied to its context: "Given the context of (present or imminent) inhuman or degrading treatment, and the real risks involved (of unlawful breach of duty), there is no lawful system that does not capture data properly and, using that data, monitor properly." (para 239). [full-text]
- Contract and duty contrasted: "The contracts envisage financial correction by the provider, and after the event, whereas the first concern of the Secretary of State is securing the provision of accommodation at the time and in the individual case because a breach of Article 3 is imminent." (para 241). [full-text]
- Stated more generally, with a limit: "Seen in its proper context, at least where a system is involved, monitoring is an essential element of a Minister’s strategy to deliver as Parliament intended by its legislation; to deliver as the law requires." (para 242). [full-text]
- What proper monitoring includes: "it follows the progress of each case"; "it alerts cases that are at risk of exceeding a reasonable time in sufficient time for this to be addressed"; and it follows alleged failures to travel, including "reason given for not travelling, response to reason given, action taken, and the situation of the individual as a result" (para 243, items 3, 4 and 9 of ten). [full-text]
- Monitoring must lead to action: "the monitoring must be accompanied by arrangements to secure action by reference to the information it provides" (para 244). [full-text]
- KPIs and service credits again: "They are well after the event and they are focused on the contractual position between the Secretary of State and the accommodation provider rather than on the legal obligations of the Secretary of State to those to whom she owes a duty under section 4(2) and to those who are disabled." (para 301). [full-text]
- A complaints route existed and was not enough: "The contract regime also provides for a complaints system for users." (para 296); the court still found no proper monitoring (paras 299-302). [full-text]
- How the system treated the individual's side: the Home Office witness said "The S4 team do not usually contact providers for explanations regarding “Failures to Travel”." (para 63); one claimant's case was closed thirteen minutes after her charity's email asking why she had not been collected (para 79); the judge found "the worst is assumed of the claimants" (para 92). [full-text]
- Reasonable adjustments: the failure to monitor disabled applicants and to prioritise meant the Secretary of State "did not take the steps it was reasonable to take to avoid the disadvantage to disabled individuals, including AA" (para 291), applying the Equality Act 2010 ss. 20, 21 and 29(7) as set out at paras 251-253. [full-text]
- Remedies: declarations that the Secretary of State "(1) was in breach of her duty under section 4(2) of the 1999 Act as regards the claimants, in failing to provide accommodation to the claimants within a reasonable period of time; (2) was and is in breach of her duties under section 4(2) of the 1999 Act and section 6 of the 1998 Act, in failing properly to monitor the provision of accommodation under section 4(2) of the 1999 Act" and (3) in breach of the public sector equality duty (para 333). [full-text]
- Damages: "a non-nominal award of £1,000 to each claimant who claims damages is sufficient for ‘just satisfaction’. The case is not about money. It is the declarations that matter." (para 341). The claims succeeded. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What kind of duty.** A public-law duty under statute, engaged by the Human Rights Act, owed by a Minister. The court's statement that no lawful system lacks proper monitoring is tied to "the context of (present or imminent) inhuman or degrading treatment" (para 239), and the wider sentence is hedged "at least where a system is involved" (para 242). It is not a rule of private law and says nothing of what a commercial host owes.
- **Not a hotel case.** The word "hotel" appears once, where one claimant was given "full-board initial accommodation at an hotel or hostel" (para 43). The providers named are contractors such as Serco and G4S (paras 51, 70). The judgment does not consider what an accommodation provider or a hotel owes a placed resident in tort or contract.
- **Monitoring by the holder, not a channel from the person.** What the court requires is that the duty-holder capture data, follow each case and act on warning signs (paras 236, 243-244). It does not hold that the individual must be given a person to contact. Its closest approach is item 9 of para 243 (following the reason the individual gave and the response to it) and its criticism of a process that recorded failures to travel without seeking the provider's explanation and closed a case while an inquiry was pending (paras 63, 79, 92). A complaints system for users is mentioned and left aside (para 296).
- **Remedy.** Declarations and modest damages after the event. In two of the cases accommodation came only after orders of the High Court (para 35), and the judge noted that performance was often achieved "through pressure to prioritise the particular case over others" (para 148). So even here the legal remedy arrived afterwards; what the court declares unlawful is the absence of means to act in time.
- **First instance.** A single judge of the Administrative Court. Whether the decision was appealed, and any later treatment, not checked.
- **Slips in the judgment.** Para 34 refers to "the 2019 Act" for the 1999 Act and gives "13 September 1999" for a 2019 decision. Neither matters.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7 (channel): supports P2 and P3 in law, in one setting; does not reach C.** A holder that performs through others must have the means to learn of failure and correct it "at the time and in the individual case" (declaration reasoned at paras 235-239; the contrast at para 241; paras 242-244 hedged or guidance), and damages or penalties "after the event" do not answer that (paras 144, 241, 301). This is a court enacting the step K7 calls the paper's own. Its limits: public law, an Article 3 context, and monitoring by the holder; the court does not require a channel from the person to someone with authority.
- **K8 (holder): supports for placed guests, on the accommodation duty.** The body that accepted the duty keeps it; the provider owes its duties to that body under contract (paras 100, 230, 241). The holder cannot disclaim responsibility for a provider's performance (para 100).
- **K4 (placed guests): answers part of the question K4 leaves open.** For people placed by the state, the party the law treats as owing the duty to accommodate is the placing authority, "the providers do not" (para 241). Who owes the reception duties inside the building is not addressed.
- **K9 (mediation): a fact pattern.** Requests, proposals and "failed to travel" flags moved through a portal by automatic information exchange (paras 104-108); once a case was actioned it left the portal and the record was gone (paras 63, 126); the team did not usually ask the provider for an explanation (para 63). The court treated the resulting ignorance as the holder's failure (paras 173, 207).

**What the paper may say**

- In *R (DMA)* the High Court stated that when the Home Secretary uses contractors to perform her accepted s. 4(2) duty to accommodate, "the duty remains hers" (para 100), and that "Regardless of the contract, the Secretary of State has her legal duty; the providers do not." (para 241). First instance, public law; the point was not contested at the hearing (paras 3, 178).
- The court declared the duty-holder in breach for "failing properly to monitor" provision (para 333), because without proper monitoring she could not "know whether she is acting lawfully" or "act immediately if there is a sign that either is not the case" (para 236).
- The court distinguished penalties under the contract, which come "after the event", from "securing the provision of accommodation at the time and in the individual case" (para 241).
- The court said that monitoring "must be accompanied by arrangements to secure action by reference to the information it provides" (para 244).
- This was a public-law ruling on a statutory duty where inhuman or degrading treatment was imminent (paras 21, 239).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the case concerns hotels, or decides what a hotel owes a guest.
- That English law imposes a general duty on anyone who delegates to monitor the delegate. The holding is tied to a statutory duty and its Article 3 context (para 239).
- That the court required a contact point, a human reviewer or a complaints channel for the individual. It required monitoring by the holder.
- That accommodation providers owe placed residents no duties at all. The court said only that they do not hold the Secretary of State's statutory duty (para 241).
- That the decision is final. Appeal status was not checked.
