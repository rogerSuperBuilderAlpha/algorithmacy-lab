## Summary

The Supreme Court held that Barclays Bank was not vicariously liable for sexual assaults allegedly committed by a doctor whom it paid, report by report, to examine job applicants. Lady Hale, for a unanimous court, restates the two elements of vicarious liability, reviews the cases that extended it beyond employment (*Christian Brothers*, *Cox*, *Armes*), and holds that none of them eroded the distinction between employment or a relationship akin to it and the relationship with an independent contractor. The question is whether the tortfeasor carries on business on his own account. The doctor did. English law; the only doctrine decided is vicarious liability.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: UK Supreme Court, judgment given 1 April 2020, heard 28 November 2019, [2020] UKSC 13, on appeal from [2018] EWCA Civ 1670. Lady Hale gave the only judgment, "with whom Lord Reed, Lord Kerr, Lord Hodge and Lord Lloyd-Jones agree" (heading before para 1). [full-text]
- Parties and claim: some 126 claimants in a group action alleged sexual assaults "between 1968 and about 1984 by the late Dr Gordon Bates" during medical examinations the Bank required of successful job applicants (paras 2-3). Many "were teenagers at the time, some aged 16, going for their first jobs on leaving school", and were examined at the doctor's home (para 4). The claimants were applicants for employment, not customers; the defendant was a commercial bank. [full-text]
- The only issue was vicarious liability, tried as a preliminary issue: "whether the Bank is vicariously liable for any assaults that Dr Bates is proved to have perpetrated in the course of medical examinations carried out at the Bank’s request" (para 6). Non-delegable duty appears only in the court's account of *Woodland* (para 19). [full-text]
- The two elements: "The first is a relationship between the two persons which makes it proper for the law to make the one pay for the fault of the other. Historically, and leaving aside relationships such as agency and partnership, that was limited to the relationship between employer and employee, but that has now been somewhat broadened." The second "is the connection between that relationship and the tortfeasor’s wrongdoing." (para 1) [full-text]
- The general rule, in Lord Bridge's words from *D & F Estates* as Lady Hale quotes them: "It is trite law that the employer of an independent contractor is, in general, not liable for the negligence or other torts committed by the contractor in the course of the execution of the work" (para 7). The Bank's case was that recent decisions "have not so expanded it as to destroy this trite proposition of law" (para 7). [full-text]
- The rejected alternative: the claimants argued that the recent cases "have replaced that trite proposition with a more nuanced multi-factorial approach in which a range of incidents are considered in deciding whether it is “fair, just and reasonable” to impose vicarious liability upon this person for the torts of another person who is not his employee. That was the approach adopted both by the trial judge and the Court of Appeal in this case." (para 8) [full-text]
- No erosion: "There is nothing, therefore, in the trilogy of Supreme Court cases discussed above to suggest that the classic distinction between employment and relationships akin or analogous to employment, on the one hand, and the relationship with an independent contractor, on the other hand, has been eroded." (para 24) [full-text]
- The test: "The question therefore is, as it has always been, whether the tortfeasor is carrying on business on his own account or whether he is in a relationship akin to employment with the defendant." The five *Christian Brothers* incidents "may be helpful" in doubtful cases, including "whether workers who may be technically self-employed or agency workers are effectively part and parcel of the employer’s business"; but "Where it is clear that the tortfeasor is carrying on his own independent business it is not necessary to consider the five incidents." (para 27) [full-text]
- Application: the Bank "made the arrangements for the examinations and sent him the forms to fill in. It therefore chose the questions to which it wanted answers. But the same would be true of many other people who did work for the Bank but were clearly independent contractors, ranging from the company hired to clean its windows to the auditors hired to audit its books." The doctor "was paid a fee for each report", "was free to refuse an offered examination", "no doubt carried his own medical liability insurance", and "was in business on his own account as a medical practitioner with a portfolio of patients and clients. One of those clients was the Bank." (para 28) [full-text]
- The Singapore Court of Appeal in *Ng Huat Seng v Mohammad*, quoted with approval (para 26): vicarious liability rests "on the ground that the tortfeasor is in fact engaged in the defendant’s enterprise", and cannot reach "an independent contractor, who, by definition, is engaged in his own enterprise." [full-text]
- On *Woodland*: Lady Hale quotes Lord Sumption's para 3, that vicarious liability "has never extended to the negligence of those who are truly independent contractors", and reads him as "casting no doubt on the conventional distinction between employees, and those analogous to employees, and independent contractors" (para 19). [full-text]
- Gig work: breaking the link between employee status for all purposes and vicarious liability "may be of benefit to people harmed by the torts of those working in the “gig” economy", but the court declined to align vicarious liability with the statutory "worker": "it would be going too far down the road to tidiness" (para 29). [full-text]
- Disposition: "I would allow this appeal and hold that the Bank is not vicariously liable for any wrongdoing of Dr Bates in the course of the medical examinations he carried out for the Bank." (para 30) The claim on this ground failed. [full-text]
- Oversight: the judgment states no duty on the Bank to choose a competent doctor, to check him, to monitor the examinations or to step in. No claim in negligence for the Bank's own fault in selecting or supervising the doctor was before the court; the preliminary issue was vicarious liability alone (para 6). [full-text]
- The judgment uses the word "non-delegable" once, in para 19, and does not call that category exceptional. It says nothing of occupiers, of a contractual promise as a ground of liability, of hotels or of guests (searched the saved text, 11 Nov 2026). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Scope.** The case decides the first stage of vicarious liability and nothing else. It leaves standing every other route by which a principal answers for a contractor: its own negligence in selection or supervision, a non-delegable duty (*Woodland*, noted at para 19), an occupier's duty, a contractual promise. A sentence that cites it for "no liability for contractors" without that limit overstates it.
- **Facts far from hospitality.** The claimants were job applicants, the tortfeasor a doctor with his own practice, the wrong an intentional assault decades earlier. The rule it restates is general; the application is not a hospitality application.
- **The window cleaner and the auditor.** Lady Hale's two examples (para 28) are ordinary outsourced services. An outsourced security firm, a cleaning contractor or a software vendor that runs its own business with several clients sits on the same side of the line. Staff who are "technically self-employed or agency workers" but "part and parcel" of the business may fall on the other (para 27).
- **An inconsistency in the text.** The judgment cites *Armes* as "[2018] AC 355" at para 8 and "[2018] AC 855" at para 23. One of the two is a misprint. *Hughes v Rattan* para 61 gives 355. I checked neither against the law report.
- **Later treatment, as far as read.** *Hughes v Rattan* [2022] EWCA Civ 107 applies the para 27 test and reads it as returning the focus to "the contractual arrangements between tortfeasor and defendant" (paras 87-88 of that case; see hughes2022rattan). The scout reports that *BXB* [2023] UKSC 15 restates the two stages; I did not read it. Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): narrows.** K8 says the holder "answers for employees" and that for independent contractors "the rule varies". *Barclays* fixes the starting point that K8's wording leaves out: in general the employer of an independent contractor is not liable for the contractor's torts (para 7), and vicarious liability reaches only employees and those in a relationship akin to employment (paras 24, 27). The variations K8 lists are exceptions to that rule and have to be shown one by one.

**K7, premise P2: narrows.** P2 says a holder that performs through others "keeps a duty to see that the work is done with care". Vicarious liability supplies no such duty. It makes an employer pay for an employee's tort and imposes nothing on a principal whose contractor runs an independent business (paras 27-28). P2 therefore has to rest on the other doctrines, duty by duty.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- In English law the employer of an independent contractor is, in general, not liable for the contractor's negligence or other torts (para 7, Lady Hale quoting Lord Bridge in *D & F Estates*).
- Vicarious liability extends to relationships akin to employment and stops where the wrongdoer carries on business on his own account (paras 24, 27).
- The Supreme Court gave a window-cleaning company and a firm of auditors as plain cases of independent contractors (para 28).
- Workers who are "technically self-employed or agency workers" may still be "part and parcel of the employer’s business" (para 27).

**What it may not say.**
- That a firm answers vicariously for an outsourced contractor because the contractor's work is integral to the firm's business or because imposing liability would be fair, just and reasonable. The court rejected that multi-factor route where the contractor plainly runs an independent business (paras 8, 24, 27).
- That *Barclays* holds a principal never answers for a contractor. It decides vicarious liability only (para 6).
- That the case concerned customers or a duty of care owed by the Bank. No such duty was in issue.
- That the judgment says anything about oversight. It does not.
