## Summary

Qualitative interviews with Airbnb hosts read through moral identity theory. Hosts' practice does not always match the moral identity they claim; the paper offers a typology (moral agents, agentic, opportunistic, amoral hosts) crossing moral identity with "professionalism".

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Aim: "explores how hosts draw on their own perceptions of morality and responsibility to inform hosting practice" (AM p. 1, abstract) [full-text]
- Diffusion of responsibility: "individuals tend to either assign responsibility to themselves or diffuse responsibility to others (McGregor, 2017)"; "some hosts tend to diffuse responsibility to the platform or even to guests whom they regard as equally responsible" (AM p. 16) [full-text]
- The setting: "collective responsibility is difficult to establish (Kaufman, 2015) particularly where there is absence of a formal regulatory framework as in the case of P2P accommodation" (AM p. 16) [full-text]
- Typology runs from hosts who see hosting as "an extension of their moral self" to "amoral hosts who justify immoral behaviour in the context of situational factors ... including the lack of a regulatory framework" (AM p. 16) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- Host self-report; the duties studied are mostly to neighbours, tax authorities and the platform community, not to guests.
- AM pagination only.
- Not independent of kaniadakis2022responsibilisation: both report 35 European hosts recruited through a COST Action network and interviewed on Skype (AM p. 6), very probably one dataset. Do not present them as independent corroboration.
