## Summary

Fox questions the assumption that transparency produces accountability. Writing about states, development agencies and firms, he argues that one does not necessarily lead to the other and that the useful question is which kinds of transparency lead to which kinds of accountability. He splits each term in two. Transparency is 'opaque' when what is released does not show how an institution behaves, and 'clear' when it does. Accountability is 'soft' when it consists in answerability, the right to call those in authority to justify their decisions, and 'hard' when answerability is joined to the possibility of sanctions. Institutional answerability is where the two concepts overlap. The article is a short conceptual essay, a revised and abridged translation of a Spanish-language chapter, with two schematic tables and no new data.

## Critical notes & threats

- **Answerability is inside accountability here, not opposed to it.** Fox makes answerability one of accountability's two dimensions and calls it accountability's 'soft face' (p. 668). No sentence in the article sets answerability against accountability as such. If the paper wants 'answerability' and 'accountability' as two things, the sourced form is: answerability, and accountability in its hard form, which adds sanction, compensation or remediation (pp. 665, 668; Table 1).
- **The definition is second-hand.** The term and the two-part analysis are Schedler's (1999), which is closed and unread. Cite as 'Fox (2007: 665), following Schedler'. Fox's reference list gives Schedler's chapter as pp. 13–28; the Crossref record the scout fetched gives 11–28. Neither was checked against the book.
- **Setting.** The institutions are governments, the World Bank, regulators and certified firms; the people owed answers are citizens and civil-society organisations (pp. 663–667). Nothing concerns a customer and a service provider. The paper would be carrying a concept from public governance into a private relation, and should say so.
- **Answerability looks backward.** In every use it is a matter of justifying decisions taken and behaviour already shown (pp. 665, 668). It is not a means of stopping or redirecting a process under way. The paper's 'interruptibility' is a different thing, and Fox supplies no term for it; the nearest is 'demand-driven access' to information (p. 665).
- **What is argued and what is asserted.** The claim that transparency does not suffice rests on a few cited cases (toxic-release reporting, US farm subsidies, pp. 667–668). The soft/hard and clear/opaque distinctions are proposed, with tables that are schematic shadings. It is a widely used framing and it is one author's proposal.
- **Journal.** *Development in Practice* is a practitioner-facing development journal; the article is nine pages. It is evidence that the distinction is established in the governance literature and a convenient place to cite it. It is not a philosophical analysis of answering.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7 (supports the vocabulary; narrows the mapping).** Supports: a published, quotable distinction between being able to demand answers and being able to sanction (p. 665), and the placing of 'compensation and/or remediation' at the hard end (Table 1, p. 668), which fits K7's pairing of accountability with repair. Supports also the paper's warning that information is offered in place of answerability (p. 664). Narrows: answerability in this sense arises after decisions are made, so it belongs with what K7 puts after a lapse, and it does not name the channel during performance.
- **K8 (bears).** 'The problem of many hands' (p. 666, citing Thompson 1987) is the public-administration name for responsibility dispersed among many contributors. Fox reports it in one sentence.
