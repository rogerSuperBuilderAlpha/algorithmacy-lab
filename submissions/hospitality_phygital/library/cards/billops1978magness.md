## Summary

Billops and others hired half of the Regency Ballroom at the Brandywine Hilton Inn, a franchised hotel, for a social event and paid in advance. On the day, the inn's banquet director demanded more money and, when refused, he and other staff harassed the hirers and their guests, would not heat the room, impounded an art exhibit, summoned the police and threatened arrest. The plaintiffs sued the franchisee and the Hilton franchisors in tort. The Superior Court granted the franchisors summary judgment, finding no actual or apparent agency. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed. On actual agency, a franchisor is the franchisee's principal "when the former controls, or has the right to control, the latter’s business", and where the franchise agreement "allocates to the franchisor the right to exercise control over the daily operations of the franchise, an agency relationship exists". A detailed and partly mandatory operating manual regulating front office procedures, room standards, staff procedures and much else, backed by inspection rights and termination for breach, raised a triable issue. On apparent agency, the franchisors' required display of the Hilton name to the exclusion of all others, and the plaintiffs' stated reliance on Hilton as a quality enterprise, also raised a jury question.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Facts**

- The suit charged "false imprisonment, invasion of privacy intentional and negligent infliction of emotional distress, battery, assault and defamation." The incident "occurred at the defendant Brandywine Hilton Inn when the banquet director of the Inn wrongfully attempted to extort funds in addition to those previously paid by plaintiffs for the use of one of the Inn’s ballrooms." (at 197). [full-text]
- "Ronald Billops, on behalf of certain of the plaintiffs, entered into a written contract for the rental of one-half of the Regency Ballroom of the Brandywine Hilton Inn for a social event consisting of an art exhibit, fashion show and dance." (at 197). [full-text]
- The Superior Court had granted summary judgment "after the Superior Court determined that no actual or apparent agency relationship existed between the franchisors and the franchisee, and that, therefore, no legal basis existed for holding the franchisors vicariously liable for the torts of the franchisee or its employees. We reverse." (at 197). [full-text]

**Actual agency**

- "A franchisor may be held to have an actual agency relationship with its franchisee when the former controls, or has the right to control, the latter’s business." (at 197). "The vicarious tort liability of a master, or franchisor, flows from an actual agency relationship." (at 197). [full-text]
- "If, in practical effect, the franchise agreement goes beyond the stage of setting standards, and allocates to the franchisor the right to exercise control over the daily operations of the franchise, an agency relationship exists." (at 197-198). [full-text]
- "It is our opinion that there are sufficient facts of record which, along with the reasonable inferences therefrom, show day-to-day control of the business of the Brandywine Hilton Inn by the franchisors so that the latter’s motion for summary judgment should have been denied leaving the issue of actual agency to be resolved at trial." (at 198). [full-text]
- The manual: "Franchisors have issued to the franchisee a detailed and in parts mandatory, operating manual which is incorporated into the franchise agreement. The manual regulates such matters as identification, advertising, front office procedures, cleaning and inspection service for guest rooms and public areas, minimum guest room standards, food purchasing and preparation standards, requirements for minimum supplies of “brand name” goods, staff procedures and standards for soliciting and booking group meetings, functions and room reservations, accounting, insurance, engineering and maintenance, and numerous other details of operation." (at 198). [full-text]
- Enforcement: the franchisor "retains the right to enter the premises of the hotel “to inspect the hotel so as to maintain the high standards and reputation of the system ...”" and has "the right of unilateral termination for violation" (at 198). [full-text]
- "While we make no judgment as to whether, in this case, an actual agency relationship exists, we cannot say that it does not. The facts of record reveal a triable issue on the question of actual agency, and defendants were not entitled to summary judgment." (at 198). [full-text]

**Apparent agency**

- "Manifestations by the alleged principal which create a reasonable belief in a third party that the alleged agent is authorized to bind the principal create an apparent agency from which spring the same legal consequences as those which result from an actual agency." (at 198). "The manifestations may be made directly to the third party, or may be made to the community in general, for example, by way of advertising." (at 198). [full-text]
- "In order to establish a chain of liability to the principal based upon apparent agency, a litigant must show reliance on the indicia of authority originated by the principal ...; and such reliance must have been reasonable." (at 198). [full-text]
- "The Hilton logo and sign are required to be displayed to the exclusion of all others. The franchise agreement forbids the mention of any name other than Hilton to the customers of the hotel as management of the Brandywine Hilton Inn." (at 199). Defendants admitted "that there is no reasonable basis which can be derived from the method of operation or the physical environment of the Brandywine Hilton Inn from which an ordinary person would have reason to know that he or she was dealing with anyone other than the Hilton Coloration." (at 199; "Coloration" is the copy's text). [full-text]
- "While the evidence on behalf of appellants by no means amounts to a clear case of liability, we are of the opinion that reasonable persons could differ regarding whether or not the defendant franchisors cloaked the franchisee with apparent authority, or whether plaintiffs relied thereon." (at 199). [full-text]
- Which Hilton entity: "It is conceivable that one or several of the Hilton entities have no, or limited contact with the Brandywine Hilton, and thus would not be vicariously liable for the latter’s torts. The exact relationships between the various defendants must be explored on remand." (at 199). Disposition: "REVERSED and the case is REMANDED" (at 199). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A triable issue, not a liability finding.** The court "make[s] no judgment as to whether, in this case, an actual agency relationship exists" (at 198), and says the apparent-agency evidence "by no means amounts to a clear case of liability" (at 199).
- **Not a lodging guest.** The plaintiffs hired a ballroom; the wrongs were a banquet director's intentional torts (at 197). The test it states for franchisor agency is general, but the facts are not the guest-room case.
- **Vicarious, not direct, liability.** Liability "flows from an actual agency relationship" (at 197); the franchisor answers for the franchisee's and its employees' torts. The case contains no theory of the franchisor's own negligence.
- **The test is the right to control daily operations, read from the documents.** Setting standards is not enough; control "over the daily operations" is (at 197-198). A brand-mandated front-office procedure or guest-facing system is the kind of manual term the court counted (at 198) *(my inference: a mandated check-in or complaints system specified in a brand manual would be evidence of the same kind)*.
- The red team's description "a mandatory operating manual regulating 'front office procedures' could 'show day-to-day control'" (T12) matches the text; the manual was "in parts mandatory" (at 198).
- Delaware law, 1978. Later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation), the brand row: refutes 'Control: No' as a general claim and supplies the test.** A brand that, through its franchise documents, has the right to control the daily operations of the hotel, including front office procedures and staff procedures, may be the franchisee's principal and answer vicariously for the franchisee's torts (at 197-198); a brand that holds the hotel out as its own may answer on apparent agency where the customer reasonably relied (at 198-199). The dossier should state the allocation among firms as turning, in this US line, on the right to control day-to-day operation and on holding out, and should cite the case as authority that the issue goes to a jury, not that the brand was liable.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Supreme Court of Delaware held that a franchisor may be the franchisee's principal "when the former controls, or has the right to control, the latter’s business", and that if the franchise agreement "allocates to the franchisor the right to exercise control over the daily operations of the franchise, an agency relationship exists" (at 197-198).
- It held that a mandatory operating manual regulating, among other things, "front office procedures", "minimum guest room standards" and "staff procedures", with inspection and termination rights, raised a triable issue of actual agency between Hilton franchisors and a franchised inn (at 198). (Narrowed after CV-G: the manual was 'in parts mandatory' and worked together with inspection and termination rights; 'staff procedures' may be part of a longer item; the court held only that the issue goes to trial.)
- It held that a franchisor's manifestations "may be made to the community in general, for example, by way of advertising", and that reasonable reliance on them can ground apparent agency (at 198-199).

**What the paper may not say**

- That Hilton was held liable. The court reversed a summary judgment and remanded (at 198-199).
- That the case concerned a hotel guest's injury. It concerned hirers of a ballroom and a banquet director's intentional torts (at 197).
- That it states the law of England or the EU.
