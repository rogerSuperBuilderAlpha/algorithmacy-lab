## Summary

YL, aged 84 and with Alzheimer's disease, lived in a care home run by Southern Cross Healthcare, a private company. Birmingham City Council had arranged and largely paid for her placement under its duty in ss. 21 and 26 of the National Assistance Act 1948, under a three-way agreement signed by the home, the council and her daughter. Southern Cross gave notice to end her residence, and she claimed that the notice breached her right to respect for her home under article 8, unlawfully under s. 6(1) of the Human Rights Act 1998. The preliminary issue was whether Southern Cross, in providing her care and accommodation, was exercising "functions of a public nature" under s. 6(3)(b). The majority (Lord Scott, Lord Mance, Lord Neuberger) held that it was not. The local authority's function was to arrange and pay; the actual provision of care and accommodation by a private, profit-earning company under private-law contracts was not inherently governmental. Close regulation, public funding of fees for a service, and the residents' vulnerability did not change that. The resident kept her Convention rights against the council, which had a continuing duty about the placement, and her contractual and common-law rights against the home. Lord Bingham and Baroness Hale dissented: provision of care at public expense pursuant to statutory arrangements, for people the state has accepted responsibility for, was a public function.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The question**

- "The issue in this appeal is whether a care home ..., when providing accommodation and care to a resident ..., pursuant to arrangements made with a local authority ... under sections 21 and 26 of the National Assistance Act 1948, is performing “functions of a public nature” for the purposes of section 6(3)(b) of the Human Rights Act 1998 and is thus in that respect a “public authority” obliged to act compatibly with Convention rights under section 6(1) of that Act." (para 1, Lord Bingham). [full-text]
- Section 6(5) as quoted: "In relation to a particular act, a person is not a public authority by virtue only of subsection (3)(b) if the nature of the act is private." (para 23, Lord Scott). [full-text]

**The majority's reasons**

- Lord Scott: "Southern Cross is a company carrying on a socially useful business for profit. It is neither a charity nor a philanthropist. It enters into private law contracts with the residents in its care homes and with the local authorities with whom it does business. It receives no public funding, enjoys no special statutory powers, and is at liberty to accept or reject residents as it chooses (subject, of course, to anti-discrimination legislation which affects everyone who offers a service to the public) and to charge whatever fees in its commercial judgment it thinks suitable." (para 26). [full-text]
- Lord Scott: "the fees charged by Southern Cross and paid by local or health authorities are charged and paid for a service. There is no element whatever of subsidy from public funds. ... The owner of a private care home taking local authority funded residents is in no different position. It is simply providing a service or services for which it charges a commercial fee." (para 27). [full-text]
- Lord Scott, private-law duties remain: "The managers do, of course, have private law duties of care to all their residents" (para 28); "In the case of a privately owned care home the manager’s duties to its residents are, whether contractual or tortious, duties governed by private law." (para 29). [full-text]
- Lord Scott, contracting out: "If every contracting out by a local authority of a function that the local authority could, in exercise of a statutory power or the discharge of a statutory duty, have carried out itself, turns the contractor into a hybrid public authority for section 6(3)(b) purposes, where does this end?" (para 30). [full-text]
- Lord Scott, protection by contract: "an obligation by Southern Cross to observe the Convention rights of residents is an express term of the agreement between the council and Southern Cross and is incorporated into the agreement between Southern Cross and YL. Any breach by Southern Cross of YL’s Convention rights would give YL a cause of action for breach of contract under ordinary domestic law." (para 32). [full-text]
- Lord Mance, the placing body's continuing duty: "it is not, and could not be, suggested that a local authority has no continuing duty in respect of the suitability of a placement, once made", quoting Moses J: "It remains under a duty to see that the applicants’ needs are met and if necessary to re-assess them." (para 114). [full-text]
- Lord Mance: "I do not regard the actual provision, as opposed to the arrangement, of care and accommodation for those unable to arrange it themselves as an inherently governmental function." (para 115). [full-text]
- Lord Mance: "In providing care and accommodation, Southern Cross acts as a private, profit-earning company. It is subject to close statutory regulation in the public interest. But so are many private occupations and businesses ... Regulation by the State is no real pointer towards the person regulated being a state or governmental body or a person with a function of a public nature, if anything perhaps even the contrary." (para 116). [full-text]
- Lord Mance, where the placed resident's public-law rights lie: "even if a publicly funded resident is not party to any contract with the care home, he or she will (unlike privately funded residents) have public law rights, including the right to invoke the protection of the Human Rights Convention, as against the local authority. These will enable him or her effectively to place on the local authority the onus to take any steps open to it as against the care home to protect the resident’s human rights." (para 118). "All care home residents have common and criminal law rights not to be mistreated" (para 118). [full-text]
- Lord Mance, compulsion distinguished: he agreed with Keith J that admission by compulsion under the Mental Health Act, as against "residence by choice", was "critical" (para 121). [full-text]
- Lord Mance, conclusion: "I would hold that Southern Cross in providing care and accommodation for YL was not and is not exercising functions of a public nature within section 6(3)(b) of the Human Rights Act 1998." (para 123). [full-text]
- Lord Neuberger: "this is not a case of contracting out a duty: under the 1948 Act, Birmingham does have a duty to arrange for the provision of care and accommodation for Mrs YL, but it has no duty to provide such care and accommodation itself." (para 147). [full-text]
- Lord Neuberger: "Mrs YL continues to enjoy Convention rights in respect of the provision of care and accommodation provided under section 21 of the 1948 Act against Birmingham, even after the care and accommodation was provided to her." (para 149). [full-text]
- Lord Neuberger, coercive powers: "it is of particular importance in relation to the issue which we have to decide that a proprietor of a care home is not given significant, or indeed (as far as I am aware) any, coercive or other statutory powers, over its residents" (para 166). [full-text]
- Lord Neuberger: "The liability of Southern Cross to provide Mrs YL with care and accommodation in the present case similarly “arises as a matter of private law”." (para 168). He dismissed the appeal (para 170) and added that if residents should have Convention rights against proprietors, "then it would be right to spell it out in terms" (para 171). [full-text]

**The dissents**

- Lord Bingham: "The performance by private body A by arrangement with public body B, and perhaps at the expense of B, of what would undoubtedly be a public function if carried out by B is, in my opinion, precisely the case which section 6(3)(b) was intended to embrace. It is, in my opinion, this case." (para 20). [full-text]
- Baroness Hale: "This was a function performed for the appellant pursuant to statutory arrangements, at public expense and in the public interest." (para 73). "I would allow this appeal and make the declaration sought." (para 75). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A care home, not a hotel.** The function was "residential accommodation plus care and attention" for people in need of it (para 14, Lord Bingham). A hotel housing people placed by a public body provides accommodation without care. Applying YL to a hotel operator is an analogy *(my inference)*, and a stronger one against public-function status than YL itself, since the reasons that troubled the dissent (vulnerability, care, potential coercion, paras 19, 69-70) are weaker for a hotel.
- **Compulsion would change it.** Lord Mance accepted that compulsory admission was "critical" in distinguishing a detained patient's case (para 121), and Lord Neuberger that statutory coercive powers are "a very powerful factor" (paras 166-167). Accommodation in which a person is required to stay under statutory powers is not covered by the majority's reasoning *(my reading)*.
- **Statute may have intervened.** Lord Neuberger invited Parliament to "spell it out in terms" (para 171). Whether legislation later made care-home providers public authorities for placed residents was not checked; nothing here supports a statement about the current law of care homes.
- **What the placed resident keeps.** Convention rights against the placing council (paras 118, 149), a continuing duty of the council about the placement's suitability (para 114), contractual rights which may include Convention terms (para 32), and common-law and criminal-law protection against mistreatment (para 118).
- **The majority's reasons were not uniform.** Lord Scott and Lord Neuberger stress private-law contract and the absence of coercive powers (paras 26, 166, 168); Lord Mance stresses the difference between arranging and providing (para 115). All three agree the provision is not a public function here.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation), the placed guest: supports T14's narrowing.** On YL's reasoning, the operator of accommodation who houses a guest placed and paid for by a public body under contract is not, by that fact, a public authority, and owes the guest private-law duties, contractual and tortious (paras 27-29, 115-116, 123, 168). The guest's Convention claims lie against the placing body, which keeps a continuing duty about the placement (paras 114, 118, 149). The dossier's placed-guest row should place the public-law duty on the placing body and the care duty on the operator in private law, as T14 proposes. *R (DMA)* (card dma2020sshd), a claim against a minister, fits this division.

**K5 (directedness): supports.** The operator's duties to the resident are "duties governed by private law" owed to that resident (para 29); the placing body's duties are public-law duties the resident can invoke against it (para 118).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In *YL v Birmingham City Council* the House of Lords held, by three to two, that a private care home providing care and accommodation to a resident placed and largely funded by a local authority under the National Assistance Act 1948 was not performing "functions of a public nature" under s. 6(3)(b) of the Human Rights Act 1998 (Lord Mance, para 123; Lord Scott, para 33; Lord Neuberger, para 170).
- Lord Neuberger and Lord Mance said that the resident kept her Convention rights against the local authority (Lord Neuberger, para 149; Lord Mance, para 118), which has, in a rider, "a continuing duty in respect of the suitability of a placement, once made" (Lord Mance, para 114). (Narrowed after CV-G: 'said', not 'the majority held'; para 114 is a rider.)
- Lord Scott said that a private care home's duties to its residents are, "whether contractual or tortious, duties governed by private law" (para 29).
- Lord Bingham and Baroness Hale dissented (paras 20, 73-75).

**What the paper may not say**

- That YL concerns hotels, or that it decides whether any hotel housing placed guests is a public authority.
- That YL is the present law for care homes, without checking later legislation.
- That a placed resident has no rights against the provider. She has contractual and common-law rights (paras 28-29, 32, 118).
