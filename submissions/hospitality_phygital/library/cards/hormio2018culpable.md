## Summary

Hormio asks how organisational practices shape what members know about the causes and effects of their acts, and when an organisation can be blamed for an individual's ignorance. Using Joanne Roberts's typology of organisational ignorance, she distinguishes 'knowable recognised unknowns' (facts an organisation could learn with resources and motivation), errors, and ignorance produced by suppressing knowledge (secrecy, denial, taboo). She argues that fragmentation of information in bureaucracies can create ignorance without anyone intending it. She then carries Holly Smith's account of culpable ignorance, on which wrongdoing from ignorance is traced to an earlier 'benighting act', into organisations: the benighting act may be done by a different person from the one who acts in ignorance, or by the organisation as a whole. So blame can lie with the collective without any bad will. Her running example is a retailer and a toaster supplier whose rushed outsourced manufacture yields faulty toasters. She ends with a forward-looking duty: an organisation that learns of such failures must fix its design and information flows, and is blameworthy if it does not.

If they do not, I argue that they are blameworthy for being negligent." (p. 15) [full-text]
- Holly Smith's account, as Hormio reports it: "all cases of culpable ignorance involve a sequence of acts: the initial act (the “benighting act”) where the agent fails to improve their cognitive position, resulting in ignorance, and a subsequent act where the wrongful act is done due to this culpable ignorance"; "frequently the benighting act takes the form of an omission, like failing to learn or find out something" (p. 15, citing Smith 1983: 552). Smith's three types, as reported: "Deficient investigation", "Preventing subsequent discovery" ("a person has either failed to remove or introduced a condition which made it impossible for him to acquire true belief of x’s wrongness") and "deficient inference" (pp. 15-16, citing Smith 1983: 544-547). [full-text]
- The extension to organisations: "Although Smith’s influential work on culpable ignorance looks at cases of individual responsibility, I see no reason why it cannot be framed in an organisational setting, allowing for the benighting act to be done by a different person than the morally inferior act that follows." (p. 16). "In this way, the benighting act happens at the organisational level and any possible blame should be directed at the collective." (p. 16) [full-text]
- Roles: "If the parameters of a given role dictate acts that lead to culpable ignorance in others, the moral responsibility for that ignorance falls (either fully or at least in part) to the collective." (p. 17) [full-text]
- A known risk left uninvestigated, without any particular warning: "If competitiveness of the prices of goods takes precedent over all other considerations in their ethos, Exciting Electronics is taking a risk that falls within known risks. They might have been very lucky in the past and gotten away without adequate safety checks, but they could not justify this way of operating by appealing to ignorance: the knowledge would have been found out given the right resources and motivation. This would be an example of deficient investigation and the resulting ignorance is blameworthy." (p. 17) [full-text]
- No bad will needed: "then the quality of will of an agent is not necessary for blame, although it remains sufficient" (p. 17); "In an organisational setting there needs to be no bad will in order for there to be culpable ignorance." (p. 18) [full-text]
- After a warning: "if the organisation does not take action to try to change its internal practices and communication flows after it has been made aware of the problems, then we can argue that it should care more." "Still, some organisational fragmentation of information is always necessary and can lead to unintended consequences." (p. 18) [full-text]
- Smith's limit, as Hormio reports it: "culpable ignorance arises only when the unwitting wrongful act falls within the known risks of the earlier act that infects the later act (Smith 1983, p. 551)." (p. 19) [full-text]
- The front-line firm answers to its customers, morally: "If no apology or compensation was forthcoming from Sell-A-Lot to their customers, although the fault was with Exciting Electronics, the customers of the retail chain would be right to blame the company for not caring for their customers sufficiently." (p. 19) [full-text]
- Automation: "What I want to argue here is that automation itself does not block out reactive attitudes." In her example an algorithm, informed of problems "by the supplier (via an internet form)", "calculated these to fall within acceptable risk parameters. Importantly, those parameters have been set by somebody." (p. 20) [full-text]
- Conclusion: "If problems emerge, or a likelihood of bad outcomes is pointed out to them, the organisation has a forward-looking duty to try to fix its design. If the organisation fails to respond, they are blameworthy for failing to improve their design or organisational practices when it comes to information flows." (pp. 20-21) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Preprint.** The text read is the December 2018 SocArXiv version. The journal version was not seen, and its record was not verified. Quotations and pages must be checked against the journal before the paper relies on them.
- **Blame, not duty or law.** Hormio's question is when an organisation is blameworthy for ignorance. She speaks of a "forward-looking duty to try to fix its design" (p. 20) only in the conclusion, without an account of where it comes from or what it requires. Nothing in the paper is a legal standard.
- **The trigger for design failures.** For ignorance that fragmentation produces without intent, the organisation's culpability for negligence arises when it does not fix its design after "a harmful outcome" (p. 15) or after "it has been made aware of the problems" (p. 18), or when "a likelihood of bad outcomes is pointed out to them" (p. 20). Before that, the paper relies on the 'known risks' route (p. 17) and on Smith's requirement that the wrong fall within the known risks of the benighting act (p. 19).
- **The ethos qualifier.** Whether an organisation may leave a matter uninvestigated depends on what falls within its ethos, which is "always a normative matter" (p. 6) and in flux (p. 6). The paper does not say how to fix a firm's ethos; that is left to "normative considerations outside simple cost and benefit analyses" (p. 6).
- **Illustrations are hypothetical.** The toaster supply chain, the spy ring and the algorithmic outsourcer are invented. Google's Project Maven (p. 9) is the only real case, mentioned briefly.
- **What Hormio adds to Rudy-Hiller's map.** She keeps Smith's tracing structure and rejects the assumption, which she says Smith shares (p. 17 n. 13), that bad will is necessary for blame (pp. 17-18). She does not engage the volitionist regress.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P3: supports the organisational form of the principle, in the moral register, and fixes when it bites.** Hormio holds that an organisation can be culpable for ignorance its own design produces: the benighting act may be collective (p. 16), and an organisation that runs a known risk without investigating "could not justify this way of operating by appealing to ignorance" (p. 17). That is the nearest published statement found in this run of the principle that a holder cannot meet a knowledge-conditioned responsibility by arranging, or merely allowing, not to know. It also reaches past the legal doctrines in *Global-Tech* and *The Star Sea*: no suspicion of a particular fact is needed where the risk is known (p. 17), and no bad will is needed (pp. 17-18). Two limits travel with it. For non-deliberate design, Hormio places blame for negligence chiefly after a harm or a warning (pp. 15, 18, 20-21), and she accepts Smith's known-risk requirement (p. 19). Applied to a host, the second limit is easily met, because that guests need to report problems and be helped is a known risk of hosting; that application is the paper's, not hers. Her automated-organisation example (p. 20), in which an algorithm receives a report through a web form and files it under acceptable risk, also bears on K9.

**What the paper may say**

- Hormio argues that "sometimes organisational design alone can create individual ignorance" and that "the act that creates conditions for culpable ignorance" can take place "at the collective level" (Hormio 2018, preprint: p. 1).
- She extends Holly Smith's account of culpable ignorance to organisations, "allowing for the benighting act to be done by a different person than the morally inferior act that follows" (p. 16), so that "any possible blame should be directed at the collective" (p. 16).
- Of her example, an organisation whose ethos puts excitement over other considerations and which runs a known risk without adequate checks, she says it "could not justify this way of operating by appealing to ignorance", because "the knowledge would have been found out given the right resources and motivation" (p. 17).
- Of "knowable recognised unknowns", she holds that "organisations have less excuses of being ignorant of facts than individuals do" (p. 5).
- She concludes that once problems emerge or are pointed out, an organisation "has a forward-looking duty to try to fix its design", and is blameworthy if it does not improve its practices "when it comes to information flows" (pp. 20-21).

**What the paper may not say**

- That Hormio states a legal rule, or a duty owed to customers or guests. She writes about blame.
- That she holds every organisation culpable for every ignorance its design produces. She exempts what is "defensibly outside the focus of the organisation" (p. 5), calls some fragmentation "always necessary" (p. 18), and ties culpability to known risks (pp. 17, 19).
- That she discusses hospitality, hosts or a channel from the customer. The application is the paper's.
