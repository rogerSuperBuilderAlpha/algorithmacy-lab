## Summary

Conceptual paper that names Digital Technology Captivity (DTC): stress and felt entrapment when
access to a needed service moves to digital-only or digitally dominant channels and consumers
lack the resources or alternatives to cope. Built on Lazarus and Folkman's stress-and-coping
model (four stages: environment change, anticipated coping, degree of stress, actual coping),
with intersectional susceptibility factors and two mitigators (social support, service design).
Distinguishes DTC from service captivity, from digital exclusion and from digital
unengagement. Hospitality appears once, as "self‑service hospitality check‑ins" in a list of
digital-only access (p. 974).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Definition: DTC is "…the condition in which consumers feel constrained when services move to digital access, and, after evaluating the situation through primary and secondary appraisal, perceive that they have few resources and options. This state produces stress through felt entrapment, as consumers are compelled to adopt adaptive or maladaptive coping strategies." (p. 975) [full-text]
- Earlier gloss: "We refer to this state of constraint and emotional strain (i.e., stress) as Digital Technology Captivity (DTC). Triggered by a shift in the access environment—rather than the service experience itself—DTC becomes especially problematic when consumers have an essential need for a service yet lack practical alternatives." (p. 974) [full-text]
- Distinction from exclusion and unengagement: "Where digital exclusion typically captures non‑access and unengagement reflects withdrawal or resistance, DTC centers on coerced participation in systems that have become unavoidable." (p. 976) [full-text]
- Unengagement defined in the literature as choosing not to engage "either by switching providers or forgoing the service" (p. 975) [full-text]
- "DTC, therefore, has the potential to generate more stress and felt entrapment than exclusion or unengagement" (p. 976) [full-text]
- Distinction from service captivity: "Service captivity arises from restrictions within the service or from limited provider options ..., whilst DTC originates from changes in access routes" (p. 977); "Consumers are therefore not captive to a single service provider but are captive to a system that requires digital skills and/or devices to participate" (p. 977) [full-text]
- A downgraded alternative still counts as a trigger: access changes "may occur in terms of substitution (analogue access replaced by digital), co‑existence (analogue retained but downgraded and more difficult to access), liquid digital (constant updating), or a move to digital‑only (systemic) access" (p. 978) [full-text]
- Cost of refusing the digital path: maladaptive coping includes "neglecting, abandoning, and distancing oneself from the service category altogether ..., which may carry a personal cost and loss (e.g., avoiding GP surgery because bookings are mandated to be online)"; it "may enhance vulnerability by restricting access to essential services"; escape by avoidance may bring "shame, embarrassment, or frustration" (p. 979) [full-text]
- Price penalty for staying analogue: patrons without smartphones face "multi‑screen checkouts and missing the best prices, which can coerce them into adopting technology" (Table 1, example C, p. 976) [full-text]
- Groups named: older, disabled and rural bank customers (Table 1, B, p. 976); "older, poorer, and unbanked customers" (Table 1, D, p. 976); "low‑income, Black, disabled, or LGBTQ+ older people" (p. 980) [full-text]
- Mitigator relevant to the fifth principle: "preserving autonomy, even in minor ways, can prevent DTC ... by allowing consumers to choose modes of access (e.g., digital, analogue), control the pacing of adoption, and better understand the reasons for the change" (p. 981); human front-line employees on hand to support self-service use (p. 981) [full-text]
- Conclusion: "While exclusion might mean simply walking away and foregoing a service, captivity is different: the consumer cannot walk away." (p. 982) [full-text]
- Limitation: "it has yet to be empirically tested" (p. 981) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

**Settled from the full text.** The distinctions the 8 August audit asserted and the Fable pass removed are in the paper: from digital exclusion and unengagement at p. 976 (and p. 975), from service captivity at p. 977. The named groups are there too (pp. 976, 980), though "unbanked" appears only in a quoted Guardian example (Table 1, D). The audit's claims were right; at the time nobody had read the full text that supports them.

**Cost of refusing the digital path.** The paper treats refusal as maladaptive coping that carries "a personal cost and loss" and restricts access to essential services (p. 979), and it gives a price example (p. 976). It does not discuss refusal as a *right* or require that an alternative be preserved as a matter of principle. It says preserved choice of access mode is a design mitigator (p. 981) and points to "analogue alternatives or inclusive regulation" in its research agenda (Table 2, p. 982). The "co‑existence (analogue retained but downgraded)" trigger (p. 978) is the most useful line for the paper: a downgraded alternative does not count as a real one.

Support verdicts:

- Revision plan §7: "Two constitutional conditions — a reachable person with authority to reverse, and a real alternative — secure accountability, and Wilson-Nash et al.'s (2026) account of digital technology captivity grounds the second of them." **PARTLY.** DTC grounds *why* a real alternative matters (pp. 974, 978, 979, 982) and names preserved access choice as a mitigator (p. 981). It frames the stake as stress, vulnerability and coping, not as accountability or standing. "Grounds the second" should read "shows what is lost without the second" unless the authors make the step to accountability themselves.
- v4 §7 roadmap: "preserving a meaningful alternative for those who decline the automated route" (line 73, cited there to Bovens). **SUPPORTED by Wilson-Nash** (pp. 978, 979, 981), not by Bovens. See the Bovens card.
- Old Summary claims (exclusion/unengagement distinction; named groups): **SUPPORTED** (pp. 975–976, 980).
- Kim et al. (2025) `kim2025shadow`: still complementary. Wilson-Nash's mechanism is appraisal, entrapment and coping (pp. 975–979). It does not model the transfer of labour.

Citing as 2026 with vol. 43(5), pp. 974–985 matches the version of record.
