## Summary

A three-year qualitative study (interviews, archival data, observation as a registered user) of
"TalentFinder", a large labour platform for high-skilled freelance work. To fix rating inflation
and prevent gaming, the platform replaced a transparent five-star score with an opaque
algorithmic evaluation. Freelancers experienced it as control but could not work out its
criteria, which changed without notice. Their responses split into experimental and constrained
reactivity, depending on platform dependence and on whether they had suffered score setbacks.
Rahman names the result an "invisible cage."

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Purpose of the opacity: "The platform implemented an opaque algorithmic rating evaluation to better differentiate which freelancers should be visible to clients and to prevent freelancers from gaming their scores" (p. 947) [full-text]
- Inflation motive: before the change "more than 90 percent of freelancers on the platform had at least a 4.00 out of 5.00" (p. 952) [full-text]
- Why platforms cannot command: they "theoretically (and legally) cannot subject workers to control measures of the past ... because explicitly exerting control over workers could jeopardize the employment relationship" (p. 949) [full-text]
- Workers "experienced this evaluation as a form of control but could not align their actions with its criteria because they could not clearly identify those criteria" (abstract, p. 945) [full-text]
- Knowledge asymmetry: "Because only a platform's algorithms can crunch the relevant data and make sense of how users' actions translate into evaluation outcomes, algorithms purport to 'know' the individuals in the invisible cage in ways that are inaccessible to them" (p. 977) [full-text]
- Moving target: "Even if people reverse-engineer or understand an algorithm at a moment in time, algorithms can update instantaneously based on real-time data, effectively rendering the current understanding of the algorithm obsolete" (p. 980) [full-text]
- No recourse: "The platform I studied did not provide freelancers with a pathway to appeal or modify their scores" (p. 979); expectations change "without notice, explanation, or recourse" (p. 976) [full-text]
- Boundary condition: "when a platform cannot specify how freelancers should behave—due to the nature of the work, to prevent gaming behavior, or for other reasons—the platform may be more likely to employ opaque third-party evaluations" (p. 981) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- (rr-2026-11) The earlier summary ("steering conduct precisely because it could not issue instructions ... while the evaluated party laboured to satisfy them") needs two corrections. The legal bar on command is background drawn from the literature (p. 949); the platform's stated reason for opacity was score inflation and gaming (pp. 947, 952). And workers did not simply labour to satisfy hidden criteria: some experimented, others withdrew to protect their scores (p. 947).
- (rr-2026-11) Strong support for the knowledge drift and for the moving target (pp. 977, 980), which also backs Hatherley's role in v4 §6. The relation is platform and freelancer; the guest is not in it.
- With Calo and Rosenblat 2017 on the revision plan's leaving list, Rahman carries the knowledge-drift sentence alone. It can.
