## Summary

Ripstein surveys philosophical accounts of tort law. The sections read set economic analysis against corrective justice, which explains a tort action by the wrong one party did the other and treats the remedy as the survival of the right that was violated. He distinguishes three versions of corrective justice (Weinrib, Coleman, Gardner) and reports Gardner's as the widest: failing to do what one had reason to do leaves the reasons standing, and the normal response is to do the next-best thing. He then sets out civil recourse theory, which accepts that torts are relational wrongs and denies that the remedy repairs them. On that theory the plaintiff holds a power to proceed against the wrongdoer, the state owes the avenue, and no duty to pay damages exists until the plaintiff sues and wins. He reports the replies.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Who starts a tort action: "a tort action is initiated by the aggrieved party rather than the state." (§2, para. 1) [full-text]
- What it yields: the remedy is "either the payment of damages to the aggrieved party or, in a limited range of cases, an injunction requiring the defendant to cease their wrongful activity." (§2, para. 1) [full-text]
- Corrective justice, Kantian version, on what survives a violation: "the plaintiff’s right to constrain the defendant’s conduct survives in the form of a claim to a remedy from the defendant in particular (Weinrib 1995; Ripstein 2016)." (§4.2, para. 7) [full-text]
- What any account must explain: "why this plaintiff is entitled to recover from this defendant and no one else, and why this defendant is liable to this plaintiff and no one else." (§4.2, para. 8) [full-text]
- Gardner's view, as reported: "For Gardner, corrective justice is a general principle about inappropriate conduct, according to which the failure to do what one has reason to do does not mean those reasons come to an end; the normal response to such failure is to do the next best thing." (§4.3, para. 3) [full-text]
- The line at the time for performance, as reported: "The promised act of meeting at noon can no longer be done, and so there can be no duty to arrive at noon, and no right that the promisor arrive by noon. Instead, there is a new duty, the duty to do the next best thing." (§4.3, para. 3) [full-text]
- Applied to injury, as reported: "those reasons give rise to a new duty to repair the wrong that I have done." (§4.3, para. 3) [full-text]
- A quotation from Gardner's version of record: "Gardner describes his view as an account of corrective justice because it regulates “the reversal of wrongful transactions on the ground of their wrongfulness” (2011: 22)." (§4.3, para. 3) [full-text]
- Ripstein's criticism of it: "Gardner’s account differs significantly from Weinrib’s because of its exclusive focus on the relation between wrongdoers and the reasons that apply to them, rather than the relation between the parties. The fact that those reasons make reference to the wronged party is not essential to the operation of Gardner’s conception of corrective justice." (§4.3, para. 4) [full-text]
- Torts as relational wrongs, on which both camps agree: "So, too, in defamation, only the person who was defamed has a cause of action, even if that person’s family members or business associates will suffer as much or more from the defamation." (§4.4, para. 2) [full-text]
- Civil recourse on the aim of the remedy: "For civil recourse theorists, by contrast, the aim of a tort remedy is not to repair the wrong, but rather to empower plaintiffs to confront and make a demand on the persons who wronged them." (§4.4, para. 3) [full-text]
- A power, and a duty on the state: "the state owes it to persons who have been wronged to provide them with a civil avenue of recourse through which they can proceed against those who have wronged them; a tort remedy is something that the plaintiff is empowered to seek from the defendant rather than something that the plaintiff is entitled to receive from the defendant." (§4.4, para. 3) [full-text]
- No duty before judgment: "Until the plaintiff initiates an action and prevails in it, the defendant is not under a duty to pay damages (Smith 2012; 2019)." (§4.4, para. 3) [full-text]
- Money often cannot repair: "No amount of money can make up for the loss of a limb, or a child, or for the humiliation of a breach of privacy or the willful infliction of emotional distress (Hershovitz 2017)." (§4.4, para. 3) [full-text]
- The reply on standing and on the duty: "in cases in which someone other than the person whose right was violated has standing to appear before the court, that standing is limited to enforcing the right (Liau 2023). The claim that there is no legal duty to pay damages unless the plaintiff brings suit has also been disputed (Steel & Stevens 2020)." (§4.5, para. 1) [full-text]
- The difference stated: "For corrective justice theory, the remedy enforces the right that was violated; the relevant duty is owed by the defendant to the plaintiff. For civil recourse theory, the remedy addresses the fact that some right was violated, and the relevant duty is owed by the state to provide an avenue of recourse." (§4.5, para. 3) [full-text]
- Rights of action can be taken away by statute: "Most prominently, New Zealand has abolished the action in negligence for personal injuries and replaced it with a system of social insurance." (§4.5, para. 4) [full-text]
- How remedies work in practice: a system "in which most cases are settled, most damages are paid by the wrongdoer’s employer or insurer" (§4.5, para. 5) [full-text]
- No general duty to protect in negligence: "the law of negligence does not suppose that there is a duty to protect or benefit others (Ripstein 2016)." (§4.2, para. 3) [full-text]
- Public duty and private claim: "If someone blocks a road, all members of the public are prevented from passing on it, but only the person who finds their passage blocked is entitled to sue." (§4.8, para. 2) Public nuisance and the torts of public officials "raise general issues of how a public requirement crystallizes into a private claim." (§4.8, para. 1) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **What the entry can carry.** It reports theories of tort law. It is no authority for what the law of any jurisdiction is, and it cites almost no cases in the sections read. Smith's denial, the civil recourse view and the replies must each be attributed to their authors 'as reported by Ripstein'. Smith (2012) has no open copy that the scout could find; Steel and Stevens (2020) and Liau (2023, carded) are named as replies and were not read here.
- **The author is a party.** Ripstein is cited throughout the entry as a corrective-justice theorist (2016), and §4.5 is headed as an assessment of civil recourse. The balance of §§4.4–4.5 is against civil recourse. Report the objection in its own terms.
- **The strongest objection to K7's line, in the legal register.** If no legal duty to pay damages exists before judgment (§4.4, para. 3), then after a breach the law gives the guest a power and the host a liability, and 'the duty to repair' is not yet a legal duty of the host. The denial is disputed (§4.5, para. 1) and concerns the law of damages only. It leaves the moral duty of repair alone.
- **An unexpected resource for the channel.** Civil recourse theory is the one legal theory in the entry whose stated aim is that the wronged party can 'confront and make a demand on' the wrongdoer (§4.4, para. 3). It values exactly what K7 values. It then puts the correlative duty on the state, and it arises after the wrong. It cannot be cited for a host's duty to be reachable.
- **A slip in the entry.** §4.2, para. 7 ends by saying that the law of negligence 'does require' a person to protect others from natural perils or third parties. The same entry says the opposite at §4.2, para. 3, and the argument of para. 7 needs 'does not'. Do not quote para. 7 for a duty to protect.
- **The lunch example.** The illustration attributed to Gardner (a promise to meet at noon, arrival at 12:15) is not in the eprint of the 2011 article that was read (gardner2011what). Attribute it to Ripstein's report.
- The entry is revised in place; the Fall 2026 archive edition is the fixed text.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7 (supports and narrows).** Supports: on the corrective-justice view the violated right 'survives in the form of a claim to a remedy' (§4.2, para. 7), and on Gardner's a duty that can no longer be performed is replaced by a new duty to do the next-best thing (§4.3, para. 3). Narrows: remedies are damages and, 'in a limited range of cases', an injunction (§2, para. 1); on one prominent view even the duty to pay damages waits for judgment (§4.4, para. 3); and Gardner's account, by Ripstein's reading, does not turn on the relation between the parties (§4.3, para. 4). The legal analogues in tort point to later repair, so K7 rests on P3.
- **K5 (supports the legal limb).** Tort wrongs are relational on every view the entry reports: the plaintiff complains of a violation of 'that very plaintiff’s right' (§4.2, para. 1), and only the person wronged has the action (§4.4, para. 2). §4.8 gives the general form of the innkeeper problem: a public requirement, and a private claim only for the person specially affected.
- **K6 (bears).** Standing to sue held by someone other than the right-holder 'is limited to enforcing the right' (§4.5, para. 1, reporting Liau), which keeps the claim and the standing to enforce it distinct.
