## Summary

After Aristotelian control and epistemic conditions for responsibility, the paper
turns to "the other side of the responsibility relation: the addressees or "patients" of
responsibility, who may demand reasons for actions and decisions made by using AI". It grounds
explainability in "responsibility as answerability", justified "not on agency, but on patiency".

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Addressees or "patients" of responsibility "may demand reasons for actions and decisions made by using AI" (abstract) [full-text]
- "responsibility as answerability thus offers an important additional, if not primary, justification for explainability based, not on agency, but on patiency" (abstract) [full-text]
- Assumes machines are not responsible agents: the paper "will assume that AI technologies do not meet traditional criteria for full moral agency ... and that therefore they also cannot be (held) responsible"; responsibility "remains and should remain with the human agents who develop and use the technology" (section 'The Problem of Responsibility Attribution') [full-text]
- The relational turn: "There is the question who is responsible for something (responsibility attribution) but also the question who is responsible to whom. Responsibility is not only about doing something and knowing what you're doing; it also means answerability." ... "The responsibility patient is the addressee, the one who is addressed in the responsibility relation." (section 'Include Responsibility Patients') [full-text]
- The 'to whom' question is credited to Duff, not to Darwall or Strawson; the words Darwall, Strawson, Hohfeld, second-personal and standing (in the normative sense) do not occur in the text (whole-text search, 2026-11-08) [full-text]
- Owed answer: "developers and users of AI owe those affected by AI an answer: the latter rightly demand reasons and explanations for actions and decisions that affect them" (same section) [full-text]
- Law: proposes "a right of explanation, rather than merely a right of information ... as is now the case in European data protection regulation (GDPR)" (same section) [full-text]
- Explanation is social: humans, not machines, should explain; "only humans can really explain and should explain what they decide and do" (same section) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Why it matters for the gap statement.** It is the closest hit to "work applying the tradition to algorithmic decision subjects". The gap statement in WS_B_standing.md is bounded accordingly: work exists that puts the decision subject on the addressee side of an answerability relation (this paper; Grant, Behrends and Basl 2023 on duties owed to decision-subjects, abstract only), but none found that applies second-personal standing or Hohfeldian correlativity to guests, or defines the subject's standing as conditional on a reachable counterparty.
- Fable (rr-2026-11), full text read: the paper cites neither Darwall nor Strawson nor Hohfeld, and never uses 'second-personal' or 'standing'. Its patient is anyone 'actually or potentially affected' by a decision, including non-humans; the demand is for reasons, and it is justified by patiency, not by a position the institution confers. So the gap statement holds: this paper puts the decision subject on the addressee side of an answerability relation but does not define a conditional standing or name the second-personal or correlativity traditions. Examples are transport (self-driving cars, Boeing 737 MAX); there is no service or hospitality setting.

## Where it enters the manuscript

Not entering. If a referee raises it, the answer is that answerability-based explainability is a
duty on the decider; the paper's standing is the guest's position, and its failure condition is the
absence of an addressee, which explanation alone does not repair.
