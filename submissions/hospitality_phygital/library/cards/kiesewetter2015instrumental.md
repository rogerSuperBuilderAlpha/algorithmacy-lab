## Summary

Kiesewetter defends the transmission principle: if A ought to φ, and ψ-ing is a necessary means for A to φ, then A ought to ψ. The 'ought' is the deliberative, all-things-considered ought that settles practical deliberation, not moral obligation as such. He argues that the recent objections to transmission (Jackson and Pargetter's Procrastinate case, and examples from Broome, Raz and Kolodny) all rest on actualism, the view that what one ought to do depends on what would happen if one did it. Actualism violates joint satisfiability (if A ought to φ and ought to ψ, A can do both), which he holds essential to the deliberative ought. Where the agent's own expected failure drives an objection, he keeps the duty to take the means and adds a wide-scope duty (make sure that if one will not perform, one does not take the means). Where the failure is not up to the agent, he denies the original 'ought'. He closes with an abductive argument: transmission follows from 'reason transmission', which best explains joint satisfiability.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- The principle: "The transmission principle: If A ought to φ, and ψ-ing is a necessary means for A to φ, then A ought to ψ." (p. 922; checked against the page image) [full-text]
- Transmission is to necessary means: "'Ought', that is, transmits to necessary means—or so it seems plausible to say." (p. 922) [full-text]
- The wider principle is weaker: Way's argument "is officially based on a more liberal transmission principle which is not restricted to necessary means, but he mentions himself that his principle is open to objections that do not apply to a transmission principle restricted to necessary means" (p. 922, n. 2; 'necessary' is italic in the print) [full-text]
- Which 'ought': "the concept that settles the conclusions of practical deliberation—the kind of deliberation that aims to answer the question of what, all things considered, one has most reason to do." "The reasons in question can be practical reasons of all sorts, encompassing hedonic, prudential, and moral reasons." (p. 923) [full-text]
- What a means is: "a means is a type of action ψ that helps to bring about some state of affairs"; following Kolodny, "'helping to bring about' need not be causing, but may also be constituting, or preventing something that would prevent A's φ-ing." Means include omissions. (p. 924; symbols as in the print) [full-text]
- Necessity left open: "Specifying the kind of necessity involved in talk of necessary means is a difficult task that is beyond the scope of this essay. I shall rely on an intuitive sense of what it means to say that something is necessary or possible for an agent." (p. 924, n. 7) [full-text]
- Not to side effects: "The transmission principle states only that 'ought' transmits to necessary means and thus does not entail that it transmits to necessary side effects or logical implications." (p. 925) [full-text]
- Moral obligation is not the deliberative ought: "If moral obligations conflict, then the right conclusion to draw is rather that moral obligations do not necessarily amount to all-things-considered 'oughts'." (p. 932) [full-text]
- The wide-scope 'ought' for Procrastinate: "Procrastinate ought to accept the invitation, and he ought to write the review, but he also ought to make sure that if he will not write the review, he rejects the invitation." (p. 934) [full-text]
- Measures that secure conformity get reasons, not a new 'ought': the likelihood of non-performance "could still make a difference to the normative situation, for example, by providing second-order reasons to adopt additional measures that ensure his conformity to his obligations. Compare the obligation not to drink and drive. If you are likely to drink, this might give you additional reasons to ensure that you will not violate this obligation, but this does not mean that with respect to drinking-and-driving you stand under obligations different from those of people who are unlikely to drink." (p. 937) [full-text]
- Two kinds of counterexample: in Raz's and Kolodny's cases "the fact that an agent is unlikely to conform to a presumed source 'ought' is not due to a failure of the agent. In such cases, the mentioned response is not available, and the instrumental 'ought' cannot be defended. I shall argue that such examples call instead for the rejection of the source 'ought'." (p. 938) [full-text]
- Low-probability means (Kolodny's Unlucky, whose taxi has a .1 chance of saving the antique): "he ought not to take the taxi"; "we should deny that Unlucky ought to save the antique, quite independently of the transmission principle." (pp. 943-944) [full-text]
- The summary rule: if we will not perform "due to the fact that we cannot perform it (or cannot perform it if we do what we otherwise ought to do), then we should reject the assumption that we ought to perform this action in the first place. If this fact is due to a failure in the exercise of our agency, then we should maintain that we ought to take the necessary means." (p. 944; the print's parentheses render as 'ð Þ' in the extracted text) [full-text]
- The argument for it: "Reason transmission: If A has a reason to φ, and ψ-ing is an incompatible alternative to φ-ing, then A has an equally strong reason not to ψ." (p. 945) "If buying is a necessary means to going to the concert, then not-buying is an incompatible alternative to going to the concert." (pp. 945-946) [full-text]
- Its status: "Like all abductive arguments, this argument is defeasible by providing an alternative plausible explanation." (p. 946) [full-text]
- Ought implies can is assumed: "'Ought' implies 'can': If A ought to φ, then A can φ." (p. 940) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Necessary means only.** The principle and its defence are restricted to necessary means (pp. 922, 925), and the author flags that a wider principle faces objections this one avoids (p. 922, n. 2). Nothing in the article supports transmission to a means that only makes performance more likely or more secure. For such measures the article offers "second-order reasons", not an 'ought' (p. 937).
- **The deliberative 'ought', not a directed duty.** The 'ought' is what one has most reason to do all things considered (p. 923). The article says nothing about duties owed to anyone, and it separates moral obligations from all-things-considered oughts (p. 932). Using it for a directed moral duty of a host requires a bridge the article does not supply.
- **Disjunctive means.** The article does not discuss disjunctive means. Its own argument suggests how they work: by reason transmission, if φ cannot be done without at least one of several means, then taking none of them is an incompatible alternative to φ (pp. 945-946). That yields an 'ought' to take some means, not a particular one *(my inference)*.
- **Probability.** In Kolodny's case a necessary means with a low chance of success is not required, and the source 'ought' falls with it (pp. 941-944). A channel that rarely brings a failure to anyone able to correct it in time would face the same verdict *(my inference)*.
- **Agent, not firm.** All the cases are of single human agents. The article does not address collective or delegated agency.
- **Contested.** A web search for this article surfaced Stephen White, 'Transmission Failures', *Ethics* 127 (2017): 719-732, doi 10.1086/690014 (Crossref record checked 11 Nov 2026). The search summary describes it as a reply to Kiesewetter. I did not read it, so it is a lead only and nothing is attributed to it.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, step from P3 to C (red team S2): narrows.** The standard defence of transmission reaches only necessary means (pp. 922, 925; n. 2). C as worded ("must keep a channel from the guest") follows by this route only for failures whose sole timely route to the holder is the guest's report. For other failures the article supports, at most, a duty to keep some timely means of learning, by the disjunctive reading noted above *(my inference)*. That is red team repair S2(ii); repair S2(i) is the narrowing to failures where the guest's report is necessary.

**K7, F2 (what kind of duty).** Kiesewetter's instrumental 'ought' is a further 'ought' to take the means, not a part of the duty to perform (pp. 922, 945-946). It sits with Hindriks's separate second-order duty against the dossier's "part of the duty it already has".

**K7, S1 (directedness): no help.** The article is about the deliberative ought and does not speak of duties owed to anyone (pp. 923, 932).

**What the paper may say**

- Kiesewetter defends the principle that if one ought to do something, and an action is a necessary means to doing it, one ought to take that means (p. 922).
- He restricts the principle to necessary means and notes that a principle not so restricted faces further objections (p. 922 and n. 2).
- Discussing an agent's own likely failure to conform, he says the likelihood 'could' supply 'second-order reasons to adopt additional measures that ensure his conformity' without changing the agent's first-order obligations (p. 937). He does not say such measures can never be obligatory.

**What the paper may not say**

- That Kiesewetter defends transmission to means that only make performance more likely or more secure.
- That his principle concerns moral duties owed to a person. It concerns the all-things-considered 'ought' (pp. 923, 932).
- That the transmission principle is uncontested. Kiesewetter calls his own argument for it defeasible (p. 946), and he answers published objections from Broome, Raz and Kolodny (pp. 938-944).
