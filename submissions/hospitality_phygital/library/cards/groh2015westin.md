## Summary

Jillian Groh, a registered guest at the Westin in downtown Denver, brought friends back to her room at about 2 a.m. Security guards confronted the group over noise and put it out of the hotel, although the group told them they were drunk and could not drive. A friend asked a guard whether they could wait in the lobby for a taxi because it was freezing; the guard blocked the door and refused. Seven people left in Groh's car with a drunk driver; the crash killed one man and left Groh in a persistent vegetative state. Her parents sued the hotel in negligence. The Supreme Court of Colorado held that "a hotel that [evicts] a guest has a duty to exercise reasonable care under the [circumstances]", which "requires the hotel to refrain from evicting an intoxicated guest into a foreseeably dangerous environment" (¶ 4), and that issues of fact barred summary judgment. Two justices dissented on the application; they accepted the duty.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- **Court, date, vote.** Supreme Court of Colorado, No. 13SC382, decided 13 April 2015, rehearing denied 11 May 2015; 2015 CO 25, 347 P.3d 606. Hood, J., "delivered the Opinion of the Court" (608). "JUSTICE EID dissents, and JUSTICE COATS joins in the dissent." (618). The opinion does not list the majority or state the size of the bench. [full-text]
- **Jurisdiction and law applied.** Colorado common law of negligence, on certiorari from the Colorado Court of Appeals (2013 COA 39), which had reversed summary judgment for the hotel (¶¶ 3, 17; 608–609, 611). [full-text]
- **Parties and claim.** Groh, "individually, and by and through her guardians and conservators", against Westin Operator, LLC, for "negligence, premises liability, breach of contract, and negligent hiring and training. Only the negligence-related claims are before us now." (¶ 11; 610) [full-text]
- **Who the guest was.** "Jillian Groh registered as a guest at the Westin"; her sister "used her employee discount to rent the room only for Groh" (¶ 5; 609; the CAP text prints "Grob's sister"). The court treats Groh as the guest throughout and draws nothing from the fact that another person rented the room. [full-text]
- **Whom the guests dealt with.** A Westin security guard "summoned another Westin security guard as well as the guest services manager-supervisor" (¶ 6; 609). "Ultimately, hotel security told everyone except the registered guests to leave the premises; Groh refused to stay without her friends. For the purpose of the summary judgment motion at issue here, the Westin concedes it evicted Groh as well." (¶ 6; 609) [full-text]
- **What the guests told the hotel.** "At least one person told the security guard that everyone in the group was 'drunk,' that the 'whole purpose' of renting the room was to allow them to drink without driving, and that they could not leave because, 'We are drunk. We can't drive.'" (¶ 7; 609) [full-text]
- **What was asked, by whom, and the answer.** "On the way out, one of Groh's friends asked a guard if the group could wait in the lobby for a taxicab because it was freezing outside." (¶ 2; 608). In the deposition account, "The security guard refused his request, blocked the door to bar re-entry", and told him to get out, with an obscenity the court prints with asterisks (¶ 8; 610). The request came from a friend, and it went to a guard. The opinion does not say what the manager-supervisor did. [full-text]
- **Other help at hand.** "Police officers happened to be on the hotel premises investigating a separate, unrelated incident. The Westin employees did not seek police intervention with Groh or her friends." (¶ 7; 609). "Groh called her brother for advice, and he told her to call a cab. She did not heed his advice." (¶ 8; 609) [full-text]
- **Holding.** "Based on the special relationship that exists between an innkeeper and guest, we hold that a hotel that [evicts] a guest has a duty to exercise reasonable care under the [circumstances]. This requires the hotel to refrain from evicting an intoxicated guest into a foreseeably dangerous environment. Whether a foreseeably dangerous environment existed at the time of eviction depends on the guest's physical state and the conditions into which he or she was evicted, including the time, the surroundings, and the weather." (¶ 4, 609; repeated at ¶ 51, 618; the CAP text prints "eviets" and "cireumstances" in both places) [full-text]
- **Two sources of duty.** "In deciding whether a duty exists, we have traditionally examined (1) the nature of the relationship between the parties and (2) a particular set of public policy factors. Either or both may confer a duty or inform the scope of the duty." (¶ 25; 612) [full-text]
- **The basis: 'special relationship'.** "This court and successive versions of the Restatement of Torts have long recognized the innkeeper-guest relationship as a 'special' one, conferring a duty to exercise reasonable or ordinary care under the circumstances and giving rise to an affirmative duty to aid or protect." (¶ 26; 612). "By virtue of the special relationship between innkeeper and guest, we conclude that a hotel has a similar legal duty to exercise reasonable care toward its guests." (¶ 32; 613) [full-text]
- **The basis: dependence.** "We observed in Whitlock that the law appears 'to be working slowly' toward recognizing a duty to aid or protect those in a dependent or mutually dependent relationship." (¶ 29; 613). When a special relationship exists, liability can follow from "nonfeasance (passive inaction or a failure to protect from harm)" as well as misfeasance (¶ 28; 612). [full-text]
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 314A, as quoted by this court** (¶ 29; 613): the section "states that an innkeeper 'is under' the following duty to its guests: '(a) to protect them against unreasonable risk of physical harm, and (b) to give them first aid after it knows or has reason to know that they are ill or injured, and to care for them until they [can] be cared for by others.'" The court marks "(emphasis added)"; the CAP text shows no italics, and prints "cam" for "can" and "§ 814A" for § 314A. The court says it had "cited with approval" the section in an earlier case (¶ 29). [full-text]
- **Restatement (Third), as described by this court.** "In 2012, section 314A was replaced by another Restatement provision, which continues to impose an affirmative duty of reasonable care on actors that are part of enumerated special relationships, including innkeepers." (n. 4, citing § 40 cmt. a) [full-text]
- **The basis: the carrier analogy.** A carrier may be liable when it ejects a passenger "at a time or place which is dangerous" (¶ 30, 613, quoting a 1912 New Jersey case), and the court quotes an 1879 Iowa case: "The rules of law, as well as the dictates of humanity, require that the ejection shall occur at such place, and be conducted in such manner, as not unreasonably to expose the party to danger." (¶ 31; 613) [full-text]
- **The basis: policy factors.** Risk, foreseeability weighed against social utility, the burden of precaution and its consequences (¶ 33; 613–614). On burden: "Relatively low-cost options are available to ensure that a particular eviction is reasonable in light of the circumstances, including, but not limited to, requesting police assistance, allowing intoxicated guests to wait in the lobby after they call a taxi, or procuring a taxi for an intoxicated guest." (¶ 36; 614) [full-text]
- **Scope: the property line does not end the duty.** "The scope of the property does not define the [scope] of the duty; whether the risk of harm originated during the eviction process does." (¶ 40; 616; the CAP text prints "seope") [full-text]
- **Scope: the duty runs while the relation lasts, and ending it is a process.** The court asks "whether the risk of harm to Groh arose while she remained a guest" (¶ 42; 616) and answers: "The 'risk of harm' to Groh arose during this eviction process, while the innkeeper-guest special relationship still existed between the Westin and Groh." (¶ 43; 616). The first Court of Appeals opinion, later withdrawn, had held that "the innkeeper-guest special relationship terminated upon her eviction" (¶ 16; 611). [full-text]
- **Limit: no duty to transport.** "In recognizing this duty, we are not implicitly holding that hotels must provide safe transportation off premises" (¶ 38; 615). [full-text]
- **Limit: hotels only.** "Likewise, we are not imposing a duty that extends to other businesses that provide entertainment (such as taverns, restaurants, concert venues, sports stadiums, and ski areas). Our holding in this case reaches only hotels that lawfully evict guests; it does not govern other entertainment-based businesses because the requisite special relationship is absent." (n. 7) [full-text]
- **Limit: the guest's own responsibility.** Comparative negligence applies (¶ 46; 617), and "the concept of individual responsibility" may bear on breach and causation (¶ 50; 618). [full-text]
- **Those through whom the hotel acts.** The negligent hiring and training claim rests "on the principle that a person or business conducting an activity through employees is subject to liability for harm that results from negligent conduct in employing those persons" (¶ 23; 612), and survives because, among other things, "the record does not contain determinative information on the adequacy of the Westin's training on eviction procedures" (¶ 45; 617). [full-text]
- **Other courts, as this court quotes them.** A private club evicting a guest was bound "to accord [the guest] decent and humane treatment" and "bound to pay proper attention to the plaintiff's condition" (n. 6, quoting a 1951 Massachusetts case, *McClean v. University Club*, not read). [full-text]
- **Other claims.** The trial court dismissed the contract claim "because Groh breached the contract when she allowed additional people into her room" and the statutory premises claim because she was injured off the premises; neither was before the Supreme Court (n. 3). The Dram Shop Act did not apply because the hotel served no alcohol (¶¶ 47–49; 617–618). [full-text]
- **Did the claim succeed?** Not decided. "Consequently, we affirm the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for further proceedings." (¶ 51; 618) [full-text]
- **Dissent (Eid, J., with Coats, J.).** On the duty: "I agree with this entirely unremarkable position" (¶ 52; 618). On the facts it would give summary judgment: taxis were available, and "while the behavior of the security guards was unquestionably unprofessional, such behavior does nothing to change the availability of taxis, nor did it prevent Groh and her companions from getting a taxi, or create a dangerous environment" (¶ 59; 620). It reads the majority as in effect requiring safe transport: "I know of no authority, and the majority cites none, that would impose a duty on innkeepers to ensure safe transportation for evicted guests." (¶ 60; 620) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Duty found, breach not.** The court did not hold that refusing the request to wait was negligent. It listed letting guests wait among low-cost options (¶ 36) and counted the refusal among the events of the eviction (¶ 43). Breach, causation and comparative fault went to trial (¶¶ 45–46). The outcome on remand was not checked.
- **The request was a friend's.** Groh herself is not recorded as asking the hotel for anything. She was a registered guest whom the hotel would have let stay (¶ 6).
- **Footnote 7 is the court's own limit.** It is not needed for the decision, since no tavern or restaurant was before the court, yet it says in terms that for those businesses "the requisite special relationship is absent". Iowa places taverns inside the special-relationship duty (*Hoyt*, card hoyt2013gutterz). The states differ.
- **The duty rests on relationship and policy together** (¶¶ 25, 37). The opinion does not say the relationship alone would have been enough on these facts.
- **§ 314A is quoted in part.** Only the innkeeper's two clauses appear, with "against unreasonable risk"; the Georgia majority's quotation of the same clause reads "from unreasonable risk" (card rasnick2011krishna). The statement that § 40 "replaced" § 314A is the court's (n. 4).
- **The defendant is the operating company.** The opinion calls it "the Westin" and "a hotel" and says nothing of owner, brand or franchise. The guards are treated as its employees (¶¶ 13, 23). It is no authority on who the host is among several firms.
- **OCR.** Paragraph signs are mangled throughout and several words are misread ("eviets", "cireumstances", "seope", "cam", "Grob's", "§ 814A"). Every quotation above should be checked against the official opinion before it is printed.
- **Later treatment not checked.**

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (reception tier): supports for a hotel, in Colorado; narrows for restaurants and bars; narrows the 'attending' sentence.** The hotel owes its guest reasonable care for her person (¶¶ 4, 32). The court says the duty does not extend to taverns or restaurants (n. 7), which cuts against "every commercial host, restaurants and bars included" for this duty in this state. The content of the duty turns on "the guest's physical state" (¶ 4), so the law here does require regard to the particular guest once the host acts on her. Section 314A as this court quotes it goes further, to first aid and care for a guest known to be ill (¶ 29).
- **K3 (grounds): supports dependence and the role; adds policy.** The court's words are "special relationship" and "dependent or mutually dependent relationship" (¶¶ 26, 29). It does not use the language of holding out or of assumed responsibility; the first Court of Appeals opinion, later withdrawn, had declined to impose a duty under the assumed-duty doctrine (¶ 16), and the Supreme Court does not take that route. Policy factors do part of the work (¶¶ 33–37).
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** The injured guest sues in her own name through her guardians, and the claim survives.
- **K7 (channel): supports P1 and illustrates P4; does not state P3 or the conclusion.** The duty lasts as long as the relation and covers how the host ends it (¶¶ 40–43). The hotel knew the risk because the guests told its guards (¶ 7), and the person on the spot with power over the single case refused the request (¶ 8). The remedy is damages afterwards. The court imposes no duty to hear or to grant a request.
- **K8/K9: thin.** The hotel answers for how it hires and trains the staff who deal with guests (¶¶ 23, 45).

**What the paper may say** (for Colorado, naming the court):
- The Supreme Court of Colorado held that a hotel evicting a guest owes her a duty of reasonable care, "based on the special relationship that exists between an innkeeper and guest" (¶¶ 4, 51).
- The duty is not bounded by the property line and attaches to risks that arise while the innkeeper–guest relationship lasts, the eviction included (¶¶ 40–43).
- The guests told the guards they were drunk and could not drive; a friend asked to wait in the lobby for a taxi; a guard refused (¶¶ 2, 7–8).
- The court counted letting intoxicated guests wait in the lobby among "relatively low-cost options" open to a hotel (¶ 36).
- The court confined its holding to hotels and said the special relationship is absent for taverns and restaurants (n. 7), and it said it was not holding that hotels must provide safe transportation (¶ 38).
- Restatement (Second) of Torts § 314A, as quoted in *Groh* at ¶ 29, puts an innkeeper under a duty to protect guests against unreasonable risk of physical harm and to give first aid to guests it knows or has reason to know are ill or injured; the court records that § 40 of the Restatement (Third) replaced it in 2012 (n. 4).

**What the paper may not say:**
- That the hotel was held liable, or that refusing the request was held negligent.
- That Groh asked the hotel for help, or that the request reached a manager.
- That the court recognised a duty to listen to guests, to grant their requests or to keep a person reachable.
- That the duty applies to restaurants and bars in Colorado.
- That the court was unanimous on the outcome.
- Anything on the Restatements' own authority, or on the authority of the cases the court cites from other states.
