## Summary

International-relations account of hospitality that argues host power does not end at the threshold: the distinction between inside and outside has to be policed and managed after entry. Illustrated with refugee camps, where the guest population is categorised and governed once inside.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Thesis: the inside/outside distinctions of hospitality "also need to be policed, managed and controlled, even after the threshold of the home is crossed" (accepted manuscript, PDF p. 3, introduction) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

I searched the text for owe, duty and obligation: the only hits are about international moral duties (PDF p. 4) and the camp setting, and Bulley analyses power, space and control, not a continuing host obligation. Bulley does treat hospitality as ethics, 'ethical responsibility-taking for others' (PDF p. 7), and the abstract calls it 'a practice that continues once the guest crosses the threshold', so an ethical responsibility does run past the threshold in Bulley. A continuing host *obligation* in the commercial sense does not. The 'owes' half is weakly grounded, not absent.

## rr-2026-11-k: bearing on the keystone (R9)

*Added by a Claude (Opus 5.5) subagent, reader R9, 11 Nov 2026, after re-reading the saved accepted manuscript whole (manuscript/rewrite_2026-11/journal/corpus/bulley2015/, byte-identical to sources/bulley2015ethics/oa0.pdf). Locators give the manuscript's printed page (AM p.) and, in brackets, the PDF page used in the notes above (AM p. = PDF p. minus 1).*

- **Power after the threshold (question a).** The distinctions hospitality draws "also need to be policed, managed and controlled, even after the threshold of the home is crossed" (AM p. 2 [PDF p. 3]). Hospitality works to "delimit space, tame it as a sphere of coexistence, restrict the multiplicity, regulating, filtering and channelling the trajectories and contacts that it allows; preventing the 'bad' trajectories, enabling the 'good', while missing and turning a blind eye to others" (AM p. 5 [PDF p. 6]). Expulsion: once inside, host and guest "will perhaps seek in various ways to assert their sovereignty, but neither can successfully do so without ending the hospitable relation and casting the other out" (AM p. 10 [PDF p. 11]). In the camp, assessment serves "the management of their behaviour once inside" (AM p. 14 [PDF p. 15]), and refugees in Dadaab were "regularly subjected to headcounts, involving being rounded up into fenced lots" (AM p. 15 [PDF p. 16]).
- **The guest's means of contest (question a).** Bulley names resistance, not a recognised route: management "can never achieve total control and always produce resistance and counter-conducts", such as "boycotting or diverting food rations" and "tearing down the fencing enclosures used for headcounts" (AM p. 15 [PDF p. 16]). The one institutional route he mentions is consultation, and it excludes: consultations between refugee representatives and international staff "are rarely if ever attended by women" (AM p. 16 [PDF p. 17]). His conclusion calls the micro-politics of hospitality "a mode of critique which displaced 'guests' are already conducting", quoting Foucault on "how not to be governed like that" (AM p. 18 [PDF p. 19]).
- **The normative wager (questions a, c).** "my wager is that 'better' forms would not only seek a more open welcome to the other, but also seeks to keep that welcome open once the threshold is passed. Such a negotiation between host and guest would perhaps allow the flourishing of other forms of life, association, community and home-making, without seeking to control every aspect of the other's behaviour" (AM p. 18 [PDF p. 19]). Bulley says any account of the 'better' "must remain tentative" (AM p. 18 [PDF p. 19]).
- **Several hosts (question b).** Agencies "perform the role of an assembled host while being themselves guests in a space to which they do not formally 'belong'" (AM p. 13 [PDF p. 14]). The assembled hosts "are separated in compounds, reducing or annihilating the space where hosts and guests can meet or mix", after Hyndman (AM p. 15 [PDF p. 16]). This is the nearest statement in the journal's literature I read to the keystone's problem: an assembled host the guest cannot reach.
- **Derrida (question e).** Bulley quotes no sentence from *Of Hospitality* pp. 25-27, 53-59 or 75-83. He paraphrases pp. 77-91 for the divide between the law and the laws and says the conditional laws "will always be contraventions, annulments of the Law"; the clause the red team cites, that what saves the laws "from being merely laws (as opposed to laws of hospitality) is that they keep at least the thought, the reference point, of the unconditional", is Bulley's sentence and is referenced to "Derrida 2003: 129", the Borradori dialogue, not to *Of Hospitality* (AM p. 4 [PDF p. 5]). From *Of Hospitality* he quotes only p. 149 (AM p. 4 [PDF p. 5]). His long block quotation on mastery is from 'Hostipitality' (Derrida 2000: 14; AM p. 9 [PDF p. 10]).
- **What the card's 'owes' note now reads against.** Bulley gives no host obligation in the commercial sense, as the note above says. What he gives the keystone is a hospitality-internal case that the host's power after the threshold is the problem, that hosts whom guests cannot meet are part of it, and that better hospitality keeps the welcome open and negotiable after entry. All of it is set in refugee camps and framed as international ethics. None of it is a channel kept by a host for correction, and the guest's means it records are counter-conduct and consultation, not complaint.
