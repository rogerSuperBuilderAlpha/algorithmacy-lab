## Summary

Gelbrich and Roschk pool the complaint-handling literature from 1980 to June 2009 (87 studies, 95 samples, N = 28,826) and fit one path model to the pooled correlations: organizational responses (compensation, favourable employee behaviour, organizational procedures) shape three justice perceptions (distributive, interactional, procedural), which shape satisfaction with the complaint's handling and with the firm, which shape loyalty and positive word of mouth. The model fits. Each response drives its matching kind of justice most strongly. Overall satisfaction with the firm rests slightly more on fair treatment than on a fair outcome, and fair treatment counts for more in service than in goods settings. The study concerns complainants only; it does not ask who handled the complaint or what that person could decide.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Sample: the search "generated 142 empirical articles", of which 55 lacked usable statistics; "The 87 studies contained 95 independent samples with a total N of 28,826. A total of 477 effects were obtained with an average total N per relationship of 3,667." (p. 28) [full-text]
- Method: reliability-corrected, sample-weighted correlations, then a structural model in AMOS with the median sample size (N = 241) as input (p. 29); fit chi-square(22) = 29.670, p = .127, AGFI = .942, CFI = .995, RMSEA = .038 (p. 31; Figure 3, p. 32). "As there is no empirical evidence for correlations among the organizational responses, correlations of .0 were assumed between them." (p. 29) [full-text]
- What counts as each response. Favourable employee behaviour: "Interpersonal communication of the employee with the complainant, which is characterized by listening carefully to the complainant, displaying regret for any inconvenience, and helping the complainant to understand why a failure occurred." Organizational procedures: "Policies, procedures, and structures a company has in place to provide a smooth complaint-handling process." (Figure 2, p. 26). Apology is coded as compensation: "we consider apology as a form of compensation" (p. 26) [full-text]
- Main finding: compensation is "the most powerful determinant of distributive justice" (gamma = .53), favourable employee behaviour "the most powerful determinant of interactional justice" (gamma = .59), and organizational procedures "the most powerful determinant of procedural justice" (gamma = .51) (p. 36; nested chi-square tests in Table 4, p. 33). The extracted text renders gamma as 'g' and '=' as a fraction sign. [full-text]
- Indirect effects: "Compensation (.35) has a larger influence on transaction-specific satisfaction than favorable employee behavior (.20) and organizational procedures (.26)." On cumulative satisfaction (.38 vs. .34 vs. .36) and loyalty (.31 vs. .25 vs. .28) the three responses are "more balanced" (Table 3 and text, p. 32) [full-text]
- Satisfaction with the firm overall: interactional justice (beta = .41) "even exerts a slightly stronger effect than distributive justice" (beta = .31), procedural justice weaker (beta = .19); "This means that the overall satisfaction with a provider to date also depends on friendly and polite employee behavior when handling a complaint rather than mainly on the response outcome." (p. 36) [full-text]
- Services: interactional justice "has a significant larger impact in service industries" (beta = .46) than in non-service industries (beta = .25) on cumulative satisfaction (p. 34; Table 6, p. 35). Of the 37 samples usable for moderator analysis, "There were 28 service settings, 4 non-service settings (i.e., consumer goods), and 5 mixed settings" (p. 34) [full-text]
- The nearest the paper comes to who handles a complaint: "The effect of procedural justice is negligible because most companies will not offer the consumer a deep insight into how complaints are handled internally. Hence, the complainant can only infer the fairness of procedures from obvious front-office actions, which only provide little insight into internal procedures." (p. 36) [full-text]
- A managerial recommendation, not a tested finding: "organizational procedures should, for instance, not prescribe a certain compensation form but allow employees to ask complainants about their expectations concerning an adequate problem solution." (p. 39); and "It is the customer, not the company, who decides what is fair." (p. 39) [full-text]
- On complaints that never reach the firm, both points taken from Blodgett and Anderson (2000): positive word of mouth "does not occur when customers do not complain and, subsequently, do not initiate failure reparation"; "Post-failure negative WOM, on the contrary, may arise prior to—or in lieu of—a complaint as well as subsequent to ineffective recovery efforts after a complaint" (p. 28) [full-text]
- A second-hand figure, not the authors' finding: "In practice, only 5% of complainants report that they had received an apology (Customer Care Alliance 2003)." (p. 39) [full-text]
- Limits the authors state: experiments in the moderator analysis are vignettes ("all experiments included in the moderator analysis are scenario based", p. 37); "Table 1 also shows that the numbers of observations between organizational responses and justice dimensions are relatively low." (p. 29); moderators left unexamined include "online versus offline complaint handling (Holloway and Beatty 2003)" (pp. 39-40) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Population.** Every effect comes from customers who complained. The paper says nothing about how many dissatisfied customers complain or how a failure first reaches the firm. Its only remark on that, borrowed from Blodgett and Anderson, is that negative word of mouth can replace a complaint (p. 28).
- **No authority variable.** 'Favourable employee behaviour' is the employee's interpersonal manner (Figure 2, p. 26), not the power to decide. Compensation must be granted by someone, but the meta-analysis does not code who, or whether the person who heard the complaint could grant it. The one passage on frontline latitude is a recommendation (p. 39).
- **Not hospitality as such.** Industry enters only as service, non-service or mixed (p. 34). The list of included studies is "available on request" (p. 28). Some hotel studies appear in the reference list, but the paper reports no hotel subgroup.
- **Model choices.** Zero correlations among the three responses were assumed (p. 29). Observations linking responses to justice are few (p. 29; Table 1, p. 30). Manipulated justice conditions in experiments were recoded as organizational responses (p. 29).
- **Dating and access.** Online December 2010, issue 2011; cite as 2011. A verifier who re-fetches will meet the same bot checks on the live repository and on CORE; the Internet Archive capture is the open route.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7, P4, first half (the guest's report as the timely signal): narrows, slightly.** The meta-analysis studies only complainants. Its one relevant remark is that negative word of mouth can arise "in lieu of" a complaint (p. 28). A failure the guest does not report may never reach the firm.

**K7, P4, second half (correction needs a person with power over the single case): consistent, not shown.** Recovery outcomes run through a remedy the firm grants (compensation, gamma = .53 on distributive justice) and an employee's manner (gamma = .59 on interactional justice), and the employee's manner weighs more in services (pp. 32, 34, 36). The study does not test whether the remedy depends on the authority of the person the complainant meets.

**K9, empirical side: no bearing.** Automated or self-service complaint handling was not studied; online versus offline handling is listed as unexamined (p. 40).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- Across 87 studies of complaint handling (95 samples, N = 28,826), Gelbrich and Roschk (2011: 28, 36) found that compensation chiefly shaped complainants' sense of a fair outcome, the employee's manner their sense of fair treatment, and complaint procedures their sense of a fair process.
- Complainants' satisfaction with the firm as a whole rested slightly more on how they were treated than on what they received (Gelbrich and Roschk 2011: 36), and treatment weighed more in service than in goods settings (34).
- Gelbrich and Roschk (2011: 39) advise that complaint procedures let employees ask complainants what remedy they expect instead of prescribing one.

**What it may not say**

- That a person with authority over the case is needed, or that frontline authority was measured. Neither is in the study.
- That the findings are about hotel guests. The moderator is service versus non-service (p. 34).
- Anything about how many dissatisfied guests complain, or about automated complaint handling (pp. 28, 40).
- The 5% apology figure as Gelbrich and Roschk's own; it is the Customer Care Alliance's (p. 39).
