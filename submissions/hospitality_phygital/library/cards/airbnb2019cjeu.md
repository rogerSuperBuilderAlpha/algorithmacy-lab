## Summary

A French association of hotels and professional tourism businesses (AHTOP) lodged a criminal complaint that Airbnb Ireland was acting as an estate agent without the licence French law (the Hoguet Law) requires. The investigating judge in Paris asked the Court of Justice whether Airbnb's service enjoys the freedom to provide information society services under Directive 2000/31 and whether the Hoguet Law could be enforced against it. The Grand Chamber answered that the service is an 'information society service'. It is a service of intermediation distinct from the accommodation it leads to, and it is not an integral part of an overall service whose main component is the provision of accommodation. The Court gave four reasons: the structured list of offers with search and comparison tools is the essential feature of the platform and is no mere accessory to accommodation; the platform is not indispensable to the provision of accommodation; Airbnb does not set or cap the rent; and its other services (listing format, photography, ratings, payment handling, damage guarantee, optional insurance) are ancillary. It contrasted Uber, which exercised decisive influence over the conditions of the transport service. On the second question the Court held that France could not enforce the Hoguet Law against Airbnb Ireland because it had not notified the measure under art. 3(4)(b) of the Directive. EU law; the reference came from a French criminal court.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**Court, parties, what was at stake**

- Grand Chamber, 19 December 2019, Case C-390/18; composed of "K. Lenaerts, President, R. Silva de Lapuerta, Vice-President, A. Arabadjiev, E. Regan, P.G. Xuereb and L.S. Rossi, Presidents of Chambers, E. Juhász, M. Ilešič, J. Malenovský, D. Šváby (Rapporteur) and N. Piçarra, Judges"; Advocate General M. Szpunar, whose Opinion was delivered on 30 April 2019 (preamble). The Court gives a single judgment; it records no vote and no separate opinion. [full-text]
- The proceedings were criminal: the request "has been made in criminal proceedings against X, inter alia, for handling monies for activities concerning the mediation and management of buildings and businesses by a person without a professional licence" (para 2). [full-text]
- Who complained: "the Association pour un hébergement et un tourisme professionnels (Association for professional tourism and accommodation, AHTOP) lodged a complaint together with an application to be joined as a civil party to the proceedings" (para 22). No guest and no host was a party; the interveners listed are YA, Airbnb Ireland UC, Hôtelière Turenne SAS, AHTOP and Valhotel (preamble). [full-text]
- AHTOP's case: "Airbnb Ireland does not merely connect two parties through its platform; it also offers additional services which amount to an intermediary activity in property transactions" (para 23). [full-text]

**What the platform did, as the Court found it**

- "Airbnb Ireland offers an electronic platform the purpose of which is, on payment of a commission, to establish contact, in particular in France, between, on the one hand, hosts, whether professionals or private individuals, with accommodation to rent and, on the other, people looking for such accommodation." (para 18). [full-text]
- Other services to hosts: "a format for setting out the content of their offer, with an option for a photography service, and also with an option for civil liability insurance and a guarantee against damages for up to EUR 800000", and "an optional tool for estimating the rental price having regard to the market averages taken from that platform" (para 19). [full-text]
- The host admits the guest and the platform holds the money: "if a host accepts a guest, the guest will transfer to Airbnb Payments UK the rental price to which is added 6% to 12% of that amount in respect of charges and the service provided by Airbnb Ireland. Airbnb Payments UK holds the money on behalf of the guest and then, 24 hours after the guest checks in, sends the money to the host by transfer, thus giving the guest assurance that the property exists and the host a guarantee of payment." (para 19). [full-text]
- Ratings: "Airbnb Ireland has put in place a system whereby the host and the guest can leave an evaluation on a scale of zero to five stars" (para 19). [full-text]
- The contracts: "users of the electronic platform at issue, both hosts and guests, conclude a contract with Airbnb Ireland for the use of that platform and with Airbnb Payments UK for the payments made via that platform" (para 21). [full-text]

**The test**

- The rule and its exception: "although an intermediation service which satisfies all of those conditions, in principle, constitutes a service distinct from the subsequent service to which it relates and must therefore be classified as an ‘information society service’, that cannot be the case if it appears that that intermediation service forms an integral part of an overall service whose main component is a service coming under another legal classification" (para 50, citing Asociación Profesional Elite Taxi, C-434/15, para 40). [full-text]
- The second limb, control: whether the platform "exercises such a decisive influence over the conditions for the provision of the accommodation services to which its intermediation service relates" (para 68). [full-text]

**The holding on the facts, and the facts that mattered**

- The list is the service: "It is the creation of such a list for the benefit both of the hosts who have accommodation to rent and persons looking for that type of accommodation which constitutes the essential feature of the electronic platform managed by Airbnb Ireland." (para 53). [full-text]
- Not ancillary to accommodation: "the compiling of offers using a harmonised format, coupled with tools for searching for, locating and comparing those offers, constitutes a service which cannot be regarded as merely ancillary to an overall service coming under a different legal classification, namely provision of an accommodation service" (para 54). [full-text]
- Not indispensable: the service "is in no way indispensable to the provision of accommodation services, both from the point of view of the guests and the hosts who use it, since both have a number of other, sometimes long-standing, channels at their disposal, such as estate agents, classified advertisements, whether in paper or electronic format, or even property lettings websites" (para 55). [full-text]
- Price: "it is not apparent, either from the order for reference or from the information in the file before the Court, that Airbnb Ireland sets or caps the amount of the rents charged by the hosts using that platform. At most, it provides them with an optional tool for estimating their rental price having regard to the market averages taken from that platform, leaving responsibility for setting the rent to the host alone." (para 56). [full-text]
- The conclusion: "an intermediation service such as the one provided by Airbnb Ireland cannot be regarded as forming an integral part of an overall service, the main component of which is the provision of accommodation" (para 57). [full-text]
- The other services are ancillary: "for the hosts, they do not constitute an end in themselves, but rather a means of benefiting from the intermediation service provided by Airbnb Ireland or of offering accommodation services in the best conditions" (para 58). [full-text]
- Ratings and listing tools "form part of the collaborative model inherent in intermediation platforms, which makes it possible, first, for those seeking accommodation to make a fully informed choice from among the accommodation offerings proposed by the hosts on the platform and, secondly, for hosts to be fully informed of the reliability of the guests with whom they might engage" (para 60). [full-text]
- Payment handling: "Such payment conditions, which are common to a large number of electronic platforms, are a tool for securing transactions between hosts and guests, and their presence alone cannot modify the nature of the intermediation service, especially where such payment conditions are not accompanied, directly or indirectly, by price controls for the provision of accommodation" (para 62). [full-text]
- Guarantee and insurance: "nor is the fact that Airbnb Ireland offers hosts a guarantee against damage and, as an option, civil liability insurance capable of modifying the legal classification of the intermediation service" (para 63). [full-text]
- The contrast with Uber: the ancillary services "do not provide evidence for the same level of control found by the Court in those judgments" (para 66); in those judgments "Uber exercised decisive influence over the conditions under which transport services were provided by the non-professional drivers" (para 67). [full-text]
- Why Airbnb is different: the matters before the Court "do not establish that Airbnb Ireland exercises such a decisive influence over the conditions for the provision of the accommodation services to which its intermediation service relates, particularly since Airbnb Ireland does not determine, directly or indirectly, the rental price charged, as was established in paragraphs 56 and 62 above, still less does it select the hosts or the accommodation put up for rent on its platform" (para 68). [full-text]
- The answer: such a service "must be classified as an ‘information society service’ under Directive 2000/31" (para 69; operative part, point 1). [full-text]

**The second question**

- France had not notified the Hoguet Law, so "that law cannot, on any view, be applied to an individual in a situation like that of Airbnb Ireland in the main proceedings" (para 99); the failure to notify may be relied on "not only in criminal proceedings brought against that individual, but also in the claim for damages brought by the individual who has been joined as a civil party" (para 98; operative part, point 2). [full-text]

**What the judgment does not contain**

- It says nothing about what a platform owes a guest, about liability for injury or loss during a stay, or about consumer law. The only liability discussed is the criminal liability of an unlicensed intermediary under the Hoguet Law and the ancillary civil claim of a trade association (paras 2, 22-24, 98). [full-text]
- The Court took the facts from the order for reference and from "the explanations provided by Airbnb Ireland" (para 20); it noted that the referring court had defined the context "briefly but precisely" (para 33) and that the Commission thought the order "vitiated by a lack of factual details" (para 32). [full-text]

**Later treatment**

- Later treatment not checked, except that Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, recital 6, read for card eu2022dsa, refers to "situations where the intermediary service constitutes an integral part of another service which is not an intermediary service as recognised in the case-law of the Court of Justice of the European Union" without naming a case. [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A classification, not an allocation of responsibility.** The question was which internal-market regime governs the platform. The Court's answer keeps Airbnb inside Directive 2000/31, the instrument that also carried the hosting exemption then in force (now in Regulation 2022/2065, art. 6; see card eu2022dsa). The judgment does not hold that Airbnb owes guests nothing. It holds that Airbnb is not the provider of the accommodation for the purpose of market-access rules.
- **The facts are from 2012 to 2017 and came from the platform.** The complaint covered "11 April 2012 and 24 January 2017" (para 22). The Court's finding on price is framed as what "is not apparent" from the file (para 56). A platform that later set or capped prices, or chose which hosts or properties to list, would fall to be tested again on the Court's own criteria.
- **The criteria are the paper's control ground under another name.** Who sets the price, who selects the provider and whether the tool is indispensable are the three things the Court weighed across this case and Uber. They match K8's 'the one that controls', applied to conditions of supply. They do not test what the guest was led to believe; that test arrives with art. 6(3) of the Digital Services Act.
- **The host admits.** On the facts found, the guest's payment follows "if a host accepts a guest" (para 19), and ratings serve "for hosts to be fully informed of the reliability of the guests with whom they might engage" (para 60). Admission, on this record, is the host's decision, informed by the platform's data.
- **The platform does hold out something.** Holding the guest's money until 24 hours after check-in gives "the guest assurance that the property exists" (para 19). The Court treated that as a tool for securing the transaction (para 62) and not as taking over the service.
- The Advocate General's Opinion is cited in the judgment at paras 64 and 87. I did not read it and attribute nothing to it.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8 (holder under delegation): narrows; refutes 'the platform is a host' as a default.** K8 names "tests of 'decisive influence' for platforms" as confirming material and "platform immunities" as refuting material. This judgment is both. It confirms that EU law has a test that can make a platform part of the underlying service, and it applies that test to an accommodation platform and finds it not met. On the Court's reasoning the host on the platform stays the provider of the accommodation: the host sets the rent (para 56), accepts the guest (para 19) and is the one whose offer the guest chooses (para 60). The platform is a separate service with its own contract with the guest (para 21).

**K3 (grounds of the duty): input to the table of ground by hard case.** For the *booking platform*, the control test (price, selection of providers) is not met on these facts; dependence is not the Court's concern, though it does hold the platform "in no way indispensable" (para 55); reliance and holding out are not tested at all. For the *selective rental host*, the judgment records that the host decides whether to accept a guest (para 19).

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- The Court of Justice has held that Airbnb's intermediation service is an information society service and is not an integral part of an overall service whose main component is the provision of accommodation (paras 57, 69).
- The Court reached that view because Airbnb did not set or cap the rents, which it left "to the host alone" (para 56), and did not select the hosts or the accommodation offered (para 68).
- The Court treated the platform's listing format, photography, ratings, payment handling, damage guarantee and optional insurance as ancillary to intermediation (paras 58-64).
- The Court contrasted Uber, which it had found to exercise decisive influence over the conditions under which drivers provided transport (paras 65-68).
- On the facts before the Court, hosts and guests each contract with the platform for its use (para 21), the host accepts the guest (para 19) and the host sets the rent (para 56).
- The case arose from a complaint by an association of hotel and tourism businesses that Airbnb was acting as an unlicensed estate agent (paras 22-24).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the Court held Airbnb not liable to guests, or held anything about liability to guests. No guest was a party and the question was not asked.
- That a booking platform can never be the provider of accommodation in EU law. The Court applied a test to facts; a platform that fixes prices or selects providers is not covered by this holding.
- That the judgment concerns hotels listed on online travel agencies. It concerns short-term rentals offered by "professional or non-professional hosts" (para 39).
- That the Court found Airbnb to be a mere passive conduit. It found a service with an "essential feature" of its own, the structured list with search and comparison tools (paras 53-54).
- Anything on the Advocate General's reasoning. The Opinion was not read.
- That the judgment is the present law on platform liability. The liability rules are now in Regulation (EU) 2022/2065.
