## Summary

Eldridge's Social Security disability benefits were ended after a state agency reviewed his file, told him its tentative finding with reasons, and considered his written reply. He could have asked for reconsideration and then for an evidentiary hearing, with back payment if he won, but sued instead, claiming that the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause entitled him to an evidentiary hearing before the payments stopped. The lower courts agreed. The Supreme Court reversed, six to two. Powell J held that what process is due, and when, depends on three factors: the private interest affected, the risk of error under the procedures used and the value of further safeguards, and the government's interest including cost. Applying them, the Court held that "an evidentiary hearing is not required prior to the termination of disability benefits" (at 349). On the way it stated that a claim to a hearing before deprivation "rests on the proposition that full relief cannot be obtained at a postdeprivation hearing" (at 331), and that only in *Goldberg v. Kelly*, where welfare payments were the recipient's means of subsistence, had it required an evidentiary hearing before a temporary deprivation (at 340). Brennan J dissented. United States federal constitutional law; the defendant was a federal official.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

**The question and the parties**

- The issue: "The issue in this case is whether the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that prior to the termination of Social Security disability benefit payments the recipient be afforded an opportunity for an evidentiary hearing." (at 323) [full-text]
- The defendant was the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare; the claimant a benefit recipient. The procedure challenged was the Secretary's own (at 324-25). [full-text]
- What Eldridge got before the payments stopped: the agency's letter "included a statement of reasons for the proposed termination of benefits, and advised Eldridge that he might request reasonable time in which to obtain and submit additional information pertaining to his condition." (at 324) He replied in writing; the agency then made its final determination (at 324). [full-text]
- What he could have had afterwards: reconsideration, then "a right to an evidentiary hearing before an SSA administrative law judge" (at 339), then review; and "Should it be determined at any point after termination of benefits, that the claimant’s disability extended beyond the date of cessation initially established, the worker is entitled to retroactive payments." (at 339) [full-text]
- What 'evidentiary hearing' means in the opinion: "a hearing generally of the type required in Goldberg", with notice, oral presentation and confrontation of witnesses, counsel, an impartial decisionmaker, a decision on the record and a statement of reasons (at 325 n. 4). [full-text]
- Result: "We reverse." (at 326) [full-text]

**The principle about timing**

- "A claim to a predeprivation hearing as a matter of constitutional right rests on the proposition that full relief cannot be obtained at a postdeprivation hearing." (at 331) The sentence appears in Part II, where the Court decides that it has jurisdiction although Eldridge had not exhausted the agency's procedures. [full-text]
- Why his claim was at least arguable: "an erroneous termination would damage him in a way not recompensable through retroactive payments." (at 331) [full-text]
- The same idea as a rule of construction: finality requirements "should, if possible, be construed so as not to cause crucial collateral claims to be lost and potentially irreparable injuries to be suffered" (at 331 n. 11). [full-text]
- "This Court consistently has held that some form of hearing is required before an individual is finally deprived of a property interest." (at 333) [full-text]
- "The fundamental requirement of due process is the opportunity to be heard “at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner.”" (at 333, quoting *Armstrong v. Manzo*) [full-text]
- What was in dispute: "The dispute centers upon what process is due prior to the initial termination of benefits, pending review." (at 333) [full-text]

**The three-factor test, in the Court's words**

- "identification of the specific dictates of due process generally requires consideration of three distinct factors: First, the private interest that will be affected by the official action; second, the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards; and finally, the Government’s interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedural requirement would entail." (at 335; the sentence begins on 334) [full-text]
- The premise: "“[D]ue process is flexible and calls for such procedural protections as the particular situation demands.”" (at 334, quoting *Morrissey v. Brewer*) [full-text]

**How the Court applied it: a later hearing was enough**

- "In light of the private and governmental interests at stake here and the nature of the existing procedures, we think this was error." (at 340, of the lower courts' holding that a prior evidentiary hearing was required) [full-text]
- The private interest was interim only: "Since a recipient whose benefits are terminated is awarded full retroactive relief if he ultimately prevails, his sole interest is in the uninterrupted receipt of this source of income pending final administrative decision on his claim." (at 340) [full-text]
- The exception: "Only in Goldberg has the Court held that due process requires an evidentiary hearing prior to a temporary deprivation." (at 340) The reason there, quoted from *Goldberg*: termination "may deprive an eligible recipient of the very means by which to live while he waits." (at 340) [full-text]
- Degree and length of loss count: "the degree of potential deprivation that may be created by a particular decision is a factor to be considered in assessing the validity of any administrative decisionmaking process." (at 341) The Court accepted that "the delay between the actual cutoff of benefits and final decision after a hearing exceeds one year" (at 342) and that "the hardship imposed upon the erroneously terminated disability recipient may be significant." (at 342) [full-text]
- The ordinary rule: "there is less reason here than in Goldberg to depart from the ordinary principle, established by our decisions, that something less than an evidentiary hearing is sufficient prior to adverse administrative action." (at 343) [full-text]
- Rules are made for the usual case: "procedural due process rules are shaped by the risk of error inherent in the truth-finding process as applied to the generality of cases, not the rare exceptions." (at 344) [full-text]
- The prior process that did exist carried weight: before the cut-off the agency gives its tentative assessment and reasons, and "Opportunity is then afforded the recipient to submit additional evidence or arguments, enabling him to challenge directly the accuracy of information in his file as well as the correctness of the agency’s tentative conclusions." (at 346) [full-text]
- Cost: "Financial cost alone is not a controlling weight in determining whether due process requires a particular procedural safeguard prior to some administrative decision." But: "At some point the benefit of an additional safeguard to the individual affected by the administrative action and to society in terms of increased assurance that the action is just, may be outweighed by the cost." (at 348) [full-text]
- What due process requires at the least: "The essence of due process is the requirement that “a person in jeopardy of serious loss [be given] notice of the case against him and opportunity to meet it.”" (at 348, quoting Frankfurter J) And: "The judicial model of an evidentiary hearing is neither a required, nor even the most effective, method of decisionmaking in all circumstances." (at 348) [full-text]
- Fit to the person heard: "All that is necessary is that the procedures be tailored, in light of the decision to be made, to “the capacities and circumstances of those who are to be heard,”" so that they have "a meaningful opportunity to present their case." (at 349) [full-text]
- The holding: "We conclude that an evidentiary hearing is not required prior to the termination of disability benefits and that the present administrative procedures fully comport with due process." (at 349) [full-text]

**Against whom the doctrine runs**

- "Procedural due process imposes constraints on governmental decisions which deprive individuals of “liberty” or “property” interests within the meaning of the Due Process Clause of the Fifth or Fourteenth Amendment." (at 332) [full-text]
- The interest protected was "a statutorily created “property” interest protected by the Fifth Amendment." (at 332) [full-text]
- The test's own terms assume a public decision-maker: "the official action" and "the Government’s interest" (at 335); "adverse administrative action" (at 343). [full-text]
- The one private dispute the opinion mentions is *Fuentes v. Shevin*, described as "a replevin suit between two private parties" in which "the initial determination required something more than an ex parte proceeding before a court clerk." (at 334) The constraint there fell on the court's procedure. [full-text]
- The opinion does not mention inns, hotels, carriers or any private provider of services (whole text read). [full-text]

**The dissent**

- Brennan J, with Marshall J: the Court's view "that a discontinuance of disability benefits may cause the recipient to suffer only a limited deprivation is no argument. It is speculative." (at 350) [full-text]
- "because disability benefits were terminated there was a foreclosure upon the Eldridge home and the family’s furniture was repossessed, forcing Eldridge, his wife, and their children to sleep in one bed." (at 350) [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **The holding runs against a prior hearing.** The case is cited for its test. On its facts the Court refused the earlier hearing, though it accepted that the wait exceeded a year and the hardship "may be significant" (at 342). A reader who cites *Mathews* for the proposition that a person must be heard before a loss falls has cited the losing argument.
- **What was refused was an oral, trial-type hearing, not all prior process.** Eldridge had notice, reasons, access to the file through a representative and a chance to reply in writing before the payments stopped (at 324, 337-38, 346). The Court relied on that prior process in finding the scheme adequate (at 346, 349). So the case does not hold that nothing is due beforehand; it holds that "something less than an evidentiary hearing" is ordinarily enough beforehand (at 343).
- **The sentence at 331 is in the jurisdictional part.** It explains why Eldridge could sue without exhausting the agency's remedies. It states the logic of a claim to a prior hearing and is not the holding on the merits.
- **The doctrine binds government.** Due process "imposes constraints on governmental decisions" (at 332). A hotel is not a government, and the third factor, the government's interest in public funds, has no counterpart that the opinion supplies for a private firm. The carry-over to a private host is by analogy of reasoning only: the form of the argument (later repair cannot make the person whole, therefore earlier process) is the form of K7's timing line.
- **The analogy also carries the test's other two factors.** If the paper borrows the first factor (what the person stands to lose in the meantime) it should expect to be asked about the second and third: how often the decision is wrong and what a person at the threshold would add, and what it costs. The Court treated cost as a proper weight (at 348).
- **Entitlement, not service.** The interest was a statutory benefit already being paid. The case says nothing about a first refusal of a service to someone with no existing entitlement.

- Later treatment not checked. *Goldberg v. Kelly* is known to this card only as *Mathews* describes and quotes it.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K7's timing line: supports the principle, narrows its reach, refutes any use as authority against a private host.** K7 says the boundary between a channel now and repair later "follows from whether performance is still possible". *Mathews* states the same logic for hearings: the claim to be heard first "rests on the proposition that full relief cannot be obtained" later (at 331), and the weight of the interim loss is the first thing a court asks about (at 335, 340-42). That is support for the shape of the argument from a leading constitutional authority. The same case is on K7's 'refutes or narrows' list ("Remedies doctrine that is satisfied by damages afterwards"): the Court held that later relief with back payment was enough, that the prior evidentiary hearing of *Goldberg* was the single exception, and that rules are set for "the generality of cases, not the rare exceptions" (at 340, 343, 344). And the doctrine is owed by the state.

One point helps K7 more than the bare holding suggests. What the Court found adequate included a channel before the effect: notice of the proposed decision with reasons, and a chance to answer it (at 324, 346). The dispute was about how formal that channel had to be.

**What the paper may say on this source's authority**

- In United States constitutional law, a claim to a hearing before a deprivation "rests on the proposition that full relief cannot be obtained at a postdeprivation hearing" (*Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. at 331).
- The Supreme Court fixes what process is due, and when, by weighing the private interest affected, the risk of error and the value of further safeguards, and the government's interest including cost (at 335).
- The Court requires the opportunity to be heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner" (at 333).
- Applying that test, the Court held that a recipient of disability benefits was not entitled to an evidentiary hearing before the benefits ended, on a weighing of all three factors (at 340-349), among them that full retroactive relief was available afterwards and that he had been able to answer the agency's proposed decision in writing (at 340, 346, 349).
- The Court said that only in *Goldberg v. Kelly*, where the payments were the recipient's means of subsistence, had it required an evidentiary hearing before a temporary deprivation (at 340).
- The doctrine constrains "governmental decisions" (at 332).

**What the paper may not say**

- That *Mathews* held a person must be heard before an adverse decision takes effect. It held the opposite on its facts (at 349).
- That due process binds a hotel or any private host. The opinion applies it to government (at 332).
- That the law generally prefers earlier process to later repair. The Court called the sufficiency of something less than an evidentiary hearing beforehand "the ordinary principle" (at 343).
- That the case concerns consumers, contracts or services. It concerns a statutory benefit.
- That the three factors are a test of when a firm must provide a person. They are the Court's test of what the Constitution requires of official action.
- Anything about *Goldberg v. Kelly* beyond what *Mathews* says of it, on this card's authority.
