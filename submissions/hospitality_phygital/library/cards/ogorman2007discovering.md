## Summary

A short illustrated article for a practitioner journal. O'Gorman argues that Roman commercial hospitality was "complex and sophisticated" and that literary sources alone mislead: archaeology, chiefly Pompeii, shows how the businesses worked. He opens with the Roman hospitality ethic in private and public guest-friendship (welcome, the duty to receive the *hospes*, duties of protection and of representing the guest in court, the *ius hospitii* under Jupiter), then turns to Roman cuisine, to the four standard categories of commercial establishment (*hospitia*, *stabulae*, *tabernae*, *popinae*, with *cauponae* and the *lupanar*), to the literary prejudice that women working in inns were prostitutes, and to the hospitality cluster in central Pompeii. He closes by calling for interdisciplinary research. Duties appear only in the opening section on guest-friendship. The commercial sections describe buildings, clienteles, reputations and commercial motives.

## Critical notes & threats

- **A practitioner piece that repeats O'Gorman (2005).** The Roman guest-friendship passage on p. 45 closely follows the wording of `ogorman2005modern` (p. 145), where it rests on Schmitz (1875); here the duties-of-protection sentence has no note at all. The sentence on who used the inns (p. 47) repeats 2005's p. 146 without its clause on "bedbugs, discomfort, violence and danger". Cite the 2005 article, which the library already holds, for the guest-friendship duties; this article adds the commercial typology and Pompeii.
- **It does not carry the counter-history either.** The red team suggests that O'Gorman's history fits a reading in which commercial lodging is where hospitality's protections lapsed and law stepped in. This article supports the first half only by implication: commercial lodging served those "too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality" (p. 47) and those travelling "where they were not known" (p. 47). It says nothing of law stepping in for guests. The law it reports protects serving women (p. 48). The 'law stepped in' half rests on the Digest (`digest4_9`, D. 4.9.1.1, 4.9.3.1), not on O'Gorman.
- **Expectations, not duties.** The one sentence that spans all antiquity names "expectations" (p. 44), which are a guest's hopes, not a host's obligations, and does not say they applied to inns.
- **Workers.** The only persons the law protects in the commercial sections are serving girls (p. 48), and the article's last word on Pompeii is that it needed "the best hospitality employees" for its reputation (p. 50). That bears on S7, not on the guest.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K1 for the commercial host (Step 1, warrant 1 and Narrowing): rival by silence.** The field's own history of Roman commercial hospitality names duties to receive, protect and represent only for the *hospes* of guest-friendship (p. 45) and none for the keeper of a *hospitium* or *stabula* (pp. 46-50). The dossier may not cite O'Gorman (2007) for a commercial host's responsibility for the guest's safety. The article fits the critical scholar's S3 reading that commercial lodging served those outside hospitality's networks (p. 47), but it does not show law taking over hospitality's protections.

**S3 (custom or prescription): supports the critic's narrowing.** If Step 1 needs a commercial host's duty in the customary register, this article does not provide it. It describes a trade, its buildings and its reputation.

**What the paper may say**

- O'Gorman describes Roman commercial hospitality as "complex and sophisticated" (p. 44), with four standard kinds of establishment (p. 46), serving "travellers, merchants and sailors" (p. 44).
- He reports that roadside and gate-side accommodation attracted "the lower classes who were too poor or socially insignificant to have developed a network of personal hospitality" (p. 47).
- He records the duties to receive, protect and represent the guest for private Roman hospitality (p. 45), which was "more precisely and legally defined" (p. 45).

**What the paper may not say**

- That O'Gorman (2007) attributes any duty to Roman innkeepers, or any legal protection to their guests.
- That the article shows law stepping in where hospitality's protections lapsed.
- That the duties on p. 45 attach to commercial hosts.
