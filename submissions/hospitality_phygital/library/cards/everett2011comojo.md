## Summary

Two men, guests of a member at the Met Bar in the Metropolitan Hotel, London, were stabbed there by another guest. They sued the company that owned and managed the nightclub, alleging that it had failed to protect them. The trial judge accepted that such a duty could exist but found none on the facts and dismissed the claim. On appeal the company argued that there was no duty at all, or only a narrow one. Smith LJ, with whom Richards and Rix LJJ agreed, applied the threefold *Caparo* test and held that the management of a nightclub owes its guests a duty of care in respect of the actions of third parties on the premises. Proximity came from the management's control of the premises, the guest's reliance on its competence, and the economic relationship between them. The scope of the duty is the flexible common duty of care, and what it requires varies with the establishment. On the facts a waitress who took her worry about a guest to the bar manager and not to a door supervisor had acted reasonably. The appeal was dismissed.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court, date, judges: Court of Appeal (Civil Division), Rix, Smith and Richards LJJ; heard 22 November 2010; handed down 18 January 2011; England and Wales (cover sheet). The only reasoned judgment is Smith LJ's (paras 1-39); Richards LJ, "I agree." (para 40); Rix LJ, "I also agree." (para 41). No dissent. [full-text]
- Who sued whom: the appellants, "who were guests at the nightclub, were injured in a knife attack perpetrated by another guest"; the attacker "was not sued. Instead, the appellants sued the company which manages the night club, alleging that it had failed to take appropriate steps to protect its guests." (para 2). [full-text]
- The place: "The Metropolitan Bar, known as the Met Bar, is part of the Metropolitan Hotel"; entry was for members and their guests and for hotel residents (para 3). The claim was for "damages for personal injury" (para 1). [full-text]
- Security was contracted out: "Security staff (door supervisors) are provided by Darkstorm Trading Ltd (trading as Panther Security)." (para 3). The security company was sued, "obtained summary judgment and were dismissed from the claim" (para 7). The judgment does not say why. [full-text]
- The only allegation on appeal concerned an employee: that a waitress "(for whom the defendant would be vicariously liable) had been negligent in reporting her concerns about Croasdaile to Mr Rosenblatt, the bar manager, rather than reporting directly to one of the door supervisors" (para 9). [full-text]
- The issue of principle: the appeal "raises issues relating to the scope of the duty of the management of a night club in respect of the actions of third parties" (para 2); counsel for the company said "the issue was of some importance for the hotel industry, which needed to know where it stood" (para 21). [full-text]
- The starting point is no general duty: Lord Goff in *Smith v Littlewoods* "began his speech by emphasising that there is no general duty to prevent others from suffering loss or damage caused by the wrongdoing of third parties" (para 28, Smith LJ's summary). [full-text]
- Basis, proximity: "The management is in control of the premises. It can regulate who enters, who is refused entry and who is to be removed after entry. The guest comes to the night club to relax and enjoy himself and for that prospect relies on the competence and prudence of its management. He expects and is entitled to expect that there will be no violence and that he will not be unsafe." (para 31). [full-text]
- Basis, the commercial tie: "Further, the management of the night club is in business and wants the guest to come to spend his money; there is an economic relationship between the two. In my judgment, those factors demonstrate sufficient proximity." (para 31). [full-text]
- Foreseeability: "It must be foreseeable to any licensed hotelier that there is some risk that one guest might assault another." (para 32). [full-text]
- Fair, just and reasonable, by analogy with the occupier's duty: "the relationship between the parties already carries with it an established duty, under the Occupier’s Liability Act 1957, in relation to the condition of the premises" (para 33). [full-text]
- Holding on the duty: "I would conclude that there is a duty on the management of a night club in respect of the actions of third parties on the premises but I stress that the standard of care imposed or the scope of the duty must also be fair, just and reasonable." (para 34). [full-text]
- No special threshold of foreseeability: "The degree of proximity (including the economic relationship) between the two is so close that I do not think any special rule of foreseeability is required in the interests of fairness, justice and reasonableness." (para 35). [full-text]
- Extended in terms to hotels: the common duty of care "should govern the relationship between the managers of an hotel or night club and their guests in relation to the actions of third parties on the premises" (para 36). [full-text]
- Content cannot be fixed in advance: "I do not think it possible to define the circumstances in which there will be liability. Circumstances will vary so widely. However, I think it will be a rare night club that does not need some security arrangements which can be activated as and when the need arises." (para 36). [full-text]
- Examples (dicta): "liability might well attach if a guest is injured in an outbreak of violence among guests and there is no one on hand to control the outbreak"; in a respectable members-only club "The duty on management may be no higher than that staff be trained to look out for any sign of trouble and to alert security staff." (para 36). [full-text]
- No breach: the judge "was also right in his conclusion that, in all the circumstances, Ms Kotze had not been in breach of duty"; "Telling Mr Rosenblatt about the situation and letting him decide what to do seems sensible." (para 37). [full-text]
- Disposition: "For those reasons I would dismiss this appeal." (para 38). Causation was left undecided (para 39). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Holding and dictum.** The existence of the duty was put in issue by the respondent's notice (para 20) and decided (para 34). The claim nonetheless failed on breach (para 37). The extension to "an hotel" (para 36) goes beyond the facts, which concerned a members' bar inside a hotel; it is considered language in a unanimous Court of Appeal judgment, and Dingemans J treated the case as binding for a hotel (alnajar2019cumberland, para 187). The examples of what the duty may require (para 36) are dicta.
- **The basis differs from Al-Najar's.** Smith LJ finds proximity under *Caparo* from control, reliance and the economic relationship (para 31). Dingemans J preferred an assumption of responsibility and recorded counsel's criticism that *Everett* applied *Caparo* in a way inconsistent with later Supreme Court authority (alnajar2019cumberland, paras 186-187). The two judgments agree on the duty and give different reasons for it.
- **Who holds the duty.** The duty is laid on "the management", which "is in control of the premises" (para 31). The defendant both "owned and managed" the nightclub (para 1). The case does not decide which of an owner, an operator and a brand would hold the duty if those were different firms.
- **Contractors.** Door security was supplied by a contractor, which left the action on summary judgment (paras 3, 7). The surviving allegations concerned the company's own system (searching guests) and its own employee (para 9). The court did not decide whether the company would have answered for a door supervisor's negligence.
- **Reliance is general, not proved.** The reliance in para 31 is the reliance any guest places on management. No evidence of these guests' reliance was called for.
- **The judgment gives two dates for the incident:** 9 October 2002 (para 1) and 8 November 2002 (para 4). The claim was issued on 4 November 2005 (para 7).
- **Later treatment.** Followed and described in *Al-Najar* [2019] EWHC 1593 (QB), paras 185-187 (read). The scout reports *Geary v JD Wetherspoon* as endorsing it; I did not read *Geary*. Other later treatment not checked.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (reception tier): supports, for a bar or nightclub, and in terms for a hotel.** The management owes guests a duty of care in respect of third parties' acts on the premises (paras 34, 36). This is the bar case K4 asks for. The judgment does not mention restaurants.
- **K3 (grounds): supports the second and third grounds in a court's words.** Reliance: the guest "relies on the competence and prudence of its management" (para 31). Control of the premises stands where K3 puts dependence. The court adds a ground K3 does not list, the economic relationship (paras 31, 35).
- **K5 (directedness): supports.** The injured guests sued the management in their own names (paras 1-2).
- **K7 (channel): partial support for P2 and P3, from dicta, and a limit.** The content of the duty includes "security arrangements which can be activated as and when the need arises" and someone "on hand to control the outbreak" (para 36). The whole appeal turned on the route by which a staff member's warning reached a person able to act, and the court held the route taken reasonable (para 37). The signal in the case came from staff, not from a guest.
- **K8 (holder): neutral to weak.** The duty sits with whoever manages and controls the premises (para 31). Nothing is decided about contractors.

**What the paper may say**

- The Court of Appeal has held that the management of a nightclub owes its guests a duty of care in respect of the actions of third parties on the premises (para 34), and said that the same duty should govern "the managers of an hotel or night club and their guests" (para 36).
- The court found the relationship close enough because management controls the premises and who enters, the guest "relies on the competence and prudence of its management", and the two stand in "an economic relationship" (para 31).
- What the duty requires varies with the establishment: it "will be a rare night club that does not need some security arrangements which can be activated as and when the need arises" (para 36).
- The guests lost: the staff had acted reasonably (paras 37-38).

**What the paper may not say**

- That the nightclub was held liable.
- That the court grounded the duty in an assumption of responsibility or in innkeeper law. It used *Caparo* proximity; innkeeper law is not mentioned.
- That the court required a guest-facing channel or a person on duty in every establishment. It said the duty might be "no higher than that staff be trained to look out for any sign of trouble and to alert security staff" (para 36).
- That the management answers for a security contractor's negligence. The contractor was out of the case and the point was not decided.
- That the duty covers restaurants as such. The judgment speaks of bars, nightclubs and hotels.
