## Summary

A Hong Kong resident on a package tour of mainland China, sold by a Hong Kong travel company, drowned when a speedboat used to carry the group across a lake hit a junk. The boat belonged to one mainland company and the guide to another; its driver was a stand-in. The Hong Kong Court of Appeal held the tour company not liable. The Privy Council restored the trial judgment against it. Lord Slynn distinguishes a contract by which a party agrees, as agent, to arrange for services, where the implied term is care in selecting the provider, from a contract by which a party undertakes to supply services, where the implied term is that the services will be carried out with reasonable care and skill whether by that party or by others. Which it is depends on the construction of the contract. The brochure's “we” showed that this company undertook to provide. Hong Kong law, argued on English and one Ontario authority.

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Court and date: Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, on appeal from the Court of Appeal of Hong Kong, “Delivered the 6th November 1995”, Privy Council Appeal No. 62 of 1994. Present: Lord Goff of Chieveley, Lord Jauncey of Tullichettle, Lord Slynn of Hadley, Lord Nolan, Lord Hoffmann; “Delivered by Lord Slynn of Hadley” (p. 1). [full-text]
- Parties and claim: “On 4th August 1988 Miss Ho Shui Yee, a resident of Hong Kong, was drowned whilst on holiday in the People's Republic of China.” Her mother, as administratrix, “claimed damages for breach of contract and for negligence” against the Hong Kong tour company and two mainland companies (p. 1). “Miss Ho and two school friends contracted to take a package tour to Pak Tang Lake offered by the first defendant.” (p. 2) She was a paying customer of a commercial tour operator; the price was “an all in price” (p. 10). The judgment does not give her age. [full-text]
- The operator had its own employee with the group: “They were accompanied by a tour leader employed by the first defendant (a Miss Chan).” (p. 2) [full-text]
- The trial judge's findings, as quoted: the speedboat journey “was an integral part of the guided tour provided by the 1st Defendant” (p. 2); the first defendant owed “a primary and contractual duty to take reasonable care for her safety which included taking satisfactory measures to ensure that if speedboats were used on the tour they would be operated by persons of reasonable competence and experience” (p. 3); and “There was no evidence that any attempt was made to ensure that the speedboat which was being used as part of the Transportation was operated in a safe manner or that a safe system of operation was adhered to.” (p. 3) [full-text]
- The Court of Appeal's contrary view, as quoted from Penlington J.A.: “It would, in my view, impose an intolerable burden if the firm which put the tour package together was to be held liable for the negligence of a transport operator in another country on the basis of a breach of a non-delegable primary contractual duty.” (pp. 3-4) On that court's reading the operator's contract “would include seeing that the transportation which it was aware would be used was supplied by reputable organisations”, which had been done (p. 4). [full-text]
- The issue: “whether in this particular contract the first defendant undertook no more than that they would arrange for services to be provided by others as their agents (where the law would imply a term into the contract that they would use reasonable care and skill in selecting those other persons) or whether they themselves undertook to supply the services when, subject to any exemption clause, there would be implied into the contract a term that they would as suppliers carry out the services with reasonable care and skill” (p. 4). [full-text]
- The rule: “The fact that the supplier of services may under the contract arrange for some or all of them to be performed by others does not absolve the supplier from his contractual obligation. He may be liable if the service is performed without the exercise of due care and skill on the part of the sub-contractor just as he would be liable if the sub-contractor failed to provide the service or failed to provide it in accordance with the terms of the contract.” (p. 4) [full-text]
- The obligation restated: “if the person undertaking to supply the services performs them himself, that he will do so with reasonable skill and care, and that if, where the contract permits him to do so, he arranges for others to supply the services, that they will be supplied with reasonable skill and care” (pp. 4-5). [full-text]
- The other category, in Lacourciere J.A.'s words from *Craven v Strand Holidays (Canada) Ltd* as the Board quotes them: “But if the contract is only to provide or arrange for the performance of services then he has fulfilled his contract if he has exercised due care in the selection of a competent contractor. He is not responsible if that contractor is negligent in the performance of the actual work or service, for the performance is not part of his contract.” (p. 5) [full-text]
- Construction decides: “the fact that it is known that another person will or may perform the services or part of them does not mean that the contract is one of agency. In each case it has to be asked as a matter of construction into which category the contract falls. This may not always be easy.” (p. 5) [full-text]
- A dispatcher that was more than a go-between: in *Rogers v Night Riders*, as the Board describes it, a minicab service that passed a booking to an owner-driver was held liable; Eveleigh L.J., as quoted, said it had not been suggested that the plaintiff “knew that the defendants were simply a kind of post box to put her in touch with someone else with whom she would be able to make an independent contract” (p. 6). The Board calls it a case where the Court of Appeal “was entitled to find that the obligation undertaken was one which could be performed by another and where the defendants remained liable for the performance of the service with reasonable skill and care” (p. 6). [full-text]
- A tour operator and a hotel: in *Wilson v Best Travel*, as the Board describes it, Phillips J. held the operator's duty to be “to exercise reasonable care to exclude from the accommodation offered any hotel the characteristics of which were such that guests could not spend a holiday there in reasonable safety” (p. 7). In *Wall v Silver Wing*, an unreported decision as the Board describes it, Hodgson J. refused to imply a term that guests would be reasonably safe in using a hotel; the Board notes that “the implied term alleged in that case is not the one relied on here” (p. 8). [full-text]
- What the customer knew does not settle it: “Miss Ho and her colleagues may have appreciated that someone other than the first defendant would carry out some parts of the tour. That does not, however, conclude the question.” The question is whether the operator “was in reality doing no more than "arranging" the tour so that they undertook no liability for any default by those providing the goods and services or, at the most, a liability to take reasonable care in the selection of those who provided the services.” The brochure contained “no clear statements that the first defendant was doing no more than arranging the tour as agent for the travellers.” (p. 10) [full-text]
- How the brochure was read: “Throughout the detailed itinerary it is always "we" who will do things - board the bus, go for lunch, live in the hotel.” “"We" includes the company offering the tour and integrates the company into each stage of the tour.” (p. 10) [full-text]
- Holding: “Taking the contract as a whole their Lordships consider that the first defendant here undertook to provide and not merely to arrange all the services included in the programme, even if some activities were to be carried out by others. The first defendant's obligation under the contract that the services would be provided with reasonable skill and care remains even if some of the services were to be rendered by others, and even if tortious liability may exist on the part of those others.” (p. 10) [full-text]
- The limit of the term: it “does not mean, to use the words of Hodgson J., that the first defendant undertook an obligation to ensure "the safety of all the components of the package". The plaintiff's claim does not amount to an implied term that her daughter would be reasonably safe. It is a term simply that reasonable skill and care would be used in rendering the services to be provided under the contract.” (p. 11) [full-text]
- The breach: “The trip across the lake was clearly not carried out with reasonable skill and care in that no steps were taken to see that the driver of the speedboat was of reasonable competence and experience and the first defendant is liable for such breach of contract as found by the learned trial judge.” (p. 11) [full-text]
- Why the burden is tolerable, and the ways out: “the tour operator has the opportunity to seek to protect himself against claims made against him in respect of services performed by others by negotiating suitable contractual terms with those who are to perform those services. He may also provide for insurance cover. He may include an appropriate exemption clause in his contract with the traveller.” (p. 11) [full-text]
- Why the customer needs the rule: “a traveller in the position of Miss Ho could have no influence on the terms negotiated by the tour operator with third parties, and if injured by their lack of care would, if having no right against the package tour operator, be obliged to pursue a claim in a foreign country. The difficulty involved in doing so does not need to be elaborated.” (p. 11) [full-text]
- Statute as a cross-check: the Board notes the Package Travel, Package Holidays and Package Tours Regulations 1992, reg. 15, under which the organiser “is liable to the consumer for the proper performance of the obligations under the contract, irrespective of whether such obligations are to be performed by that other party or by other suppliers of services” (p. 11, as quoted), and adds: “These terms do not of course apply to the present contract but they do throw some light on the contention that an unreasonable burden would be imposed” (p. 12). [full-text]
- Conclusion: “if the tour operator agrees that services will be supplied whether by him or others on his behalf, to imply a term that those services will be carried out with reasonable skill and care is not imposing on the tour operator a burden which is "intolerable", as the Court of Appeal thought.” (p. 12) [full-text]
- The exemption clause was never tested: “It was not pleaded in the defence that clause 1 of the brochure constituted a valid exemption clause upon which the first defendant could rely.” (p. 12) [full-text]
- Disposition: “their Lordships will humbly advise Her Majesty that the appeal should be allowed and the judgment of Mayo J. restored.” (p. 12) The claim succeeded, in contract; damages had been assessed at “HK$575,050.00” (p. 2). [full-text]
- The phrase “non-delegable” appears only in the Hong Kong Court of Appeal's words (pp. 3-4, 8). The Board does not adopt it and does not call its rule exceptional; it treats the rule as the ordinary meaning of a promise to supply services (pp. 4-5). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **A rule about what was promised.** Everything turns on the construction of the contract (p. 5). The Board found a promise to provide in the brochure's wording; it did not hold that every seller of travel answers for its suppliers. A firm whose terms say clearly that it acts as agent to arrange owes, on this judgment, care in selection and no more (pp. 4, 5, 10). Booking sites and some brands draft for exactly that. The Board asks what the firm “was in reality doing” (p. 10), so labels may not be conclusive, but it decides nothing about a contract that says 'arrange' in terms.
- **The exemption clause.** The Board twice treats exclusion as open to the operator (“subject to any exemption clause”, p. 4; “He may include an appropriate exemption clause”, p. 11). In a United Kingdom consumer contract the Consumer Rights Act 2015 now bars a term excluding the trader's liability for want of reasonable care and skill (s. 57(1)) and liability for death or personal injury from negligence (s. 65(1)); see uk2015consumer. *Wong Mee Wan* is a Hong Kong appeal of 1995 and says nothing of that.
- **Liability for performance, not for failing to supervise.** The operator is liable because the service was carried out without care, by whoever carried it out. The Board's phrase “no steps were taken to see that the driver ... was of reasonable competence” (p. 11) describes the defective performance; it does not lay on the operator a separate duty to monitor. The trial judge had framed the duty as “taking satisfactory measures to ensure” competent operation (p. 3), which is closer to oversight, and the Board restored his judgment while stating the term in its own, simpler words (p. 11). The operator's tour leader was with the group (p. 2); the Board draws nothing from her presence.
- **Not a safety warranty.** The term is care and skill in rendering the services, and expressly no undertaking that the customer “would be reasonably safe” (p. 11). The hotel cases the Board reviews (*Wilson*, *Wall*) concerned the safety of premises abroad and went for the operators (pp. 7-8); the Board distinguishes the term claimed there and does not overrule them.
- **Jurisdiction and age.** An appeal from Hong Kong, persuasive in England. The scout reports that *X v Kuoni* [2021] UKSC 34 dealt with a tour operator's liability under the 1992 Regulations; I did not read it. Later treatment not checked.
- **Cases known only through this judgment:** *Craven*, *Rogers v Night Riders*, *Stewart v Reavell's Garage*, *Jarvis v Swans Tours*, *Wilson v Best Travel*, *Wall v Silver Wing*, *British Wagon Co v Lea*. Each may be cited only as the Board describes or quotes it.

## Where it bears on the argument

**K8, the contract limb: supports, with two stated ways out.** K8 says “The party that promised the stay answers for its performance by others.” The Board states that rule for services: a supplier who may arrange for others to perform is not absolved, and is liable if the sub-contractor performs without due care (p. 4). The ways out are a contract that only arranges (pp. 4-5, 10) and an exemption clause (pp. 4, 11). The rule covers care and skill in the services promised; it is no warranty of safety (p. 11), and for dangers on premises entered under contract English law has a different rule (see maguire2006sefton).

**K8, allocation among firms: supports the test.** The firm that holds itself out as doing the things (“we”, “our staff”, an all-in price) is the one that promised (p. 10). This fits K3's holding-out ground and K8's 'the one that held out', and it is the contractual twin of *Woodland*'s distinction between performing and arranging.

**K7, premise P2: supports for the contract.** What stays with the promisor is the obligation itself: the services will be supplied with care by whoever supplies them (pp. 4-5, 10).

**K7, premise P3: neither supports nor denies as law.** The Board imposes liability for the delegate's performance and tells the operator how to protect itself: terms with suppliers, insurance, an exemption clause (p. 11). Those are ways to shift the cost afterwards. The judgment does not say the promisor must be able to learn of a failure or correct it during the tour. Its reason of policy, that the traveller “could have no influence” on the operator's arrangements and would otherwise have to sue abroad (p. 11), is a reason for one answerable party and gives the paper a located statement of why the customer needs the holder.

**What the paper may say on this authority.**
- A supplier of services who arranges for others to perform them is not absolved of its contractual obligation, and may be liable if the sub-contractor performs without due care and skill (p. 4).
- The law distinguishes a party who undertakes to supply services from one who agrees, as agent, to arrange for them; the second promises care in selecting the provider (pp. 4-5).
- Which contract a firm made is a matter of construction, and the customer's knowing that others will perform does not make the firm an agent (pp. 5, 10).
- The Privy Council read a brochure's “we” as integrating the tour company into each stage of the tour (p. 10).
- The Board gave as reasons that the traveller cannot influence the terms the operator makes with third parties and would otherwise have to pursue a claim in a foreign country (p. 11).
- The Board said the operator could protect itself by its terms with suppliers, by insurance and by an exemption clause (p. 11).

**What it may not say.**
- That anyone who sells a stay or a trip answers for whoever performs it. A contract to arrange carries only a duty of care in selection (pp. 4-5, 10).
- That the promisor warrants the customer's safety. The Board said the term was not that (p. 11).
- That the promisor must monitor its sub-contractors or keep a channel to the customer. The judgment does not say so.
- That the rule is non-delegable duty in tort. The Board decided the case on an implied term of the contract (pp. 10-12).
- That this is English law by its own force. It is a Privy Council appeal from Hong Kong.
