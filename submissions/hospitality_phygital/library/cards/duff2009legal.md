## Summary

Duff asks whether criminal responsibility should track moral responsibility and answers that the question is two questions, because responsibility has two dimensions. To be answerable for an action is to be its author and to be called on to answer for it. To be liable is to be open to blame, or to conviction, when no exculpating answer is given. Answerability comes first and creates a presumption of liability, which a justification or an excuse can defeat. He illustrates with a driver who damages a parked car and owes its owner an explanation, and then shows the same structure in the criminal trial, where the prosecution proves that there is something to answer for and the defendant may answer with a defence. His closing section argues that moral answerability is typically strict and asks why criminal answerability should not be.

## Critical notes & threats

- **The contrast is with liability, not with accountability.** Duff does not use 'accountability' as a term of art in this article. His pair is answerability and liability. To get from here to 'answerability as distinct from accountability' the paper must either equate hard accountability with liability to consequences, which is the paper's step, or use Fox for that pair (fox2007uncertain, p. 668).
- **To whom one answers is shown, not argued.** The example fixes the addressee as the person wronged, 'you as the owner' (typescript p. 4), and in criminal law the court (typescript pp. 5–8). The article gives no account of what makes someone the proper addressee. Duff's fuller treatment of the question is in *Answering for Crime* (2007), which is closed and unread.
- **What it gives the second route to K7, and its limit.** Typescript p. 3 is a published statement that the person who caused the damage is expected to explain and may be criticised for walking off. That is close to saying that evading the answer is a further fault. It is one sentence in an example, about a face-to-face moral case, and it concerns the time after the damage. It is no argument that a duty-bearer must keep itself reachable.
- **Answerability is for something done.** One answers for 'an action or event' already attributed to one (typescript p. 4). Like Fox's, Duff's answerability is retrospective. It can arise during a stay, for a lapse that has already happened, and it does not describe a guest's power to stop a process before it fails.
- **Criminal, not private, law.** Tort is set aside in the opening sentence. The trial structure he describes (prosecution proves the offence, defendant offers a defence) has no direct counterpart in a hotel's dealings with a guest.
- **Version.** Page numbers are the typescript's. A quotation in the paper needs the journal's pages (978–986) and a check of the wording against the version of record.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K7 (supports the vocabulary and the after-stage; narrows the second route).** Supports: answering is a stage distinct from, and prior to, bearing consequences (typescript pp. 4–5); it is owed to a particular person, the one whose car it was (p. 4); and it does not wait for proof of fault (p. 8), which suits a host that must explain a failure whether or not it was to blame. Narrows: the article contains no duty to remain available to be called to answer, and its answerability is after the event.
- **K6 (bears).** Being positioned to call someone to answer is shown for the person wronged. The article does not connect this to rights or claims, so it neither supports nor contradicts K6's refusal to rest on a theory of standing.
