## Summary

The Act replaces the common law of innkeepers in Ireland with a statutory code for hotels. A hotel is an establishment that provides or holds itself out as providing sleeping accommodation, food and drink for reward for all comers without special contract, and includes every hotel on the tourist board's register. Its proprietor, the person carrying on the business, is under four duties: to receive at the hotel all persons who present themselves and require a bed, food or drink, unless he has reasonable grounds of refusal (s. 3); to take reasonable care of the person of a guest who has been received and to ensure that the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them (s. 4); to receive the property of a guest for whom a bed is engaged (s. 5); and to answer for damage to or loss of that property without proof of fault, up to £100 unless one of three conditions lifts the cap (ss. 6-7). No notice or agreement can exclude any liability under the Act (s. 9). Breach of the duty to receive persons or property is also a summary offence, "without prejudice to his civil liability" (s. 12).

## Key facts / verified quotes with loci

- Enacting body and date: "AN ACT TO AMEND THE LAW RELATING TO INNS AND INNKEEPERS AND FOR PURPOSES CONNECTED WITH THAT MATTER. [14th March, 1963.]" (long title); in operation "on the 1st day of May, 1963" (s. 14); Ireland. [full-text]
- What a hotel is: "“hotel” means an establishment which provides or holds itself out as providing sleeping accommodation, food and drink for reward for all comers without special contract and includes every establishment registered in the register of hotels kept under Part III of the Tourist Traffic Act, 1939" (s. 1(1)). [full-text]
- Who holds the duties: "“proprietor”, in relation to a hotel, means the person carrying on the business of the hotel and, in the case of a hotel registered in the said register, includes the person entered in the register as the proprietor thereof" (s. 1(1)). [full-text]
- The Act replaces the common law: "The duties, liabilities and rights provided for by this Act shall have effect in place of the duties, liabilities and rights which heretofore attached by the common law to innkeepers as such." (s. 2(1)). [full-text]
- Admission: "The proprietor of a hotel is under a duty to receive at the hotel as guests all persons who, whether or not under special contract, present themselves and require sleeping accommodation, food or drink and to provide them therewith, unless he has reasonable grounds of refusal." (s. 3(1)). [full-text]
- At the going rate: "Subject to the terms of any special contract, the proprietor is under a duty to provide such accommodation, food or drink at the charges for the time being current at the hotel." (s. 3(2)). [full-text]
- Reception, the person and the premises: "Where a person is received as a guest at a hotel, whether or not under special contract, the proprietor of the hotel is under a duty to take reasonable care of the person of the guest and to ensure that, for the purpose of personal use by the guest, the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them." (s. 4(1)). [full-text]
- Separate from occupier's liability: "This duty is independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier of the premises." (s. 4(2)). [full-text]
- Reception, property: "Where sleeping accommodation is engaged for a person as a guest at a hotel, whether or not under special contract, the proprietor is under a duty to receive any property brought to the hotel by or on behalf of that person for which the proprietor has suitable accommodation." (s. 5(1)). [full-text]
- Liability for property without fault: "the proprietor is liable for any damage to, or loss or destruction of, property received by him from that person or from some other person on his behalf" (s. 6(1)); the statutory notice describes this as liability "even though it was not due to any fault of the proprietor or staff of the hotel" (First Schedule). [full-text]
- Exemptions: the proprietor is exempt "to the extent that the damage, loss or destruction is due to an unforeseeable and irresistible act of nature or an act of war or is due to the guest himself or to any person accompanying him or in his employment or visiting him" (s. 6(5)). [full-text]
- A vehicle is covered only after notice to a person with authority: liability does not apply "unless the proprietor of the hotel or some servant of his authorised, or appearing to be authorised, for the purpose has been previously notified that the motor vehicle has been brought to the hotel" (s. 6(3)). [full-text]
- The cap and what lifts it: liability "to any one person shall not exceed £100, except where— (a) the property was damaged, lost, stolen or destroyed through the wrongful act, default or omission of the proprietor or some servant of his" (s. 7(1)(a)), or it was deposited for safe custody (s. 7(1)(b)). [full-text]
- The cap falls where the guest could not reach anyone to take a deposit: "either the property was offered for deposit as aforesaid and the proprietor or his servant refused to accept it, or the guest or some person acting on his behalf wished so to offer the property but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so" (s. 7(1)(c)). [full-text]
- The cap depends on a displayed notice, and the Act contemplates a hotel with no desk: the notice must be displayed "at or near the reception office or desk or, where there is not a reception office or desk, at or near the main entrance to the hotel" (s. 7(3)). [full-text]
- No contracting out: "Any notice or agreement which purports to exclude or limit any liability under this Act shall to that extent be void." (s. 9). [full-text]
- The guest's own action: the Circuit Court has jurisdiction "to hear and determine all civil actions arising under this Act" (s. 10(1)) and the District Court over "claims for damages under this Act" up to a limit (s. 10(2)). [full-text]
- A public offence as well: "Where there is a breach of a duty imposed by section 3 or 5 the proprietor of the hotel shall, without prejudice to his civil liability, be guilty of an offence and shall be liable on summary conviction to a fine not exceeding £100." (s. 12). [full-text]
- Read with the general law of civil liability: "This Act shall be construed subject to the provisions of the Civil Liability Act, 1961" (s. 1(2)). [full-text]
- The lien: the proprietor "has a lien upon property, to whomsoever belonging, brought to the hotel by or on behalf of any guest for any debt due by the guest" (s. 8(1)). [full-text]

## Critical notes & threats

- **Read as enacted.** The Statute Book's directory entry, updated to 3 November 2026, lists amendments to s. 10 (court limits) only. I did not read the amending Acts or a consolidated text, so the money limits in s. 10 (£1,000 and £100 in 1963) and the fine in s. 12 are the 1963 figures and may not be current. The directory lists no amendment to any other section.
- **No case law read.** How Irish courts have applied s. 3's "reasonable grounds of refusal" or s. 4's duty to "ensure" is not known to this run.
- **The conditions of admission are thinner than at common law.** The Act does not list fitness, capacity or ability to pay; it folds them into "reasonable grounds of refusal" (s. 3(1)). It also drops the word "traveller": the duty runs to "all persons who ... present themselves". And it reaches a person who wants only food or drink at a hotel (s. 3(1)), while the property duties are confined to guests for whom a bed is engaged (ss. 5(1), 6(1)).
- **The offence does not cover the safety duty.** S. 12 makes breach of s. 3 or s. 5 an offence. Breach of s. 4 (care of the person) has only a civil remedy.
- **Section 4 and contractors.** The duty "to ensure that ... the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them" tracks the warranty in *MacLenan v Segar* (maclenan1917segar, pp. 325, 333), without the words "on the part of any one". Whether the Irish duty makes the proprietor answer for an independent contractor is a question of construction I cannot settle from the text.
- **Hotels only.** Restaurants, bars and guesthouses that do not meet the s. 1(1) definition and are not on the register fall outside. The Act says nothing of platforms or short-term lets.
- **Jurisdiction.** Ireland. v6 does not name it. The Act cannot be used as a statement of English law; it is a comparator from a neighbouring common-law system whose innkeeper law had the same source (s. 2(1); the Second Schedule repeals the Innkeepers' Liability Act 1863).
- **Later treatment** not checked beyond the directory entry.

## Where it bears on the argument

- **K4 (two tiers): supports, in a legislature's own words, for hotels.** Admission is s. 3; reception is s. 4 (person and premises) and ss. 5-7 (property). The statute also draws K4's line between hotels and other establishments (s. 1(1)).
- **K5 (directedness): supports, and shows both routes.** The duties run to the persons who present themselves and to "the guest" (ss. 3-4); the Act provides for "civil actions" and "claims for damages" (s. 10) and, for ss. 3 and 5, an offence "without prejudice to his civil liability" (s. 12).
- **K7 (channel): a statutory analogue, limited to property.** The proprietor loses the cap where a guest "wished so to offer the property but, through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so" (s. 7(1)(c)), and a guest's notice counts when given to a servant "authorised, or appearing to be authorised" (s. 6(3)). The law here attaches a consequence to the guest's being unable to reach a person with authority. The consequence is fuller damages afterwards.
- **K8 (holder): supports for goods; indicates the holder.** Strict liability for property stays with the proprietor whoever caused the loss, save the listed exemptions (s. 6(1), (5)), and cannot be excluded (s. 9). The holder is "the person carrying on the business of the hotel" (s. 1(1)), which points to the operator.
- **K3 (grounds): consistent with holding out.** The definition turns on an establishment that "holds itself out" to "all comers" (s. 1(1)); the duties then bind by statute.

**What the paper may say**

- Ireland's Hotel Proprietors Act 1963 puts a hotel's proprietor "under a duty to receive at the hotel as guests all persons who ... present themselves", "unless he has reasonable grounds of refusal" (s. 3(1)).
- Once a guest is received, the proprietor "is under a duty to take reasonable care of the person of the guest and to ensure that ... the premises are as safe as reasonable care and skill can make them" (s. 4(1)), a duty "independent of any liability of the proprietor as occupier" (s. 4(2)).
- The proprietor is liable for guests' property without fault (s. 6(1)), up to a cap that falls away where the guest wished to deposit the property and "through the default of the proprietor or a servant of his, was unable to do so" (s. 7(1)(c)).
- No notice or agreement can exclude liability under the Act (s. 9).
- Refusing to receive a person is both a civil wrong and an offence (ss. 10, 12).
- The Act defines the proprietor as "the person carrying on the business of the hotel" (s. 1(1)).

**What the paper may not say**

- That this is the law of England, or of any jurisdiction v6 names.
- That the Act applies to restaurants, bars, short-term lets or platforms.
- That the text quoted is the current consolidated text; it is the Act as enacted, and s. 10 has been amended.
- That the proprietor answers for independent contractors under s. 4. The text does not say, and no case was read.
- That failing to take care of a guest's person is a criminal offence under the Act. S. 12 covers ss. 3 and 5 only.
