# Section brief

*Who Hosts the Guest?* for the *Hospitality & Society* special issue on phygital hospitality. The
argument itself, section by section.

## 1. Introduction

Phygital hospitality research describes AI, apps and automated interfaces as tools for delivering
seamless, personalized, guest-centric experience. Hospitality is not the efficient provision of
services. It is a relational practice of welcome, recognition, care, presence and negotiated access.
Every term the field uses for the algorithmic party names a capacity: tool, mediator, facilitator,
co-creator, social presence. None names an obligation. A host owes the arriving stranger a welcome,
and no employment relation creates that duty or discharges it. Service research filled the seat
marked employee and left the seat marked host empty. *Hospitality & Society* has already asked what
becomes of hospitality when platforms erase the human host. The question left standing is whether the
party that took over the host's functions inherited any of the host's obligations. It has not.

## 2. Hospitality is more than service

Being a guest is a position, not a service level. The position carries standing: the right to ask, to
decline, to have a decision explained, to be received as an exception to the ordinary rules. An
incident-free stay does not mean anyone was received. Hospitality also runs both ways in the ethical
tradition and one way in the commercial one, which is why the guest's side has never been theorized:
the field measures how welcome guests feel and has no instrument for anything flowing from the guest
to the host. Under algorithmic mediation the guest's standing loses its counterparty. She keeps
somebody to complain to and be compensated by. She loses the party with authority over the rules. She
can be admitted and still have nobody to negotiate the terms with.

## 3. The three-party arrangement

The intermediary classifies, recommends, gates, prices, queues and personalizes, which are hosting
functions. Three things redistribute. Knowledge moves to the system, which sees the guest's history
and the property's inventory at once. Discretion moves partly to the system and partly into new work
for staff, who now spend their time translating machine decisions to guests. Authority moves to
whoever configures the rules, which is nobody the guest can reach. The party best placed to know,
decide and revise is the party least available to answer. This is the paper's central asymmetry, and
it is not a malfunction. It is what the arrangement does when it works.

The craft point matters for practitioners. A good front desk manufactures the guest's sense of
control: it defers visibly, decides invisibly, and heads off choices the guest will regret. That
skill rests on holding the decisions it appears to surrender. Algorithmic direction breaks it at the
hinge, because felt control now has to be produced by an interface that holds nothing.

## 4. When technology helps and when it replaces

Sophistication does not decide the question. Three withholdings do. Common understanding fails when
neither party can model the other. Predictability inverts when the system forecasts the guest while
revising its own rules silently. Accountability fails wherever no forum owes an answer. Mediation is
substitutive when it withholds all three at once and augmentative when it does not. An opaque system
with stable rules and an empowered employee is augmentative. A perfectly transparent system that
answers to nobody is substitutive.

Five questions apply the test at any touchpoint. What decided this? Who can see the decision? Who can
override it? What answers a dispute? What does refusing cost? A hotel pricing algorithm that biases
its own recommendations because it anticipates staff overriding them fails the first two. A field
experiment where guests were indifferent between a human and a robot at an information desk passes
all five, which is why indifference there tells you nothing about the check-in desk.

## 5. What people can learn, and what they cannot

Guests and employees build working theories of these systems and learn to phrase requests the system
will accept. Three competences: interpreting what the system is doing, specifying wishes in terms it
can act on, and tracking when its rules change. Each answers one withholding. The agent who rephrases
a guest's request into the system's categories is lending her his own specification.

The competence has a ceiling, and naming it is the paper's sharpest move. Skill cannot manufacture a
counterparty. The expert guest who understands the pricing model exactly has no more influence over
it than the novice. A recently published scale of algorithmic competency folds the capacity to appeal
into the competence itself, which is the error. Lodging an appeal is something a person can do.
Being answered is something an institution has to grant. Competence is learned; standing is
conferred. Employees show the same split: those who understand the system best often stop speaking
about it, on top of a hospitality workforce that was already silent for other reasons.

## 6. Five design features

Explain decisions where they take effect, not in a policy document. Let guests adjust parameters
without leaving the relationship. Announce rule changes, because silent revision turns competence
into a moving target. Keep a person reachable who can actually reverse the decision, since a reachable
person without authority absorbs the complaint and changes nothing. Leave a real alternative, because
an option that exists but is punished is not an option.

The division is the practical contribution. The first three can be trained and designed. The last two
cannot be supplied by any individual, however skilled, and require a staffing model, a reviewer with
authority, and a decision about who answers. A property that responds to a contestability problem
with guest education has misread its own condition.

## 7. Against frictionless design

Seamlessness is a stated goal in the phygital frameworks, not an accident of them. The field has
begun criticizing its own technocentrism and AI's harms while leaving smoothness alone. Smoothness
has a cost. Negotiation needs an occasion, and frictionless design removes the occasions. A system
engineered never to fail is engineered never to be noticed, and a decision nobody notices cannot be
contested. The value of voice does not depend on winning; it depends on there being a point at which
speaking is possible. Guests whose needs fall outside the system's categories lose the pause at the
desk where an employee would have improvised. Audits of AI travel recommendations find bias in every
category tested, and language models fail on exactly the relational, local knowledge that hosting
runs on.

## 8. Discussion

Three contributions: AI repositioned from neutral service technology to constitutive mediator of
hospitality relationships; a checkable condition for when mediation augments rather than substitutes;
and competence separated from standing. Well-being runs positive, negative and conditional at both
the individual and the collective level, and the evidence on automation and the evidence on community
well-being have never met in one study. The roadmap has five stages of ascending institutional cost:
audit the touchpoints, specify the affordances, build the first three, constitute a forum that
answers, keep the exit open. A property that stops after stage three has automated its hospitality
without deciding who answers for it.

## 9. Conclusion

Guest-centric phygital hospitality does not depend on making technology invisible. It depends on
guests and employees keeping the agency to determine how technology participates, and on
organizations accepting that part of that agency cannot be trained into people and has to be designed
into the arrangement. Somebody still owes the guest a welcome.
