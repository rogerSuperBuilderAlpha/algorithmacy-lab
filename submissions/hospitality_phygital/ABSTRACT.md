Phygital hospitality scholarship frequently presents artificial intelligence, mobile applications,
automated interfaces, and immersive technologies as tools for delivering seamless, personalized, and
guest-centric experiences. Yet hospitality is not simply the efficient provision of services. It is
a relational and culturally situated practice involving welcome, recognition, care, presence, and
negotiated access. When algorithmic systems increasingly recognize guests, personalize environments,
mediate communication, allocate organizational attention, and determine available choices, they do
more than support hospitality: they participate in defining how hospitality is performed and
experienced. This conceptual paper therefore asks: How does algorithmic mediation reconfigure
relationships among guests, hospitality employees, and organizations, and under what conditions does
it enhance rather than diminish the human and relational qualities of hospitality?

The paper theorizes phygital hospitality as a system of triadic coordination among the guest, the
algorithmic intermediary, and the human or organizational host. Within this arrangement, algorithms
perform functions traditionally associated with hosting: interpreting guest needs, directing
encounters, regulating access, anticipating preferences, and responding to requests. Their
participation redistributes knowledge, discretion, and authority across the hospitality encounter.
Although such mediation can expand responsiveness and inclusion, it may also produce
depersonalization, surveillance, categorical exclusion, and dependence on opaque technological
systems.

To explain these contrasting outcomes, the paper distinguishes between **augmentative hospitality**,
in which technology supports human recognition, discretion, responsiveness, and care, and
**substitutive hospitality**, in which algorithmic direction displaces relational judgment and
restricts the capacity of guests and employees to shape the encounter. It introduces **hospitality
algorithmacy** as the situated capacity of guests and employees to recognize, interpret,
strategically engage with, contest, and, when necessary, circumvent algorithmic mediation. This
capacity supports **coordinative sovereignty**: the practical ability of participants to retain
meaningful influence over how hospitality is organized and enacted.

The proposed framework identifies transparency, adjustability, contestability, human accessibility,
and bypass-ability as central affordances of guest-centric phygital design. These affordances
influence whether algorithmic mediation strengthens or weakens guest agency, employee discretion,
interpersonal recognition, belonging, trust, and well-being. The paper also challenges the
assumption that seamlessness is inherently hospitable. By concealing decisions and eliminating
opportunities for negotiation, a frictionless encounter may become less relational, less culturally
responsive, and less capable of accommodating guests whose needs fall outside algorithmically
constructed norms.

The paper contributes to hospitality theory by repositioning AI from a neutral service technology to
a constitutive mediator of hospitality relationships, meanings, and power. It offers hospitality
organizations principles for designing phygital systems that preserve human contact, explain
consequential decisions, enable employee intervention, and provide meaningful alternatives to
digitally prescribed encounters. Ultimately, guest-centric phygital hospitality depends not on
making technology invisible, but on ensuring that guests and employees retain the agency to
determine how technology participates in the performance of hospitality.
