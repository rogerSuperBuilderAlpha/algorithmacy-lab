**Title:** Algorithmacy: The Competence to Coordinate When an Algorithm Judges Both Sides

## Abstract
Organizations increasingly manage people through algorithms that evaluate their work, yet they rarely ask what competence that arrangement demands. Research offers several answers, and each treats the system as standing in one place. AI literacy treats the system as a partner to one user. Studies of computer-mediated communication treat it as a channel between two users. Work on algorithmic competency treats it as a manager over one worker. None of them covers a common case in algorithmically managed work: two people who depend on each other, such as a seller and a buyer, a hotel and its guests, or an employee and a manager reading an AI-generated appraisal, while a system evaluates both and binds both to its verdict.

This paper classifies communicative competence by what stands between the people coordinating. In oracy they speak directly. In literacy a text moderates them, but a human still interprets and judges it. In what this paper calls algorithmacy, an opaque, adaptive system stands between them as an arbiter that neither party chose. Following Okhuysen and Bechky, coordination depends on accountability, predictability and common understanding. The arbiter dissolves all three. Neither party can tell whether an outcome came from the counterpart or from the rule, every message to the counterpart is also scored, and the rule changes without notice.

Algorithmacy is the competence that restores those conditions, and it has three practices. Interpreting ambiguous results restores accountability. Specifying intent so the counterpart can read it through the system restores common understanding. Tracking rule changes the operator does not announce restores predictability. The paper describes two failure modes. In the first, a person collapses the triad into a pair with the machine and ignores the counterpart. In the second, a person treats the system as a neutral channel and ignores its judgement.

For research on managing people through algorithms, the argument bears on job quality, autonomy and fairness in three ways. First, simplifying a task through algorithmic management raises its relational demands, so deskilling and the demand for algorithmacy rise together. Second, people subject to the same rules will differ in outcomes according to this competence, a fairness problem that training in AI literacy does not address. Third, if generative AI enters appraisal, hiring and collaboration, these demands will move from platform work into professional work.

**Keywords:** algorithmic management; algorithmacy; coordination; competence; triads
