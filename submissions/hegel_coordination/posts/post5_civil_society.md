In the first post I split a market between two rooms to show what my criterion does. A market
coordinates a million strangers who never meet, dense with dependence, every price a trader sees set
by everyone else — and you can cut it clean. Put the traders in two rooms, let each room clear its own
prices, and each half goes on doing what it did. The price mechanism survives the cut. Nothing the
market *does* died on the table. Enormous dependence, and still an aggregate.

Hegel reached that verdict in 1821, by hand, on the largest interdependent system anyone had described.
He had read the political economists. He looked at the web of buying and selling and mutual need a
modern economy runs on — the thing he called the system of needs — and he did not call it a state. He
called it "the external state, the state of necessity and of the understanding" (*der äußere Staat, Not-
und Verstandesstaat*), a "system of all-round interdependence" whose unity stays outside the parts that
depend on one another (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §183). That is the verdict my two-rooms cut reaches too — the
same conclusion by a different road, a century and a half earlier, in a register that had no number and
wanted none.

Say the limit before the coincidence gets any further: this is an agreement of verdicts, not an identity
of method. Hegel is not doing my arithmetic in advance, and my instrument is not cashing out something
he already meant. Two different questions land on the same case and vote the same way; that is worth a
paper, and it is not worth more than that.

I trace that coincidence and its exact limit here. Hegel put the market economy on the near side
of a boundary and called its unity external; my partition test puts the same case on the near side of a
boundary it draws its own way. The two verdicts land together. The two boundaries do not: his is a stage
in an ethical development, mine a fact about which cuts cost the parts something. Connection was already
not constitution — for him and for the test, on this case, and for reasons that are not the same reason.
My job is to hold the agreement and the difference together at once, and to keep either
from annexing the other.

The instrument needs restating before the texts do. The program models a small coordination as a
network of parts whose states constrain one another, and asks whether any way of cutting the system —
severing the dependencies while every part stays exactly where it is — leaves the parts making the same
differences to each other that they made when intact. Some cut that costs nothing means the
configuration was an aggregate wearing the shape of a whole; every cut costing the parts something means
the configuration is integrated, and the parts so bound form its irreducible core. The formalism behind
the operation is exact integrated information (Φ; Albantakis et al., 2023; Oizumi et al., 2014), read at
the scale of small coordination systems, as in the earlier papers of this series. One operation, one
question: does division destroy anything? Hegel asked a version of that question about the market a
century and a half early, in his own terms and toward his own end, and this paper reads what he found
against what the instrument finds — two roads to one verdict on one case, not one road walked twice.

## What Hegel Had Actually Read

One fact grounds everything else here. When Hegel wrote about interdependence he was not
gesturing at "the economy" from a philosopher's armchair. He had read Adam Smith, Jean-Baptiste Say, and
David Ricardo, and in the Remark to §189 he names the three of them and praises what they had
accomplished: extracting the simple governing principles from the endless mass of concrete detail that
economic life presents (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §189, Remark). That is a philosopher tipping his hat to a
science he thinks has found something real, and Ferro's account of Hegel's relation to political economy
backs the description: Hegel is appropriating and transforming the science on its own terms, not
decorating a preformed argument with borrowed vocabulary (Ferro, 2023). Political economy, for Hegel, had
done genuine work: it had found the intelligible order inside the apparent chaos of the market, the
Understanding at work in the thing itself.

Norbert Waszek has documented how deep the reading went. Hegel did not skim Smith for a slogan. He
worked through the Scottish tradition — Ferguson, whose *Essay on the History of Civil Society* gave the
tradition its title, and behind him Steuart and Smith, who supplied the economic content: division of
labor, market interdependence, the machinery Hegel folds into the system of needs (Waszek, 1988). None
of these three figures is named in the Remark to §189, which names only Smith, Say, and Ricardo;
Waszek's contribution is reception, not citation, and I follow him only that far, without quoting a
sentence of his own. Lisa Herzog's systematic comparison of Smith's and Hegel's accounts of commercial
society reaches the same reception claim from the far side, reading Hegel's economics as continuous with
and critical of Smith's own (Herzog, 2013); I return to her when the question turns from what Hegel read
to what he thought the reading left unsolved.

Hegel was concrete about the mechanism, and so was his source. Smith's emblem for the
division of labor is the pin factory: one untrained workman "could scarce . . . make one pin in a day,"
while about ten workers, dividing the roughly eighteen operations of pin-making between them, turn out
upwards of forty-eight thousand (Smith, 1776/1981, Book I, Chap. 1). Hegel took the lesson and pushed it
into a structural claim about civil society. Already at §182 he had stated the general form: each
particular person "asserts itself and gains satisfaction through the others, and thus at the same time
through the exclusive mediation of the form of universality" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §182). Particularity
secures itself only by passing through a universal it does not control. That is the two-principles
paragraph; the next paragraph asks what kind of universal this is.

## The System of Needs, Interwoven

Hold on the word "interwoven." It is the whole question in one image, and it is Hegel's own image, not
a gloss added later. In a developed economy, he writes, "the subsistence and welfare of the individual
and his rightful existence are interwoven with, and grounded on, the subsistence, welfare, and rights of
all, and have actuality and security only in this context" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §183). That sentence sits
in the same paragraph as the "system of all-round interdependence" it explains, not in the paragraph
before it, and the locus matters: the interweaving and the externality it produces are one continuous
argument, not two separable claims.

Interwoven threads make a fabric, and a fabric can be a genuine whole or a heap of tangles; the image
alone will not tell you which. Everything depends on whether the weave is the kind a cut destroys or the
kind a cut merely interrupts. Hegel had the image and did not stop at it. He asked what kind of unity the
weave was, and gave an answer in the same breath he used to describe it.

## Division of Labor: The Tie Tightens, the Task Hollows

The engine of the interweaving is the division of labor, and Hegel watched it do two things at once.
The more finely labor divides, the more each worker's product becomes useless to the worker and needful
to everyone else, so the tie of mutual dependence tightens with every subdivision. At the same time, the
work itself grows more abstract. "Furthermore," Hegel writes, "the abstraction of production makes work
increasingly mechanical, so that the human being is eventually able to step aside and let a machine take
his place" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §198). He is watching interdependence intensify and the individual task
hollow out in the same motion, more binding and more mechanical together. The natural inference — that
intensifying dependence must be building toward a whole — is the one he does not draw. Neither does the
test built to weigh it.

## The Verdict: External Unity

His verdict is the same §183 that supplied the interweaving, and it is blunt — with one hedge worth
keeping. Civil society, the whole system of interwoven needs and labor and exchange, is "the external
state, the state of necessity and of the understanding" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §183). Hegel calls it this
*in the first instance* — *zunächst* — before the corporation and the state raise it into something its
members inhabit; "external" is his verdict on civil society considered under its first moment, not his
last word on it.

That staging matters more than a hedge. Hegel's *zunächst* names a moment built to be superseded, on the
way to the corporation and the state that raise civil society's unity into something its members own. My
partition test has no next moment. Aggregate is not a stage in an argument the test is building toward a
whole; it is where the analysis stops for this case, because no cut costs the parts anything, full stop.
The two verdicts answer to opposite ends: Hegel's external state is provisional by design, staged so it
can be overcome; the test's aggregate is terminal, because nothing in the causal structure demands
overcoming it. Reading the same word off the same object toward opposite ends is not a weaker form of
agreement — it is what keeps the agreement from sliding into identity. (Hegel's organism, for comparison,
is a whole not because some cut fails to sever a circulation, but because its members have no essence
apart from it — a claim about what the parts are, prior to any test run on them.)

Under that moment, three words carry the weight.

*External* is the load-bearing one. The unity of civil society is real but it does not live in the
members. It stands over them, a resultant of their private pursuits that none of them intends and that
holds them together from outside. Each person wants a livelihood; the interweaving of all those private
wants into a working system is a fact about the aggregate, not a purpose any member carries. A market
clears, and no trader meant for it to clear. The order is genuine and the order is external — the
condition my two-rooms cut exposes. Where the unity of a system sits outside the parts, in a resultant
they produce but do not inhabit, some partition will find the seam and the system will survive being cut
along it.

*Necessity* — *Not*, need, want, distress — names the glue. What binds civil society is that its members
need each other, materially, to live. I make shoes and you grow grain and neither of us survives alone,
so we are chained together by mutual dependence. That is a strong bond and a low one: strong enough that
no one leaves, loose enough that the system re-founds itself in each half of a cut population. Dependence
is not the same as membership, and Hegel marks the difference by calling the tie one of necessity rather
than of ethical life.

*Understanding* — *Verstand* — names the faculty whose kind of unity this is. In the Encyclopaedia Logic,
Hegel treats the Understanding as the power that fixes a determinacy and holds it apart in its
distinctness, relating terms to one another while leaving each to subsist on its own account; Reason, by
contrast, grasps a unity in which the terms are moments of one thing rather than neighbors standing side
by side (Hegel, 1830/2010a, §§79–82). Civil society is a *Verstandesstaat*, a state as the Understanding
pictures it: a well-related manifold, an order of standing parts, not yet a whole in the sense Reason
means. This is the strongest thing in the comparison, because it is genuinely structural: parts that
subsist on their own account, related without being constituted, are — on this case — just what a
partition can hold apart without loss. That the two descriptions land together here is the finding; it is
not a law that Verstand and my test must always agree. Hegel's Vernunft is a claim about self-relating
spirit, and nothing in the Encyclopaedia treats it as a measure over causal structure. Hegel filed the
market under the faculty that fixes and relates without constituting; that filing lands where my test
lands, in words rather than in a number — a resonance across two registers, not one claim wearing two
costumes.

Notice what he did *not* say. He did not say civil society is a heap, a chaos, an absence of order. The
external state is highly organized; it has laws, a police, courts, a rational structure the economists
mapped. Hegel's point is sharper than "disorganized." A system can be maximally organized, dense with
lawful dependence, and still have its unity outside itself. Organization is not wholeness, and Hegel kept
that distinction in 1821, where the market's apologists and its harshest critics both tend to lose it.

## The Lab's Version: A Quorum That Reads Everyone

Here is where the criterion earns the comparison. It exhibits a mechanism — why a count that reads
everyone can still track no one — that a market this interdependent could be running too, and the
mechanism is not the sweeping one a first pass at the data invites.

Build the smallest thing that shows it: a mediator S and three parties, A, B, and C, each on or off. On
the next tick S fires when at least *k* of the three parties are active, and each party then reads S
under its own randomly assigned rule. By the crude measure of how many wires run between the parts,
nothing is missing: no party settles its next state without the mediator, and the mediator answers to
all three.

Now run the test at three thresholds, over 400 random draws (seed 11, four nodes). At *k* = 1 — any
single party enough to fire the mediator — the draw commits to an irreducible, three-way binding in 12
of 400 forms, three percent. At *k* = 3 — all three required — the rate is the same low number: 12 of
400. At *k* = 2 — a majority — the rate is zero. Not low: zero. None of the 400 draws at the majority
threshold produces the irreducible binding the extremes occasionally produce.

That contrast is the finding, and rounding it up loses it. It is not that any count-reading system is an
aggregate — the extremes can and occasionally do bind, at three percent, which is not nothing. And the
majority does not factor because it reads a shared tally while the extremes do not: all three read a
tally, and a threshold of any kind is a count. What sets the majority apart is that it stops caring which
parties made the count. At *k* = 1 a single party is enough, so any one party's flip can decide the
firing; at *k* = 3 every party is needed, so each is held to it. At *k* = 2 the gate goes indifferent to
which two — once enough are active it forgets the rest, and a determination that does not track which
particular party is live is one no partition has to keep whole. That indifference, not the mere presence
of a shared number, is what factors the interior threshold. It is why the program files this result under
substitutability rather than under mediation: its own structural record puts it exactly — "majority/redundant
determinations factor entirely."

The zero at *k* = 2 is not a fluke of one seed and one triad. A separate sweep of the same quorum logic
across party counts finds the identical shape at every size tried, though the two sweeps are not reading
quite the same thing: the 400-draw seed randomizes how each party reads the mediator, while the atlas
fixes the canonical quorum read and varies only the threshold and the party count. Read that way, the
atlas is a cleaner cut at the same law, not a competing number. At *k* = 1 and at the unanimity threshold
*k* = *n*, the whole system integrates with every party in the core, at Φ = *n* − 1; at every interior
threshold in between — *k* = 2 at *n* = 3, *k* = 2 and *k* = 3 at *n* = 4, *k* = 3 at *n* = 5 — the system
reads exactly zero, no partial binding, no gradient down from the extremes to the interior. The finding
names its own mechanism: at an interior quorum no single party is pivotal, so no partition has to cross a
party that was ever decisive on its own. The atlas states the law in one line — "no gradient, no partial
Φ at the interior thresholds, only zero" — and that line is what turns the 400-draw seed into a registered
regularity rather than a one-off.

One number complicates the picture and belongs here rather than in a footnote. Whenever some coalition
inside the system integrates — even where the whole three-way form does not — the mediator sits at the
bottleneck. 170 of 400 draws integrate some coalition at *k* = 1, 247 at *k* = 2, 158 at *k* = 3, and S
is a veto player in every one of them. The majority threshold is the busiest here and the emptiest above
it: more of its draws coordinate *something* than at either extreme, and none of them binds all three.
What integrates at the majority is the mediator locked with a subset of parties, never the full triad. So
"the majority factors" is true of the whole and precise about it: the three-party unity always comes
apart, and the pieces that survive the cut are sub-parts, not the coordination the system was built to
be. That is the quorum.

The market reading rides on that receipt, and the mechanism is the one the majority result just showed.
A price is a shared number that summarizes the whole supply and forgets which supplier produced any unit
of it — the farmer needs shoes, not this shoemaker. That forgetting is the majority gate's indifference
to which party, at the scale of a thousand traders: a coupling redundant in who satisfies it, and
redundancy is what a partition routes around. The receipt reaches only so far. No form in
this corpus represents a price or a market scalar directly; the quorum is the nearest in-silico case the
program has built, three parties and a threshold, not a price index and a thousand traders. Reading the
market through the quorum is an interpretation of a computed result, not a computed model of a market,
and the two-rooms split from the first post illustrates the same structure without running it. The
computed claim is narrow and it is real: a determination that reads everyone but tracks no one in
particular can still be an aggregate.

## Specialization Tightens the Tie and Never Closes the Seam

There is a natural objection here, and Hegel's §198 is where I want to meet it. The objection says the
division of labor is going somewhere. As specialization deepens, each worker depends on more and more
others, the web thickens, and surely at some point thick enough dependence becomes a whole. The organism
is the proof of concept: a body is a triumph of the division of labor, heart and liver and nerve each
doing one thing for all the rest, and a body is as irreducible a whole as there is — the intuition IIT
trades on, though no organism is modeled here. Why should the
specialized economy not be climbing the same ladder toward the same result?

Because the two specializations are coupled in different ways. In the body, the liver and the heart are
bound by specific reciprocal maintenance: the heart's output is not a public number the liver reads off a
ticker, it is blood the liver in particular receives and returns in particular, and cutting the coupling
costs both organs the function they shared. The parts are held by what each does *for this other*, and no
shared scalar stands in for the coupling, so no partition preserves it. In the specialized economy, the
shoemaker and the grain farmer are bound by the market: the farmer does not need this shoemaker, he needs
shoes, and he reaches them through price, a public variable that summarizes the whole supply and forgets
which shoemaker made them. Deepen the division of labor as far as you like and this stays true. A modern
supply chain runs tens of thousands of specialized firms, each helpless without the others, and it can
still be split into regional chains, re-sourced, routed around a failure, because the coordination rides
on prices and contracts — variables every node reads and none owns. The dependence is extreme and the
seam is intact. Specialization made the members need each other more and did not make them members.

So the intensity of interdependence is orthogonal to wholeness, and this is what Hegel's §198 already
contains. He watched the division of labor deepen and the work grow mechanical, and he did not read the
deepening as an ascent toward the state. He read it as the external state perfecting itself as external —
more binding, more abstract, and no closer to being a unity its members inhabit. The market's answer to
"how do I get what I need from a thousand strangers" is a public price, and a unity carried by a public
price lives outside the parts. The organism's answer is a specific circulation no member could read off a
number. Both the criterion and Hegel tell the two apart by the same feature: not how much the parts
depend on each other, but whether a common variable can carry the dependence or a specific irreducible
constraint must.

## The Other Register: What Civil Society Does to People

Hegel's verdict that civil society is the external state is a claim about its *form* — the kind of unity
it has. It is not the complaint he spends the most pages on. His heavier complaint is about what civil
society *does to people*, a different claim in a different currency, and in Hegel the two are joined at a
hinge my criterion cannot see.

Read the pages and the ethical charge is unmistakable. Civil society "affords a spectacle of extravagance
and misery as well as of the physical and ethical corruption common to both" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §185).
The system of needs, left to itself, does not merely fail to be one thing. It polarizes, and the
polarization is driven by the same engine that drives the wealth — the multiplication and refinement of
needs that moves the whole system forward is also what drives its lower edge under (García Mills, 2024).
At §243 Hegel names the two sides of one process: as association through needs universalizes, wealth
accumulates on one side while, on the other, rises "the dependence and want of the class which is tied to
such work," with a consequent "inability to feel and enjoy the wider freedoms, and particularly the
spiritual advantages, of civil society" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §243). Push the process to its end and, at
§244, "when a large mass of people sinks below the level of a certain standard of living . . . that
feeling of right, integrity, and honour which comes from supporting oneself by one's own activity and
work is lost," and what is left, in Hegel's word for it, is a rabble (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §244). The rabble, for
Hegel, is not defined by poverty as such. "Poverty in itself does not reduce people to a rabble," he
adds; the rabble is made by "the disposition associated with poverty, by inward rebellion against the
rich, against society, the government, etc." (Hegel, 1821/1991a, Addition to §244). What civil society
produces at its lower edge is not just want but the loss of a feeling of right — people the system has
failed so specifically that they no longer recognize its claims on them.

That last point is where the two registers interlock in Hegel, and it cuts against the separation I am
about to draw. The rabble is defined by a *recognitive*
failure — the lost feeling of right, the inward rebellion — and recognition is Hegel's own upgrade
condition, his road out of civil society into the state. So the ethical deficiency and the structural
incompleteness are not two sealed compartments for Hegel; they meet at recognition. The rabble is where
civil society's failure to constitute a recognized ethical membership shows up as a lived pathology,
which is exactly why Hegel puts the corporation and the state next.

Hegel names the structural character of the failure directly at §245: "despite an excess of wealth,
civil society is not wealthy enough — i.e. its own distinct resources are not sufficient — to prevent an
excess of poverty and the formation of a rabble" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §245). The sentence is not saying
the distribution happened to come out badly, as bad luck might. It is saying the
deficiency is a deficiency of the *form* of provisioning: the system of needs, working as designed,
generates a class it cannot secure, and no larger stock of wealth by itself closes the gap. Frank Ruda's
monograph on the rabble is the standard treatment of this passage, and it reads the rabble the same way:
a structural side effect of civil society's own contradictory constitution, produced by the system
working as designed rather than by its accidental failure, and severe enough that it drives Hegel's own
argument toward the corporation and the state (Ruda, 2011). I follow that diagnosis; the question I am
adding is narrower than Ruda's, and comes next.

What comes apart, then, is not something Hegel holds apart — it is what *my test* can register. The
criterion reads partition-survival and brackets recognition, so on the criterion distribution and
structure are orthogonal even where Hegel binds them. A market engineered to distribute perfectly would
remain, on my criterion, exactly as external as before, because externality is a fact about where the
unity sits, not about how the proceeds are shared. Run the partition test on the system of needs and it
reports "external unity, aggregate," and it reports the identical thing whether the distribution is just
or monstrous, whether the lowest members flourish or fall into the rabble, because distribution and
standing are the variables the test brackets.

I should say what that costs, because it is not nothing and it is not comfortable. My instrument returns
the same verdict on a civil society that feeds its lowest members and on one that grinds them into
Hegel's rabble — and the pauperization is the thing Hegel spends the most pages on, the thing that would
have mattered most to anyone actually living in the system of needs. There is a real sense in which the
test is deaf to the loudest thing in the room. I do not think that makes the structural verdict false;
externality and injustice are different facts, and a criterion earns its keep partly by refusing to
collapse them. But I would not want the cleanness of "external unity, aggregate" to read as indifference
to which civil society a person has to live in. The measure brackets the pauperization. I do
not, and the bracketing is a cost I am paying for a narrow, decidable answer, not a discovery that the
suffering does not matter.

## Kain and the Remedy in the Other Register

Philip Kain has written the paper that keeps this distinction honest. The standard line on Hegel and
civil society is Marxist, and Kain states it in order to reject it: "On what might be called a Marxist
reading, Hegel's analysis of civil society accurately recognizes a necessary tendency toward a
polarization of classes and the pauperization of the proletariat, a problem for which Hegel, however, has
no solution" (Kain, 2015, p. 43). That "no solution" belongs to the reading Kain is refuting, not to his own
conclusion. His own thesis runs the other way: "The present paper tries to show how it is plausible to
understand Hegel as proposing a solution, one that is similar to that of social democrats, and one that
could actually work" (Kain, 2015, p. 43). Civil society left entirely to itself cannot cure its own
polarization, on Kain's reading, but civil society is never left entirely to itself in Hegel's text: it
comes bound up with institutions above the market — the police in the old broad sense of public
administration, the corporations that organize a trade, and the state standing over both — and Kain
argues that with their assistance the deficiency at §245 is workable (Kain, 2015). Herzog's comparison of
Smith and Hegel lands on the same reading of where the deficiency sits: Hegel treats poverty in a market
economy as structural rather than incidental, which is exactly why redress has to come from institutions
standing above the market rather than from the market correcting itself (Herzog, 2013). Two independent
readings of Hegel's economics agree that §245 names a defect the market cannot fix from inside; they
disagree, if at all, only about how confidently the institutions above it can fix it.

Kain's remedy is a remedy in the ethical register: the police, the corporations, and the redistributive
hand of the state keep members from falling into the rabble, secure standing and livelihood, and repair
the failure of distribution and freedom §245 names. Here I add a claim that is mine laid over Kain's,
not his own: that working the deficiency changes the distribution without touching where the unity sits.
Kain argues the deficiency is workable; I am adding that a workable remedy leaves the market's *formal*
status — external unity — just as it was. A civil society with a strong welfare apparatus and strong
corporations still coordinates its economy through the market, still binds its members by mutual need,
still carries its economic unity as a resultant they produce and consult from outside. Fixing the
distribution does not fix the externality, because the two are different defects.

The case that tests this hardest is a Nordic social democracy, which distributes about as well as a
market economy has anywhere: strong unions, a deep welfare state, public authorities that keep almost no
one in material want. In the register of the ethical complaint, this is close to Hegel's own remedy
realized. Read against my criterion — and this is a reading, not a run, since no form in the corpus
models a Nordic economy — it looks unchanged: the economy still coordinates through markets, still binds
its members by mutual need, still carries its unity in prices every member reads from outside. A just
distribution repairs the ethical deficiency and leaves the structural externality where it was. (One
caution the Nordic case invites: whether a welfare state actually empties Hegel's rabble is contestable,
since the rabble is defined by disposition — inward rebellion, lost feeling of right — not by the poverty
line, and precarity can reproduce the disposition amid provision. The case needs only that material want
is nearly gone, which is the part that is true.)

I am not the first to walk the same arc from Smith through the system of needs to the rabble.
Lazarus's recent reading of that arc gets there by a route close enough to require saying so:
Smith's ontological individualism against Hegel's ontology of labor and recognition, the rabble read as
the place that ontology breaks (Lazarus, 2025). Lazarus's boundary is recognitive — an argument inside
the same register Kain and Ruda work in, about what kind of social being the market does and does not
constitute for its members. Mine is a causal-partition boundary, about what a cut costs a set of coupled
parts. The two arcs run alongside each other on the identical text and do not compete for the same
ground: recognition is a claim about self-relating spirit, and nothing in the partition test measures it
or needs to. Naming Lazarus here marks exactly where my move differs from his, not a concession that mine
has already been made.

## Same Side, Two Boundaries

The two verdicts land civil society on the same side of a line, and they draw the line differently, and
that difference is the thing worth ending on.

Even the word "external" is doing double duty, and the coincidence is the point. Hegel calls the market
external because its unity is unwilled — a universal produced behind the members' backs, its parts
standing on their own account, related but not constituted. My criterion calls it external because a
partition survives it. On the modern market those two grounds land the same verdict. They are not the
same ground. An organism's unity is unwilled too, and no cut preserves it; the market is simply the case
where Hegel's "external" and mine happen to agree, and their agreement is a finding, not a definition.

So the upgrade conditions differ, and this is where the roads part for good. What my criterion counts as
crossing the boundary is a change in causal structure: a coupling no partition preserves, a unity that
stops being a shared scalar and becomes an irreducible constraint. What Hegel counts as crossing it is
the external state *raised* into a unity its members inhabit as their own substance, held by mutual
recognition and ethical life — freedom that knows itself as freedom in the whole. His condition is
recognition, a claim about self-relating spirit. A causal criterion does not entail it and cannot stand
in for it.

That is the cleanest case the series has. Reading the political economists, Hegel saw universal
interdependence and refused to call it a whole; the partition test refuses too. The refusals coincide,
and the reasons do not, and on civil society in 1821 the gap between them is small enough to see the
agreement through and real enough that mistaking it for identity would falsify both sides at once —
Hegel, by scoring him for landing where the test happens to agree, and the test, by loaning it an ethical
content it does not have. My criterion and Hegel put civil society on the same side of the line. They do not mean
the same thing by the line, and the corporation is the first place that difference starts to cost
