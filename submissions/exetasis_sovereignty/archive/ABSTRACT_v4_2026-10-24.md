**Title (DRAFTED):** After Literacy: Algorithmacy and the Conditions of Sovereignty under Algorithmic Coordination

---

[DRAFTED by Claude: opening paragraph]
Under its theme "AI, Politics, and the Transformation of Sovereignty," the special issue asks about "algorithmic governance and democratic accountability." This paper takes up that pairing as a question: what must citizens have in order to hold algorithmic authority to account? Democratic states answered it with literacy, which let citizens read what authority wrote, and with institutions that let them act on what they read. Algorithmic governance offers neither. I argue that a new competence, algorithmacy, is necessary for accountability under algorithmic governance, and that it becomes sovereignty only through collective standing within mediations that citizens cannot leave.

[Roger's text, verbatim, 24 Oct]
Democratic theory has historically linked popular sovereignty to civic competence. In fifth-century Greece, where most free citizens "could apparently read the laws," democracy was "from the beginning associated with widespread literacy" (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 332). Contemporary AI scholarship maintains this premise by advocating for "technology literacy" in response to algorithmic feeds (Son, 2022, pp. 13, 16) and by emphasizing civic education to restore epistemic agency (Coeckelbergh, 2023). However, the history of literacy shows that reading/writing, while historically necessary for sovereignty, are no longer sufficient.

Goody and Watt (1963, p. 333) noted that the "dream of an 'educated democracy'" was never realized, as public schooling expanded primarily under nondemocratic regimes that used education to instill obedience (Paglayan, 2022, p. 1242). In these regimes, literate subjects lacked institutional standing, not qualifications. This historical evidence challenges Mill's (1861, ch. VIII) assertion that "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement." It confirms that reading enabled sovereignty only where rule of law, a free press, and franchised citizens demand public accountability.

Algorithmic coordination does not reproduce the civic infrastructure necessary for democratic sovereignty. Instead, it operates through "a triangular structure" with the platform operator at the apex (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Within this triadic architecture, machine-learning opacity evades literacy (Burrell, 2016). Sorting mechanisms are co-optive, as operators "enroll users and providers in a system of ratings" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Furthermore, authority issues no public decrees, resulting in an algorithmic governmentality that "produces no subjectivation" (Rouvroy & Berns, 2013, pp. 173–174, my translation), leaving citizens with nothing stable to interpret.

Functioning within this structure requires algorithmacy: the competence to infer intermediary evaluations, encode intent, and adapt as predictive models evolve. However, algorithm literacy is "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions" (Gagrčin et al., 2026, p. 442) and offers only a competitive advantage over peers unless paired with institutional standing. This standing constitutes coordinative sovereignty, where market exit is ineffective because "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025, p. 163), making collective voice the only viable democratic recourse.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy; literacy; sovereignty; algorithmic coordination; opacity; co-optation; exit and voice; coordinative sovereignty
