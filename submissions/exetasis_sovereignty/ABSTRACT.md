**Title (DRAFTED; alternatives at the foot):** After Literacy: Algorithmacy and the Conditions of Sovereignty under Algorithmic Coordination

---

Popular sovereignty has always depended on a competence its holders had to acquire. Goody and Watt (1963: 332) state the link: "Democracy as we know it, then, is from the beginning associated with widespread literacy." A citizen who could read the laws could set one decree beside another and make an official answer for the difference. Recent work on AI keeps the premise: Son (2022: 13) holds that "literacy is the necessary condition for democracy" and prescribes "technology literacy" against algorithmic recommendation, and Coeckelbergh (2022) asks education to restore citizens' epistemic agency. The question is whether a literacy of the machine still suffices once the machine coordinates people rather than informs them.

I argue that it does not, and literacy's own history shows why. Literacy was necessary for democratic sovereignty and never sufficient. Goody and Watt (1963: 333) concede that the "dream of an 'educated democracy'" went unrealized, and Paglayan (2022) shows that mass schooling spread mostly under nondemocratic regimes, to teach obedience. The claim concerns exercising sovereignty, not qualifying for it; it rejects Mill's rule that "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement." Reading became sovereignty only when published law, a press and the franchise gave readers standing.

Those conditions are what algorithmic coordination removes. It is triadic: Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024: 14) find "a triangular structure" with the platform operator at the apex. It is opaque: Burrell (2016) separates the opacity that code literacy addresses from the opacity of machine learning itself. It is co-optive: participants' own dealings supply the data that ranks them. Rouvroy and Berns (2013: 173–174) draw the consequence: algorithmic governmentality "produces no subjectivation" (my translation). Literacy read what authority wrote. This authority writes nothing to read.

What it demands instead is algorithmacy — the capacity to infer how an opaque intermediary evaluates both sides of an interaction, to encode intent into the signals it accepts, and to track its rules as they shift. Algorithmacy stands to algorithmic coordination as literacy stood to the written state: necessary, and insufficient alone. Gagrčin et al. (2026: 442), reviewing 169 studies, call algorithm literacy "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions." Exercised alone, algorithmacy buys rank against rivals. It becomes sovereignty only inside an institution, which I call coordinative sovereignty: standing to contest and co-govern a mediation one cannot bypass. Where parties could deal directly, exit disciplines the gatekeeper. Where the mediation is necessary, exit fails — "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025: 163) — and only collective voice remains.

Collective voice answers the call's question about the limits of "algorithmic sovereignty" by moving its bearer. Verhulst and G. Otero (2026) measure Europe's sovereignty by "who has the power to shape, govern, and challenge these systems"; this paper locates it in the coordinated worker, seller or citizen. The full paper develops the genealogy, the bypass test, and institutions, such as data trusts and collective bargaining, that join competence to standing.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy; literacy; sovereignty; algorithmic coordination; opacity; co-optation; exit and voice
