## Abstract

Democratic theory has long tied popular sovereignty to civic competence. In fifth-century Greece, where most free citizens "could apparently read the laws," democracy was "from the beginning associated with widespread literacy" (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 332). Current responses to algorithmic power repeat the premise by calling for technology literacy and civic education to restore epistemic agency (Son, 2022; Coeckelbergh, 2023). The history of literacy shows the premise to be incomplete. Public schooling expanded mainly under nondemocratic regimes that used it to teach obedience (Paglayan, 2022, p. 1242). Literate subjects in those regimes lacked institutional standing, not qualifications. Reading enabled sovereignty only where law, a free press and an enfranchised public could compel authority to account.

Algorithmic coordination reproduces this gap in sharper form. It operates through "a triangular structure" with the platform operator at the apex (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Its machine-learning opacity evades ordinary literacy (Burrell, 2016). Its authority issues no public decrees, so that algorithmic governmentality "produces no subjectivation" (Rouvroy & Berns, 2013, pp. 173–174, our translation). Operating within it requires algorithmacy: the competence to infer intermediary evaluations, encode intent and adapt as predictive models evolve. Yet algorithm literacy is "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions" (Gagrčin et al., 2026, p. 442), and on its own it yields only positional advantage over peers. Exit offers no remedy either, since "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025, p. 163). Collective voice is what remains.

Between competence and voice lies a step that literacy policy and its algorithmic successors leave unexamined: the formation of the collective that could speak. We argue, drawing on network science, that the capacity for collective mobilisation is a property of the structure of relations in a population. Mobilisation that requires social reinforcement crosses between communities only through wide bridges of multiple converging ties (Centola & Macy, 2007). A minimal simulation of threshold contagion on a modular network illustrates the consequence. Competence distributed at random leaves mobilisation confined largely to its community of origin, while the same budget of competence placed at bridge positions carries it across most of the population. Recommendation systems shape which ties form and where bridges lie, with no political intent required (Santos et al., 2021). The intermediary therefore takes part in producing, or failing to produce, the collective subject that might contest it.

We call the missing condition coordinative sovereignty: voice within the coordinative environment that binds the coordinated, sustained through relations that no single intermediary controls. It is the condition for sovereignty as such. Under algorithmic coordination no individual sovereignty remains to be protected; competence, position and exit are what an individual holds, and none of them is sovereignty. A population is sovereign only where it has voice within the mediation, and it has voice only where the relations that carry collective action can form. The concept stands in the lineage of constituent power and names what neither state technological sovereignty nor individual autonomy captures. AI-literacy policies, like literacy campaigns before them, are necessary. They cannot by themselves secure self-government, because the conditions under which relations form are themselves a question of sovereignty.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy; literacy; coordinative sovereignty; algorithmic coordination; complex contagion; network structure; constituent power; exit and voice
