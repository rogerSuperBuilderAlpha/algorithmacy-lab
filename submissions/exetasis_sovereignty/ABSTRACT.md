**Title (DRAFTED):** After Literacy: Algorithmacy and the Conditions of Sovereignty under Algorithmic Coordination

---

Democratic theory ties popular sovereignty to an acquired civic competence. In fifth-century Greece most free citizens "could apparently read the laws," so democracy was "from the beginning associated with widespread literacy" (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 332). AI scholarship keeps the premise. Literacy remains "the necessary condition for democracy," now prescribed as "technology literacy" against algorithmic recommendation (Son, 2022, pp. 13, 16), and education remains the remedy for lost epistemic agency (Coeckelbergh, 2023). A competence modeled on reading cannot suffice once machines coordinate human action rather than inform it.

Literacy's own history shows why: it was necessary for sovereignty and never sufficient. The "dream of an 'educated democracy'" went unrealized (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 333), and public schooling emerged and mostly expanded under nondemocratic regimes that used it to teach obedience (Paglayan, 2022, p. 1242). The literate subjects of those regimes lacked standing, not qualification. That evidence rejects Mill's rule that "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement" (Mill, 1861, ch. VIII). Reading became sovereignty only where codified law, a press, and the franchise let readers demand accountability.

Algorithmic coordination reproduces none of that infrastructure. Platforms coordinate through "a triangular structure" with the platform operator at the apex (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Within it, machine-learning opacity eludes the remedies of code literacy (Burrell, 2016); sorting is co-optive, since operators "enroll users and providers in a system of ratings" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14); and authority issues no public decree, since algorithmic governmentality "produces no subjectivation" (Rouvroy & Berns, 2013, pp. 173–174, my translation). Democratic literacy engaged what authority wrote. Algorithmic coordination writes nothing stable enough to read.

Operating within this structure requires algorithmacy: the competence to infer how an intermediary evaluates transactions, to encode intent into signals it accepts, and to adapt as its models shift. Algorithmacy stands to platform governance as literacy stood to the state, indispensable and politically inert on its own. Algorithm literacy is "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions" (Gagrčin et al., 2026, p. 442). Because platform sorting ranks participants against one another, individual algorithmacy yields only advantage over peers. It becomes sovereignty only through institutional standing, which I call coordinative sovereignty: the collective power to contest and co-govern a mediation that participants cannot bypass. There exit fails, because "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025, p. 163). Collective voice is what remains.

Grounding sovereignty in collective voice relocates its bearer. Verhulst and Gonzalez Otero (2026, p. 1) fault Europe's 2026 sovereignty package for addressing chiefly dependency, since the crucial factor is "who has the power to shape, govern, and challenge these systems"; they answer with a European stewardship architecture. This paper locates that power in coordinated workers, sellers, and citizens, inside mediations they cannot leave. The full paper traces the genealogy from literacy to algorithmacy, specifies a bypass test for compulsory mediation, and evaluates mechanisms such as data trusts and collective bargaining that convert competence into standing.

**Keywords:** algorithmacy; literacy; sovereignty; algorithmic coordination; opacity; co-optation; exit and voice; coordinative sovereignty
