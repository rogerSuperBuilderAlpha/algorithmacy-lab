### Proposed Title
**Coordinative Sovereignty: Triadic Mediation, Algorithmacy, and Political Standing under Artificial Intelligence**

### Final Abstract (467 words)
Prevailing accounts of artificial intelligence and sovereignty share a single false premise. They assume an autonomous agent must stand apart from the mediating system. Liberal and classical theory identifies sovereignty with independence and exit, following Bodin on indivisible authority and Mill on the private sphere (Bodin, 1576/1992; Mill, 1859/1989). Statist and regulatory accounts of digital sovereignty transpose territorial jurisdiction and bureaucratic literacy onto computing infrastructure through domestic clouds, data localization, and statutory disclosure mandates (Floridi, 2020; Pohle & Thiel, 2020). Both traditions treat an algorithmic system as either an external tool or a territory to enclose. Yet a platform enterprise or state agency can satisfy every requirement of infrastructural ownership and written disclosure while leaving the workers, sellers, and citizens coordinated through its models without any share in the determinations that rank, route, price, and score them.

Algorithmic systems alter the problem of sovereignty because an interested third party stands between interacting actors, evaluates both sides at once, and sets the conditions under which each side recognizes its options and acts (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Oral orders govern through speech, and bureaucratic states govern through written law that a literate public can read and contest (Habermas, 1996; Scott, 1998). An algorithmic intermediary governs instead through formal co-optation (Selznick, 1949), enrolling participants as positional rivals whose own dealings supply the behavioral telemetry that scores and disciplines them. High-dimensional machine learning models defeat text-based oversight through mathematical opacity, unannounced parameter recalibration, and strategic reactivity (Burrell, 2016; Espeland & Sauder, 2007; Rahman, 2021). Coordinating through such an intermediary requires algorithmacy: the capacity to infer how an opaque system evaluates both sides of an interaction, encode intent into the signals it accepts, and track its rules as they shift. Competence alone confers no standing.

When an algorithmic intermediary performs integrative work that coordinated parties cannot reproduce on their own, autonomy takes the form of coordinative sovereignty: institutional standing to contest arbitrary determinations and hold an enforceable share in governing an unbypassable mediation. Four traditions supply its normative terms: republican non-domination (Pettit, 1997), relational autonomy (Nedelsky, 2011), democratic involvement (Markell, 2008), and commons governance (Ostrom, 1990). Three moves link this construct to a formal diagnostic. First, an edge-level bypass counterfactual restores a direct tie between the mediated parties to separate contingent gatekeepers, where exit and interoperability suffice (Hirschman, 1970), from necessary mediators, where only institutional voice works. Second, a cooperative game over integrated causal structure measures standing by a party's Shapley pivotality and the security of that standing by its core membership, showing why a lone mediator captures the veto position and the largest share of integrative value. Third, the resulting priority rule specifies how collective bargaining, fiduciary data trusts, and platform cooperatives rewrite the game to move coordinated actors' claims into the core, converting individual algorithmacy into durable political standing.

**Keywords:** coordinative sovereignty; digital sovereignty; algorithmacy; triadic mediation; non-domination; relational autonomy; exit and voice
