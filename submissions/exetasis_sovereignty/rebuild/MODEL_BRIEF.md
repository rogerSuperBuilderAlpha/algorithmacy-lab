## Thesis and its shape
- **The thesis:** "certain algorithms can facilitate new relationships of domination by sustaining a socio-technical system in which the owners and managers of a company dominate workers" (A4). It is restated as a definition in I22: "algorithmic domination occurs when an individual is subjected to an uncontrolled power, the operations of which are determined by an algorithm."
- **Its shape:** a coined concept, defined by necessary and sufficient conditions, then applied to one case class. The paper hedges at the front ("can facilitate", "certain algorithms") and concedes continuity in I28: "This is not a radically new form of power, but an augmentation of existing capacities."

## Contribution moves, in order
1. A theoretical account of a named concept (A3, I11).
2. A framework for applying it to a case class (A3, I23).
3. A structural claim: domination lies in the power relation, not in the technology (I25–I26, I41).
4. An alternative that is possible (platform co-operatives, I42).
5. Extension to other cases (A5, I43).

## Move vocabulary (read off this model)
- **field-claim:** a plain present-perfect statement of change ("have changed how people work").
- **literature-concern:** "Empirical studies … have raised concerns about …"; "an emerging critical literature has called into question …".
- **gap:** "While X have received widespread consideration, political philosophers have so far paid less attention to …".
- **concept-offer:** "Drawing on …, we offer …"; "In this article, we develop the concept of …".
- **scope:** "X can occur in a variety of different domains, but we focus here on …".
- **example-run:** "Consider the following examples." Then three short, concrete, cited cases, then "What these examples have begun to point to is …".
- **question:** "This raises the question of whether …"
- **thesis:** "We argue that …"
- **beneath:** "Our analysis emphasizes that beneath the appearance of … lies …".
- **concession:** "This is not a radically new form of power, but …".
- **stakes:** "Technology companies claim … However, if we are right, what they can enable is …".
- **distinctive:** "X can give rise to distinctive …"; "X is also distinctive in …".
- **roadmap:** "This article proceeds as follows. First, we … We then … In the following section, we … Finally, we conclude by …".
- **implication:** "This analysis has implications for …".

## Register
- **Verbs (30):** have changed, have raised, offer, argue, facilitate, sustaining, dominate, has implications, is deployed, has led, doubled, has resulted, are employed, promise, has called into question, reproduce, exacerbate, alleviate, have contributed, have paid less attention, develop, address, provide, focus, report, point to, raises the question, constitute, occurs, emphasizes, increase, weaken, embed, claim, enable, give rise, allow, proceeds, draw on, identify, define, explain, examine, conclude. Every one is plain.
- **Metaphor:** almost none. The rare figures are "a hidden army of 'microworkers'", "Silicon Valley mythology", "the sham behind their claims", "beneath the appearance … lies". Each sits in a stakes or "beneath" sentence, never in a definition.
- **Key term:** it repeats "algorithmic domination" without variation, 9 times in the introduction. It never finds a synonym.
- **Abstraction level:** middle. Definitions are formal ("A is dominated by B if and only if …", p. 590), and examples are numerical and concrete ("just nine seconds to process a package"; "US$2–3 an hour").
- **Naming:** it coins one term, defines it, and then names everything else plainly ("bosses", "workers", "the app").
- **What it never does:**
  - no aphorism, and no paragraph-final epigram;
  - no "actually";
  - no paired abstract nouns for cadence;
  - no colon-staged reveal in its own voice (its colons introduce lists or quotations).

  It uses one rhetorical question, and it is the research question (I21). It uses "Importantly," (p. 590), and the house style bans that word; see the precedence notes below.
- **Citations:** author–date, often at the end of a sentence ("(Kitchin, 2017)", "(Huws 2020: 4)"). Scholars are the subject when they find something ("Rosenblat and Stark (2016) found that", p. 593).
- **Person:** "we" for the authors' moves ("we offer", "we argue", "we focus", "we define").

## Precedence notes
1. **Person.** The model writes "we", while the author is solo. The house style (repo `CLAUDE.md`, rule 1) says first person marks authorial labour, so the rebuild writes "I argue", "I develop", "I call".
2. **"Importantly,".** The house style bans it and the venue is not unanimous with the model, so the house style wins and the word is not used.
3. **Citation position.** The model often ends sentences on a citation. The house style prefers the scholar as subject. The rebuild follows the model where it reports a fact ("(Paglayan, 2022: 1242)") and puts the scholar first where the scholar is doing something ("Goody and Watt (1963: 333) observe"), as the model itself does on p. 593.
