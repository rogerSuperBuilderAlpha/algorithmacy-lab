Democratic theory has tied popular sovereignty to a civic competence that citizens must acquire. In fifth-century Greece most free citizens "could apparently read the laws," and democracy was "from the beginning associated with widespread literacy" (Goody and Watt, 1963: 332). Recent discussions of artificial intelligence keep this premise: Son (2022: 13, 16) treats literacy as "the necessary condition for democracy" and asks for "technology literacy," and Coeckelbergh (2023) looks to education to protect epistemic agency.

Political philosophers have paid less attention to whether a reading competence can secure sovereignty once algorithms coordinate people rather than inform them. In this paper, I develop the concept of algorithmacy to address this question. I argue that literacy was necessary for democratic sovereignty but never sufficient, and that algorithmacy stands in the same relation to sovereignty under algorithmic coordination. Algorithmacy is the competence to infer how an intermediary evaluates transactions, to encode intent into signals the intermediary accepts, and to adapt as the intermediary's models change.

Goody and Watt (1963: 333) observe that the "dream of an 'educated democracy'" was never realized, and public schooling emerged and mostly expanded under nondemocratic regimes that used it to teach obedience (Paglayan, 2022: 1242). The literate subjects of those regimes lacked standing, not qualification. This calls into question Mill's rule that "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement" (1861: ch. VIII). Only where codified law, a free press and the franchise existed could literate citizens hold authorities to account.

Algorithmic coordination provides none of these institutions. Platforms operate through "a triangular structure" with the operator at the apex (Stark and Vanden Broeck, 2024: 14). Machine-learning opacity resists the remedies of code literacy (Burrell, 2016), and operators co-opt participants by enrolling "users and providers in a system of ratings" (Stark and Vanden Broeck, 2024: 14). Authority issues no public decree, and algorithmic governmentality "produces no subjectivation" (Rouvroy and Berns, 2013: 173–174, my translation), so a literate public has nothing stable to read.

Algorithmacy meets the same limit that literacy met. Gagrčin et al. (2026: 442) find algorithm literacy to be "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions." Because platforms rank participants against each other, individual algorithmacy yields only advantage over peers. Algorithmacy becomes sovereignty only through institutional standing, which I call coordinative sovereignty: the collective capacity to contest and co-govern a mediation that participants cannot bypass. In such a mediation exit fails, since "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025: 163), and collective voice remains.

Verhulst and Gonzalez Otero (2026: 1) locate digital sovereignty in "who has the power to shape, govern, and challenge these systems" and answer with a European "stewardship architecture." If I am right, that power lies with the workers, sellers and citizens inside mediations they cannot exit. In the full paper I trace the genealogy from literacy to algorithmacy, specify a bypass test for compulsory mediation, and examine data trusts and collective bargaining as institutions that could turn competence into standing.
