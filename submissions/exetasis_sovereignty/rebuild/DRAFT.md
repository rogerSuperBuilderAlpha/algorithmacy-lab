Democratic theory links popular sovereignty to acquiring civic competence. In fifth-century Greece, most free citizens "could apparently read the laws," so democracy was "from the beginning associated with widespread literacy" (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 332). Contemporary AI scholarship maintains this premise. Literacy remains "the necessary condition for democracy," now reframed as "technology literacy" in response to algorithmic recommendation (Son, 2022, pp. 13, 16), and education is still seen as the remedy for diminished epistemic agency (Coeckelbergh, 2023). However, a competence modeled on reading is insufficient when machines coordinate human action rather than merely inform it.

The history of literacy demonstrates its necessity for sovereignty, but also its insufficiency. The "dream of an 'educated democracy'" was never realized (Goody & Watt, 1963, p. 333), and public schooling often emerged and expanded under nondemocratic regimes that used it primarily to instill obedience (Paglayan, 2022, p. 1242). The literate subjects of these regimes lacked political standing, not qualification. This evidence challenges Mill's assertion that "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement" (Mill, 1861, ch. VIII). Reading conferred sovereignty only where codified law, a free press, and the franchise enabled readers to demand accountability.

Algorithmic coordination fails to reproduce the institutional infrastructure that supported democratic literacy. Platforms operate through "a triangular structure" with the platform operator at the apex (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14). Within this structure, the opacity of machine learning resists the solutions offered by code literacy (Burrell, 2016); sorting mechanisms are co-optive, as operators "enroll users and providers in a system of ratings" (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024, p. 14); and authority does not issue public decrees, since algorithmic governmentality "produces no subjectivation" (Rouvroy & Berns, 2013, pp. 173–174, my translation). Democratic literacy engaged with the written outputs of authority. In contrast, algorithmic coordination produces nothing sufficiently stable to be read.

Functioning within this structure requires algorithmacy: the competence to infer how an intermediary evaluates transactions, to encode intent into acceptable signals, and to adapt as models evolve. Algorithmacy relates to platform governance as literacy did to the state, being indispensable yet politically inert in isolation. Algorithm literacy constitutes "but one facet of competences needed to improve working conditions" (Gagrčin et al., 2026, p. 442). Since platform sorting ranks participants competitively, individual algorithmacy provides only relative advantage. Sovereignty emerges only through institutional standing, here termed coordinative sovereignty: the collective capacity to contest and co-govern a mediation that participants cannot avoid. In such contexts, exit is ineffective, as "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either" (Lazar, 2025, p. 163). Only collective voice remains.

Grounding sovereignty in collective voice shifts the locus of authority. Verhulst and Gonzalez Otero (2026, p. 1) criticize Europe's 2026 sovereignty package for focusing primarily on dependency, arguing that the central issue is "who has the power to shape, govern, and challenge these systems"; they propose a European stewardship architecture in response. This paper identifies that power within coordinated workers, sellers, and citizens embedded in mediations they cannot exit. The full paper traces the genealogy from literacy to algorithmacy, introduces a bypass test for compulsory mediation, and assesses mechanisms such as data trusts and collective bargaining that transform competence into institutional standing.
