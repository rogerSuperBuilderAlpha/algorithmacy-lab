## What the sources do to the thesis

### The first premise, that literacy is necessary for democratic sovereignty
- **Mill (1861), *Considerations on Representative Government*, ch. VIII.** This is the strongest classic statement, and also the most dangerous one. Verified passage:
  - Mill: "I regard it as wholly inadmissible that any person should participate in the suffrage without being able to read, write, and, I will add, perform the common operations of arithmetic."
  - Mill: "universal teaching must precede universal enfranchisement."

  For Mill, literacy is a **qualification for holding** the franchise. That is the literacy-test logic, and Bateman (2018) records its history. The paper needs Mill as the foil: **literacy as the competence for exercising sovereignty, not a test for holding it.**
- **Habermas (1962/1989):** the reading public that subjects authority to the test of argument. Read via an abstract only.
- **Goody & Watt (1963):** written rules can be compared, and Greek literacy came with a critical public. Held as a record only.
- **Anderson (1983/2006):** the print community. Read via an abstract only.
- **Nussbaum (2006) and Sen (1999):** read via abstracts only. The parent audit (O4) says neither states proportionality, so cite them only for "depends on".
- **Lipset (1959)** and **Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer (2007):** education and democracy correlate. Glaeser et al. offer a causal mechanism: schooling "raises the benefits of civic participation" (WP abstract).
- **Coeckelbergh (2022)** carries the necessity premise into AI. "Democratic theories assume that citizens have some form of political knowledge"; AI "risks to diminish the epistemic agency of citizens" (abstract). It sits closest to theme J7.

### The contest, that literacy did not cause democracy
- **Paglayan (2022), verified from the full text, p. 1242:** public school systems' "emergence and most of their expansion usually took place under nondemocratic regimes". The motive was obedience, as the 2022 abstract and the 2024 book both put it.
- **Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson & Yared (2005):** "The conventional wisdom views high levels of education as a prerequisite for democracy." The correlation "disappear[s] once we look at within-country variation" (WP abstract).
- **Graff (1979/1991):** the literacy myth. **Street (1984), Collins & Blot (2003), Halverson (1992):** against the great divide. All are read via abstracts only.

**How the thesis survives.** The contest is aimed at a **causal** claim, that literacy produces democracy. Roger's claim is about **necessity**: no exercise of sovereignty through a medium without the medium's competence. That claim is compatible with Paglayan and Acemoglu, and it gains from them. Literacy was necessary and never sufficient. It spread for order rather than emancipation, and it became sovereignty only once institutions such as published law, a press and the franchise gave it standing. That is the same "necessary, not sufficient" structure the paper needs for algorithmacy, and it answers Rouvroy & Berns.
