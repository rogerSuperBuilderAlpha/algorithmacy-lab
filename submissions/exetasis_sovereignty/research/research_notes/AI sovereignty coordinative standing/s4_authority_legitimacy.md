# S4: Authority, legitimacy and the normative core of standing inside an unexitable mediation

Compiled 2026-10-24 for the *Exetasis* "AI and Sovereignty" abstract (J1 foundations of political authority; J2 algorithmic governance and democratic accountability; J3 transparency, explainability, procedural fairness, public vs private governance).

The claim under test comes from `coordinative_sovereignty.md` ("What coordinative sovereignty is") and AGENDA.md item 6. Coordinative sovereignty is "standing within the coordination, neither independence from the mediator nor command of the infrastructure". It is also "the power to shape the terms on which one is mediated, to contest the mediator's arbitrary power, and to hold a share in the governance of the arrangement one cannot leave". The central claim is that it "leaves actors more sovereign than the preservation of independence does". AGENDA item 6 adds the proviso that demanding exit is right "because the gate is contingent".

## Q1. Platform political philosophy: Lazar on what legitimates intermediary power; Gowder; Aytac; Benn & Lazar; Vredenburgh; Hasan; Cicerchia; Fourcade & Healy; Paul

### Takeaway
Lazar is the closest and strongest ally on the normative core. He says intermediary governing power must meet three tests: substantive justification ("what"), procedural legitimacy ("how") and proper authority ("who"). He rejects ease of exit or tacit consent as grounds of authority, and he locates authority in democratic authorisation by the governed community plus a real possibility of resistance. He also anticipates the claim, so the abstract must say how coordinative sovereignty differs. Lazar asks what makes the *intermediary's* power legitimate. Coordinative sovereignty asks what standing the *coordinated actor* holds, and it adds the bypass diagnostic (necessary vs contingent mediator), which Lazar does not have. Gowder supports democratising the platform from within. Aytac and Muldoon & Raekstad support the domination diagnosis. Hasan contests the agent-centred footing.

**Lazar on the three legitimacy conditions.** All quotations are verbatim from the arXiv preprint.
- Cost–benefit is not enough: "we're not just weighing benefits and harms here; we're talking about the justification of governing power. To justify governing power, cost–benefit analysis is not enough." (§3.4, p. 22) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- The three questions: "All-things-considered justification of power therefore depends on it being exercised not only for the right ends but also in the right way by those with the right to do so. As well as answering the question of what power is used to do, we must also ask how is it being used? And by whom?" (§3.4, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Procedural legitimacy as reciprocal power. This is close to the construct's "terms that hold": "If A has power over B, but A must abide by standards of procedural legitimacy, then B also has power over A, since A's power is limited, and B can force A to comply with those limits." (§3.4, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Benevolence is insufficient: "Benevolent, procedurally legitimate authoritarianism remains authoritarian and is inadequate for governing." On the Oversight Board: "the Board's authority derives from a unilateral decision by (then) Facebook to create it. It lacks authority to govern." (§3.4, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- The source of authority: "the ultimate source of the authority to exercise power is the community subject to that power. The authority to exercise power must therefore derive from authorisation by that community through democratic procedures." (§3.4, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Lazar on terminology, useful for J1 vocabulary: "Most political philosophers refer to this as legitimacy, not authority. … legitimacy literally means constrained by rules, which is more obviously connected with procedural legitimacy than with the right to govern." (n. 89, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- The five underlying procedural values: "I suggest that the key underlying constraints on governing power are authorship, transparency, consistency, accountability, and resistibility." Procedural legitimacy "presupposes that the governed should be able to limit their rulers' power, holding them to strict rules, ensuring we can verify compliance with those rules and challenge them if they overstep." (§5, p. 30) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Contestation of norms, not only of cases: "Contestation and resistance, not just of particular judgements but of the norms they apply, are essential both for moral progress and for respecting our standing as free and equal members of the moral community." (§5, p. 33) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Collective self-determination, the closest match to "a share in governing the mediation": "We cannot collectively shape the shared terms of our social existence if algorithmic intermediaries unilaterally decide them for us. … If decided for us, not by us, we are not collectively self-determining." (§4, p. 28) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Lazar rejects ease of exit (§7.3, pp. 42–44). He treats it as an objection: "If you don't like how this corner of the Algorithmic City is governed, you can simply leave—or set up your own competitor". He answers with three arguments:
  1. Consent fails under externalities and for collective goods.
  2. "One's decision not to exit a regime only confers authority on its ruler if one has reasonable alternatives. For most of us, the costs of total exit from the Algorithmic City are too significant for our decision to remain to imply morally effective consent." Also: "a choice between awful regimes cannot confer authority on either."
  3. "Tacit consent is a fragile foundation for proper authority because it is consistent with A telling B, C, and D to 'take it or leave it'".
  — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Lazar's positive replacement for exit, which reads almost as coordinative sovereignty's "voice made effective": "Rather than grounding authority in tacit consent derived from the ease of exit, I think that governing with proper authority when the stakes are relatively high requires allowing people to resist your rule." Resistance "indirectly favours collective self-determination, since it relies on our having direct influence on our community's direction of travel rather than only indirect influence through the threat of exit." Then: "algorithmic intermediary power does not afford resistance. Algorithmic intermediaries need to be intentionally (re)designed to create this possibility. And to do this requires more than simply enabling people to move between different intermediaries" (§7.3, pp. 43–44) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- **A caution that cuts against the construct's exit proviso:** "While ease of exit might not play a significant role in justifying governing authority, it is a fundamental obstacle to successful governance. Some degree of central authority is a precondition of effective governance. This should provide a serious counterweight to attempts to resolve the pathologies of our current digital ecosystem through competition law and other cognate measures." (§7.3, p. 44) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Resistibility is structurally reduced in algorithmic governance: "the scope for resistibility is radically limited". Interstitial evasion by hackers does not count, because "the value of the Algorithmic City lies in how it mediates social relations at scale, and algorithmic intermediaries' strength is in coordinating collective action, corralling audiences and markets." (§5, p. 33) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Algorithmic power is not to be eliminated: "algorithmic power itself is not inherently objectionable. Indeed, when used to govern algorithmically-mediated relationships … algorithmic power is morally necessary." (§3.3, p. 22) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Stakes are graded by degree, scope and concentration. Governance is distinguished from "non-governance influence", and "procedural legitimacy and proper authority are fundamental when A adjudicates disputes between B and C, thus shaping power relations between them." (§7.2, pp. 39–40) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)

### Inferences
- **Support.** Lazar's §7.3 is the best single authority for the hinge "where exit is foreclosed, exit cannot ground legitimacy; only voice/resistance can". Several of his phrases line up with the construct:

  | Lazar | Coordinative sovereignty |
  |---|---|
  | "direct influence on our community's direction of travel rather than only indirect influence through the threat of exit" | voice made effective |
  | "B can force A to comply with those limits" | "terms that hold because the coordinated actors have the standing to make them hold" |

- **Anticipation (novelty risk).** Lazar already argues that exit is insufficient, that authority comes from the governed community, and that algorithmic intermediaries must be redesigned to afford resistance. The abstract cannot present "voice over exit for algorithmic intermediaries" as new. What I found no counterpart for, in Lazar's lecture or in Gowder's book (searched for exit/polycentric passages), is the bypass test: a structural diagnostic that sorts mediators into contingent gates (where exit or competition is the right remedy) and necessary mediators (where only voice is available). That diagnostic, together with the actor-side framing of *standing* rather than intermediary-side *legitimacy*, is the defensible novelty. This is a scoped gap statement, not a full novelty search.
- **Tension to manage.** Lazar's line that ease of exit is "a fundamental obstacle to successful governance" and a "counterweight" to competition-law remedies sits awkwardly with the construct's proviso that opening the bypass is right for contingent gates. The two can be reconciled. Lazar speaks of governance capacity; the construct speaks of the actor's sovereignty. But a referee may press the point.
- Gowder supports the institutional programme (polycentric, Ostrom-derived, participatory) but addresses platform executives and governments. The construct's actor-standing unit and its legal allocation of standing from outside (Paul) separate it from Gowder.
- Hasan is the main contradictor on the agency footing. If domination is structural, then "contest the mediator's arbitrary determinations" may misdescribe the remedy. A necessary mediator that no one can bypass is arguably a Hasan-type structure.

## Q2. The republican tradition: Pettit (1997, 2012), Lovett (2010), Markell (2008), Nedelsky (2011), Anderson (2017), Ostrom (1990), and a republican contradictor (Taylor 2017)

### Takeaway
The republican strand supports the construct's first condition: non-arbitrary power, made non-arbitrary by contestation. Markell supports the "share in governing" condition (involvement against usurpation). Nedelsky supports the relational frame, Anderson the claim that the right to quit is not freedom, and Ostrom the collective-choice institution. The sharpest contradictor is from inside republicanism: Robert S. Taylor's *Exit Left* (2017) argues that exit, resourced by the state, is *the* remedy for domination. That directly opposes the claim that voice beats independence. The abstract should cite and answer Taylor, using the necessary/contingent diagnostic: Taylor's remedy works only where the gate is contingent.

## Q3. Hirschman (1970): exit, voice and loyalty, and when voice beats exit

### Takeaway
Hirschman supplies the exit/voice hinge and the warning that exit can undercut voice. Lazar and Gowder supply the platform-specific reasons exit fails: switching costs, network effects, the absence of reasonable alternatives, and harms one cannot exit. The chapter-level locators for Hirschman's conditions under which voice beats exit are not verified.

## Q5. Raz on legitimate authority (the service conception) as vocabulary for algorithmic determinations

### Takeaway
Raz's service conception is usable J1 vocabulary. An authority is legitimate when following it helps subjects conform better to the reasons that already apply to them. An algorithmic determination could plausibly pass that test on accuracy alone. That is exactly why Lazar insists on the separate "who" question, and why coordinative sovereignty should not rest on performance-based legitimacy. Bibliographic verification for Raz is weak this session.

### Cited Findings
- Lazar's summary of Raz: "Raz defends the authority of law, arguing that we have content-independent reasons to obey it insofar as doing so reliably improves on independently judging what we have most reason to do." He cites Raz 1979 and 1986. (§4, p. 24) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Lazar's counter-premise against "what"-only justification: "Some think that if A's power meets the 'what' and 'how' standards, it meets the 'who' standard by default. This is, I think, especially likely to be false when rulers shape power relations between other people." (§3.4, p. 23) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
- Lazar argues the right to govern correlates with "a duty not to resist or interfere with that governing power", not with a duty to obey, because pre-emptive algorithmic governance needs no obedience. (§4, p. 29) — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2410.20720)
