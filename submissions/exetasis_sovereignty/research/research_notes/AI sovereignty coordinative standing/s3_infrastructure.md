## Q1. How do the core authors define digital or technological sovereignty, and which of them treat it as control or ownership versus capacity or relationship?

### Takeaway
The canonical texts split three ways:
- **Control or ownership:** Floridi, the policy core that Falkner et al. map, and Nvidia's industry usage.
- **Capacity or agency within interdependence:** Edler et al., and partly Pohle et al. (2025).
- **Discursive practice that no fixed referent can settle:** Pohle & Thiel, Adler-Nissen & Eggeling, Fratini et al.

Lehdonvirta alone puts the remedy in voice institutions inside the provider. That makes him the nearest precedent for a "necessary mediator, so voice" argument, although he applies it to users and platforms, not to states and AI providers.

### Cited Findings

**Control or ownership**
- **Floridi (2020).** Floridi defines digital sovereignty as control over "data, software, standards, protocols, hardware, services and digital infrastructure." He frames the contest as companies setting "the nature and speed of change" while states "can control the direction of change." He would articulate it at both the national and the supranational (EU) level. Classification: control. — [card: floridi2020fight, verified: abstract]; DOI [10.1007/s13347-020-00423-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-020-00423-6)
- **Falkner, Heidebrecht, Obendiek & Seidl (2024).** This is the introduction to a *JEPP* special issue covering nine EU digital-policy areas. They find "policy change towards more control of the digital across all policy areas examined," but the shift is "only sometimes accompanied by discursive change towards digital sovereignty." They explain the gap by the ideational trade-offs actors face in each venue. Classification: the operative variable is control; the paper's contribution is the rhetoric-versus-reality gap. — [card: falkner2024digital, verified: abstract]; Crossref record [10.1080/13501763.2024.2358984](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2024.2358984) confirmed this session ([record]; Crossref holds no abstract)
- **Bradford (2023), *Digital Empires*.** Bradford describes three regulatory models that compete for reach:
  - US: market-driven.
  - China: state-driven.
  - EU: rights-driven.

  Classification: sovereignty as jurisdictional and regulatory power, and its export. That is control exercised through law, not through ownership of the stack. — [card: bradford2023digital, verified: abstract] (OUP description only)

**Capacity or agency within interdependence**
- **Edler, Blind, Kroll & Schubert (2023).** They define technology sovereignty as state-level agency, a capacity for governmental action within an international system, not territorial control or self-sufficiency. They position it between "naive globalism" and autarky and accept that some interdependencies are cooperative and worth keeping. Classification: capacity or relational. This is the clearest scholarly warrant for saying sovereignty need not mean a domestic stack. — [card: edler2023technology, verified: abstract; the card's abstract was reconstructed from OpenAlex and is not verbatim]; Crossref title confirmed this session: "Technology sovereignty as an emerging frame for innovation policy. Defining rationales, ends and means," *Research Policy* 52(6), 104765 [record]

**Discursive practice, relationship or practice**
- **Pohle & Thiel (2020).** Digital sovereignty is "understood more as a discursive practice in politics and policy than as a legal or organisational concept." It "seeks to reinstate the nation state, including the national economy and the nation's citizens, as a relevant category in the global governance of digital infrastructures." They sort claims into state, economy and individual levels. Classification: discursive, but its policy content is mostly reasserted control. — [card: pohle2020digital, verified: full]; [10.14763/2020.4.1532](https://doi.org/10.14763/2020.4.1532)
- **Pohle, Nanni & Santaniello (2025).** This editorial introduces a *Policy & Internet* special issue that sets out to "unthink" digital sovereignty. It traces separate genealogies:
  - the EU, where the lack of domestic tech giants drives an autonomy discourse;
  - China, with information sovereignty since the 1990s;
  - Russia, with the securitised RuNet;
  - the BRICS and African states.

  They treat digital sovereignty as practices and discourses that confer legitimacy on autonomy demands, whoever makes them. "Demanding digital sovereignty to express a desire for more autonomy in times of digital interconnectedness is most certainly not a unique European feature." Classification: practice or relational, detached from state ownership. — [card: pohle2025unthinking, verified: full]; [10.1002/poi3.437](https://doi.org/10.1002/poi3.437)
- **Fratini, Hine, Novelli, Roberts & Floridi (2024).** A corpus analysis of 271 peer-reviewed articles yields four national models: rights-based, market-oriented, centralisation and state-based. They find that "none of the models fully combine comprehensive regulation of digital technologies with a sufficient degree of responsiveness." Classification: a descriptive typology; the models are all framed as state capacity to regulate and control. — [card: fratini2024digital, verified: abstract]; [10.1007/s44206-024-00146-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44206-024-00146-7)
- **Adler-Nissen & Eggeling (2024), not in the brief but directly relevant.** Using the cloud project Gaia-X as their case, they find "no singular understanding of digital sovereignty in the EU." Instead they identify "six different conceptions across the domains of security, economy and rights." They sketch three futures for the agenda: constitutional tolerance, hegemony or collapse "due to inbuilt conceptual contradictions." Classification: discursive. It is also the closest scholarly study of a cloud-sovereignty project. — [abstract] Crossref record, *JCMS* 62, 993–1011, [10.1111/jcms.13594](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcms.13594). Lawfare (GREY) attributes to them the line that "sovereignty" is used by cloud firms as a compliance label "empty in substance and impossible to implement." I could not find that wording in the abstract, so the quotation is **UNVERIFIED** against the article. — [Sukumar, Lawfare, 9 Oct 2025](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/tech-s--sovereignty-washing--in-europe-will-ripple-in-the-global-south)

**Relational, with the remedy placed in voice**
- **Lehdonvirta (2022), *Cloud Empires*.** Platforms are "states without estates, empires in the cloud," and their authority is not bound to territory. Users cannot easily leave because "viable alternatives often do not exist," and antitrust and decentralisation have proved weak remedies. He prescribes treating platforms as polities, protecting "indigenous proto-democratic institutions," and adopting a charter of rights. Classification: relational. Sovereignty-like power sits in the provider, and the remedy is voice inside it, not exit. — [card: lehdonvirta2022cloud, verified: abstract; quotations via Seidl 2022 review]

**Supporting EU AI-policy reading**
- **Mügge (2024).** EU AI policy "prioritises jurisdictional independence over citizens sovereignty, … embraces a global AI race logic, and … largely neglects its impact on people beyond the EU." He insists this reflects "political choices" rather than technological necessity. Classification: the operative EU notion is jurisdictional independence, which is a control notion. — [card: mugge2024eu, verified: full]; [10.1080/13501763.2024.2318475](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2024.2318475)

### Inferences
- **The literature already holds the necessary-versus-contingent axis without naming it.** Control readings (Floridi; the EU policy core in Falkner et al.; Mügge's "jurisdictional independence") assume the mediator is contingent: sovereignty is restored by owning or regulating the stack. Edler et al.'s "agency within interdependence" and Lehdonvirta's voice-in-the-polity both assume some dependencies cannot or should not be exited.
- **Pohle & Thiel's level split (state, economy, individual) lets the author say who is dependent on what.** A state dependent on a foreign model provider is not the same as citizens dependent on it, and Mügge shows EU policy collapses the second into the first.

### Gaps
- **Crawford (2021), *Atlas of AI*, was not read.** The lab card (crawford2021atlas, verified: search) withholds its summary, and I did not reach the text this session. I make no claim about how she defines sovereignty. **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Full texts of Falkner et al., Edler et al., Floridi and Bradford were not reached.** The classifications above rest on abstracts and publisher descriptions.

## Q2. The "sovereign AI" discourse and programmes: what each promises, and what dependence remains (dated, as of 2026-10-24)

### Takeaway
"Sovereign AI" is, at origin, a vendor's sales category. Nvidia frames it as a nation owning its infrastructure, data and "intelligence," and the infrastructure it means is built from Nvidia chips.

The 2025–26 programmes promise domestic compute capacity and, in the EU, graded cloud-sovereignty assurance. Every one of them leaves three dependencies standing:
- **Chips:** US and Taiwanese chip supply, under US export licensing.
- **Frontier model weights and access:** the US owners can revoke them, and in June 2026 they did.
- **Cloud capacity:** US hyperscalers that the programmes themselves often enlist as partners.

### Cited Findings

**Nvidia and the origin of the term (INDUSTRY and secondary)**
- **Dale's column (2025).** Dale reports Jensen Huang at London Tech Week (June 2025) calling the UK "the largest AI ecosystem in the world without its own infrastructure." Dale calls the criticism "somewhat self-serving," because UK infrastructure is "certain to consist largely of chips sold by Huang's company." He notes that Huang "has been pitching the idea of 'sovereign AI' since at least 2023, conscious that nation states are the next deep pockets to target after the hyperscalers." — Dale, R. (2025). Sovereign AI in 2025. *Natural Language Processing* 31, 1312–1321, [10.1017/nlp.2025.10007](https://doi.org/10.1017/nlp.2025.10007) [abstract]. This is an industry-watch column in a Cambridge journal, not a research article.
- **Huang's own framing.** At the World Governments Summit in Dubai, Huang reportedly framed sovereign AI as a country's "ownership and control over its data and the intelligence it produces." — [record, secondary: HyScaler](https://hyscaler.com/insights/sovereign-ai-nvidia-ceo-vision-data-ownership) (GREY; the primary transcript was not located, so the wording is **UNVERIFIED**)
- **Nvidia's CFO.** Colette Kress, on an earnings call, reportedly said: "Sovereign AI refers to a nation's capabilities to produce artificial intelligence using its own infrastructure, data, workforce and business networks." — [record, secondary: Benzinga, May 2024](https://drupal-cloudfront.benzinga.com/news/earnings/24/05/38982843/nvidia-at-the-center-of-global-sovereign-ai-wave-heres-what-it-means) (GREY; the earnings-call transcript was not opened, so the quotation is **UNVERIFIED**)
- **Nvidia blog, July 2026 (INDUSTRY).** Calista Redmond's post never defines the term. It calls for AI factories that are "locally owned, operated and governed AI clouds for training and inference," with models run on local infrastructure "subject only to local laws." It praises India's Sarvam for keeping "data, compute and governance under national control." — [Nvidia blog, 6 Jul 2026](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/what-is-sovereign-ai/) [full, as served at that URL]. Nvidia's original "What Is Sovereign AI?" post, reportedly from Feb 2024, could not be located; only its title appears on the [Nvidia tag page](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/tag/sovereign-ai/page/3) [record].
- **No agreed meaning.** Bloomberg reports that Europe "can't agree on what [sovereign AI] means." — [Bloomberg newsletter, 20 Oct 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-10-20/europe-wants-sovereign-ai-but-can-t-agree-on-what-it-means) [record; headline and snippet only] (GREY)

**EU: AI Continent Action Plan, gigafactories and CADA (official, plus GREY where noted)**
- **AI Continent Action Plan (Commission, April 2025).** The plan treats compute scale-up as its central pillar. InvestAI is to mobilise €20 billion for up to five AI gigafactories, each described as "4x more powerful" than an AI Factory. The AI Factories line has €10 billion for 2021–27, with "at least 13 operational AI factories by 2026." The Commission expects the initiative to stimulate "the domestic design and – in due course – the future manufacturing of indigenous EU AI processors." — [Commission AI Continent factpage](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/factpages/ai-continent-action-plan) [record: the page returned 403; figures come from the search-engine extract of the page and the Commission's AIGF topic page](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/competitiveness/competitiveness-coordination-tool-projects/ai-gigafactories_pt); [heise, Apr 2025](https://heise.de/-10346290) (GREY)
  - The gigafactories are "private-led," with EU and member-state support. — same Commission AIGF page [record]
- **AI Gigafactories tender (EuroHPC JU, open).** The call (ref. EUROHPC-2026-CEI-AIGF-01) was published on 30 July 2026, in two lots:

  | Lot | Scale | Deadline |
  |---|---|---|
  | Lot 1 | Medium | **4 February 2027** (extended "for administrative reasons") |
  | Lot 2 | Large | **3 December 2026** |

  Selected consortia will "establish and operate" the facilities, and EuroHPC and the states will acquire compute access time from them: the public side is an anchor customer, not the owner. Each facility must integrate "at least three to four times the number of the most advanced AI processors" found in the largest European AI factories. — [EuroHPC JU call page](https://www.eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/call-tenders-selection-artificial-intelligence-gigafactory-consortia-and-establishment-ai_en) [full page read]
  - The call page says nothing about EU ownership or control of consortia, or about the origin of chips.
  - GREY coverage puts the procurement at about €10 billion (Lot 1 about €4bn, Lot 2 about €6bn) for "up to seven" gigafactories, with awards by early 2027 and operations within 18 months of signature. I did not verify these figures on the official page. — [PNO Innovation](https://www.pnoinnovation.com/insights/ai-gigafactories-understanding-the-european-call-for-tenders/); [EuroHPC Decision 47.2026 PDF](https://www.eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/document/download/27f3cf51-f016-4453-89f0-b19d38a7aa9e_en?filename=Decision+47.2026+Approval+of+the+Launch+of+AIGF+Call.pdf) [record only, not opened]
  - **Conflict:** one secondary source gives a single 12 Nov 2026 deadline. The official page gives the dates in the table above. Use the official page.
- **Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), proposed, not law.** The Commission adopted the proposal on **3 June 2026** within a "Tech Sovereignty Package." It is COM(2026) 502, procedure 2026/0138(COD), and is pending in the ordinary legislative procedure. Hunton gives 2 July, which conflicts with the Commission page and Practical Law; use 3 June. — [Commission CADA library page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/proposal-cloud-and-ai-development-act-cada) [record]; [EP Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-cloud-and-ai-development-act) [record]
  - **Aims.** The Commission's stated aim is to "at least triple EU data centre capacity within 5 to 7 years." — [Commission cloud policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cloud-computing) [record via search extract]
  - **Four "Union assurance levels" (CADA's core).** — [Covington, Inside Global Tech, 11 Jun 2026](https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2026/06/11/the-eu-cloud-and-ai-development-act-in-depth/) [full] (GREY, law-firm analysis)

    | Level | Main requirements | Who can qualify |
    |---|---|---|
    | L1 (all public-sector providers) | Infrastructure, assets and customer data in the EU. A third-country-controlled provider must guarantee it is not required to report unexploited vulnerabilities to third-country authorities. | Third-country-controlled providers |
    | L2 | All personnel and operations in the EU. EU certification. Measures "to prevent third-country access to customer data" and to avoid exposure to third-country sanctions or trade controls. SBOM and separation of EU operations. | Third-country-controlled providers |
    | L3 | Must be "owned and controlled in the EU." | A derogation exists only for third countries the Commission recognises (GDPR adequacy, no power to compel service disruption, reciprocal market access). |
    | L4 | Not controlled in any third country, "without any derogation." Effective control of all software components. No third-country control over their "design, development, maintenance, or evolution." | No third-country-controlled provider |

  - **Procurement.** A "Union added value" criterion must be "ancillary and not decisive," reportedly at most 15 of 120 points.
  - **What the levels imply.** This is my reading of Covington's analysis. US hyperscalers can qualify at L1–L2, can reach L3 only if the US is recognised, and cannot reach L4. The levels the member states actually assign remain undecided.

**US export controls: chips, then models (official, plus GREY)**
- **AI Diffusion Rule (chips).**
  - May 2025: BIS rescinded the Biden-era rule and promised a replacement.
  - 14 Jul 2026: Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler told Congress the administration "no longer intends to replace" it and is turning to new rulemaking.
  - Advanced-computing controls on China, Macau and D:5-headquartered entities remain in force, as clarified in May 2026. — [Morgan Lewis LawFlash, 21 Jul 2026](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/bis-upgrades-uae-export-control-status-with-ai-chip-access-limited-to-approved-entities) [full] (GREY); [WilmerHale, 15 May 2025](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20250515-us-export-controls-on-ai-diffusion-officially-paused-but-new-guidance-elevates-risk-for-ai-related-exports) [record]
  - Secondary sources report two further points: (a) a January 2026 BIS rule moved chips such as the H200 to case-by-case review for China, with US third-party testing; (b) an "Affiliate Rule" is suspended until 10 Nov 2026. — [search-result extracts: safeguard.sh](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/us-ai-chip-export-controls-on-china-timeline-official-sources); [Global Trade & Sanctions Law, 13 Mar 2026](https://www.globaltradeandsanctionslaw.com/reported-draft-rules-signal-new-semiconductor-export-controls-framework/) [record] (GREY; not checked against the Federal Register, so **UNVERIFIED**)
- **First export control on a model (June 2026).**
  - **12 Jun 2026.** Commerce sent Anthropic a letter, which is not public, requiring a BIS licence for any foreign person, inside or outside the US, to access its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models. It reportedly relied on ECRA interim authority over emerging technologies, plus EAR §§744.22 and 734.13. CSIS notes that no EAR regulation implements that authority, so it "had never been used this way before."
  - **Anthropic's response.** Anthropic said it "must abruptly disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all our customers."
  - **Reaction.** CSIS reports that European politicians cited the episode as evidence of the need for sovereign AI. CSIS also suggests uncertain access could push foreign buyers toward "small open-weight models" on local hardware. — [CSIS, Koren, Kurland & Mehta, 16 Jun 2026](https://www.csis.org/analysis/department-commerce-restricted-access-anthropics-latest-models-what-comes-next) [full] (GREY, think tank)
  - **Lifted, 30 Jun 2026.** Anthropic reported the controls lifted after "a series of agreements with the government":
    - a jailbreak classifier tested by Commerce's Center for AI Standards and Innovation;
    - expanded pre-release access for government evaluators;
    - a shared voluntary security standard.

    Fable 5 was restored globally. The Record says Mythos 5 controls were "fully lifted," yet Mythos 5 itself stays limited to "vetted U.S. organizations" through Project Glasswing.
    — [The Record, Martin, 1 Jul 2026](https://therecord.media/us-lifts-export-controls-anthropic-cyber-models) [full] (GREY, trade press); [HSF Kramer, Jul 2026](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/2026-07/license-to-model-emerging-us-rules-impact-global-access-to-frontier-ai) [record]; [CAP, "de facto licensing system"](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-trump-administration-has-created-a-de-facto-licensing-system-for-frontier-ai-models/) [record] (GREY)
  - **Interpretation in the literature.** Woodward & Rogoyski (preprint, 13 Aug 2026) read the episode this way: governments of the states hosting frontier developers can decide which countries get access, "access to frontier AI is becoming part of national cyber defence," and that access "can be revoked." They judge sovereign capability "only partly feasible for most states." They propose a layered strategy:
    - negotiated access guarantees;
    - inference-level sovereignty;
    - open-weight models as a hedge, which they warn is "more politically exposed" than assumed;
    - pooled regional capability.

    Their account of a worldwide withdrawal omits the 30 June lifting. — [arXiv:2608.13272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13272) [abstract] (preprint, not peer-reviewed)

Secondary sources name the two firms as G42 and Core42 and say Humain remains on case-by-case licensing; this is **UNVERIFIED** against the Federal Register — [vision2030.ai](https://vision2030.ai/geopolitics/us-chip-export-controls-saudi-uae-divergence/) [record] (GREY)
- **Stargate UAE.** It was announced in May 2025 with OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, Cisco and SoftBank, targeting 1 GW, with the first 200 MW in 2026. — [safeguard.sh timeline](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/uae-ai-strategy-chip-export-licences-g42-stargate-timeline) [record] (GREY, single source, **UNVERIFIED**)

**The geography of compute (peer-reviewed)**
- **Lehdonvirta, Wú & Hawkins (2024).** Their census of hyperscale cloud regions divides the world into:
  - "Compute North": compute relevant for training;
  - "Compute South": inference-grade compute;
  - "Compute Desert": no public-cloud AI compute.

  Their premise is that "states that host AI compute capacity within their territorial jurisdictions are likely to be better placed to impose their rules on AI systems." — *Proc. AIES* 7, 828–838, [10.1609/aies.v7i1.31683](https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v7i1.31683) [abstract via Crossref]

### Inferences
- **What each programme promises, and what dependence remains.** All three entries leave model-weight dependence unaddressed except through open-weight hedges.

  | Programme | Promise | Dependence that remains |
  |---|---|---|
  | EU gigafactories | Territorial compute | Chips: no EU-origin processor at scale; the plan says EU processors come only "in due course." Private operators, because the public side is an off-taker. |
  | CADA | Graded cloud sovereignty | Only L3–L4 exclude US control, and assignment of those levels is deferred to member-state risk assessments. |
  | Gulf | Large compute | Chips under US licence. Operator conditions that push the UAE firms to "become US companies" (my reading of the 270-day clause). Hosting by US hyperscaler subsidiaries. |

- **The June 2026 episode is the strongest dated evidence that model access is a chokepoint distinct from chips.** A state can own its data centres and still lose a frontier model overnight by a US administrative letter.
- **The way access came back matters for the voice argument.** It was restored through negotiated governance (classifiers, evaluator access, a shared standard) between the US government and the provider. Non-US states had no voice in that bargain.

- **AI Continent Action Plan.** I did not reach its primary text (the Commission page returned 403, and the press release did not render). Figures come from Commission page extracts and press coverage.
- **National AI compute programmes outside the EU and Gulf were not researched.** That includes the UK AI Research Resource, India's AI Mission, Japan and Canada. Sarvam appears only in Nvidia's own blog.
- **Primary texts not read.** I did not open the Federal Register text of the 10 Jul 2026 UAE rule, the January 2026 China chip rule, or the Nov 2025 G42/Humain licence conditions.
- **Where model weights sit in the gigafactory programme is unclear.** Whether the gigafactories will train EU-owned frontier models, or host US models, is not stated on the call page.

## Q3. Critiques of the gap between rhetoric and reality: sovereignty-as-a-service, sovereignty washing and hyperscaler sovereign cloud

### Takeaway
The Grohmann & Costa Barbosa paper exists and is verified at the record and abstract level. It names how hyperscalers turn sovereignty into a configurable product provisioned on corporate terms. "Sovereignty washing" is mainly a GREY and vendor-competitor term; Lawfare gives it a short scholarly-adjacent statement. The academic analogues are Falkner et al.'s rhetoric/reality gap, Adler-Nissen & Eggeling on Gaia-X, and Jang (AIES 2025) on "sovereign AI" as a narrative that frames dependence as empowerment.

### Cited Findings

**Peer-reviewed critiques**
- **Grohmann & Costa Barbosa (2026).** Using critical discourse analysis of 2022–23 corporate materials, they introduce "sovereignty-as-a-service." Microsoft, Amazon and Google answer European regulatory pressure with modular sovereign-cloud products that frame sovereignty as a technical, legal and infrastructural property one can configure and buy. Sovereignty is no longer exercised over platforms but provisioned by them. — *Media, Culture & Society* 48(2), 416–424, [10.1177/01634437251395003](https://doi.org/10.1177/01634437251395003) [card: grohmann2026sovereignty, verified: abstract]. The card notes that no verbatim sentences were captured, so do not quote it.
- **Jang (2025), AIES.** Jang applies critical discourse analysis to Nexis Uni news coverage of "sovereign AI," current to 19 Apr 2025. The paper finds that sovereignty works as a symbolic strategy as well as a policy goal. It frames material dependence, especially on Nvidia, as empowerment, and it justifies public investment while obscuring structural dependencies. — Jang, H. (2025). Exporting autonomy, importing dependency: The geopolitical work of "sovereign AI." *Proc. AIES* 8(2), 1335–1336, [10.1609/aies.v8i2.36634](https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v8i2.36634) [abstract; two-page extended abstract]
- **Ashraf & Veneziano (2026).** They critique the Tony Blair Institute's "strategic agency" framework. In their reading it underspecifies how infrastructural power constrains choice and how "dependence becomes coercion." ## Q4. Miller's chokepoints and Farrell & Newman's weaponized interdependence

### Takeaway
Farrell & Newman supply the mechanism. States with jurisdiction over central network nodes can use a "chokepoint effect" (denial) and a "panopticon effect" (information). The June 2026 model controls and the 2025–26 chip licensing are textbook chokepoint uses on the AI stack. Miller's *Chip War* is confirmed only at the record level this session.

### Cited Findings
- **Farrell & Newman (2019).** Their abstract makes five moves:
  - Globalized networks grow asymmetrically, centring on the US and a few other states.
  - States with "(1) effective jurisdiction over the central economic nodes and (2) appropriate domestic institutions and norms" can weaponize them.
  - The "panopticon effect" gathers "strategically valuable information."
  - The "chokepoint effect" denies "network access to adversaries."
  - Their cases are SWIFT and the internet.

  — *International Security* 44(1), 42–79, [10.1162/isec_a_00351](https://doi.org/10.1162/isec_a_00351) [abstract via Crossref; MIT Press page returned 403]
- **Miller (2022), *Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology*.** Simon & Schuster/Scribner, 2022. — [Open Library record](https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=chip+war+chris+miller) [record only]. The publisher page returned 403. Its chokepoint content (TSMC, ASML and others) was not read this session and is **UNVERIFIED**; do not attribute specific claims without reading.
- **Dale (2025).** Dale confirms the duopoly framing: "the only real contenders in the race for AI supremacy are the US and China." — [10.1017/nlp.2025.10007](https://doi.org/10.1017/nlp.2025.10007) [abstract]

### Inferences
- **Farrell & Newman's two conditions fit the June 2026 episode.** The US had jurisdiction over the node (a US model developer) and domestic institutions to act (ECRA, BIS). The case extends weaponized interdependence from chips (hardware) to model access (software), which The Record calls "the first known use of export control authorities to pull AI software." — [The Record](https://therecord.media/us-lifts-export-controls-anthropic-cyber-models)
- **Weaponized interdependence also identifies a contingent dependence.** For a target, the chokepoint is a reason to exit, so Farrell & Newman can cut against the "necessary mediator" thesis. The thesis survives only if the node also does integrative work the state cannot reproduce. Woodward & Rogoyski's "only partly feasible" judgement on sovereign frontier capability is the relevant evidence.

- ***Chip War* was not read.** The 2021 Brookings volume *The Uses and Abuses of Weaponized Interdependence* is known only from a search snippet and was not opened.

## Q5. Has anyone applied Hirschman's exit and voice to states' relations with AI providers?

### Takeaway
I found no work that applies Hirschman's exit, voice and loyalty to a *state's* relation with foreign or private compute or model providers. My searches covered the web (standard and extended modes), arXiv results surfaced by search, and the lab library. Scholar Gateway returned "access denied," so I could not search that database. The nearest applications concern users or individuals, not states.

### Cited Findings
- **The lab's own use.** The library's deep-research report already uses Hirschman as "the hinge": where exit is available, independence-sovereignty works; where it is foreclosed, "only voice remains." — `coordinative_sovereignty/literature/deep_research_report.md`; [card: hirschman1970exit, verified: abstract]
- **#Keep4o backlash study (arXiv, 2026).** Users' backlash against retirement of a chatbot model: protest becomes more likely when exit is costly and voice channels are limited. Individual users, not states. — [arXiv:2602.00773](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.00773) [record]
- **"Hard Choices in Artificial Intelligence" (arXiv, 2021).** It argues that a lack of meaningful exit or voice can motivate collective action. — [arXiv:2106.11022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11022) [record]
- **"Dignity-Centric Stack" (arXiv, 2026).** It notes that network effects raise the cost of exit in AI ecosystems. — [arXiv:2606.06083](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.06083) [record]
- **Smith & Burrows (2021), a false friend.** Their "post-neoliberal politics of exit" concerns neoreactionary secession via Urbit, not Hirschman. The lab cut it for that reason. — [card: smith2021software, verified: abstract]
- **Nearest functional analogues (not Hirschman).**
  - Woodward & Rogoyski propose "negotiated access guarantees," a voice-like remedy, beside inference sovereignty and open weights, which are exit-like. — [arXiv:2608.13272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13272) [abstract]
  - Lehdonvirta's polity remedy is voice inside the platform. — [card: lehdonvirta2022cloud]

### Inferences: the necessary-versus-contingent test
- **Contingent mediator (exit or a domestic stack suffices).**
  - **Inference compute and data hosting.** CADA L1–L2, gigafactories and Gulf data centres show territorial hosting is buildable, given chips.
  - **Generic cloud.** The CADA L4 tier assumes an EU-owned alternative can exist.
  - **Open-weight models.** CSIS expects buyers to move toward them after June 2026.
- **Candidate necessary mediator (only voice in its governance helps).** Three layers fit:
  - **Frontier model capability.** Woodward & Rogoyski: sovereign capability is "only partly feasible."
  - **Leading-edge chips.** The EU plan defers EU processors to "in due course."
  - **Governance of model release.** The June 2026 restoration ran through a US government–provider bargain.

  On these layers, "sovereign AI" programmes buy territorial custody of foreign-made components. They do not buy the integrative work itself: frontier training, chip design and fabrication.
- **The "liberal exit reflex scaled to the state" hypothesis gets mixed support.**
  - **For.** The programmes' main instrument is exit-by-substitution: domestic compute, local hosting, EU-owned tiers. Nvidia sells the programmes as exactly that ("locally owned, operated and governed"). Mügge shows EU policy targets "jurisdictional independence," and Jang shows the narrative frames dependence as autonomy.
  - **Against.** Edler et al. explicitly reject autarky in favour of agency within interdependence. The Gulf model is not exit at all: it buys loyalty to the US stack in exchange for access. Woodward & Rogoyski's "negotiated access guarantees" are a voice strategy.
  - **Net.** The rhetoric is exit, while the practice is often loyalty-for-access. Real voice — standing in the governance of the frontier provider or its regulator — is largely absent for non-US states. — sources as cited above
- **A J8 angle ("sovereignty beyond the territorial state").** Lehdonvirta's territory-unbound "cloud empires," together with the US government's power over global access to a model (June 2026), show that the sovereignty-relevant decision sat in a provider–home-state dyad. Voice would therefore have to be exercised there, not over territory.
