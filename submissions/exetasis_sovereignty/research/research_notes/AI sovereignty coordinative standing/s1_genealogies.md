## Q1. Bodin, Hobbes, Rousseau: what is the core of sovereignty, and who assumes the sovereign party can stand apart?

### Takeaway
All three put sovereignty in one undivided seat: Bodin in "absolute and perpetuall" power, Hobbes in a single Person that the multitude authorizes, Rousseau in an inalienable and indivisible general will. Hobbes is the most useful foil for coordinative sovereignty. His subjects become sovereign's *authors* by an act of authorization and then cannot change the form of government. Coordinated parties make up the sovereign, but they cannot stand apart from it or leave it, and the theory gives them no voice inside it. Bodin and Hobbes also place the sovereign apart from and above the law. Rousseau places sovereignty in the members, but only as a body that cannot be represented. That denies the mediated condition the construct starts from.

### Inferences
- The brief asks who assumes the sovereign party can stand apart. The answer comes in three parts. (a) Bodin and Hobbes place the *sovereign* apart from and above law and subjects (SEP §1). (b) In Hobbes the *subjects* are inside the sovereign as authors but cannot exit ("Cannot Change The Forme", "Cannot Be Forfeited"). Hobbes therefore already theorizes a mediator its constituents cannot leave, and he resolves it by giving them authorship without voice. (c) Rousseau gives the members sovereignty but requires it to be unmediated ("cannot be represented"). Under mediation, then, Rousseau would deny there is sovereignty at all.
- Coordinative sovereignty reads as an inversion of Hobbes. It keeps the no-exit premise and refuses the bargain of authorization without voice. Rousseau's own phrase "the political machine" (I.7) is a ready hinge to "machine sovereignty". Use it as a rhetorical bridge only, and do not treat it as an argument.
- The indivisibility doctrine (Bodin, Hobbes ch. XVIII, Rousseau II.2) is the standing objection to any "shared, partial" sovereignty. The lab's existing answer runs through Habermas (Q5) and Repetto 2025 (Q7).

## Q2. Schmitt: is the platform or algorithmic mediator that suspends an account or deactivates a worker a Schmittian sovereign? Who has argued this?

### Takeaway
Schmitt's test fits the platform in a narrow sense. A platform decides, outside any rule the user can invoke, that the normal order does not apply to this account. Scholars have made the analogy, mostly about high-profile deplatforming: Monaci 2024 descriptively, Gowder 2023 as a Schmittian episode that the Oversight Board then answers. Bratton gives the structural version ("automation of the exception", *The Stack* p. 33, as quoted by Wark). I found no source that applies Schmitt to the routine automated deactivation of gig workers. That gap looks open, and it suits the construct. In the gig case the "exception" is automated and serial, which strains Schmitt's personal, visible decision.

### Cited Findings
- **Schmitt's formula and its gloss (SEP, Vinx, rev. 6 Mar 2025), fetched:** the entry renders it "the sovereign is he who decides on the state of exception" (PT 5, Schwab tr., Chicago 2005). Sovereignty belongs to whatever "person or institution" "possesses the ability, as a matter of fact, to take a decision on the exception" (§2). "Any legal order, Schmitt bluntly concludes, is based on a sovereign decision and not on a legal norm (PT 10, 12–3)" (§2). In democracies the decision must be "an exercise of the people's constituent power" (§2). The entry does not extend Schmitt's sovereignty to non-state actors — [Vinx, "Carl Schmitt", SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schmitt/). Status: SEP full text via fetch. *Political Theology* itself was **not** opened, so treat "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception" (Schwab, p. 5) as UNVERIFIED wording against the Chicago text. SEP's version adds "state of".
- **Gowder 2023, ch. 5 (pp. 140–166), abstract plus one body sentence fetched:** the chapter "also serves as a counterexample to some of Carl Schmitt's ideas about sovereignty and emergency power". On the Trump deplatforming (§5.2): "Ultimately, a state of exception was needed, and was declared." — [Gowder, *The Networked Leviathan*, ch. 5, Cambridge Core, doi:10.1017/9781108975438.006](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/networked-leviathan/actually-existing-platform-selfconstraint-up-to-a-point/D2D397F9FC54E271951E3EA246AA80F9). - **Monaci 2024, full text read (IJoC 18, 4298–4313):** platform sovereignty "describes control over content moderation, and the prerogative to decide who or what platforms to exclude from the media ecosystem through deplatforming and deplatformization actions. - **Niu 2026 (arXiv preprint, abstract only):** AI-mediated administration "hollows" sovereignty. ### Inferences
- The structure of Schmitt's definition, which the SEP gives as "as a matter of fact" plus "person or institution", allows a non-state decider. Gowder and Monaci show the move has been made for content moderation at the level of heads of state. Applied to the deactivated worker, the Schmittian reading clarifies one thing and distorts another. It clarifies that the decision is unbound by any norm the worker can invoke. It distorts because deactivation is routinized and often automated, the opposite of the singular, visible decision on an emergency. Bratton's "automation of the exception" names that shift. The abstract can claim the platform is sovereign in Schmitt's sense *only on the side of the decider*, and that sovereignty for the *decided-upon* must then be defined differently. That is the opening for coordinative sovereignty.
- Gowder's Oversight-Board counterexample, in which lawlike review follows the exception, gives one existing model of "voice inside the mediation".

- No source found applying Schmitt specifically to algorithmic deactivation of gig or platform workers (two searches). That gap is reported, not proven.
- Klonick 2018 and Suzor 2019 (in the lab report) were not rechecked for Schmitt.

## Q3. Foucault: from sovereign power to discipline and governmentality. How does it reframe sovereignty under AI?

### Takeaway
Foucault denies that sovereignty is the right lens for modern power. The "cut off the head of the king" line comes from *History of Sexuality* vol. 1, **Part Four** ("The Deployment of Sexuality", pp. 88–89), **not Part Five** as the brief has it. Part Five ("Right of Death and Power over Life", from p. 133) gives the sovereign/biopower contrast. Under AI the Foucauldian move turns the question away from who decides and toward how conduct is shaped continuously. That makes a single sovereign decider the wrong place to look for algorithmic power, and it supports a standing defined inside the mediation rather than against a sovereign.

### Cited Findings
- **FLAG (brief correction):** the passage is in Part Four, not Part Five. In the 1978 Pantheon (Hurley) first American edition: "At bottom, despite the differences in epochs and objectives, the representation of power has remained under the spell of monarchy. In political thought and analysis, we still have not cut off the head of the king." (pp. 88–89, page break after "we still"). Foucault continues: "Hence the importance that the theory of power gives to the problem of right and violence, law and illegality, freedom and will, and especially the state and sovereignty" (p. 89) — [Foucault, *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1*, tr. Hurley, Internet Archive djvu text](https://archive.org/details/foucault-the-history-of-sexuality-volume-1). Full text read (OCR text). The page numbers come from the running heads in the OCR and the contents page: Part Four begins p. 75, Part Five p. 133.
- **Part Five, full text read (OCR):** "The right which was formulated as the 'power of life and death' was in reality the right to take life or let live. Its symbol, after all, was the sword." (p. 136). "One might say that the ancient right to take life or let live was replaced by a power to foster life or disallow it to the point of death." (p. 138) — [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/foucault-the-history-of-sexuality-volume-1).
- **Variant of the head-of-the-king line:** the 1977 interview "Truth and Power" (*Power/Knowledge*, ed. Gordon) has "We need to cut off the King's head: in political theory that has still to be done", per a secondary summary — [UBC blog summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/chendricks/2015/04/21/foucault-the-sovereign-discipline-bio-power/) (secondary, not the text; UNVERIFIED wording and page).
- **Arendt/Foucault secondary:** "Cutting off the king's head: the social in Arendt and Foucault" — [Bath Spa repository record](https://researchspace.bathspa.ac.uk/14533/) (record only; author and venue not captured).

### Inferences
- Foucault gives the abstract a pre-emptive objection: why keep "sovereignty" at all if algorithmic power is disciplinary or governmental? A reply consistent with the construct: coordinative sovereignty does not name the mediator's power, which Foucault rightly treats as non-sovereign. It names the *standing* of the governed inside a governmental apparatus, a juridical claim made from within a regime of conduct. Foucault's parenthesis (p. 89), where sovereignty is "questioned insofar as it is personified in a collective being", also bears on Rousseau.

## Q5. Habermas, *Between Facts and Norms* (1996): subjectless popular sovereignty and governing through law a public can read

### Takeaway
Habermas gives the strongest existing licence for a sovereignty that is dispersed, procedural and without a subject. Sovereignty "withdraws into democratic procedures" and "makes itself felt in the power of public discourses". That is close to "voice inside the mediation". The quotation is now confirmed verbatim from Flynn 2004, which cites Appendix I, "Popular Sovereignty as Procedure". The page pinpoint still rests on the lab card's "pp. 486–487".

### Cited Findings
- **Flynn 2004 (EJPT 3(4) 433–454, doi:10.1177/1474885104045914), full text read**, quoting BFN Appendix I: "The idea of popular sovereignty is thereby desubstantialized. . . . This fully dispersed sovereignty . . . is found in those subjectless forms of communication that regulate the flow of discursive opinion- and will-formation in such a way that their fallible outcomes have the presumption of practical reason on their side. Subjectless and anonymous, an intersubjectively dissolved popular sovereignty withdraws into democratic procedures and the demanding communicative presuppositions of their implementation. . . . Set communicatively aflow, sovereignty makes itself felt in the power of public discourses." (Flynn's n. 20 cites "Popular Sovereignty as Procedure", appendix 1) — [Flynn, "Communicative Power in Habermas's Theory of Democracy"](https://faculty.fordham.edu/jeflynn/Flynn_Communicative_Power.pdf). Flynn also quotes the Postscript: "the democratic process bears the entire burden of legitimation" (BFN p. 450, his n. 19).
- Lab card `habermas1996facts` (verified: **search**) holds the same quotation at pp. 486–487 via Flynn 2004 and Murkens 2007, and notes that Appendix I runs pp. 463–490. This session upgrades the *wording* to secondary-verbatim (Flynn), but the *page* is still unconfirmed against BFN itself.

### Inferences
- Habermas answers the Bodin-Hobbes-Rousseau indivisibility objection (Q1): sovereignty can be dispersed and procedural and still be sovereignty. The abstract's "law a public can read" maps onto his insistence on the communicative presuppositions of procedures. An opaque algorithmic mediator fails that condition. That failure is where coordinative standing (effective voice) does its work.

### Gaps
- The BFN page (486–487) still needs checking against the MIT text. I have no direct BFN quotation on "law a public can read". That phrasing is the brief's paraphrase and should not be attributed to Habermas as a quotation.

## Q6. Bratton, *The Stack* (2015): platform sovereignty and planetary computation

### Takeaway
Bratton gives the main theoretical account of platform sovereignty: an "infrastructural sovereignty" produced by platforms regardless of ownership. He tells it as a succession of nomoi, with a "nomos of the cloud" replacing Schmitt's nomos of the earth. His sovereignty is systemic and belongs to no actor. It says where sovereignty sits (the stack), not what standing a coordinated user has. The construct can take his premise (no outside) and supply the missing actor-side account.

## Q7. "Algorithmic sovereignty" and "machine sovereignty": who uses them, in which senses, with what critiques?

### Takeaway
"Algorithmic sovereignty" already has at least three owners: (1) geopolitical, state control over algorithms and their ownership (Liang & Li 2025); (2) Indigenous, algorithms using Māori data subject to Māori governance (Brown et al. 2024, building on CARE); (3) participatory or design-practice (Kera 2021, unread). "Machine sovereignty" is mainly Yuk Hui's *Machine and Sovereignty* (2024). Hui does not mean AI-as-sovereign. He reworks sovereignty under the "megamachine" of planetary technology. I found no serious philosophical defence of AI itself as a sovereign. The concept looks coherent only when sovereignty is reassigned (to the state over the algorithm, the people over their data, or the system as a whole), never when it is given to the machine as an agent. The broader critique says the term is overstretched (Mueller 2020) or fragmented into clusters (Repetto 2025).

### Inferences
- For theme 1 ("possibility and limits"), the abstract can sort uses by *whom sovereignty is assigned to*. (a) Sovereignty *over* the machine: state, people, community. Coherent, but these are ordinary sovereignty claims with a new object. (b) Sovereignty *of* the machine or stack: Bratton, Schmittian platform readings, Hui's megamachine. Coherent only as systemic or de facto decision power, with no standing for the governed. (c) Sovereignty *through* the machine: the coordinated actor's standing inside an inescapable mediator. This is the unoccupied cell (consistent with the lab report's finding that "coordinative sovereignty" is not an established term). Habermas's subjectless, procedural sovereignty (Q5) is its best canonical licence, and Hobbes's no-exit authorization (Q1) is its foil.
- "Machine sovereignty" as AI-as-sovereign agent finds no serious defender in what I searched. The arXiv AI-and-law paper surfaced in search argues the opposite, that machines lack the self-governance sovereignty requires (search summary only; not cited).
