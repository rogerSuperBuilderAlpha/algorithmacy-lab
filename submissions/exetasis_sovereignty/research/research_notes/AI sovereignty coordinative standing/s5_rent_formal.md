## Q1. What the lab's spec (formal_standing.md) and AGENDA item 3 claim, and what they leave open

### Takeaway
The spec defines standing as a coordinated actor's Shapley share in the game v(S) = φ_s(S), and the security of that standing as core membership. It reports that a lone mediator is the veto player and the Shapley-argmax (115 of 115), taking 2/3 of value on the read-recipient triad and just over half on structured triads. AGENDA item 3, the rent/arbitrariness link, is still open: the spec never mentions rent or arbitrariness. The lab's own rent essay partly cuts against the AGENDA hypothesis.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec claims (full text read).** Standing is the actor's pivotality, i.e. its Shapley share. Its security is core membership: "Low standing is a small Shapley share held outside the core; high standing is a large share the game cannot evict." — [formal_standing.md, §"Standing, formally"](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/instruments/formal_standing.md)
- The spec's empirical anchors are as follows. The single mediator is "the pivotal party, the veto player, and the Shapley-argmax, in every form the lab found (`veto_player`, 115 of 115)". Its share is 2/3 on the read-recipient triad and "a little over half" on the structured mediated triads. On those structured triads the core is non-empty. A party outside the "irreducible core" carries negative Shapley value. — [formal_standing.md](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/instruments/formal_standing.md)
- **Exit and voice.** A bypass collapses a contingent mediator's share and does not move a necessary mediator's share. Cooperatives, data trusts and bargaining count as voice moves that "rewrite the game". Interoperability and portability count as exit moves. — same file, §§"bridge", "institutions"
- **Limits the spec states itself.** The forms are random Boolean truth tables. "no mediator, platform, or outside option is wired into the population". The bridge to organizations "is unbuilt". The reading is "a candidate formalization, offered for its suggestiveness". — same file, §"What this is and is not"
- **AGENDA item 3, verbatim:** "is a mediator's arbitrariness measurable as the rent it can extract without losing the coordination, and is coordinative sovereignty the constraint of that rent by the coordinated parties' standing?" The AGENDA progress note lists item 3 as open. — [AGENDA.md](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/AGENDA.md)
- **The lab's caveat on the veto theorem (FORMAL-Φ).** "The standard theorem that a veto player carries the maximal Shapley value is about simple or monotone games ... this game is neither simple nor monotone ... so the theorem motivates the question but does not apply. The result is therefore empirical, not deductive." In the random sample, "A veto player guarantees a non-empty core in a monotone simple game; this game is neither, and the guarantee fails." — [phi_as_a_cooperative_game.md, §"The mediator has three names" and §core](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/org_frontier/essays/phi_as_a_cooperative_game.md)
- **The lab's rent results (FORMAL-Φ).** On the faithful triad the mediator takes 1.333 of Φ = 2.0, and each outer party takes 0.333. On the conjunctive mediator, self-interest "destroys value instead of extracting rent": value falls from 2.0 to 0.5 and the mediator's share falls to a co-equal third. On loosely mediating forms, self-interest can tighten the mediator into a full bottleneck that then takes 2/3. "The rent is the price of being the thing both parties must pass through, not a transfer the mediator can enlarge by ceasing to be it ... Φ is not money." (Q111, Q131, Q132) — [political_economy_of_interested_mediation.md, abstract and Discussion](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/org_frontier/essays/political_economy_of_interested_mediation.md)

### Inferences
- **Terminology collision (flag for the author).** The spec uses "core" in two senses. "Irreducible core" in the negative-Shapley sentence is IIT's sense (the integrating set). "Core membership" is the game-theoretic sense. In game theory the core is a set of *allocations* (Gillies), not a set of players. A player cannot be "in the core". Only an allocation can, and a player's share can lie within the range of core allocations. The spec should say "the actor's claim is supported by some or every core allocation". It should not say "the actor is in the core".
- **AGENDA hypothesis versus the lab's own Q131.** If "arbitrary" means overriding the parties to serve the mediator's own agenda, then on the conjunctive mediator the Φ-rent *falls* as the mediator acts arbitrarily. On loose mediators it can rise. So in the lab's models, rent extracted without losing the coordination is bounded by the mediator's *faithful* bottleneck share, and arbitrariness of that kind is not what earns it. That is a FORMAL-Φ result. It contradicts the simple form of the AGENDA conjecture for one class of forms and supports it for another.
- The spec says nothing about who *chooses* among core allocations. Arbitrariness, in Pettit's and Lovett's sense, lives exactly there (see Q4).

### Gaps
- I did not open the `veto_player`, `structured_forms` or `shapley_membership` thread files. The 115/115 and "a little over half" figures are reported here as the spec and essay state them. I did not recheck them, and that would need the thread data rather than new Φ.

## Q2. Primary formal sources, and why a lone necessary mediator takes the veto position and a large share

### Takeaway
All four primary records are confirmed. The precise ordinary-game result is this: in a simple game, the core is non-empty if and only if there is a veto player. In that case the core is exactly the set of imputations that give zero to every non-veto player. A lone necessary mediator therefore takes *everything* in the core and, in the canonical triad, 2/3 by Shapley **[computed]**. None of this transfers deductively to the Φ game, which is neither simple nor monotone.

### Cited Findings
- **Shapley (1953).** "A Value for n-Person Games", in *Contributions to the Theory of Games II* (Annals of Mathematics Studies 28), Princeton UP, pp. 307–317/318, DOI 10.1515/9781400881970-018 — **record only** — [Crossref/De Gruyter](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400881970-018). I read none of the text. The axioms (efficiency, symmetry, additivity, dummy) are confirmed only through a secondary summary — [Wikipedia, Shapley value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley_value), **secondary only**.
- **Shapley & Shubik (1954).** "A Method for Evaluating the Distribution of Power in a Committee System", *American Political Science Review* 48(3), 787–792, DOI 10.2307/1951053 — **abstract only** (opening passage via OpenAlex) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1951053). They offer "a method for the a priori evaluation of the division of power" based on game theory "applied to ... 'simple games' and 'weighted majority games'". They ask: "How ... is one to predict the degree of protection which a proposed system affords to minority interests?" — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2307/1951053)
- **Gillies (1959).** "Solutions to General Non-Zero-Sum Games", in *Contributions to the Theory of Games IV* (AM-40), Princeton UP, pp. 47–86, DOI 10.1515/9781400882168-005 — **record only** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400882168-005)
- **Veto/core theorem (FORMAL-TU), exact statement.** Let (N, v) be a simple game with v(C) ∈ {0,1}. Player i is a veto player if i ∉ C implies v(C) = 0. "Proposition 1: A simple game (and thus also a voting game) has a nonempty core iff it has at least one veto player." The proof gives each of k veto players 1/k and everyone else 0. — [Endriss, *Game Theory 2026*, "Stability in Coalitional Games", slide 15, U. Amsterdam](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/u.endriss/teaching/game-theory/slides/gt11.pdf), **full text read**
- **The core is concentrated on veto players (FORMAL-TU).** "Theorem 7.2.1. Let (N, v) be a simple game. Then Core(N, v) = {x : x is an imputation, x_i = 0 for each non-veto player i}." The proof runs: for a non-veto i, x(N\{i}) ≥ v(N\{i}) = 1, so x_i = 0. — [Airiau, Lecture 7 "Voting Games", Dauphine cooperative-games notes 2012](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~airiau/Teaching/CoopGames/2012/votingGames.pdf), **full text read**. The same result, assuming monotonicity, labelled "this is OR Exercise 261.1": "if there are few veto players it predicts dire consequences for the others". Theorem 7.2.2 adds that a simple game is convex iff it is the unanimity game of its veto players. — [Modica, *Class notes on Cooperative Games*, §1.9](https://sites.unipa.it/modica/CooperativeGamesNotes.pdf), **full text read**
- **Textbook locator.** Osborne & Rubinstein, *A Course in Game Theory* (MIT Press, 1994), Exercise 261.1 (ch. 13, "The Core"). I have this locator **secondhand from Modica's notes**. I did not open the book, because the free PDF on the author's site returned 404 and the Osborne book site needs a login — [O&R book page](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/osborne/cgt/). The book record is confirmed via a review record: [Games and Economic Behavior 11(1), 1995, DOI 10.1006/game.1995.1043](https://doi.org/10.1006/game.1995.1043). Maschler, Solan & Zamir, *Game Theory* (CUP 2013, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511794216; 2nd ed. 2020, DOI 10.1017/9781108636049) is **record only** — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511794216). **UNVERIFIED locator.** I found no chapter or page in Maschler et al. for the veto/core result.
- **Toy mediator games [computed] (FORMAL-TU).** These use plain {M, A, B} games, not Φ.
  - **(a) Lone necessary mediator.** v(S) = 1 iff M ∈ S and |S| ≥ 2. Shapley values are M = 2/3, A = B = 1/6. By the Airiau/Modica theorem the core is the single allocation (1, 0, 0): the core gives the mediator *all* the surplus.
  - **(b) Unanimity.** All three are needed. Shapley gives 1/3 each, and every imputation is in the core.
  - **(c) Game (a) with a direct A–B tie worth b.** M's Shapley value falls linearly: 2/3, 7/12, 1/2, 5/12, 1/3 for b = 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1. Formally, φ_M = 2/3 − b/3. For every b > 0 the core is empty, because {M,A} and {M,B} are each worth v(N) = 1.
- **Exhaustive check [computed].** In every monotone simple game on 3 players (10 games) and 4 players (53 games) that has a veto player, every veto player is a Shapley-argmax. There were 0 counterexamples. This supports the "standard theorem" the lab essay invokes. It is a finite check, not a proof, and I found no published source for the general statement (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- **Accessible statement.** Count value only when the mediator is present. Every coalition that can produce anything then has to include the mediator, so it is pivotal in every ordering where it does not arrive first (4 of 6 orderings in the triad), and the Shapley value gives it 2/3. The core is harsher. Each coordinated party can be dropped and replaced by the other, so neither can block anything, and the only allocation no coalition can improve on gives the mediator everything. Core-stability secures the *mediator's* hold, not the coordinated actors'.
- **Implication for the spec (flag).** Under the ordinary-game reading, "security = core membership" protects the veto player. A coordinated actor in a lone-mediator simple game has core payoff 0. The spec's sobering reading is therefore stronger in plain TU games than the spec states. In the Φ game this need not hold, because it is not simple (structured triads have non-empty cores in which the outer parties get positive shares, per the spec). This is exactly where FORMAL-TU stops and FORMAL-Φ must speak for itself.
- Toy (c) gives the spec's exit claim a TU counterpart. A bypass lowers a contingent mediator's Shapley share in proportion to the direct tie's worth. In this specification it also destabilizes the allocation (empty core), which is a different statement from "the parties' shares rise".

### Gaps
- No primary or textbook page for "a veto player has maximal Shapley value in monotone simple games". I checked only n ≤ 4 by computation. **UNVERIFIED as a cited theorem.**
- O&R Exercise 261.1: locator secondhand. Maschler–Solan–Zamir locator not found.
- Shapley 1953 and Gillies 1959: I read no text. The core concept is commonly credited jointly to Gillies and to Shapley's earlier lectures. I did not verify that here.

## Q3. Platform economics and value capture: what the literature says about a mediator's share

### Takeaway
The value-capture literature gives the formal bridge the spec needs. Brandenburger and Stuart's "added value", v(N) − v(N∖i), bounds what a party can capture. Ryall and Sorenson show that a broker profits only without substitutes, with more than two parties, and without lock-in. The two-sided-market papers explain the mediator's price structure, not its share as a cooperative-game quantity.

### Cited Findings
- **Brandenburger & Stuart (1996).** "Value-based Business Strategy", *Journal of Economics & Management Strategy* 5(1), 5–24, DOI 10.1111/j.1430-9134.1996.00005.x — **abstract only** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1430-9134.1996.00005.x). The "added value" of a firm is "shown under certain conditions to impose an upper bound on how much value the firm can capture". "The key to a firm's achieving a positive added value is the existence of asymmetries". "Cooperative game theory provides the underpinnings." — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1111/j.1430-9134.1996.00005.x)
- **Brandenburger & Stuart (2007).** "Biform Games", *Management Science* 53(4), 537–549, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1060.0591 — **abstract only**. A hybrid model: "The noncooperative component ... models the strategic moves. The cooperative component models the resulting competitive environment", and strategy is "making moves to try to shape the competitive environment in a favorable way". — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1287/mnsc.1060.0591)
- **Ryall & Sorenson (2007).** "Brokers and competitive advantage", *Management Science* 53(4), 566–583 — lab card `ryall2007brokers` (verified: full). In a biform game, brokers gain "only if (1) they do not face substitutes either for the connections they offer or the value they can create, (2) they intermediate more than two parties, and (3) interdependence does not lock them into a particular pattern of exchange". With endogenous ties, "profitable positions of intermediation only arise under strict assumptions of unilateral action". — [card](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/literature/library/cards/ryall2007brokers.md)
- **Rochet & Tirole (2003).** *JEEA* 1(4), 990–1029, DOI 10.1162/154247603322493212 — card `rochet2003platform` (verified: abstract). The price *structure* across sides, not only the level, determines volume ("get both sides of the market on board"). The card's audit note says not to attribute "bottleneck" to them. — [card](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/literature/library/cards/rochet2003platform.md)
- **Armstrong (2006).** *RAND J. Econ.* 37(3), 668–691 — card `armstrong2006competition` (verified: abstract). "Competing bottlenecks": each platform has monopoly access to its single-homing side, and the multi-homing side pays. — [card](file:///Users/ludwitt/iit-playground/pyphi-experiments/submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/literature/library/cards/armstrong2006competition.md)
- **Hagiu & Wright.** I found no Hagiu–Wright paper whose subject is the platform's share of value as such. The closest are listed here.
  - "Multi-sided platforms", *IJIO* 43 (2015), 162–174, DOI 10.1016/j.ijindorg.2015.03.003 — **abstract only** (RePEc). It models the multi-sided-platform versus vertical-integration trade-off between coordinating spillovers and motivating professionals' unobservable effort. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/indorg/v43y2015icp162-174.html)
  - "Marketplace leakage" (2024) — card `hagiu2024marketplace` (verified: abstract). Fee avoidance by disintermediation, and the substitutable instruments (parity clauses, communication limits, referral fees) a platform uses to prevent it.
  - "Marketplace or reseller?" (2015) — card `hagiu2015marketplace` (verified: abstract).
- **Rent extraction by design.** Card `piolatto2024platform` (verified: full): adding an anonymous channel "improves rent extraction" by decoupling the firms' outside option. Card `oreilly2024algorithmic` (verified: abstract): an attention rent is "a deviation from the best attention allocations of which the platform is capable, and on whose promise the platform drew its users".

### Inferences
- **Formal bridge (FORMAL-TU).** In toy (a), A's added value is v(N) − v({M,B}) = 0 and M's is 1. The core outcome (1, 0, 0) is exactly the Brandenburger–Stuart bound binding for A and B. "Standing" in TU terms is best read as **added value**: what the coalition loses if you leave, i.e. a formal counterpart to Lovett's exit cost (Q4). Shapley share is an *average* of marginal contributions. Added value is the *security* bound. The spec conflates the two under "pivotality".
- Ryall and Sorenson's conditions (1) and (3) correspond to the spec's necessary/contingent test (substitutes for the broker's connections means contingent), but this is ANALOGY at the level of the Φ game. Their endogenous-tie result warns that the bottleneck rent is fragile once parties can form ties. That fits the lab's toy (c) and the spec's exit claim.
- O'Reilly et al.'s attention rent, defined as deviation from the best allocation the platform *could* make, is the closest economics analogue to "rent extracted without losing the coordination". It is a discretion-based, not a position-based, rent, which matches Pettit/Lovett arbitrariness better than the Shapley share does.

### Gaps
- Brandenburger and Stuart's exact conditions for the added-value bound, and the biform-game "confidence index", were not read (abstract only).
- Hagiu–Wright on "value capture" specifically: **not found**. If the abstract cites a specific Hagiu–Wright value-capture paper, it needs a separate search.

## Q4. Philosophy: Lovett, Pettit, Reich, and whether anyone has formalized domination by cooperative games or bargaining power

### Takeaway
Lovett's three-part analysis maps onto game objects only in part. Dependency, which Lovett defines through exit costs, maps cleanly to outside options and added value. Imbalance of power maps to asymmetric added value. *Arbitrariness*, which Lovett reads procedurally, is not a property of v at all. I found game-theoretic models of domination (Lovett's Appendix II; Ingham & Lovett 2019). I found none that uses the Shapley value or the core.

### Cited Findings
- **Lovett (2010).** *A General Theory of Domination and Justice*, Oxford UP, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.001.0001 — **abstract only** (book and chapter abstracts) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.001.0001)
  - Domination is "a condition experienced by persons or groups to the extent that they are dependent on a social relationship in which some other person or group wields arbitrary power over them" (the "arbitrary power conception"). The methods include "formal modeling". — [OpenAlex book abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.001.0001)
  - Ch. 2, "Social Relationships and Dependency", pp. 25–54: "dependency is analyzed in terms of the costs of exit faced by the subjects of domination." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.003.0002)
  - Ch. 3, "The Imbalance of Power Conception", pp. 55–84: an imbalance of social power is "a necessary, but not sufficient condition of domination". Power is "the ability of one person or group to change what another person or group would otherwise want to do", with attention to "strategies and rational expectations". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.003.0003)
  - Ch. 4, "Arbitrariness and Social Conventions", pp. 85–124: "Democratic and other substantive interpretations of arbitrariness are rejected in favor of a procedural interpretation." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.003.0004)
  - Appendix II, pp. 239–256: "I experiment with some formal models of social relationships characterized by domination ... the formal models offered here are static models." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199579419.005.0002), **extract only**
- **Ingham & Lovett (2019).** "Republican Freedom, Popular Control, and Collective Action", *American Journal of Political Science* 63(4), 774–787, DOI 10.1111/ajps.12439 — **abstract only**. They offer a "novel interpretation of the republican concept of domination with the help of a game-theoretic model that clarifies the significance of collective action problems". Domination is "others' having unconstrained abilities to frustrate their choices". — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1111/ajps.12439); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v63y2019i4p774-787.html)
- **Pettit (1997).** Lab card `pettit1997republicanism` (verified: search; record only, "summary withheld"). Pettit's "capacity to interfere with impunity and at will in certain choices that the other is in a position to make" reached me only as a quotation in a search result. **UNVERIFIED against Pettit's text, page not confirmed.** Related cards: `pettit2012people` (abstract), `muldoon2023algorithmic` (abstract; algorithmic domination: "subjected to a dominating power, the operations of which are ... determined directly by an algorithm"), `cicerchia2022structural` (abstract), `hasan2021republicanism` (full), `gadeke2020mugger` (full), `christiaens2025platform` (abstract).
- **Reich (1964).** "The New Property", *Yale Law Journal* 73(5), 733–787, DOI 10.2307/794645 — **record only**. Crossref and OpenAlex give the first page as 733 only. The end page 787 is **UNVERIFIED**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/794645). Secondary accounts describe the argument as follows. Reich argued that government benefits, contracts and licences, which he called "largess", should be treated as rights. He worried about the state's "unbridled discretionary authority" over largess and a "new feudalism". *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) drew on the article. — [Resnik, YLJ Forum 2020](https://www.yalelawjournal.org/forum/constituting-security-and-fairness-reflecting-on-charles-reichs-imagination-and-impact), [Peñalver, "Reich's New Property at Fifty"](https://law.loyno.edu/sites/law.loyno.edu/files/property_power_and_freedom_1.pdf), **secondary only** (search snippets; not opened).

### Inferences
- **Mapping Lovett onto the game (formal vs analogy).**
  1. *Dependency = exit cost.* The formal counterpart is the coordinated party's outside option v({i}) and its added value. In toy (a), A's exit cost is all of its share, and its added value is 0 because B substitutes. This is FORMAL-TU, and it is the cleanest link in the stream.
  2. *Imbalance of power.* The counterpart is asymmetric added value or Shapley share (veto versus non-veto). This is FORMAL-TU.
  3. *Arbitrariness.* This is about whether the mediator's power is checked by procedures, i.e. conventions known to all. The characteristic function says nothing about it. In toy (a) the core pins the mediator's take at 1. In games with large cores, the *range* of core allocations is room for bargaining, and arbitrariness could be read as the mediator's unilateral power to pick a point in that range. That reading is ANALOGY, and I do not attribute it to any source.
- **Answer to AGENDA item 3.** "Arbitrariness = rent extractable without losing the coordination" names Lovett's *dependency* and *imbalance* conditions, not his *arbitrariness* condition. A regulated, fee-capped necessary mediator could hold the veto position and a large Shapley share and still not be arbitrary in Lovett's procedural sense. The rent measures the *stakes* of arbitrariness, i.e. how much the mediator can withhold, not arbitrariness itself. The defensible version is that coordinative sovereignty constrains the mediator's discretion over the division (procedural) *and* raises the parties' added value (structural). The first is political theory, and only the second is formal.
- Reich supplies the dependence-on-a-dispenser-of-largess figure. In game terms, the state there acts as a veto player over each holder's entitlement. This is ANALOGY only.

### Gaps
- I did not read Lovett's Appendix II model (extract only). It is unknown whether it uses bargaining or cooperative-game tools. This is the single most important source to read in full before the abstract claims novelty.
- Ingham & Lovett's model type (non-cooperative or cooperative) is not established from the abstract.
- **Gap search (documented).** WebSearch for "domination arbitrary power formal model bargaining republicanism game theory" returned Ingham & Lovett 2019, Lovett 2010, and a noncooperative voting-power chapter. I found no work that formalizes republican domination with the Shapley value or the core in those results. A survey, Chung & Kogelmann (2024), "Formal models in normative political theory", *Journal of Theoretical Politics* 36(3), 256–274, appeared only in a citation list and was **not checked (UNVERIFIED)**.

## Q6. Has anyone used the Shapley value or the core for platform workers' or users' standing, or modelled bargaining, trusts or cooperatives as coalition formation that changes the core?

### Takeaway
I found no work that measures platform workers' or users' bargaining position or "standing" by the Shapley value or the core. I also found no work that models collective bargaining, data trusts or platform cooperatives as coalition formation that changes the core. The nearest neighbours are non-cooperative or qualitative accounts of gig-worker power, a classic Shapley wage-bargaining precedent, and the brokerage biform game. On the evidence of these searches the gap looks open, but my searches were limited.

### Inferences
- **[computed] Formal sketch the paper could offer (FORMAL-TU, not Φ).** Take toy (a), where M plus any one worker wins, and merge A and B into a bloc W that negotiates as one, as collective bargaining or a trust would. The game becomes two-player unanimity: v({M,W}) = 1, with both M and W veto players. By the Airiau/Modica theorem the core becomes *every* split (x_M, x_W) with x_M + x_W = 1, and Shapley gives 1/2 each. The workers' core payoff rises from exactly 0 to anywhere in [0, 1], and their Shapley share rises from 1/3 in total to 1/2. Coalition formation that removes substitutability turns the coordinated parties into veto players. This is a textbook consequence, not a new theorem: Theorem 7.2.2 says unanimity games are the convex simple games. It gives the spec's "voice rewrites the game" claim a precise ordinary-game form. It also shows where *arbitrariness* re-enters: the core no longer fixes the split, so who picks the point in [0, 1] is a procedural question (Lovett ch. 4) that the game leaves open.
- **Positioning.** The abstract can claim, with a documented search, that "I found no work that uses Shapley value or core membership to measure platform-mediated actors' standing, nor that models collective bargaining, data trusts or cooperatives as coalition formation that changes the core". The searches were the two WebSearch queries above, plus the lab library index. It should not claim this formalization as a new theorem, because the TU results are textbook. The novelty is the reading, together with the Φ instantiation (the lab's structured triads, where the core is non-empty and the outer parties hold positive shares).
- **Relation to Φ.** The TU sketch shows *why* the lab's Φ game is worth exploring rather than replaceable by the toy. In plain simple games the outcome is fixed by who is a veto player. The Φ game's coalition worths come from irreducible cause-effect structure, so the size of the mediator's share (2/3 versus "a little over half"), whether the core is empty, and negative Shapley for diluting parties all depend on the coordination's actual mechanism. That is information a 0/1 winning-coalition list does not contain. The two layers are analogous (same solution concepts), not homologous (different value functions).
