## Hobbes already theorized the mediator its authors cannot leave

Bodin, Hobbes and Rousseau all place sovereignty in one undivided seat, but they do not all assume that the sovereign party stands apart. Bodin calls sovereignty "the most high, absolute, and perpetuall power over the citisens and subiects in a Commonweale" (Knolles tr. 1606, I.8, **[full text read]**) ([Perseids](https://fgh.perseids.org/read/pdlpsci/bodin/livrep/perseus-eng1/1.8)). The indivisibility gloss on the lab's Franklin card is the publisher's paraphrase, not Bodin's words (`card: bodin1992sovereignty`, verified: search) ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Bodin_On_Sovereignty.html?id=vGRdVXJ4IIMC)), and the common Franklin wording "absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth" is UNVERIFIED. Philpott's SEP entry (rev. 17 Sep 2024, **[full text read via fetch; recheck wording]**) says Bodin and Hobbes "conceived the sovereign as being above the law" (§1) and calls Hobbes's sovereign "a mortal god unbound by any constitution" (§2) ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sovereignty/)).

Hobbes is the most useful foil, because his *subjects* sit inside the sovereign. In ch. XVII each man says "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man", and the Commonwealth becomes "One Person, of whose Acts a great Multitude … have made themselves every one the Author" (**[full text read]**, Gutenberg #3207, chapter and marginal head only, no page numbers) ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3207/pg3207.txt)). Chapter XVIII then closes the door: "1. The Subjects Cannot Change The Forme Of Government", "2. Soveraigne Power Cannot Be Forfeited", "These Rights Are Indivisible", with the rights "incommunicable, and inseparable". In other words, Hobbes describes a mediator its constituents author and cannot leave, and he settles the problem by giving them authorship without voice. Coordinative sovereignty reads as an inversion of that bargain: it keeps the no-exit premise and refuses authorship without voice. Rousseau closes the other door. Sovereignty "can never be alienated" (II.1), "is indivisible" (II.2) and "cannot be represented" (III.15) (Cole tr., **[full text read]**) ([Gutenberg #46333](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46333/pg46333.txt)). On his terms a mediated condition has no sovereignty at all. His phrase "the key to the working of the political machine" (I.7) is a rhetorical bridge to "machine sovereignty", and S1 advises using it only as rhetoric.

Habermas answers the indivisibility objection. Flynn (2004, *EJPT* 3(4) 433–454, **[full text read]**) quotes Appendix I of *Between Facts and Norms*: "Subjectless and anonymous, an intersubjectively dissolved popular sovereignty withdraws into democratic procedures … sovereignty makes itself felt in the power of public discourses" ([Flynn PDF](https://faculty.fordham.edu/jeflynn/Flynn_Communicative_Power.pdf)). The wording is now secondary-verbatim; the page (pp. 486–487 on `card: habermas1996facts`, verified: search; Appendix I runs pp. 463–490) is still unchecked against the MIT text. Flynn also quotes the Postscript, "the democratic process bears the entire burden of legitimation" (BFN p. 450, his n. 19). The abstract's phrase "law a public can read" is the brief's paraphrase, not a Habermas quotation.

Gowder (2023, ch. 5, pp. 140–166, **[abstract plus one body sentence]**) says of the Trump deplatforming that "a state of exception was needed, and was declared" (§5.2) ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/networked-leviathan/actually-existing-platform-selfconstraint-up-to-a-point/D2D397F9FC54E271951E3EA246AA80F9)). Monaci (2024, *IJoC* 18, 4298–4313, **[full text read]**) defines platform sovereignty as the "prerogative to decide who or what platforms to exclude" (p. 4299) and writes of platforms' "sovereign role" since 2021 (p. 4304 by sequence; check the pinpoint) ([IJoC PDF](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/21896/4754)). Foucault supplies the strongest objection to keeping "sovereignty" at all. ## Algorithmic power addresses profiles, so competence earns no standing

The best-supported claim in the starting abstract is "competence alone confers no standing". Rouvroy & Berns (2013, *Réseaux* 177(1) 163–196, **[full text read]**, French; English glosses are S2's) supply its theory ([UNamur PDF](https://pure.unamur.be/ws/files/54965417/7413.pdf)). Algorithmic governmentality "ne produit aucune subjectivation" and bypasses reflexive subjects through infra-individual data and supra-individual profiles. That sentence runs across **pp. 173–174**; `card: rouvroy2013gouvernementalite` wrongly gives p. 173 alone. People are addressed "quelles que soient par ailleurs leurs capacités d'entendement, de volonté, d'expression" through their profiles (p. 175). The system gives "ni lieu, ni prise" to any subject "susceptible de le légitimer ou de lui résister" (p. 181). Rouvroy and Berns hedge more than their slogan suggests. They reject the desubjectivation reading (pp. 175–176), describe a scarcity of occasions to become a subject rather than its destruction (p. 180), and grant that the same order breeds "hyper-sujets" (p. 179). Their remedy is "le commun", and they call person-centred data rights "aussi inefficaces que mal fondées" (p. 193). Other located passages are the statistical double (p. 167), default adhesion in place of consent (p. 173), "récalcitrance" (p. 174), correlation that draws "aucun peuple" (p. 191) and "multiple sans altérité" (p. 192). Their footnote 15 on p. 181 is the warning an algorithmacy argument must meet: self-tracking "présuppose un sujet qui s'affine, plus qu'elle ne témoigne d'un sujet qui se produit". The English translation is **[record only]**, and sources disagree on the translator's name.

Foucault cuts both ways. "The Subject and Power" (1982, *Critical Inquiry* 8(4) 777–795, **[full text read]**) supports the claim: "Power is exercised only over free subjects", and freedom is power's "precondition" and "permanent support" (p. 790), so the capacity to read the field is the material government works on ([PDF](https://crabgrass.riseup.net/assets/122934/subject.pdf)). Item 5 of his list of struggles, against "the privileges of knowledge" linked to "competence, and qualification" (p. 781), warns that algorithmacy could become a new privilege. He undercuts the step to *sovereignty*, though. His programme is to "refuse what we are" and "promote new forms of subjectivity" (p. 785), and resistance is immanent to every power relation (p. 794). Other located passages are the aim of the work (p. 777), the two meanings of "subject" (p. 781), "action upon … acting subjects" (p. 789), "To govern … is to structure the possible field of action of others" (p. 790) and agonism (p. 790). In "Technologies of the Self" (1988, **[full text read]** for pp. 17–19; chapter range 16–49 UNVERIFIED; one OCR word bracketed as "[souls]" needs a print check), governmentality is "This contact between the technologies of domination of others and those of the self" (p. 19). That definition places algorithmacy, read as a self-technique (p. 18), at the hinge of governmentality rather than outside it ([Monoskop](https://www.monoskop.org/images/0/03/Technologies_of_the_Self_A_Seminar_with_Michel_Foucault.pdf)). The *Discipline and Punish* line "power produces; it produces reality" is UNVERIFIED (commonly p. 194, Vintage) and must not be quoted.

Deleuze (1992, *October* 59, 3–7, **[full text read]**, section locators only; October pages UNVERIFIED) supplies the image: "the code is a password", codes "mark access to information, or reject it", and "Individuals have become 'dividuals'" (§2) ([Purdue PDF](https://deleuze.cla.purdue.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Deleuze_Gilles-Postscript-on-the-Societies-of-Control.pdf)). He asks whether unions can adapt and leaves it "up to" the young "to discover what they're being made to serve" (§3). In those terms algorithmacy produces the right password, which wins passage but not standing.

The ordinary-game results are exact and harsh (FORMAL-TU). A simple game has a non-empty core if and only if it has a veto player (Endriss 2026, slide 15, **[full text read]**) ([UvA](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/u.endriss/teaching/game-theory/slides/gt11.pdf)). That core is exactly the set of imputations giving zero to every non-veto player (Airiau 2012, Thm 7.2.1, **[full text read]**) ([Dauphine](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~airiau/Teaching/CoopGames/2012/votingGames.pdf)). Modica's notes (**[full text read]**) give the monotone version as Osborne & Rubinstein Exercise 261.1, a locator S5 has only secondhand, and add that a simple game is convex if and only if it is its veto players' unanimity game (Thm 7.2.2) ([Modica](https://sites.unipa.it/modica/CooperativeGamesNotes.pdf)). S5 computed the toy games in exact fractions (**[computed]**, plain TU, no Φ). With a lone necessary mediator (v(S) = 1 iff M ∈ S and |S| ≥ 2), Shapley gives M 2/3 and A and B 1/6 each, and **the core is the single allocation (1, 0, 0)**. A direct A–B tie worth b lowers M's share as φ_M = 2/3 − b/3 and empties the core for every b > 0. Merging A and B into one bloc W yields two-player unanimity: the core becomes every split, Shapley gives 1/2 each, and the workers' core payoff rises from exactly 0 to anywhere in [0, 1]. An exhaustive check of monotone simple games found every veto player to be a Shapley-argmax (10 games on three players, 53 on four, zero counterexamples). That is a finite check, and S5 found no published statement of the general theorem. Shapley 1953 and Gillies 1959 are **[record only]** ([Shapley](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400881970-018); [Gillies](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400882168-005)). Shapley & Shubik 1954 is **[abstract only]** ([doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/1951053)). Maschler–Solan–Zamir is **[record only]**, with no locator for the result.

Three corrections follow. First, in game theory the core is a set of allocations (Gillies), so a player cannot be "in the core". Only a share can lie within the range of core allocations. The spec also uses "irreducible core" in IIT's sense in the same passage. Second, the spec folds two quantities into "pivotality". The Shapley share is an average of marginal contributions, while Brandenburger & Stuart's added value, v(N) − v(N∖i), is the security bound (1996, **[abstract only]**) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1430-9134.1996.00005.x)). In the lone-mediator toy each coordinated party's added value is 0. Third, the Shapley value equals pivot probability only when cooperation decisions are exchangeable (arXiv 1808.02696, **[record only]**, S6) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.02696)). Ryall & Sorenson (2007, `card: ryall2007brokers`, verified: full) show that brokers gain only without substitutes, with more than two parties and without lock-in, which parallels the necessary/contingent test as analogy. Rochet & Tirole (verified: abstract; do not attribute "bottleneck" to them), Armstrong ("competing bottlenecks", verified: abstract) and Hagiu & Wright (2015 IJIO **[abstract only]**; 2015 and 2024 cards at abstract) explain price structure rather than share. S5 found no Hagiu–Wright value-capture paper. Piolatto (2024, verified: full) shows that an anonymous channel "improves rent extraction".

The rent reading names the wrong part of domination. Lovett (2010, **[abstract only]**) splits domination into dependency, "analyzed in terms of the costs of exit" (ch. 2, pp. 25–54), an imbalance of power that is "necessary, but not sufficient" (ch. 3, pp. 55–84), and arbitrariness under "a procedural interpretation" (ch. 4, pp. 85–124). Dependency maps onto outside options and added value, and imbalance onto asymmetric shares, both FORMAL-TU. Arbitrariness is not a property of v at all. Rent extractable without losing the coordination measures the *stakes* of arbitrariness, not arbitrariness itself. A fee-capped, regulated necessary mediator could hold the veto position and still not be arbitrary in Lovett's sense. Ingham & Lovett (2019, **[abstract only]**) model domination game-theoretically as others' "unconstrained abilities to frustrate their choices" ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12439)). Pettit's "interfere with impunity and at will" is UNVERIFIED with no page. **Option A: the sovereign who cannot leave (genealogy plus the distinction map).** This angle would read coordinative sovereignty as the inversion of Hobbes: keep his no-exit premise, refuse his authorship without voice, and use S1's three-cell map (sovereignty *over*, *of* and *through* the machine) to show that the third cell is empty. The evidence for it is strong. The Hobbes ch. XVII–XVIII quotations are **[full text read]**, and the Rousseau and Habermas (via Flynn) quotations are full or secondary-verbatim. The map answers J1's "possibility and limits" bullet directly, and the bearer novelty is the claim S6's searches support best. The Schmitt gap on automated deactivation offers a vivid case, and the angle suits the continental-leaning register S7 infers. It also delivers AGENDA item 2 without reusing the IGI chapter. The evidence against it is substantial. Foucault (Part Four, pp. 88–89) asks why "sovereignty" should be kept at all. Agamben, Hui, Kera and *The Stack* itself were not read, and Bratton reaches the notes only secondhand. Lazar anticipates the voice-over-exit move, so the map must carry the novelty without it. Hasan's structural domination questions whether a "standing" claim has an addressee. A genealogy also spends many of 500 words on history.

**Option B: algorithmacy as counter-subjectivation.** This angle would read algorithmacy as the "rapport" with one's statistical double that Rouvroy and Berns find missing (p. 181), and argue that without an institution the rapport stays private refinement, so standing needs a political form. Its support is real. The angle answers the PhilEvents call's Foucauldian framing head-on. No verified source frames algorithmic literacy as counter-subjectivation, and Foucault's "promote new forms of subjectivity" (p. 785) gives a canonical warrant. The gaming line (Petre et al.; Ziewitz) and *Louisiana v. US* give concrete anchors, and the angle uses Roger's own construct. The case against it is also strong. Footnote 15 (p. 181) says reflexive self-tracking only "refines a presupposed subject". Magalhães (2018) finds self-harmful subjectivation. Foucault p. 790 makes competence power's "permanent support", and p. 781 warns of a new "privilege of knowledge". Rouvroy and Berns want "le commun", not sovereignty (p. 193). Almost every empirical source is abstract-only, the novelty search here was thin (Isin & Ruppert and the MDPI article unchecked), and the angle engages little of J2, J4 or J8.

**Option C: arbitrary power as rent.** This angle would argue that a necessary mediator's veto position measures the *stakes* of arbitrariness, and that coordinative sovereignty does two jobs. It raises the parties' added value (formal, shown by the bloc sketch), and it subjects the mediator's discretion over the division to procedure (political theory). For it: AGENDA item 3 is open, J4 names "the distribution of technological rents", the TU results are exact and verified at full text, the bloc computation makes "voice rewrites the game" precise, and S5 found no Shapley- or core-based account of worker standing. The lab's Φ game adds information a 0/1 game lacks. Against it: the lab's own Q131 shows that self-interest *destroys* Φ-value on conjunctive mediators, so the simple AGENDA conjecture fails for one class of forms. Lovett treats arbitrariness as procedural and not a property of v. Lovett's Appendix II, the most important source, is unread. Huang & Xie and Alarcón et al. are formal precedents. The results are about Boolean models and hold as analogy, not homology. A formal argument may sit poorly in a philosophy-first, footnote-style venue, and 500 words leave little room to explain it.

**Option D: sovereign AI as exit at state scale.** This angle would read "sovereign AI" programmes as the liberal exit reflex scaled up to the state, buying territorial custody of foreign-made parts. Frontier models, leading-edge chips and model-release governance would count as candidate necessary mediators where only voice helps, and the June 2026 restoration would be the case in which non-US states had no voice. **[COI flag applies to the June 2026 evidence.]** The support is current and dated. The angle hits J2, J4 and J8 squarely, with dated 2026 evidence (EuroHPC deadlines, CADA tiers, the 10 July UAE rule). It draws on Nvidia's own framing, Jang and Mügge, and no work found applies Hirschman to states and AI providers. Woodward & Rogoyski's "negotiated access guarantees" are a voice remedy in all but name. The counter-evidence is serious. Moving the bearer to the state collides with Hao (2026) and Edler et al. (2023), which already place state sovereignty inside dependence, and that erodes the bearer novelty S6 relies on. Farrell & Newman's chokepoint is a reason to exit, so it marks a contingent mediator. The Gulf case is loyalty, not exit. Much of the evidence is GREY and fast-moving, and the decisive case involves the report writer's maker.

Two combinations also fit the evidence: A as the frame with D's June 2026 case as the anchor (J1, J2, J8), or A as the frame with C's bloc result as the formal coda (J1, J4, J8). Either would let the "competence alone confers no standing" sentence from B survive as a single move.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the abstract should put its weight. The original draft spent its novelty on the claim that sovereignty need not mean independence, and Hobbes, Habermas, Edler, Hao and Lazar have each already made some version of that claim. What none of them supplies is a test that tells a gate one should leave from a mediator one cannot. That test, and the actor who stands inside the second kind, is the contribution. The formal work turns from an ornament into an argument once the core result is stated honestly: under a lone necessary mediator the core secures the mediator's hold, and only a coalition that removes the coordinated parties' substitutability changes that. That reframing gives the paper something a Lazar-reading referee has not seen. It also explains why the Φ game earns its place: it ties shares and core emptiness to the coordination's actual mechanism rather than to a winning-coalition list.

The venue is a separate decision. A 300–500-word abstract costs little, but the 8,000-word paper would land in an unindexed first issue whose editors cannot be identified, under length rules that conflict. Asking the four questions S7 lists before committing the full paper would cost one email, which Roger, not Claude, would send.
