# Deep research: who claims Simmel's deferred case (Packet 2)

Run 2026-08-12 via the deep-research workflow, 4 search angles, 3-lens adversarial verification, vote
counts recorded per finding. Forty-nine claims went into verification. Thirty survived — twelve at 3-0,
eighteen at 2-1 — and nineteen were refuted: nine unanimously by all three lenses, one unanimously by
both lenses that reported on it, and nine by two of three. The twenty-seven findings below merge
claims that say the same thing and print the vote for each. Where lenses disagreed I have kept the
disagreement in the confidence line and in the prose, because in this packet the dissents are where the
work is: three of them caught page locators that were off by one, and two of them caught negatives
built on Crossref deposits that carry no author names at all.

Gap row covered: **A-S7** — does the brokerage tradition, or anyone downstream of it, claim the third
Simmel deferred at *Soziologie* p. 103 / Wolff p. 145?

**The run's central question came back negative.** No scholar was found who says that Simmel postponed
or set aside a third and that they are taking it up. That negative survived every framing tried, in
four angles, across Crossref reference deposits, the Semantic Scholar citing graph, and two full-text
semantic sweeps of the Wiley corpus. But the negative is narrower than the chapter currently needs, and
three findings below (Feld, Lindemann, Vandenberghe) show the chapter has neighbours it must cite
rather than pre-empt. **Read the Absences section before writing a word**, and read it before the
Confirmed-citations list: several of the strongest-sounding results here are absences bounded by an
index, not by the literature.

## Finding 1 — Kwon et al.'s definition makes the broker and the brokered parties alike network actors, so the position has no occupant that is not an actor (high on the text, contested on the entailment; 2-1) [C1]

The field's most-cited recent brokerage review opens with a stipulation, not an argument: brokerage is
an activity of a network actor standing between two or more otherwise disconnected actors. Actorhood
sits at both ends of the relation. All three lenses found the sentence character-for-character where
the claim put it, in the first paragraph of the Introduction. The dissent is about reach, and it is
worth carrying. Lens 3 refused the entailment — "nothing that is not an actor can occupy the position
by definition" — on the ground that Kwon et al. state no exclusion clause anywhere, that their "actor"
is expressly broad (individuals, groups, organizations, communities, which they say "could be
theoretically treated the same"), and that the field does not honour the exclusion anyway: Ajunwa
(Finding 9) puts a software platform in the tertius position and the field's own vocabulary calls it a
broker. Lens 2 conceded the same point in gentler form and supplied wording that keeps the finding
without pretending Kwon et al. assert it. The defensible statement is that the definition leaves the
non-actor third unasked rather than ruled out — which is a weaker claim than the chapter has been
making and a more durable one. Note also, from lens 1: the sentence reads "two or **more** otherwise
disconnected actors," so it establishes an actor requirement, not a known-pair requirement. Do not use
this quotation to carry the pair premise. COMPLICATES; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Seok-Woo Kwon, Emanuela Rondi, Daniel Z. Levin, Alfredo De Massis and Daniel J. Brass,
"Network Brokerage: An Integrative Review and Future Research Agenda," *Journal of Management* 46, no. 6
(2020): 1092–1120 ; DOI 10.1177/0149206320914694 ;
https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/142636/1/Kwon_et_al._2020_JOM_Network_Brokerage.pdf (author
accepted manuscript, Lancaster EPrints)

**Evidence:** Accepted manuscript p. 3, running header "NETWORK BROKERAGE 3," opening of the
Introduction: "Network brokerage (“brokerage” for short) describes an activity of a network actor
(broker) occupying a structural position (bridge, structural hole) between two or more otherwise
disconnected actors (hereafter referred to as alters), and it typically involves an exchange or
interaction between the broker and the alters."

*Correction applied, from lens 2:* "Kwon et al. define brokerage as 'an activity of a network actor
(broker) occupying a structural position (bridge, structural hole) between two or more otherwise
disconnected actors' (accepted MS p. 3). On that stipulated definition the broker and the brokered
parties are alike network actors — individuals, groups or organizations — and an entity that is not an
actor has no place to stand. Kwon et al. state no exclusion; the exclusion follows from their
definition rather than being asserted in it."

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* the published-version locator "JOM 46(6): 1093" is unverified.
Crossref gives the span 1092–1120 and no lens opened the published PDF. Cite the accepted manuscript
page, or cite the article without a pinpoint.

## Finding 2 — Kwon et al.'s exclusion criteria remove non-human networks, computer simulations, and every network outside a work or professional context (high, 3-0) [C2]

The review's population is human and work-based by construction, and the authors say so in their own
methodological supplement. Criterion 5 throws out non-human social networks and computer simulations of
human networks; criterion 6 throws out networks outside a work or professional context — students,
children, families, friends, local community members. Both criteria are quoted verbatim by all three
lenses. Two lenses added the same narrowing, which strengthens the finding rather than weakening it:
the criteria alone do not make the population *organizational*. Step 2 of the search protocol does most
of that work by confining the corpus to 58 named management, organisation and sociology journals, and
criterion 6's own category is "work or professional context," which is wider than "organizational."
One locator correction, agreed by two lenses: criteria 5 and 6 sit wholly on accepted-manuscript p. 94;
p. 93 carries the Step 3 heading and criterion 1. SUPPORTS; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Kwon, Rondi, Levin, De Massis and Brass, "Network Brokerage," *Journal of Management* 46,
no. 6 (2020): 1092–1120, Methodological Supplement Step 3 ; DOI 10.1177/0149206320914694 ;
https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/142636/1/Kwon_et_al._2020_JOM_Network_Brokerage.pdf

**Evidence:** Accepted manuscript p. 94, under "STEP 3. Omit less relevant articles by reviewing titles,
keywords, and abstracts according to the six exclusion criteria" (heading and criterion 1 on p. 93):
"5. Articles focusing on non-human social networks (e.g. animal societies, molecular systems) or
computer simulations of human networks (e.g., we excluded Faust (2010) for this reason), 6. Articles
focusing on social networks outside of a work or professional context, such as students, children,
families, friends, or local community members (e.g., we excluded Frank, Muller, and Mueller (2013) for
this reason)." In-text summary, accepted manuscript p. 5: "Each of the remaining articles was then
analyzed according to six exclusion criteria, eliminating articles that (1) did not specifically and
explicitly focus on brokerage, (2) considered networks as a collection of dyads, thereby neglecting the
role of third-party, (3) considered brokerage as an occupation rather than network intermediary, (4)
focused exclusively on methodological and measurement issues, (5) focused on non-human social networks
or relied on computer simulations, or (6) dealt with networks outside a work or professional context."

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* "Criteria 5 and 6, verbatim, accepted manuscript p. 94
(Methodological Supplement, Step 3, which runs pp. 93–94) … so the review's population is human and
work-or-professional by construction."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "together with Step 2's confinement of the search to 58 management,
organisation and sociology journals, this makes the review's population human and work-based by
construction."

## Finding 3 — Gould and Fernandez open the formalization by importing Marsden's definition verbatim: three actors, two of them the actual parties to the transaction (high, 3-0) [C5]

Section 2 of "Structures of Mediation" is headed "WHAT IS BROKERAGE?" and answers with a quotation. All
three lenses retrieved the JSTOR scan independently, confirmed the running head "STRUCTURES OF MEDIATION
91," and found the passage verbatim at the top of the section. The one drift is in the claim's own
paraphrase rather than in the quotation: Gould and Fernandez write "the actual parties to the
transaction," not "the identified parties." Their whole apparatus presupposes that the two endpoints
are given actors whose group memberships the analyst knows, so the substance survives the correction
intact. SUPPORTS; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Roger V. Gould and Roberto M. Fernandez, "Structures of Mediation: A Formal Approach to
Brokerage in Transaction Networks," *Sociological Methodology* 19 (1989): 89–126 ; DOI 10.2307/270949 ;
https://www.jstor.org/stable/270949 (full scan read via the Oregon State mirror,
https://explorer.bee.oregonstate.edu/Topic/InfluenceNetworks/Documents/270949.pdf)

**Evidence:** p. 91, opening section 2: "Marsden (1982, p. 202) defines brokerage as a process ‘by which
intermediary actors facilitate transactions between other actors lacking access to or trust in one
another.’ Thus, any brokered exchange can be thought of as a relation involving three actors, two of
whom are the actual parties to the transaction and one of whom is the intermediary or broker."

*Correction applied, from lenses 1 and 2:* "…a relation among three actors, 'two of whom are the actual
parties to the transaction and one of whom is the intermediary or broker,' importing Marsden's
definition verbatim."

## Finding 4 — All five Gould–Fernandez types are two-step paths over an ordered pair, and the authors call the list exhaustive under their own scope conditions (high on the two lenses that read it; one lens could not retrieve the article at all; 2-1) [C6]

Two lenses read the full 39-page scan, checked every quoted string against the running heads, and
confirmed the whole chain: exactly five structurally distinct types given a partition into
non-overlapping subgroups; a raw brokerage score t_j that is literally a count of ordered pairs (i,k);
and an authorial exhaustiveness claim at p. 101. Lens 2 could not open the article by any route —
Unpaywall closed, no repository copy, the Internet Archive microfilm item access-restricted, four
course-page mirrors dead — and refused on principle to certify quotations it had not seen, marking the
claim class D for want of retrieval rather than for error. That dissent does not overturn the finding,
because two independent retrievals confirm the text, but it does tell you what a referee without
JSTOR will experience. Two handling notes from lens 1, both real: the quoted snippets are presented in
an order that reads continuous but is not (the representative/gatekeeping sentences precede the "More
systematically … five" passage on p. 92), and the p. 99 sentence breaks across the page, so cite it as
pp. 99–100. Carry the double scope condition in the authors' own words: exhaustive over *two-step*
paths, given a partition into non-overlapping subgroups, with all longer paths excluded by fiat at p.
96. SUPPORTS; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Gould and Fernandez, "Structures of Mediation," *Sociological Methodology* 19 (1989):
89–126 ; DOI 10.2307/270949 ; https://www.jstor.org/stable/270949

**Evidence:** p. 92: "More systematically, we can identify exactly five structurally distinct types of
brokers (or equivalently, five types of brokerage relations) that follow from a partitioning of actors
into nonoverlapping subgroups (see Figure 1)." p. 96, scope: "we deal only with forms of brokerage that
involve a single intermediary… The brokerage measures we propose and formalize in the following section
therefore exclude from consideration all paths of length greater than two." p. 97: "We can now give a
simple definition of an actor j’s total brokerage activity in a network with N actors or nodes. This
measure, which we write as tj, is the number of ordered pairs (i,k) in the network for which the
condition ijk holds." pp. 99–100: "Identification of subgroup memberships for all three parties in a
brokerage relation generates the five structurally distinct forms of two-step brokerage shown in Figure
1." p. 101: "This classification of the forms of brokerage relations is an exhaustive listing of the
possible types of two-step paths on which any actor may lie, and it is thus an exclusive and exhaustive
partition of any actor j’s total raw brokerage score t," (the OCR "t," is the printed subscripted t_j).

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "All five Gould–Fernandez types are brokerage over an identified
ordered pair: each is a two-step path i-j-k, and the raw score tj is 'the number of ordered pairs (i,k)
in the network for which the condition ijk holds' (p. 97). Given a partition of actors into
nonoverlapping subgroups, the authors call the five-type list 'an exhaustive listing of the possible
types of two-step paths on which any actor may lie' (p. 101) — so within their scope conditions there
is no sixth type."

The founding formalization of the brokerage typology contains no Simmel. All three lenses re-downloaded

The epithet "the founding formalization" is the chapter's, not the source's. SUPPORTS; bears on A-S7.

## Finding 6 — Burt's structural hole is a non-actor object — buffer, insulator, setting — but it is not the absence of a relation, and it is not a party (high on the quotations, refuted on the closing gloss; 2-1) [C12]

Burt does have a theoretical object that is not an actor. He calls holes buffers, likens them to an
insulator in an electric circuit, and casts them as "the setting for tertius strategies." Both
quotations are verbatim and both locators check. Where the lenses split is on the sentence the claim
appends: "the hole has no relation TO the parties, it is the absence of their relation." Lens 3 refused
it and is right on the text. The sentence immediately before the quoted one says a structural hole
"does not mean that people in the groups are unaware of one another" — Burt's hole is non-attention
between focused groups, a nonredundancy relation, not a null tie. And at manuscript pp. 232–233 of the
same chapter he distinguishes active holes, whose "interests attached to the hole resist bridges," from
passive ones, which sits badly with "no relation to the parties." Lens 1 made the same point in milder
form. What survives, and it is the part the chapter needs: in these passages the hole is the opportunity
structure a personal broker exploits, and Burt never gives it standing as a party. COMPLICATES; bears on
A-S7.

**Sources:** Ronald S. Burt, "The Social Capital of Structural Holes," ch. 7 in Mauro F. Guillén, Randall
Collins, Paula England and Marshall Meyer (eds.), *The New Economic Sociology: Developments in an
Emerging Field* (New York: Russell Sage Foundation, 2002), author's manuscript p. 208 (no DOI;
published page range unverified — see Do NOT use) ; https://ronaldsburt.com/research/files/SCSH.pdf ;
and Ronald S. Burt, "The Network Structure of Social Capital," *Research in Organizational Behavior* 22
(2000): 345–423 ; DOI 10.1016/S0191-3085(00)22009-1 ; preprint p. 10,
https://ronaldsburt.com/research/files/NSSC.pdf

**Evidence:** Burt 2002 ch. 7, manuscript p. 208: "The structural hole between two groups does not mean
that people in the groups are unaware of one another. It only means that the people are focused on
their own activities such that they do not attend to the activities of people in the other group. Holes
are buffers, like an insulator in an electric circuit. People on either side of a structural hole
circulate in different flows of information." The next sentence, which lens 1 says to quote rather than
drop: "Structural holes are an opportunity to broker the flow of information between people, and
control the projects that bring together people from opposite sides of the hole." Burt 2000, preprint
p. 10: "Structural holes are the setting for tertius strategies, and information is the substance."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Burt does have a non-actor theoretical object — the structural hole,
which he calls a buffer, likens to 'an insulator in an electric circuit' (2002 ch. 7, p. 208), and casts
as 'the setting for tertius strategies' (2000). In these passages it is the opportunity structure a
personal broker exploits, not a party to a relation. But it is not the absence of a relation either:
Burt is explicit that a hole 'does not mean that people in the groups are unaware of one another,' and
he distinguishes active holes, whose 'attached interests' resist bridges, from passive ones (2002 ch. 7,
pp. 232–233)."

*Correction applied, from lens 2:* cite the Russell Sage volume by its full subtitle. Burt's own preprint
header names the volume by the working title *New Directions in Economic Sociology*, and *The New
Economic Sociology* is also the short title of Frank Dobbin's 2004 Princeton reader, which reprints Burt
at pp. 325–348.

## Finding 7 — Nagel, Roth and Kleve do claim an impersonal third whose appearance or disappearance reconfigures a dyad — but it arrives through Bateson, it is drawn as a node, and it is not brokerage literature (high on the text, refuted on "non-node"; 2-1) [C13]

This is the strongest falsifier the run produced, and it falsifies less than it first appears. All three
lenses confirmed the quotations verbatim and confirmed the substance: an object, a procedure, a family
constitution, an amulet, a scapegoat, heavy rain, an economic crisis can each be a third element, and
its presence or absence switches a dyad between symmetrical and complementary modes. All three also
confirmed the genealogy: Bateson's cybernetics and family-systems theory, not Simmel — the article's 34
deposited references contain no Simmel, no tertius, no Burt, no Obstfeld. Lens 3 refuted the "non-node"
half on the source's own apparatus, and the refutation holds: Figures 2 to 4 place the third element as
a labelled vertex C with edges to A and B, the mechanism is relational triangulation, and the leading
instances are mediators, consultants and scapegoats. Lens 2 reached the same conclusion without calling
it a refutation. So: impersonal, yes; outside the pair's interaction, no. Two corrections of fact travel
with this finding — "[places]" is a conjecture over a genuine lacuna in the indexed text and must not be
printed, and the issue is dated 2024 (online 29 September 2023). BOTH; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Lina Nagel, Steffen Roth and Heiko Kleve, "Theory of the third: A cybernetic approach to
escalative conflict dynamics in business families," *Systems Research and Behavioral Science* 41, no. 3
(2024): 395–403, published online 29 September 2023 ; DOI 10.1002/sres.2977 ; open access, CC BY-NC-ND,
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1002/sres.2977 (read via indexed full text; locators
are section headings, not pages)

**Evidence:** Section "CORE CONCEPTS OF BATESONIAN CYBERNETICS: SCHISMOGENESIS AND THE OUTSIDE ELEMENT,"
quoting Bateson 1972, p. 81: "It is certain that either type of schismogenesis between two groups can be
checked by factors which unite the two groups either in loyalty or opposition to some outside element.
Such an outside element may be either a symbolic individual, an enemy people or some quite impersonal
circumstance[—]the lion will lie down with the lamb if only it rain hard enough." Same section, the
authors' own extension: "In so extending the concept of outside element to a more comprehensive concept
of the third …" Section "THE ROLE OF THIRD ELEMENTS IN ESCALATIVE CONFLICTS": "Thus, the identification
or development of procedures, a common focus, shared values, family protocols, or family constitutions
all can take on the function of a deescalating and conflictsolving third element." Conclusion: "What we
already know is that such third elements include family members, other trusted persons, specific
procedures for decisionmaking, emotionally charged physical items, or a scapegoat."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Nagel, Roth and Kleve generalise Bateson's 'outside element' into a
theory of third elements that can be impersonal — an object, a procedure, a family constitution, heavy
rain — and whose appearance or disappearance reconfigures the relation between two parties. It arrives
through Bateson's cybernetics and family-systems theory rather than through Simmel, and it is not
brokerage literature. It is not, however, a non-node third: the paper's Figures 2 to 4 place the third
element as a labelled vertex C with edges to A and B, and its leading examples are mediators,
consultants and scapegoats. (Cite as 41, no. 3 (2024): 395–403, published online 29 September 2023.)"

*Correction applied, from lenses 1 and 2:* delete the bracketed "[places]". The indexed text has a
genuine lacuna there ("the impact objects, processes, or may have"), and the missing word is not
recoverable without the Wiley PDF.

## Finding 8 — Khurana reports that the field took two of Simmel's three categories and left the third underdeveloped — "less attention," not none (high on the text, refuted on the quantifier; 2-1) [C14]

Khurana is the one author in this set who counts Simmel's categories and says what happened to each.
Two — tertius gaudens and divide et impera — are, in his words, "building blocks for Burt's theory of
structural holes" that "have received well-deserved attention." The third, which he calls the mediator
or intermediary, "has received less attention in the literature and is theoretically underdeveloped."
All three lenses verified every clause, including the internal locator "(Simmel, 1902: 146)" for
"concord of two." Lens 3 refused the claim's paraphrase and is right: Khurana does not say the field
took *only two*; he says the third got less attention, and his own article is an instance of the field
taking it up. Lens 3 also caught an imported term — "the non-partisan mediator" is Wolff's phrase, not
Khurana's, who writes "mediator or 'intermediary.'" One thing the chapter should absorb from lens 2:
Khurana's own mediator is still a party inside the triad brokering between two mutually aware actors, so
he corroborates the pair reading rather than breaking it. COMPLICATES; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Rakesh Khurana, "Market Triads: A Theoretical and Empirical Analysis of Market
Intermediation," *Journal for the Theory of Social Behaviour* 32, no. 2 (2002): 239–262 ; DOI
10.1111/1468-5914.00185 ; read via indexed full text, section "The Third Party in Social Exchange"
(section-addressed, not paginated)

**Evidence:** "A close reading of Simmel's analysis reveals three categories of three[-]party
interactions. The first category is the tertius gaudens … Closely related to the tertius gaudens is
Simmel's second category, divide et impera or ‘divide and conquer.’ … Both of these categories are
building blocks for Burt's theory of structural holes (Burt, 1992; Krackhardt, 1999) and have received
well[-]deserved attention in theories of market structure and market competition. … Simmel's third
category of triadic exchanges, however, has received less attention in the literature and is
theoretically underdeveloped. Simmel calls this category mediator or ‘intermediary.’"

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Khurana refines rather than confirms the formulation: he reads
Simmel as offering three categories of three-party interaction and reports that the first two — tertius
gaudens and divide et impera — are 'building blocks for Burt's theory of structural holes' and 'have
received well-deserved attention', while the third, which he calls the mediator or intermediary, 'has
received less attention in the literature and is theoretically underdeveloped' — a third Simmel kept,
left underdeveloped until Khurana takes it up himself."

## Finding 9 — Ajunwa's tertius bifrons is a broker between a named pair inside the transaction, working for one party while appearing to work for both (high, 3-0) [C15]

Ajunwa coins a category for automated hiring platforms and defines it inside a triad she names herself.
All three lenses read the Cardozo PDF and confirmed the quotation verbatim, including the page break:
the sentence runs to the foot of p. 1725, the running head "1726 CARDOZO LAW REVIEW [Vol. 41:1671]"
intervenes, and the final clause opens p. 1726. Two sentences earlier she writes that "the automated
hiring process comprises a triad, with the automated hiring platform as the broker negotiating between
the applicant and the employer" — a named pair, both parties inside the transaction. Two qualifications
the chapter should carry rather than suppress, both from lens 3: Ajunwa presents the bifrons as a *new*
category "beyond the two categories of brokerage," so "a variant of the thirds Simmel kept" is the
chapter's classification and not hers; and her platform brokers between an employer and a pool of
applicants, so the known pair is idealised in her own triad rather than literal. SUPPORTS; bears on
A-S7.

**Sources:** Ifeoma Ajunwa, "The Paradox of Automation as Anti-Bias Intervention," *Cardozo Law Review*
41, no. 5 (2020): 1671–1742, at pp. 1724–1726 ; no DOI ;
http://cardozolawreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/1.-Ajunwa.41.5.6.FINAL-3.pdf

**Evidence:** pp. 1725–1726: "Furthermore, I propose that automated hiring platforms, which can be
customized at the request of the employer (but not that of the applicant) belong to a new category of
brokers that I term 'the tertius bifrons' (that is, 'the two-faced third'). With the introduction of
this new term, I am arguing that automated hiring platforms represent a type of broker which works both
in its own interest (to maintain its coordinative role) and in the interest of one of the parties to the
triad (the employer), while maintaining the appearance of working for both parties (employer and job
applicant)."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Ajunwa's tertius bifrons is defined inside a triad she names herself
— 'the automated hiring platform as the broker negotiating between the applicant and the employer' — and
works for one party while appearing to work for both (Cardozo L. Rev. 41: 1725–26). She offers it as a
new fourth category beyond iungens and gaudens; on the chapter's own taxonomy it still falls among the
thirds Simmel kept, since it is a partisan mediator between parties to a transaction, not a third at
Distanz."

## Finding 10 — Ajunwa attributes the tertius iungens / gaudens pair to Simmel and sources the definitions to Obstfeld at the very next footnote (high, 3-0) [C16]

The misattribution is real, specific and printable. In the body sentence Ajunwa credits Simmel with
both orientations; note 284 reads, in full, "SIMMEL, supra note 33"; note 285 sends the reader to
Obstfeld 2005 at p. 102 for the iungens definition. All three lenses read the page and confirmed every
string. Two corrections come out of the verification, both of which make the finding sharper rather
than softer. First, the Simmel citation is not locator-free: through supra note 33 it carries the range
"THE SOCIOLOGY OF GEORG SIMMEL 145-69 (Kurt H. Wolff ed., trans., 1950)," which makes Ajunwa the one
author in this set who pinpoints the Wolff triad chapter. Second, from lens 3, the misattribution is
wrong by half rather than whole: *tertius gaudens* is Simmel's own term — it heads his section and
recurs sixteen times in the 1908 German — and only *tertius iungens* is Obstfeld's 2005 coinage. Lens
2 flags that the sub-claim about Wolff's translation is convergence-supported, not directly verified,
since no lens opened Wolff. SUPPORTS; bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Ajunwa, "The Paradox of Automation as Anti-Bias Intervention," *Cardozo Law Review* 41, no.
5 (2020), p. 1724 and nn. 284–285 ; no DOI ;
http://cardozolawreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/1.-Ajunwa.41.5.6.FINAL-3.pdf ; citing David
Obstfeld, "Social Networks, the Tertius Iungens Orientation, and Involvement in Innovation,"
*Administrative Science Quarterly* 50, no. 1 (2005): 100–130 ; DOI 10.2189/asqu.2005.50.1.100

**Evidence:** p. 1724: "According to the sociologist Georg Simmel, brokers, as part of a triad, perform
the function of brokering information between two separate groups, acting as either tertius iungens or
tertius gaudens. [n.] 284" — n. 284, in full: "SIMMEL, supra note 33." — n. 285: "David Obstfeld,

## Finding 23 — Feld's focus theory occupied the non-node coordinator cell in the *American Journal of Sociology* in 1981 (high on Feld's text, refuted on three appended glosses; 2-1 on each of three claims) [C35, C36, C37]

Three claims, three separate 2-1 votes, one finding. Feld defines a focus as an entity — social,
psychological, legal or physical — around which joint activities are organised, and holds that people
whose activities share a focus become interpersonally tied and form a cluster. He allows the focus to be
a person or an impersonal thing, and to work either by actively bringing people together or by
passively constraining them to interact. And he calls foci *extra-network* by definition, which is his
own word, used repeatedly across pp. 1015–1016. Every quotation checked out at every locator; the
lenses converged on the text and split on what the chapter appended to it. Three glosses failed, and
all three should come out of the chapter's prose. Feld nowhere says the tied individuals "share no
prior relation" — the words "stranger," "previously unrelated" and "otherwise unrelated" do not appear
in the article. "Brokerage-by-a-node cannot represent" this is a claim about formalism, not about Feld,
and it is false as stated, since foci are routinely modelled as the second node set of a two-mode
structure — which is exactly what Feld's own method requires when he asks for data on "each
individual's relations to extra-network foci." And "directly contradicts the premise about the
*third*" over-reaches, because Feld never calls a focus a third, and his foci are settings that generate
ties among many rather than parties to a relation between two others. What survives is decisive enough
without them: a setting-that-organises-ties, explicitly outside the tie graph among persons, was
published in AJS in 1981 with no brokerage vocabulary anywhere in it — "broker" 0, "tertius" 0.
REFUTES (of the chapter's "unoccupied cell" framing, in its broad form); bears on A-S7.

**Sources:** Scott L. Feld, "The Focused Organization of Social Ties," *American Journal of Sociology* 86,
no. 5 (March 1981): 1015–1035 ; DOI 10.1086/227352 (read from the JSTOR scan; the mirror at
https://smg.media.mit.edu/library/Feld.SocialTies.pdf has no text layer and was read by OCR and by eye)

**Evidence:** p. 1016: "A focus is defined as a social, psychological, legal, or physical entity around

## How this lands in the chapter

The negative holds and it is the chapter's asset: no one in the brokerage tradition claims Simmel's
deferred third, no one cites the page, and the two literatures that both study brokers-as-thirds do not
cite each other in either direction. Gesigora's chapter is the exhibit to lead with, because it is a
single bibliography containing both vocabularies and no contact between them, and because it is
checkable by anyone with a Crossref key. Behind it stand the 720 deposited references with zero German
third-theory citations, and Fischer's 33 with zero brokerage citations.

Three things must change in the prose. First, drop "unoccupied cell." Feld put a legal or physical
entity that organises ties without being in the tie graph into the *American Journal of Sociology* in
1981, Small and Adler canonised the mechanism in 2019, and the chapter's own C42 exhibit shows the
formalism operationalised in *Organization Science*. What the field never did was call any of it a
third. The chapter's opening is terminological, and it is stronger stated that way: two literatures, one
about foci and one about tertii, that describe overlapping objects and share no vocabulary.

Second, cite rather than claim. Lindemann said in 2005 that Simmel's third has "no foundational
relevance" for the constitution of social order; Vandenberghe said in 2018 that Simmel "did not
sufficiently consider" the third as medium. Both objections are neighbours of the chapter's, not
identical to it — theirs is a third the parties relate *through*, the chapter's is a power they relate
*to* — and saying so precisely is the move that earns the distinction.

Third, keep the distinction narrow. What separates Simmel's Distanz third from every occupant found here
is standing plus distance: a power outside the parties, to which they stand in a common relation, that
takes no side and joins no named pair. Feld's focus has no standing. Burt's hole is an opportunity, not
a party. Ajunwa's platform, Khurana's intermediary and Tatarynowicz and Keil's suffering third are all
pair-brokers. Nagel et al.'s third element has standing but sits inside the diagram. State the
distinction in those two words and the chapter survives everything this packet threw at it.

One warning for the writing. Two of the most quotable sentences in the packet come from sources nobody
opened — Wolff p. 145 and Obstfeld 2005 — and two more carry locators that three lenses had to fix by
reading page furniture rather than trusting page markers. In this material the folio sometimes heads the
page and sometimes closes it. Check every page number against the running head before it goes into a
draft.
