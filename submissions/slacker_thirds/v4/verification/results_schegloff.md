## Schegloff's own thesis, stated from the retrieved text

The paper's target is the opening of two-party conversation, and its instrument is the "summons-answer" (SA) sequence: a first item (a phone ring, a name called out, "pardon me," a tap on the shoulder) that requires a second item (an answer) before anything else can properly happen. Two properties do the work. Nonterminality: an SA sequence cannot be the last exchange of an encounter — having summoned, the summoner is obligated to talk again, which is why the sequence functions as a preamble to something further. Conditional relevance: the answer's occurrence is tied so tightly to the summons that its absence is not neutral but "officially absent," and official absence licenses strong inferences — that the summoned party is out of earshot, asleep, or, if none of those apply, insolent, sulking, cold-shouldering. A member of society, Schegloff writes, "may not 'naively choose' not to answer a summons" (1968: 1086), because non-answering is never read as a bare fact; it is always read as a sign of something. And once someone does answer, the same structure binds the other way: "the answerer, by virtue of his answer, commits himself to staying with the encounter" (1968: 1083). The empirical spine of the paper is a corpus of tape-recorded calls to and from a police complaint desk, but Schegloff repeatedly generalizes the SA-sequence apparatus itself beyond the telephone — in his own taxonomy of summonses, in a footnoted excerpt from Kafka, and in street- and bus-level illustrations of presence and availability that have nothing to do with a phone line.

## 2. The extension to co-present strangers

**Verdict: paraphrase-faithful.** Schegloff makes this move himself, and does it in three separate places, one of them inside the very passage that produces the "commits himself to staying" quotation.

First, immediately after stating the commitment property and illustrating it with a household example (a mother calling a child in from a play area — itself not a telephone case), Schegloff attaches footnote 7, which reads in full: "The same phenomenon is presented in a rather more exalted setting in the following excerpt from Kafka's *The Trial*" — and then quotes the cathedral scene in which a priest calls out "Joseph K.!" to a man he has never met, and the narration notes that if K. turns to face him, "that would amount to an admission that he had understood it very well, that he was really the person addressed, and that he was ready to obey." This is Schegloff's own chosen illustration of the "commits himself to staying" property, and it is face-to-face, not telephonic, and it is between strangers (the priest is later revealed to K. as the prison chaplain, someone K. has never met).

Second, earlier in the paper (1968: 1080), Schegloff explicitly denies that a summons is telephone-specific and lists three non-mechanical classes of summons, one of which is "courtesy phrases (e.g., 'Pardon me,' when approaching a stranger to get his attention)," alongside "physical devices (e.g., a tap on the shoulder, waves of a hand, raising of a hand by an audience member, etc.)." This is the generalization stated as a taxonomy, not folded into a single illustration.

Third, in the discussion of presence and availability (1968: 1088–1089), Schegloff uses two field observations of strangers in physical co-presence — two people sitting silently on the same bus bench, two people walking toward each other on a college campus — to work out the difference between mere physical presence and interactional availability, the same conceptual pair that underwrites the commitment claim.

The chapter's sentence — "extending the argument himself to strangers in each other's presence" — is accurate to what the source does. It should be read as a fair compression of these three moves, not as a claim that Schegloff states, in one sentence, "this also applies to strangers who are physically co-present." He does not write that sentence. He does, however, generalize the summons concept to face-to-face strangers, and he illustrates the specific commitment property with a face-to-face, cross-stranger case, in the same breath as the quotation the chapter uses. That is the chapter's own move riding on the source's real work, not the chapter inventing a move the source never makes — which is the distinction that matters, given this project's prior failure of putting words in a source's mouth.

## 3. The empirical base

**Verdict: verbatim-confirmed.** Schegloff's own footnote (attached to the paper's second paragraph): "This discussion is a shortened and modified version of chapters two and three of the author's Ph.D. dissertation (Schegloff 1967). It is based on the analysis of tape-recorded phone calls to and from the complaint desk of a police department in a middle-sized Midwestern city." (1968: 1094.) The chapter's description — "recordings of calls to a police complaint desk in a middle-sized Midwestern city" — matches this exactly, including the "middle-sized Midwestern city" phrase.

Corpus size is not stated as a number anywhere in the retrieved text. Schegloff numbers individual calls (e.g., "#364," "#66," "#398," "#86") but never gives a total call count or total recording time for the corpus. One example used elsewhere in the paper (Note 6, discussing a second SA sequence) is flagged by Schegloff himself as drawn from "a collection of calls to and from a public agency other than the one from which the bulk of the data are drawn" — so the corpus is not perfectly homogeneous, though the bulk of it is the police complaint desk as described. If the chapter asserts a specific number of calls or hours anywhere, that number is not supported by anything in the retrieved text and should be checked against wherever the chapter got it; `claim_map.md`'s row for V-019 does not itself assert a number, so nothing here is contradicted, only unconfirmed as to size.
