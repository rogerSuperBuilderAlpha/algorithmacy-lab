# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

The film now feels like a feed. In 2011 Richard Linklater told Marc Savlov he saw the film as "either channel surfing or … going in and out of different movies," a "primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."[^1] The film scholar Maria Teresa Soldani never mentions Instagram. She makes the resemblance a matter of form. *Slacker*, she writes, is a twenty-four-hour urban narration whose camera drifts from character to character and from place to place without splitting those two jobs.[^2] A face talks for a stretch and then it is gone. Another face arrives. The inside of that experience is doomscrolling.

The same drift is how a platform works. A platform holds strangers available to one another and arranges none of their meetings. It is a self-less third, and still an agent: it charges, it lists, it refuses, it withholds a reason. Whether that agent is a person or an algorithm I leave open. Platforms have a history. Georg Simmel and Josiah Royce ran into the problem in the first years of the twentieth century. Charles Reich made the legal form speakable in 1964. Linklater films a day in which those problems remain on the sidewalk – a city that unifies without introducing, a shared day nobody claims as his own, access still spoken aloud to a person who can answer. I read the film as an observation and a reaction to how those older arrangements set us up for the triadic world of the platform.

In 2020 David Stark and Ivana Pais described platform owners who co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^3] *Slacker* already has contribution without a contract on the street, and a third that still has a face. The day lets us watch, and practice, how to argue with that third. I call fluency with determinations that arrive without reasons, and without anyone to answer them, algorithmacy.[^4] The film is where that competence can still be seen working, and that is its use for making the most of the world the platforms inherited.

David Bordwell has warned against treating multi-protagonist films as symptoms of the internet. Network narratives solve storytelling problems and predate the technologies invoked to explain them.[^5] *Slacker* is one of those older constructions. The arrangement it films is older still. Platforms inherited a third that was already on the sidewalk.

I come to this film from organisation theory. The chapter reads the sidewalk as an organisation.

## Philosophical Origins in Simmel's Postponement and Royce's Canoe

Georg Simmel named a third that unifies people who never meet, then postponed the analysis. Josiah Royce defined community so that membership waits for each person's yes, a test no day of unclaimed strangers can meet. The film opens on a taxi and a woman struck in the street, two parties who share a city and no relation.

In Simmel's triad chapter the non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties. The *tertius gaudens* – the laughing third – profits by standing between them. *Divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets them against one another in order to rule both.[^7] Each already knows the pair. On the opening page of that same chapter he writes of a third that unifies without standing between anyone, then sets the thought aside. Kurt Wolff translates the observation as "it is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them." He offers two examples: an alliance against a common enemy, and the invisible church, which joins all believers through their equal relation to the one God. Then comes the postponement: "The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context." The German is *Diese gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung eines dritten Elementes ist indes in späterem Zusammenhang zu behandeln*.[^8]

He sets it aside because of distance. The third here stands at such a *Distanz* from the other two that no properly sociological interaction encompasses all three alike. What remains are *Zweierkonfigurationen* – pairs – either the relation among those who join, or the relation between them as a unit and the outside centre of interest. God and two believers do not constitute one three-way field. Two allies and a far enemy do not either. The chapter requires three elements close enough to form a group, so the distant case falls outside its concern and he sets it aside. I will call what he set aside the deferred third: a third that unifies by standing so far off that it never comes between an identified pair. Austin holds that shape for ninety minutes. The man in from the bus station and the people standing around a woman struck in the street occupy one city and no relation whatever to one another.

The phrase occurs exactly once in the 1908 *Soziologie*, at the postponement. The theme, however, returns some two hundred pages later, in the chapter on conflict. Here the very *Distanz* that had been his ground for deferring becomes a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them turns out to be an especially favourable constellation for the bond. Both of the original examples come back on that page – the league formed against a common enemy, and the God whose infinite remoteness is exactly what lets him gather the most heterogeneous peoples into one church. Simmel indexed the passage himself, under *Distanz des vereinigenden Momentes*, and the alliance half recurs a dozen pages earlier still.[^9] The promise returns. What never returns is the third *as a third*. The theme comes back in the dyadic register the postponement had itself predicted – unification described from the side of the parties who are unified, with the distant unifying element analysed as a condition of their bond rather than as an element of a triad. The cut from the taxi to the hit-and-run inherits that omission: two parties and a city, never three terms in one field.

Gesa Lindemann treats the absent third as the precondition of any durable dyadic order, not an addition to it. For Jan Fuhse the problem is observational: network research can see a concrete third and cannot see an abstract one. Christoph Neuberger has taken *gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung* to journalism and to platforms.[^10] Albrecht Albrecht Koschorke, with Michel Serres behind him, reverses the chapter's order. The third comes first, and the dyad is what a relation looks like once a third has made it possible.[^11] Simmel omitted the ground. The taxi is already moving before anyone in *Slacker* has met anyone.

Network-brokerage research took the pair-brokering thirds from the triad chapter. It has no instrument for the deferred one. Ronald Burt's structural-hole broker and David Obstfeld's *tertius iungens* each know which two parties they stand between. The integrative review that defines the field treats broker and brokered as network *actors*, and it excludes non-human networks and networks outside a work or professional context.[^12] Austin's sidewalk and Linklater's cut fall outside those instruments before any measurement begins. The film has the postponed third. Neither the taxi nor the woman struck in the street is a node in anybody's network.

Royce's problem is how strangers become members. In *The Problem of Christianity* he asks what makes a community out of people who need not have met. He answers with a definition. A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation by the same relation to shared future events.[^13] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. The illustration is chosen for its coldness. They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^14] The line is quoted often as a licence for membership without meeting. Royce lists three conditions, and the scholarship reads them as cumulative: the self's power to extend itself ideally in time; a plurality of distinct selves capable of social communication and in general engaged in it; and at least one identical event included in the ideally extended lives of all.[^15] Without the second condition there is no community at all, and Royce frames membership as holding among contemporaries. Acceptance is written into the definition itself. The member must accept the past event as part of his own life, so there is no stage at which the conditions are met and the taking-as-mine is still outstanding. Royce's membership waits for the member's yes. He permits aloofness, enmity, and the absence of any meeting. What he does not permit is a community of people who have not each, individually, claimed the same past. A day in a city cannot meet that test. When a stranger walks into a café on the Drag and asks whether anyone has seen Gary, the room he is asking fails every part of it.

## 'You seen Gary around?' – The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

The film opens twice on a street within four minutes, and both times the deferred third is already there. Linklater himself, credited only as the man who should have stayed at bus station, rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a silent driver. His subject is a dream in which nothing happened, and then a theory that every unchosen path continues somewhere as its own reality. At the station he had almost skipped the cab, and in that other life a woman picks him up and he moves in with her. The driver never answers and he never seems to notice. He gets out on "Man, shit. I should've stayed at bus station."[^6]

Then the film is on a different street, where a woman lies struck and a jogger jogs in place and barks at no one to touch her. Somebody goes for help. A man who has pulled over asks whether he is needed, hears that he is not, and presses his card on the jogger anyway – "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it" – before driving off ahead of the police. The camera then leaves the body entirely and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother.

No character bridges those beats, and nothing in the story connects them. Austin contains them both, and the cut decides which of them we watch. In the taxi the man had said there is always someone getting run over, and the next thing we see is a son who has run over his mother, an echo that no one on screen is in a position to notice. The echo is the arrangement at work: a third that holds strangers available to one another and arranges not one of their meetings.

Down at the Blue Bayou on South Congress, meanwhile, a man credited as S-T-E-V-E With A Van has spent the afternoon telling a friend about the night ahead – his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he had made the roommate write – and at the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them. The doorman charges him three dollars. He spells his way onto a list he believes he owns, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and is refused on it.

> "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you."
>
> "Fuck. I'm not on the list."
>
> "Figures."

Steve is refused without a reason, and the refusal has a shape that Reich had described twenty-six years earlier. Wealth of the modern kind, he argued, is increasingly held not as property but as largess – a grant – whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^16] The Blue Bayou's door does that work in miniature. It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum and owes nobody an explanation. Reich's diagnosis covers all four. I lean on the diagnosis rather than on his remedy deliberately. Reich also demanded, in his closing pages, that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated." The reception has never treated that sentence as the centre. William Van Alstyne, an early critic of the article, twice characterises those closing pages as tentative suggestion and mild conjecture, well short of the article's central work.[^17] Reich gave the law a description of the dependence. The demand was a hope appended to that description.

The film adds the face to Reich's diagnosis. Outside the same door, a man offers a hand stamp – "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in" – and copies it wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that does not stop anyone, and the two of them walk in free. They can copy the stamp because a stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers. The guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it. Later at closing someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it – "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Even that peremptory sentence is addressed to people who could in principle argue back. The platforms that inherit this arrangement keep the charge, the list, the refusal and the withheld reason. They drop both the person who can state the rule and the stamp that strangers could copy among themselves.

Gary is missing, and nobody in the room can answer for him. In a café later identified as Captain Quackenbush's on the Drag, a passerby breaks in to ask after a man who is not there.

> "You seen Gary around?"

Nobody has seen him for months, and the asker leaves without an answer. Outside, a man who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited. The asker heads off toward a friend's place, and the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed before stopping a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" and then waving him along – "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears. The sidewalk had held both men within reach of one another and the cut selected their meeting, and no one introduced them.

They are contemporaries, and they are in communication, so Royce's second condition holds on the street in a way it does not hold for most of the people the film shows us. The third condition fails, and the acceptance built into it. Neither man takes Gary's absence into his own life as the same past event. The asker is looking for a friend. The talker converts the absence into evidence for a conspiracy. The day they share remains a day neither of them claims. Sharing a city is not sharing a past. On Royce's own terms these people are not members of anything, and Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

Through the rest of the day that holding keeps working, while the mechanism of the joins changes hands. Early on the cut does the joining, as it did between the taxi and the body. A missing-friend question pulls a conspiracy into the frame. A friend breaking off a voting rant to say that Paul has vanished follows it with "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle of the day the people on screen have taken the joins over, and they do it with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud – a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at the door, a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud: shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Austin made those meetings possible and the participants closed them themselves. By dawn the cut has resumed, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a wordless Super-8 thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell. When the cut is doing the joining there is nothing handed over and nobody addressed.

The same day shows labour without a contract. Kids hawk Cokes. A man sells T-shirts. Outside Les Amis a hitchhiker fresh from his stepfather's funeral tells a camera crew that he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and then refuses to say what the true call is. The city holds him within reach and hires him for nothing, and he withholds his reason exactly as the door withheld Steve's. Stark and Pais call the later version co-optation without delegated authority. On the sidewalk in 1990 there is always somebody standing there to be asked.[^3] Contribution without employment, with a person still available to answer for it, is still legible.

The same powers, once they drop the face, are written down. Amazon's Prime Video licensing documentation states that removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection sits at the company's sole discretion, and that it "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^18] Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus, surveying 810 California drivers, found two-thirds reporting deactivation and thirty per cent of those given no reason at all.[^19] Drivers contribute without being hired, as the people at that party did, and the counterparty that removes them has removed anyone who could be argued with. The four powers that the Blue Bayou's doorman exercised in person – price, evaluate, remove, withhold the reason – now attach by rule.[^20] The Video Backpacker gets at the loss from the other side. A video image is more powerful than an actual event, he explains, because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind," can't pause it, can't fix the hue. A driver cannot replay an allocation once the queue has moved on. In the three years after the film's release the same form appeared in finance, where Reuters Dealing 2000-2 and EBS matched professional traders without themselves buying or selling anything.[^21] Steve's list failed in public and the group renegotiated on the sidewalk. An app assigns a queue position whether or not the driver assents to it, and the inclusion waits for nobody's yes.

Because Royce makes acceptance constitutive, the app's assignment is not membership. Calling it one would spend his term twice and in opposite senses. Salomé Viljoen describes the mechanism: a horizontal, population-based data relation includes a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he has in common with others rather than anything he has claimed.[^22] Enrollment of that kind is the platform's answer to Royce's question, and the answer is that it has stopped asking. Rideshare Drivers United exists because drivers organised to compel an answer from a third that will not give one.

## Reach, Selection, and the Refusal to Resolve

The film keeps a strict division of labour between reach and selection, its two thirds.

In 1981 Scott Feld asked how people come to know one another without a matchmaker. His answer was the focus: a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organised – a workplace, a voluntary organisation, a hangout, a family – such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^23] Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organised. But the people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster. The shared activity that produces Feld's ties is missing, and what remains is the bare holding I have been calling reach. Sarah Barns describes digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange with "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom, which is nearer to selection, and to what the camera does when it chooses the next face.[^23] The city supplies the one and the cut supplies the other. *Slacker* never lets the city do the cut's job, or the cut do the city's.

Michael Warner's account of a public constitutes strangers as a collectivity precisely through their common relation to something outside them, with no interaction among them required and membership arriving by mere attention.[^24] The deferred third adds the selection half. Warner's public is addressed, and addressed publics do not have someone choosing which two of their members are put in front of each other next. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga already split match quality from match probability in their account of what cities do for the people in them, a precedent for treating reach and matching as separable.[^25] They split the same two functions in economic form. The film shows those functions performed by visibly different agents, a city and a camera.

Linklater refuses to resolve. He does away with the closure a hundred-character film would demand. To produce Gary, or to let the hitchhiker name the true call, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk, would be to supply from outside precisely the introductions the arrangement does not make. The film's recurring question – asked of Gary, and of Paul, and implicitly of everyone who leaves the frame and does not return – is a question the city is not built to answer. Every time an answer fails to arrive the mechanism becomes a little more legible. A door still has to say the rule out loud. A camera still has to choose the next face without telling anyone why.

## Conclusion

The opening cut, the club door, and the day walked underneath them show one third holding strangers within reach and arranging none of their meetings. The taxi and the struck woman share a city and a cut and nothing else. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away and give no reason for it. Gary is asked after and never produced, and the hitchhiker will not say what the true call is. Linklater leaves those questions hanging so that the form of the arrangement stays in view.

In 1908 Simmel postponed the third that unifies from a distance. When the theme returned two hundred pages later it returned as a fact about pairs, and the third he had promised to treat was never restored to the analysis. Royce made acceptance part of the definition of community, so membership waits for each member's yes. Reich described the legal form of a dependence that the Blue Bayou's doorman was exercising in person. Linklater films the interval in which all three descriptions are true of one afternoon, and in which the people subject to them can still argue with somebody.

The film keeps a third you can argue with. The platform removes it. The competence that the day in Austin demands of its participants – knowing how to argue with a door, how to copy a stamp, how to say a join out loud – is the way to make the most of a third that has dropped its face. I called that competence algorithmacy at the outset.[^4] Simmel describes the structure, Royce the membership test, Reich the grant, Stark and Pais the co-optive enrollment. None of them has a word for the competence the day requires. The people on screen still have it. They still know how to ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
