**Manuscript:** `chapter/chapter_v9.md` (Roger Hunt; Notes 1–25; Books / Chapters and articles / Reports / Film).
**Venue:** Bloomsbury collection *Slacker: Answering the True Call*, eds. Bizarro and Remark. Chicago endnotes, US English, Merriam-Webster 11th. Final draft 2026-11-01.
**Standpoint:** Citations seat only. Mechanical integrity of the note–bibliography circuit, Chicago house form (including 3-em dashes and shared keys), and whether the load-bearing quotations actually sit where the notes say they sit. Not the argument, not the film, not the prose.

## Step 0 — Register and bar

The chapter is a first-person film-studies collection essay. That register is not my problem. Quoted dialogue, quoted scholarship, first person, and contractions are off-limits as prose defects. I will not rewrite the deferred-third thesis.

What I judge hard:

- Every body key has a definition; every definition is used; no silent orphans.
- Notes ↔ bibliography, both directions.
- Chicago 3-em dashes mean *same author as the immediately previous entry*. A leading dash after a byline, after a section head, or after a different author is a false attribution.
- Shared note keys: reuse of a number versus one note carrying two works.
- Italics on *Slacker* inside reference titles; alphabetization; US English; Chicago notes (not author-date); sequential numbering.
- Load-bearing quotations checked against sources opened this session. Verified or unverified. I do not invent a page I have not seen, and I do not “correct” a locator I have not opened.

The house bar that still binds this seat is verified citations. A Bloomsbury copyeditor who also knows CMOS will catch a 3-em dash that assigns Reich to Warner. That is the embarrassment this seat exists to find.

## Part 1 — Findings, ranked by damage

### 1. The first Books entry and the first Articles entry are false attributions

**Claim.** The bibliography opens both of its main author lists with a 3-em dash. CMOS treats `———.` as “same author as the previous entry.” There is no previous Books author. The previous Articles author is Warner.

**Quote.**

> ### Books
>
> ———. *Conflict and The Web of Group-Affiliations*. Translated by Kurt H. Wolff and Reinhard Bendix. Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1955.

> ### Chapters and articles
>
> ———. "The New Property." *The Yale Law Journal* 73, no. 5 (1964): 733–787.

**Why it damages this lens.** This is the one defect that would actually print as a howler. A typesetter who follows the dashes will catalogue *Conflict* as an anonymous or authorless first book, or — worse, if the dash is read across the chapter byline — as Hunt. The Articles dash is worse: the last Books entry is Warner, Michael, *Publics and Counterpublics*. The next line, under a new heading that does not reset the author, is “The New Property.” That is Reich assigned to Warner. Reich’s 1990 essay then appears later under his own name, so the bibliography simultaneously hides the 1964 article and double-lists Reich without connecting the two works.

The LSEG pair later in Reports is the control case that shows the author knows the rule:

> LSEG. *LSEG FX SEF Rulebook*. Version 4.1, effective 11 August 2026.
>
> ———. *LSEG FX UK MTF Rule Book*. Version 1.2, effective 1 December 2025.

That dash is legal. The two section-opening dashes are not.

**Fix.** Restore the author names. Put Simmel’s *Conflict* with Simmel (after *The Sociology of Georg Simmel*, or before it if the list is alphabetical by title). Put “The New Property” under Reich, Charles A., with “The Liberty Impact of the New Property” as the other Reich article and a dash only between those two. Do not dash across a section heading.

### 3. Shared keys: 3 and 4 reused; 23 used twice for two authors; 6 appears after 7–15

**Claim.** Markdown `[^n]` reuse is not Chicago note practice. Combining two works in one note is.

**Quote.** `[^3]` is planted after Stark and Pais in the opening and again after the hitchhiker / co-optation paragraph. `[^4]` is planted after the first algorithmacy sentence and again in the conclusion. `[^23]` is planted after the Feld focus definition and again after the Barns match-making sentence. `[^6]` (the film / subtitle-track note) first appears in the taxi paragraph, after the body has already called 7 through 15.

**Why it damages this lens.** Chicago numbers notes consecutively in order of first citation. A subsequent citation of the same work gets a *new* number and a shortened form, or “see n. 3 above.” Reusing superscript 3 produces two identical note markers pointing at one block. That is convenient in Markdown. It is not what a Bloomsbury endnote list will look like once the file is typeset. If the compositor preserves the numbers, the chapter prints two “3”s. If the compositor renumbers, the author’s cross-references move.

Note 23 is a different problem. One note that cites Feld *and* Barns is Chicago-legal — notes may carry more than one work. Using that same number twice in one paragraph, once after Feld and once after Barns, is a collision. The first marker tells the reader the note is Feld’s definition. The second marker, still “23,” is Barns. A reader who stops at the first 23 never learns that the note also holds the platform sentence the next sentence is standing on.

Note 4 is the cleanest of the three reuses (same unpublished term, same definition). It is still not Chicago. Give it a second number: “See n. 4 above.”

Note 6 is a skipped-number defect in first-appearance order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, **7**, 8 … 15, **6**. No number is missing from the definition list. The first-use sequence is.

**Fix.** Renumber in order of first citation. Split 23 into two notes, or keep one note and plant it once, after the Barns sentence, so both works sit under one marker. Replace reused 3 and 4 with new numbers and short forms.
