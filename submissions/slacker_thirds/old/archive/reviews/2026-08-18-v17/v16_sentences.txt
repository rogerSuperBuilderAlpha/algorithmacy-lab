
=== SECTION: "You seen Gary around?": The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

-- para 1
[1] The 1991 film *Slacker* (Richard Linklater), aside from being a founding document of American independent cinema, can also be considered a coordination puzzle: it depicts, as its first reviewer wrote, "no plot, no major characters, no suspense: just fleeting glimpses of bohemia in its twilight phase." In this chapter I use *Slacker* as an illustration and as a limit case of the multi-character film, particularly when it comes to the issue of coordination.
[2] I describe the arrangement through which the film holds roughly one hundred characters, most of them unacquainted with most of the others, in one legible day, and I argue that the arrangement divides into two jobs and a third act.
[3] Austin holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody.
[4] The camera selects which stranger the viewer sees next.
[5] And at the film's doors, access arrives as a grant: priced, recorded, refused, and left unexplained by a person who can still be argued with.

-- para 2
[6] The film opens with the arrangement already at work.
[7] A man played by Linklater himself, credited as "Should have stayed at bus station," rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a taxi driver who never once answers him.
[8] His monologue proposes that every road not taken continues somewhere as its own reality, and it wanders through his dreams, where, he says, there is always someone getting run over.
[9] He gets out on the line that gives him his credit.
[10] Moments later a woman lies struck in the street, a jogger barks at bystanders not to touch her, and a motorist who has pulled over presses his business card on a stranger, "Take my card.
[11] Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you.
[12] I mean it," before driving off ahead of the police.
[13] The camera abandons the victim and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother.
[14] The dream has leaked into the street inside five minutes.
[15] The viewer soon learns to anticipate such hand-offs: a character walks out of one scene and into the next, and the film goes with whichever body the camera prefers.

-- para 3
[16] The scholarship on *Slacker* has answered the coordination question in three ways.
[17] The oldest answer is the relay: a "fluid camera and a kind of 'baton-passing' among the characters," in the production history's phrase, each walker handing the film to the next.
[18] The relay describes the surface accurately, and it stops at the surface, because a baton names the hand-off and says nothing about who holds the film between hands or why any particular stranger receives it.
[19] A second answer locates a property that all the scenes share.
[20] The characters "unite by unanimously doing nothing," so the film coheres as a common ethic of refusal.
[21] Alternatively, "conversation, and ideas themselves, become the determining force of the narrative," so the film coheres as one continuous exchange of talk.
[22] These observations hold for the film's tone, which is remarkably consistent.
[23] A property that every scene possesses, however, cannot join any two of them, and the monologues do not in fact travel: each dies with its speaker, unanswered by the scene that follows.

-- para 4
[24] A third answer attributes the continuity to drift.
[25] The camera "drifts along the street space, from character to character and from place to place," revealing a generation in the act of performing itself; the characters' walking is philosophy in practice, flânerie raised to a method; the scenes flow into one another until they create "the sense of a seamless urban tissue." The drift readings describe the camera's path with real precision.
[26] What they mistake is the grammatical person.
[27] Flânerie and the dérive are first-person figures: the walker chooses where to turn.
[28] In *Slacker* the walkers choose nothing about what we see.
[29] At every hand-off the film picks a single passerby from several available, and it never goes back.
[30] Whatever performs that choice is not strolling.

-- para 5
[31] The formal literature names the structure and stops there.
[32] In Charles Ramírez Berg's taxonomy of alternative plots, *Slacker* defines the daisy chain, in which the story jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit any of them.
[33] The philosophical literature goes further and makes the namelessness a virtue.
[34] On the Strawsonian reading, the film's people are Episodics, selves who do not experience their lives as stories: "We get only episodic moments of these characters' lives, without any larger meaning to the story, except for how these characters choose to spend their time," and their apathy is "a refusal to participate in traditional narrativity." That reading is right about the characters, and this chapter ends by supplying it with a complement.
[35] Its formal extension is the position that needs pressure.
[36] One commentator draws the consequence: "the frustration that viewers experience in the film's refusal to provide a coherent plot *is the point*." At the level of plot the refusal is total.
[37] As an account of the film's construction it proves too much, because a genuine refusal of coordination would play as shuffle, and *Slacker* is legible at every hand-off; viewers who cannot say what the film is about can always say where they are.
[38] The characters can afford to be Episodic because something else is doing the joining.
[39] If neither a relay, nor a shared ethic, nor the circulation of talk, nor the camera's drift, nor the refusal of narrative accounts for the hand-offs, does coordination play any role in *Slacker* at all?
[40] And if so, what performs it, and what form does it take?

-- para 6
[41] Theories of the network narrative, where the question might find an answer, run their principal accounts through convergence: plotlines that intertwine, consequences that propagate across strands, strangers whose paths finally cross.
[42] *Slacker* appears on the form's filmographies and supplies almost none of this; after the opening accident nothing propagates, and no line re-crosses.
[43] The nearest theory does without convergence: for Margrit Tröhler, the mosaic film coheres topologically, through an associative cohesion that montage initiates and the spectator completes, presided over by an enunciative instance she figures as Tyche, the goddess of chance.
[44] Her joining agents are textual through and through.
[45] The question of a worldly arrangement, of what in the depicted city keeps the next stranger available for the montage to find, does not arise, and *Slacker* surfaces in her work once, as a variant, unanalyzed.
[46] I would argue that the film's coordination is real, external to its story, and lodged in the world the story shows.
[47] Austin holds the day's strangers within reach of one another and introduces none of them.
[48] The camera selects, at each juncture, which of them we see, and no character selects what the film follows next.
[49] At the doors, a person still pronounces the grant and can still be argued with.
[50] The platforms that later assembled a comparable arrangement kept both jobs and the grant intact and withdrew the person.
[51] The film asks after a man named Gary and declines to produce him, and I argue that this refusal and the platform's withheld reason are the same gesture seen at forty years' distance.

=== SECTION: Simmel's postponed third

-- para 1
[52] Simmel's postponed third

-- para 2
[53] The sociology of the third begins in Simmel's triad chapter, and the chapter is best known for the thirds who stand between a pair.
[54] The non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits from the position between them; *divide et impera* sets the two against one another in order to rule both.
[55] Each of these figures presupposes an acquaintance: the third works on a pair it already knows.
[56] The taxi passenger and the woman in the street have no such third and no such acquaintance.
[57] What they share is a city, and nobody introduces them.

-- para 3
[58] On the chapter's opening page Simmel registers a third of another kind:

-- para 4
[59] The postponement was already old.
[60] The chapter's first English serialization, in 1902, carries the same sentence as "This society-constructing mediation of a third element is, however, to be treated in a later connection." The promise is not redeemed within the triad chapter, and when Simmel makes good on it, two hundred pages later, the third has changed kind.

-- para 5
[61] His reason for deferring is distance.
[62] The third stands so far from the other two that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three alike; what remain are configurations of two, the relation among those who join, and the relation between them as a unit and the outside center of interest.
[63] In plainer terms, God and two believers never sit at one table, and neither do two allies and a far enemy; there are only ever pairs.
[64] I call the figure Simmel set aside the deferred third: a third so distant that it never comes between an identified pair.
[65] The definition supplies the figure, and the film's city supplies the departure, because Austin differs from Simmel's examples at the instructive point.
[66] An enemy unifies, and a God unifies.
[67] Austin does neither.
[68] It holds the man from the bus station and the people around the struck woman in one city, all day, and introduces none of them.

-- para 6
[69] When the theme returns, in the chapter on conflict, the distance that had been grounds for deferral has become a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them is an especially favorable condition of the bond, and the same two figures return in altered form, a league held together by a standing enemy threat, a distant God gathering the most heterogeneous peoples.
[70] One stipulation will keep the term honest for the rest of this chapter.
[71] The deferred third is never a member of a triad; on Simmel's own account, only pairs of relations obtain around it.
[72] When I call Austin, a door, or a platform a third, I mean the outside term of the second pair, the far point over against the people it holds in one field.
[73] Austin occupies the deferred third's geometry while withholding its unifying effect, and whether holding without unifying can ever amount to membership is the question the next section's test decides.
[74] Platforms have entered Simmel's triad before, as brokers standing between the parties and as mediating thirds of the public sphere, and the recent literature on algorithmic management describes the platform's outside position without Simmel's vocabulary.
[75] The third of the postponed passage, the one that stands outside without mediating, has remained where Simmel left it.

=== SECTION: Royce's test for membership

-- para 1
[76] Royce's test for membership

-- para 2
[77] Holding is one thing, and belonging is another, and Royce supplies the exact test for the difference.
[78] A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation stands in the same relation to shared future events.
[79] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui.
[80] They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other.
[81] They may be enemies."

-- para 3
[82] The definition is permissive at every point where one might expect strictness.
[83] It tolerates aloofness, enmity, distance, and the absence of any meeting; two believers with incompatible theologies still share the one God.
[84] It is strict at a single point instead.
[85] Royce writes acceptance into the definition itself, and he sets three conditions, which the scholarship reads as cumulative and which come to this: each self can stretch its life in imagination to take in a past it never lived; the selves can talk to one another; and every one of them claims at least one identical event as its own.
[86] Without the second condition there is no community at all, and without each person's act of claiming there is nothing to have in common.
[87] A day of unclaimed strangers cannot pass the test; sharing a city is not sharing a past.
[88] The film administers the test in a scene built around a man who never appears.

=== SECTION: The two jobs, with someone still there to be asked

-- para 1
[89] The two jobs, with someone still there to be asked

-- para 2
[90] The film's clearest jurisprudence occupies about a minute at a club door, at night.
[91] A man credited as S-T-E-V-E with a van has been telling friends about the evening ahead: his roommate's band is playing, he has the van, and he made the roommate put him on the guest list.
[92] At the door he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them.
[93] The doorman charges him three dollars.
[94] He spells his name at the list, "S-T-E-V-E.
[95] Steve plus three or four," and the door answers, "Sorry, Steve.
[96] Don't see you," and Steve supplies the gloss himself: "Fuck.
[97] I'm not on the list." The list is real, and the club keeps it; Steve's error concerns what being on it would have been worth.
[98] Wealth of the modern kind, Reich argued in 1964, increasingly takes the form of largess, a grant whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient." Reich wrote about government largess, and he later gave up the public–private line for large organizations himself; the door performs his structure in miniature and in private.
[99] It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum, and declines to say why the list does not hold Steve's name.
[100] Reich also demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated." The door never reaches that sentence.

-- para 3
[101] What the scene preserves is the grant's address, and the address should be measured carefully, because it buys Steve nothing.
[102] He is charged, checked, and turned away, and arguing leaves him three dollars poorer.
[103] A person at a door can be as arbitrary as any system, on grounds no better disclosed; the history of club doors is a history of exactly that.
[104] Addressability and accountability are distinct properties, and the film keeps only the first in view.
[105] The grant is spoken to a person, by a person.
[106] Steve can talk back to the door even though the door owes him nothing.

-- para 4
[107] Later, at another door, a man offers a hand stamp, "I got a stamp.
[108] I think I can, you know, get you in or something," and copies it by licking wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man.
[109] I don't want him licking my arm") that stops nobody, and the group walks in free.
[110] The pair of doors is as close as the film comes to a controlled comparison.
[111] A stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers on their own initiative.
[112] The guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it.
[113] At closing time someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it, "It's past 2:00.
[114] Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere.
[115] You can't be here.
[116] And I wanna go home." Even the eviction addresses people who could in principle argue back.

-- para 5
[117] Gary is missing, and nobody can answer for him.
[118] In a café on the Drag a man asks after a friend, "You seen Gary around?," and gets the answer he has evidently gotten before: "I told you.
[119] No one's seen him for months." He leaves with an answer and without Gary.
[120] Outside, a stranger who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited.
[121] The asker heads off toward a friend's place; the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed, stops a second stranger with "Just a little security check.
[122] There've been some people missing," and waves him along: "I mean, you're in a hurry.
[123] That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears.
[124] The sidewalk pairing is the one that matters here.
[125] The asker and the talker had never met; the city held them within reach of one another; the camera carried their meeting in one unbroken drift from the café into the street.
[126] Nobody introduced them.

-- para 6
[127] On Royce's terms the two are contemporaries in communication, so the second condition holds on this sidewalk as it holds almost nowhere else in the film.
[128] The failure sits in acceptance, and it sits on both sides.
[129] Royce's test tolerates divergence; the Tai-Nui men may fight over what the voyage meant and remain one community, because each has claimed the crossing as his own.
[130] Neither man on the Drag claims anything.
[131] The asker is still seeking the fact of the absence, holding it at arm's length as a question, and the talker never accepts the absence at all, substituting for it an event of his own manufacture, a recruitment nobody witnessed.
[132] No identical event has entered both lives, and so, on Royce's terms, there is no community, however fluent the conversation.
[133] Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

-- para 7
[134] Through the rest of the day the holding keeps working, and two kinds of join take turns.
[135] Early on, the camera does the joining for us, placing the taxi and then the body in one sequence, and pulling a conspiracy into the frame behind a missing-friend question; a voting rant ends when a second friend interrupts it to say that Paul has vanished, "Why don't you come look?
[136] It's really weird," and the ranter follows.
[137] By night the people on screen close meetings themselves, with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man.
[138] Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car?
[139] I have a van"), the hand stamp, and a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud, shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back.
[140] Characters select one another constantly, and the talker singling out his listener is the film's hard case, a choice inside the story that the camera then ratifies.
[141] What no character ever chooses is which use of the city's reach the film follows next.
[142] The doorman, meanwhile, administers a grant, which belongs to neither job.
[143] By dawn the camera is joining scenes on its own again, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a camera thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell, and in those joins nothing changes hands and nobody addresses anybody.

-- para 8
[144] The same day shows a hustle economy running on the city's reach: kids hawking Cokes, a man selling T-shirts, a woman offering what she swears is a Madonna Pap smear, a hitchhiker back from his stepfather's funeral who takes a lift into town, says the man "was a serious fuck-up," and asks for a cigarette.
[145] Outside Les Amis a man credited as Hitchhiker awaiting "true call" tells a camera crew he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and when they ask what that is he says he will know it when he hears it.
[146] The city holds him in reach of whoever might call, and it asks nothing of him: holding without enrollment, reach with no management attached.
[147] The PixelVision camera at the party is the closer ancestor of what came next, contribution handed along under conditions somebody states out loud.
[148] When David Stark and Ivana Pais describe platforms co-opting "the behavior of providers and users, enrolling them in the practices of algorithmic management without managerial authority having been delegated to them," they describe what happened once the enrollment was added and the person who states the conditions was removed.
[149] On the sidewalk in 1990, somebody is always standing there to be asked.

=== SECTION: The same jobs without an answerer

-- para 1
[150] The same jobs without an answerer

-- para 2
[151] Linklater glossed the structure twenty years on: "I saw Slacker as you're either channel surfing or you're going in and out of different movies.
[152] And that was a really primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex." The comparison runs deeper than viewing habits, because the four acts at Steve's door recur, act for act, in the documents of a platform he could not have named.
[153] Uber sets the fare and takes its fee.
[154] The ratings ledger is the list: drivers "who don't meet the minimum average ratings for their city may lose access to all or part of the Uber Marketplace Platform." The refusal arrives as deactivation, at the company's "sole discretion." And the company's own deactivation page carries the withheld reason as policy, promising to communicate "the reasons for the decision, except when doing so poses a risk to other users." A survey of a self-selected sample of 810 California drivers by Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus found two thirds reporting suspension or deactivation, and thirty percent of those given no reason at all.

-- para 3
[155] What the platform withdrew is more exact than an address.
[156] Uber maintains a Review Center where a deactivated driver can lodge an appeal, and researchers who studied the channel found template responses, drivers writing "escalate to manager" into their messages in the hope of reaching a person, and suspension notices whose entire explanation read that the account had violated the terms and conditions.
[157] The address exists.
[158] The answerer of the doorman's kind, someone standing in the doorway who hears the objection as it is made, does not.
[159] Steve's list failed in public, and the group could renegotiate on the sidewalk; a deactivation notice arrives after the fact, and the queue has already moved on.
[160] The film supplies the image for that irreversibility.
[161] The Video Backpacker explains that a video image is more powerful than an actual event because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind." A driver cannot rewind an allocation.
[162] The law has begun trying to legislate the doorman back into the doorway: the European Union now requires statements of reasons for platform terminations, and its platform-work directive orders that any decision to suspend or terminate a platform worker's account "shall be taken by a human being." The remedies exist because the vacancy is real.

-- para 4
[163] Because Royce makes acceptance constitutive, the platform's enrollment is not membership, and calling it one would spend his term twice and in opposite senses.
[164] Salomé Viljoen describes the mechanism: a horizontal, population-based data relation places a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he shares with others he has never met.
[165] Grouped without claiming, enrolled without a yes.
[166] Viljoen's own remedy is collective, democratic governance of the data relation, and Rideshare Drivers United is that remedy in embryo: drivers organized to compel, together, an answer no one of them could compel alone.
[167] What it took to live with a grant while a person still pronounced it is what the film shows.
[168] What it takes when nothing pronounces it is a further question.

=== SECTION: Keeping the jobs apart

-- para 1
[169] Keeping the jobs apart

-- para 2
[170] The city and the camera do not swap jobs, and the film's doors mark the boundary between them.
[171] People use the city's reach: a lift, an address, a stamp, a van.
[172] The doorman administers a grant inside that arrangement, and neither act belongs to the camera.

-- para 3
[173] In 1981 Scott Feld asked how people come to know one another without a matchmaker.
[174] His answer was the focus, a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized, such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster." Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organized.
[175] The people on screen, however, share sidewalks and form no cluster, and the joint activity that produces Feld's ties never materializes.
[176] What remains is the holding I have been calling reach.
[177] The newest scholarship on the film reads its Austin as a myth, the emblem a generation uses to mourn a city that has since become something else; the reading is right about the film's afterlife, and the arrangement described here is the thing the myth mythologizes.
[178] Digital platforms, in Sarah Barns's phrase, add "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects" to that kind of urban reach, the picking of who actually encounters whom, and picking is the camera's job in this film.
[179] Urban economics has long treated the probability of a match and the quality of a match as separable functions of a city; the film stages the separation as a division of labor between visible agents.

-- para 4
[180] The film refuses to resolve what the city cannot resolve.
[181] To produce Gary, to let the man at Les Amis specify the true call, to close Paul's disappearance, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk would be to supply from outside the introductions the arrangement does not make.
[182] A door still says the rule out loud.
[183] A camera still chooses the next person without telling anyone why.

=== SECTION: Conclusion

-- para 1
[184] Conclusion

-- para 2
[185] What the network theories lacked was an instance: the thing that remains when convergence, consequence, and coincidence have all been subtracted and a film still holds.
[186] This chapter has named it the deferred third and given it two agents and a door.
[187] The taxi and the struck woman share a city that introduces nobody.
[188] Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who charges him, checks a list, turns him away, gives no further reason, and hears about it to his face.
[189] Gary is asked after and never produced, because producing him would supply the one thing the arrangement never supplies.

-- para 3
[190] The platform keeps the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason, and withdraws the person who had to say them out loud.
[191] Now that no one has to answer, we can still hear the people on screen ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
