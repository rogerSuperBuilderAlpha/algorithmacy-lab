cannot go to an editor in its current form, and the three questions cannot go to print without a

# Part 1 — theoretical rigor and structure

## 1.1 FATAL. The novelty claim is false as printed, and the chapter's own research told it so.

§2 prints:

> "The setting was named in 1981 and has been studied since; the broker has a literature of its own;
> nobody has set the two side by side and held both to one test."

`research/deep/2026-08-01_s2.md` names one source as "the single source most likely to embarrass the
novelty claim," reports that no search angle could open it, and instructs: "Prior art the chapter must
read before claiming novelty." `outline_v3.md` repeats the instruction in the §2 author-only list:
"**Read before the claim is printed.**" It was printed unread.

I opened it. Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi, "Dynamics of Dyads in Social Networks: Assortative,
Relational, and Proximity Mechanisms," *Annual Review of Sociology* 36 (2010): 91–115, DOI
10.1146/annurev.soc.34.040507.134743. Bibliographic record confirmed through Crossref; title and
abstract retrieved through OpenAlex. The abstract:

> "This article reviews sociological research examining the processes through which dyadic ties form,
> persist, and dissolve. Three sociological mechanisms are overviewed: assortative mechanisms draw
> attention to the role of actors' attributes, relational mechanisms emphasize the influence of
> existing relationships and network positions, and proximity mechanisms focus on the organization of
> interaction in social networks."

Relational mechanisms are network positions — the broker. Proximity mechanisms are the organization of
interaction — the setting. One review, one explanandum (whether a particular tie forms), three families
held against each other. That is setting beside broker, in *Annual Review of Sociology*, sixteen years
ago. The sentence "nobody has set the two side by side" is dead. It does not matter that the chapter's
test and Rivera's are different tests; the printed clause is a conjunction and its first half is false.

The escape route is narrow and the chapter has to take it explicitly. What survives is: nobody has
sorted the two by *what the third knows about the parties it joins*. That is the third question, it is
the only one of the three that does any real work (see 1.2), and it is a smaller claim than the one on
the page. Note also that the surviving claim is not helped at all by the three-empty-sets warrant. That
warrant establishes that Halevy, Kalmijn and Finkel do not cite each other. Non-citation among three
documents licenses "these three documents do not cite each other." It does not license a universal
negative over a literature, and printing it as "the warrant is documentary and anyone with three PDFs
can check it" invites a referee to check three different PDFs. I checked one.

Two further pieces of prior art the chapter suppresses, both in its own research file:

- The German debate names the distinction outright as *konkreter* versus *abstrakter Dritter* (`s2.md`
  cites Fuhse 2013 with a verbatim quotation). It appears nowhere in the chapter. A German-reading
  referee will treat a novelty paragraph that omits a literature named after this exact distinction as
  a suppression, not an oversight.
- The chapter's own concession refutes the claim two paragraphs later: "Finkel's team draws something
  close to my distinction inside a single platform, between a site's access … and its matching." Drawing
  the two inside one platform and evaluating both in one review *is* setting them side by side and
  holding both to one test. The rebuttal offered — "they get there and stop; the distinction stays
  inside dating" — concedes the priority and changes the claim to "nobody has done it outside dating,"
  which is not the claim printed above it.

**Demand.** Rewrite the sentence to the surviving claim, cite Rivera et al. by name in the same
paragraph, and get the article before the chapter goes anywhere. A referee who reads §2's warrant as an
invitation will find Rivera in one search.

## 1.2 FATAL. The three questions misclassify the camera and the landing page — the chapter's own two exhibits.

§2 concedes one misclassification honestly and does not notice that the same failure eats the rest of
the chapter.

The concession: Simmel's *divide et impera* prohibition "acts on everyone and knows nothing about
particulars, so on two of my three questions it scores as a setting — while destroying reachability."
Fine. The chapter's defence is that the questions sort kind, not harm.

But run the questions on the camera, as §2 does:

> "The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and knows
> nothing about anybody."

Question 3 is answered *setting*. The chapter says so in the same clause and again in §4 — "it arranges
what reaches you and knows nothing whatever about you." So the camera scores two of three. The
prohibition scored one of three as a selector and the chapter called that a misclassification worth
flagging. The camera scores two of three and the chapter calls it a selector without comment. There is
no stated threshold anywhere. How many of three makes a selector? The chapter never says, and it cannot
say "two" without readmitting the Simmel case, and cannot say "three" without losing the camera.

Question 1 is worse, because the referent moves. For Austin, "everyone" is people on the street. For
the camera, "one person out of the crowd" is a character. But §4 concedes that the camera does not
coordinate the characters at all — "the camera coordinates *me*." Run question 1 on what the camera
actually coordinates and the answer flips: the cut acts on every viewer in the theatre at once, alike,
knowing nothing about any of them. On its own coordinatees the camera is a setting on questions 1 and
3 both.

Question 2 is vacuous for the camera. "Could the encounter have happened without it?" A viewer and a
character cannot meet without a medium, ever, by the nature of the case. So question 2 returns
*selector* for every film, every novel, every broadcast, every painting. A test that classifies all
representation as selection is not discriminating anything in the domain where the chapter's title
object lives. It discriminates beautifully in the Uber case, where there is a real dyad that could have
transacted otherwise — which is exactly the case nobody needed a test for.

Then §7's climax repeats the failure a third time:

> "A page that chooses what you see next, without knowing who you are, is the nearest thing streaming
> has to Linklater's camera."

Acts on everyone alike (the source's own method cleared cookies daily precisely to guarantee it), knows
nothing about you. Two of three: setting. The chapter's closing exhibit is classified by the chapter's
own test as the opposite of what the chapter calls it.

So the score sheet, run honestly: Austin 0/3, the prohibition 1/3, the camera 2/3, the landing page
2/3, Uber 3/3. The only object the test sorts cleanly is the one whose classification was never in
doubt. **This is the load-bearing failure of the piece,** and it is worse than the novelty problem
because the novelty problem has a repair that costs one sentence and this one costs a redesign.

I do not think the apparatus is beyond saving, and the honest repair is more interesting than the
current version. It is roughly this: question 2 does the sorting for transactional thirds, questions 1
and 3 do the sorting for attentional thirds, and they point opposite ways because attentional selectors
are settings *in reach* and selectors *in sequence*. The chapter half-knows this — "selectors do two
different jobs" — and never lets the knowledge back into the test.

## 1.3 SERIOUS. §4 buys an agent and pays for it with the analogy.

The narrator paragraph runs to a fallback: "taking the fallback the philosophers offer, that a story
'is actually told by the author in his or her creation of the work.'" Then: "When I write 'the camera,'
I mean whatever makes those choices."

Whatever makes those choices, on the fallback just adopted, is Richard Linklater. Which means §4's 900
words of Bordwell, Chatman, Slugan and Thomson-Jones deliver the conclusion that Linklater chose the
order of the scenes — a fact §1 has already established from Linklater's own mouth ("We'll hold {the
viewer's} hand. We'll have these transitional characters"), and which Ramírez Berg's definition of the
daisy chain already assigns to Linklater, as note 43 admits.

Worse, it destroys the parallel the chapter is built on. What makes the platform comparison worth
making is that the selecting is done by an impersonal system with no one behind the choice. The moment
the chapter relocates the agent to the author to win the film-studies argument, the camera stops being
an impersonal selector and becomes a man with a shot list, and the shared property with Uber's dispatch
shrinks to "both choose" — which is true of a maître d'.

The chapter cannot have both. Either the selector is impersonal (in which case the author fallback is
unavailable and Bordwell's no-sender position is a live problem the chapter must actually beat), or it
is Linklater (in which case §4 should be a third its length and the platform parallel needs a different
foundation). "Whatever makes those choices" is an evasion and a referee will name it as one.

## 1.4 SERIOUS. §5's three powers dissolve the distinction they were supposed to complete.

Two problems, and they compound.

First, the middle power has no evidence for the thing it is a power over. §5 announces "The second thing
is that it reads the parties," then, four sentences later: "no published engineering account describes
ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver identity in order to choose the pair… The evidence sits on
the pricing side." So there is no evidenced reading power *in matching*; there is a pricing power that
happens to involve reading. The triad has two members and a placeholder. Rule 7 of the house style
exists for this: a triad whose middle term collapses into its first term is a rhetorical shape, not an
analysis.

Second, and this is the real damage: neither surviving power follows from selecting. Pricing a
transaction and expelling a party are things a firm with market power does. A pure setting can do both.
A bulletin board can charge for listings and ban a user; Craigslist deactivates accounts; a landlord
prices a market square. Nothing in "chooses which two parties come together" entails, implies or
predicts either. So the payoff the whole chapter builds toward — what platforms can do to you that
cameras cannot — is carried entirely by properties the setting/selector distinction does not track.

Which brings the hostile question directly: **is this a distinction or a relabelling?** My answer, and
I want the author to answer it in the text rather than around it: it is a real distinction that does not
earn its place, because it is doing no explanatory work at either end. At the Austin end it renames
Feld's focus. At the Uber end it is idle, because the powers that matter are not selection powers. It
does its only genuine work in the middle, on the camera — and that is precisely the case where the test
that defines it returns the wrong answer (1.2).

## 1.5 SERIOUS. §7 switches the selected party without saying so, and its two numbers are denominator artifacts.

The switch. §5's three powers are exercised over *persons* — a driver is priced, read, deactivated. §7
says "three of the powers described above are in" Amazon's page, and then quotes a page whose subject
is *titles submitted by content providers*: reviewed for performance metrics, removable at sole
discretion, unappealable. Those powers fall on suppliers, not on viewers. The chapter's stated analogue
to Linklater's camera in §7 is the landing page, which selects among titles for a viewer and prices
nothing and removes nobody. So §7 runs two arguments spliced at the seam: camera → landing page
(attention, no powers) and Uber → Amazon licensing (transaction, all the powers). The paragraph reads as
a payoff and is a change of subject.

The numbers. "15,584 British titles in European video-on-demand circulation become 1,165 in Netflix's
European catalogues" compares the union of every VOD service's holdings against one service's. Any
single service will be a small fraction of that union arithmetically, whatever it licenses; the ratio
carries no information about narrowing. "Fewer than one title in ten reaches the landing page" is
likewise fixed by geometry — a landing page holds a few dozen tiles no matter what anyone intends. And
the chapter has already forbidden itself the comparison it then makes: *Slacker* on sixteen screens is
about 0.06 percent of American screens, so on the chapter's own metric 1991 theatrical narrowed far
harder than Netflix does. The clause "which is the scale to hold in mind before comparing any of this
with a recommender" reads like a caution and functions as permission.

What the source will actually bear is the comparison *between the two stages* — licensing and prominence
narrow by comparable amounts, and the funnel is not nested. Say that and drop the enclosure arithmetic.

## 1.6 SERIOUS. §7 converts "nobody ran the study" into a positive finding.

§6 is scrupulous: "Whether that skill transfers to reading an algorithm is unknown in a specific sense:
nobody has run the study… The default expectation here is against transfer."

§7 then writes: "audiences learned to follow a hidden arranger and **learned nothing about questioning
one**."

That is an assertion of a negative result from an absence of studies — the exact move the project's own
§1 do-not-use list forbids in another context: "Do not replace an unsupported assertion with its
unsupported negation." A default expectation against transfer is a prior, not a finding. Heiland does
not repair this either: he shows that riders' theories about *one* parameter were wrong and about
another were right, in food delivery, with no film variable measured anywhere.

And the larger structural question a hostile referee will ask: **what is §6 for?** It establishes that
reading cuts is learned, then establishes that the learning probably does not transfer to the thing the
chapter cares about. As the chapter stands, §6's conclusion is that its own premise is inert. The
abstract the editor accepted promises the opposite ("cinema has trained viewers to read engineering
thirds for a century"). Either §6 argues for a narrower connection it can defend — that *Slacker*
trained viewers to notice the choosing, because it removed the plot that usually absorbs the credit,
which is a claim about this film and not about transfer — or it is a 660-word detour that ends in a
shrug. The material for the narrower version is already on the page: "Remove the plot and the choosing
is the only thing left to notice." Build the section toward that and stop reaching for the algorithm.

## 1.7 SERIOUS. Two evidentiary integrity problems.

**(a) The one review that cuts the other way is buried.** §1's premise is an argument from silence over
seven reviews: "no critic, friendly or hostile, reports losing track of who was on screen or why the
film had moved on." `outline_v3.md` records that *Variety*'s notice — "interest quickly rises and falls
by virtue of who happens to be on screen" — "leans toward a comprehension complaint," and instructs:
"include it *because* it cuts the other way. A reader will find it." The chapter lists *Variety* in the
body's roll of retrieved reviews and in note 4 and never quotes or characterises it. In a section whose
whole load is negative evidence, silently omitting the one item that leans against is the most damaging
thing in the chapter after 1.1 and 1.2. It is also unnecessary — the notice does not actually report
losing the thread, and saying so out loud strengthens the premise.

Related and smaller: one third of the Sundance audience fell asleep. Walking out is dealt with. Sleeping
is not, and a hostile reader will say that a viewer asleep is a viewer who stopped following.

**(b) Feld was never opened and the chapter does not say so.** Note 10 discloses "I have read the
abstract only" for Boom, a source doing almost no work. Note 18 cites Feld 1981 flat, and Feld supplies
the definition on which the chapter's entire first term rests. `outline_v3.md`: "**The article itself was
never opened** — Unpaywall reports closed, no OA PDF. The p. 1016 definition is stable across five
independent quoting sources but remains second-hand." Disclosing the trivial case and not the
load-bearing one is the asymmetry a referee notices. Burt 1992 is in the same position ("held from the
project's prior record; not re-opened this pass"), though less is riding on it.

## 1.8 MINOR but visible. §3's numbers argue against §3's topic sentence.

"Cheap space buys unstructured time, and unstructured time is what this film is about." Then, in the
next clause: "Dallas that year sat at 15.3 percent vacancy and Houston at 15.8, so Austin was the
tighter market of the three." The chapter presents its own disconfirmation and moves on. No rent
comparison is offered, so "cheap" is unanchored — $346 median contract rent means nothing without
Dallas's and Houston's beside it. And the 48.0-vs-34.6 percent comparison sits inside boundaries that
moved, as note 27 admits, which the reader is told after being asked to believe the number.

Mion and Naticchioni are the same problem in miniature. Negative assortative matching in larger markets
is introduced as "a complication worth keeping" and then not kept — nothing later in the chapter uses
it, answers it, or is changed by it. A counterweight that costs nothing is decoration.

## 1.9 Two absences the research pass flagged and the chapter walks past.

`s2.md` names an "opposing default" the section "must answer if it proposes two kinds of selector":
O'Reilly, Strauss and Mazzucato deliberately unify attention and transaction platforms ("Each of the
dominant internet platforms is an attention gatekeeper of one kind or another, matching requests from
billions of consumers to content, services, and products from millions of suppliers"). §4 proposes
exactly two kinds of selector and never meets it. `s2.md` also records Meyer et al. 2024 dividing
matching from attention mechanisms and finding that "both … coexist on most platforms" — an anticipation
of the split *and* a complication for the chapter's clean assignment of one platform to each side.
Neither is cited. I have not verified either source myself; both are in VERIFY below.

# Part 2 — the four bans, and the slop audit

## Ban 1 — no metaphor doing argumentative work. **One clear violation, in a load-bearing move.**

§4:

> "His word is string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order."

The chapter derives a structural claim about the film from the entailments of a simile in someone
else's sentence. Bordwell wrote "a wiggly, knotted string" as a throwaway figure for a film he could not
categorise; he did not assert that presentation order is the film's organising property. The argument
here needs stating literally, and it can be: the scenes reach the viewer in a fixed sequence, and no
category in the network-narrative taxonomy records sequence. The Moretti clause already proves that
half. The string is illustration; here it is the premise.

Borderline, judged clean on re-reading: "daisy chain" (a term of art, cited); "dérive" (glossed on the
spot as "a drift, which is movement without a chooser"); "Every link in the chain that carried *Slacker*
to an audience was a person who could be telephoned" (the claim is literal — each was a person — and the
chain is decoration on top of it).

## Ban 2 — no throat-clearing. **Two violations.**

- §4: "That decision is constrained, and the constraint matters later." Announces a point and then makes
  it in the next two sentences, which stand perfectly well alone.
- §5: "Surge pricing is the wrong example here, and saying why sharpens the distinction." The second
  clause announces the value of the paragraph the reader is about to read.

Near-misses I would leave: "What I am claiming is narrow" (a scoping move that does argumentative
work); "Two qualifications, both real" (does work — it commits to conceding); "so I want one instance
rather than a pattern. One instance is enough" (states a methodological limit, which is content).

## Ban 3 — no meta-commentary or forward reference. **One violation, the same sentence.**

"the constraint matters later" is a forward reference to §5, unmistakably. It is the only one left; the
cut ledger shows two others were caught in the long draft. Fix it and the ban is clean.

## Ban 4 — no jargon left unexplained. **Four violations, all easy.**

- §3, "assortative matching runs *negatively* with local market size." Two unexplained terms in one
  clause, and the reader cannot tell whether this helps or hurts the argument.
- §3, "Economists have a name for what a place like that does… how agglomeration works." The chapter
  announces that there is a name, gives the name, and never says what it means.
- §6, "Adding diegetic sound to the clips."
- §6, "cuts violating the 180-degree rule."

Glosses supplied in Part 3.

## AI-slop audit, in this chapter's register

The prose is genuinely good and most of the usual tells are absent. There is no agentless-passive
opener anywhere in the body; the sentences vary in length; the concessions are real concessions and not
performances of balance. Three things are worth fixing.

**(a) The "X rather than Y" corrective, thirteen times in 5,414 words, six of them in §§5 and 7.**
Lines 279, 287, 365, 374, 382 and 399 all run the same move — a claim, then a clause pulling it back to
what the source will bear. Individually each is honest. Consecutively they make the last two sections
read as a run of qualifications with an argument buried inside, and they train the reader to wait for
the retraction rather than take the claim. Convert three of the six into positive statements. Example,
§7: "starved by a division applying a default rather than killed by the bankruptcy" → "a division
applied a default and starved the second window; the bankruptcy came later and did not cause it."

**(b) Self-narrating rigor, about ten instances.** "The warrant is documentary and anyone with three
PDFs can check it." "I will assert the differential pay and leave the personalisation where she leaves
it." "The fourth power is missing from the page and I am not going to supply it." "That is a real
difference from Uber and it is worth keeping." "I want to draw that difference in the right place,
though." "One instance is enough." Bordwell earns one or two of these a chapter. Ten reads defensive,
and in a chapter that has just been through a verification pass it reads specifically like a chapter
that has just been through a verification pass. Keep the four where the scruple *is* the content (the
Dubal one, the missing fourth power). Cut the ones that only advertise care.

**(c) Uniformity, mild.** Fourteen of fifty-five paragraphs close on a sentence of twelve words or
fewer — under the threshold I would flag, so I am not flagging it, but the closers cluster: §§1, 3 and
4 have three apiece and they land in the same rhythm ("So I want to know what people were following." /
"It did not decide which of them the film would follow." / "Nobody is owed a return.") Vary two.

# Verdict

**Major revisions.** Findings 1.1 and 1.2 are conditions, not suggestions: the novelty sentence is false
as printed, and the sorting test misclassifies both of the chapter's own exhibits with no threshold
stated. Everything else on this list is a day's work. If 1.1 and 1.2 come back unrepaired I would move
to reject, because the chapter's contribution is exactly one distinction plus one test, and at present
the distinction is not new in the form claimed and the test does not sort.

I want to be plain that this is a hostile report on a good chapter. Nothing here is the kind of problem

# Biggest genuine strength

The concessions are real and they are placed where they cost the most, which is rare. §5 says out loud
that the best evidence for its second power is not where the reader expects and sits on the pricing
side. §6 says the transfer study does not exist and that the field's default is against it. §2 brings
forward the Simmel case that its own test mishandles. Nobody who is protecting a thesis writes those
sentences. That habit is also what makes the chapter fixable: 1.2 is not a hidden flaw, it is a flaw the
author has already half-articulated on the page and stopped one step short of following. The step is
short.

Second, the *Slacker* reading itself is excellent and independent of the apparatus. "Two abandonments in
the opening minutes — the director, then a tragedy — teach the rule the rest of the film runs on. Nobody
is owed a return." That is the best sentence in the chapter and it needs no sociology behind it.

# The one thing only the author can supply

**Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi 2010, read end to end, and a decision about what survives it.** I can tell
the author the abstract holds proximity beside relational mechanisms; I cannot tell them whether the
body cites Feld, whether it runs anything the chapter would recognise as a common test, or whether it
already asks the knowledge question the chapter wants to claim. That article decides how much novelty
is left, and the answer changes what §2 can say, which changes what §5 is for. Nobody without library
access can settle it, and the chapter's own research pass tried four angles and failed. It has to be a
library trip.
