### A. The four fatals (fix before anything else) — 4 items

**A1. §6 — the Münsterberg quotation is about the theatre, not the photoplay.** Two reviewers retrieved
the source independently and agree sentence for sentence. The chapter prints:

> Hugo Münsterberg answered in 1916, in the first book of film psychology: "our attention is constantly
> drawn in accordance with the intentions of the producers."

Film-narrative retrieved Gutenberg #15383, ch. IV; critical-theory retrieved the page-marked 1916 D.
Appleton scan (archive.org, `photoplaypsychol00mnrich`) and puts it at p. 76. The paragraph is about the
stage: preceded by "How does the theater performance differ in this respect from life?", followed by
"Surely the theater has no lack of means to draw this involuntary attention to any important point," and
the film argument does not begin until p. 77 ("The question arises: how does the photoplay secure the
needed shifting of attention?"). Both reviewers further show Münsterberg's thesis runs *opposite* to the
use made of it — the photoplay reproduces the mind's own acts: the close-up "has objectified in our world
of perception our mental act of attention." That is nativist. No skill is acquired. It is contradicted
four paragraphs later by the chapter's own first-time viewers, who "perceived them to be wholly separate
from each other."

Adjudicated **verified** (two independent retrievals, identical surrounding text). Note the origin:
`outline_v3.md` line 815 records the research pass concluding "**an ally, not a rival.** He was offered
to this run as the rival to §6's training claim and turns out to be its first statement." That verdict is
what propagated. Re-reading the outline will not catch this.

**A2. §2 — the novelty claim is false as printed.** The chapter:

> The setting was named in 1981 and has been studied since; the broker has a literature of its own;
> nobody has set the two side by side and held both to one test.

Network-theory and reviewer 2 both retrieved Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi, "Dynamics of Dyads in Social
Networks," *Annual Review of Sociology* 36 (2010): 91–115, which sorts dyad formation into assortative
mechanisms, "relational mechanisms that emphasize the influence of existing relationships and network
positions," and "proximity mechanisms that focus on the social organization of interaction" — the broker
and the setting, one explanandum, one review. Network-theory resolved 219 of its 222 references through
OpenAlex and found Feld 1981, Feld 1982, Feld 1997, Bossard 1932, Burt 1997, Burt 2004, Burt & Knez,
Lazega & Burt, and Fernandez & Gould all in the same list.

I confirmed the project knew: `research/deep/2026-08-01_s2.md` line 532 heads it "**Prior art the chapter
must read before claiming novelty, and which no angle could open**," and `outline_v3.md` line 504 carries
it. The claim was printed unread against the project's own instruction. The three-empty-sets warrant
licenses only "these three documents do not cite each other," and the chapter's own next paragraph
concedes Finkel's team draws the distinction — which *is* setting them side by side.

**Both reviewers' bases are the abstract, not the body.** Neither has read it end to end. What survives
is a narrower and probably still-true claim (Rivera et al. sort by *what makes a tie form*, not by *what
the third knows about the parties*) but the wording cannot be fixed until somebody reads the article.

**A3. §3 — the census table contradicts the word the paragraph is built to earn.** Urban-sociology
downloaded 1990 CH-1-45, sec. 1, Table 1 and read the place rows: Austin's $346 median contract rent is
**above** Houston ($324), San Antonio ($307) and Fort Worth ($335), and below only Dallas ($373). Austin's
11.6 percent rental vacancy is matched to the decimal by San Antonio. The chapter prints the comparison
that flatters ("Dallas that year sat at 15.3 percent vacancy and Houston at 15.8") and omits the one that
kills. Two other reviewers noticed the paragraph refuting itself without opening the table — the general
reader: "Room or tight? Claim, evidence, counter-evidence, re-assertion in eighty words"; reviewer 2:
"'cheap' is unanchored." **One verified reviewer outranks the two who inferred it, and all three point the
same way.** The argument does not need cheapness. It needs slack, which the table gives outright.

**A4. §5/§7 — the powers do not add up, and they break in the paragraph where the argument lands.**
Four reviewers converged (platform-studies, media-industries, copyeditor, bans). §5 names exactly three:
"it comes to three things" / "The first is that it prices each match" / "The second thing is that it
reads the parties" / "The third thing is that it can remove a party." §7 then writes "three of the powers
described above are in it," lists **reading, removal and declining-to-explain** — and declining to explain
was never named a power — then calls pricing "The fourth power," though pricing is §5's first. The
copyeditor traced the origin: `outline_v3.md`'s §7 entry works from a four-power list; §5 shipped with
three and §7 kept the outline's count. Verifiable on the page in thirty seconds by anyone tallying.

### Ban 1 — metaphor doing argumentative work (7)

- **§4, the load-bearing one.** "His word is string, and **the important property of a string is that it

## 8. THE ONE ARCHITECTURAL DECISION

**What are the three questions for?**

Three reviewers reached it independently through three different lenses and proposed three incompatible
repairs, which is the signature of a decision rather than a defect. The chapter says:

> Three questions tell them apart. Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could the
> encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what it
> does accordingly?

Run honestly, the test splits on the chapter's own paradigm cases. **Austin** answers setting three times.
**Uber** answers selector three times. **The camera** — the chapter's paradigm selector — answers selector,
selector, *setting*, because it "knows nothing about anybody," a phrase §4 repeats as "knows nothing
whatever about you." **Simmel's prohibition** splits two-to-one, and the chapter says so out loud.
**§7's landing page** answers the first and third like a setting ("chooses what you see next, without
knowing who you are") and is then offered as the nearest thing streaming has to Linklater's camera. No
threshold is ever stated. A reader who tallies gets a different answer from the chapter's in three of five
cases.

The three options, none of which is obviously right:

**Option A — the questions grade rather than sort.** Q1 and Q2 decide the kind; Q3 grades the selector,
because knowing who you are is what lets a selector do more than pick. Cheapest, fixes exactly what a
reader stumbles on, and leaves §2's structure alone. Cost: Q3 stops being a criterion and becomes a
severity scale, which weakens the Uber column where the chapter most wants force.

**Option B — the questions are diagnostics that presuppose the definition.** They sort thirds that already
put people within reach; Simmel's superior never enters the field they sort, which is why he scores
strangely. Truest to what §2 half-says already, and turns the Simmel case from an embarrassment into a
demonstration of what the questions are for. Cost: the chapter has to admit its instrument is not a
decision procedure, and the bolded definitions have to carry more weight than they now do.

**Option C — the two kinds of selector are the real distinction, and the questions serve them.** §4
already draws it — selectors that choose what an audience sees next versus selectors that choose who does
business with whom — and the camera and the landing page are both the first kind, which is exactly why
they answer Q3 like settings. On this reading the misclassification is not a bug: it is the taxonomy
telling you which kind you have. Cost: the most rewriting, and it demotes the three questions from the
section's centrepiece to a subroutine.

Two subordinate decisions ride on it and should be made after, not before:

- **Is "declines to explain" a fourth power?** §7 needs it to be; §5's enumeration does not contain it.
  The two strongest pieces of evidence in the chapter's second half — the Amsterdam **disclosure** remedy
  and Amazon's "may not be resubmitted or appealed" — are both about explanation rather than price,
  matching or removal. If the answer is yes, §5's enumeration changes and the chapter gains a power that
  has nothing to do with money, and which **a camera arguably shares**, since a cut never explains itself
  either. A4 has to be fixed regardless; this decides in which direction.
- **Is §7 about enclosure or about replacement?** The assembled evidence supports replacement cleanly and
  enclosure only with the Smits 2017 → invitation-only closure added — and even then Tzioumakis's refilled
  middle tier and Waldfogel's "substantial growth in the number of movies brought to market" are standing
  counter-evidence the section neither cites nor concedes. Retitling is cheaper than winning that argument.

And one thing the panel raised that is a claim, not a decision, and that the author alone can price:
critical-theory's observation that everything §6 measures is a competence at **receiving** an arrangement
