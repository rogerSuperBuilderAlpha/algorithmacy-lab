# Review - General reader

**Verdict:** minor revisions
**Target:** chapter_v3.md (post-followup; panel 3)

## Step 0 - register

The first-person register works. It gives the chapter a guide, keeps the scenes in front of the
argument, and makes the concessions feel earned. I am judging this as an intelligent reader who does
not own the film-studies, urban-sociology, or platform-studies vocabulary. The chapter mostly reaches
that reader. The remaining problems are local: a central test still needs its scoring rule, several
expert terms still arrive bare, and two stretches ask the reader to stay with platform or industry
evidence after the film has gone quiet.

## Part 1 - Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. The setting/selector distinction is clear. The three-question test is almost clear.

The definition is the strongest general-reader passage in the chapter:

> **A setting puts people within reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one
> approach another. It arranges no particular meeting.** Against it, **a selector chooses: which two
> parties come together, or which one an audience meets next.**

That lands on first read. Austin and the camera are easy to hold after that. The trouble comes in the
next paragraph, where the test itself produces mixed answers:

> Three questions tell them apart. Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could
> the encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what
> it does accordingly? Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at
> most. The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and
> knows nothing about anybody. Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both
> parties.

The camera is the key case, and it answers the first two questions as a selector and the third question
as a setting. The chapter now anticipates this better than the earlier draft did:

> These are diagnostics and not a decision procedure, and they presuppose the two definitions above.

and:

> One more thing the questions do not do is demote. A selector that answers no to the third question is
> still a selector - the camera picks a person out of the crowd and knows nothing whatever about
> anybody.

Those sentences help, but they still leave a small piece of arithmetic for the reader. A diagnostic that
is not a decision procedure can still need a rule of use. The rule seems to be: the first two questions
identify the kind of third; the third question grades the power of a selector once it exists. The
chapter should say that in the paragraph where the questions first appear.

Paste-ready addition after "Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both
parties":

> The first two questions do the sorting. If a third picks out particular parties and is the only route
> between them, it is a selector however little it knows. The third question tells what that selector
> can then do.

That lets the later "does not demote" paragraph read as reinforcement instead of rescue.

### 2. There is enough film in the chapter as a whole, but not enough film in section 5.

Sections 1, 3, and 4 are film-forward in the right way. The taxi, the woman in the road, the
moon-landing monologue, the Madonna pap-smear seller, the Old Anarchist's house, and the handoffs all
do argumentative work. The reader gets the claim by seeing the film move.

Section 5 is the place I would put the chapter down. It opens:

> Uber does not put drivers and riders within reach of each other and leave them to it. It chooses
> which driver gets which ride, and nothing in the design lets the two settle anything between
> themselves.

That sentence is clear, but then the film disappears for most of the section. The reader gets dynamic
pricing, an audit, surge pricing, Dubal, Amsterdam, Seattle, and Cameron before the camera returns:

> A camera drops people for good - *Slacker* does it in the first three minutes, twice - and it costs
> the dropped party nothing outside the frame. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take you off
> the street.

That is the sentence a general reader needs at the front of the section. It tells me why the Uber
material belongs here. It also gives me a simple question to carry through the evidence: what can Uber
do that Linklater's camera cannot?

Paste-ready replacement for the opening of section 5:

> A camera can drop people for good - *Slacker* does it in the first three minutes, twice - and it
> costs the dropped party nothing outside the frame. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take
> you off the street. Uber does not put drivers and riders within reach of each other and leave them to
> it. It chooses which driver gets which ride, and nothing in the design lets the two settle anything
> between themselves.

Then the later paragraph can lose the repeated camera sentences and begin:

> The difference has to be drawn in the right place, though, because Lindsey Cameron's seven-year study
> of ride-hailing found that...

### 3. The chapter's hardest jargon is no longer in the thesis. It remains in support clauses.

This is good news and bad news. The key terms - setting, selector, third - are defined in ordinary
language. The bare terms that remain tend to appear in subordinate evidence, which means they slow the
reader without buying much precision.

The main examples:

- "nonterminal" in section 3. The phrase "offering no natural exit" explains it, but the expert word
  comes first.
- "subject-access data" in section 5. A reader outside data-rights law may not know this means data the
  drivers obtained about themselves.
- "take rate" in section 5. The chapter gives the numbers before saying what the term means.
- "preliminary injunction" in section 5. The legal posture matters less than the plain point that the
  court had not made a final ruling.
- "second window" in section 7. The sentence means home video.
- "lowest tier" and "tier above it" in section 7. The Tzioumakis paragraph assumes an industry map the
  reader does not have.
- "oblique-strategies cards" in the final paragraph. The quotation is clear, but the object is not.

The pattern is fixable. Each term needs either deletion or a short appositive. The reader should not
have to leave the sentence to understand the clause.

### 4. Section 7 has a strong human chain, then drifts into an industry argument the reader cannot grade.

The opening of section 7 is excellent:

> Every link in the chain that carried *Slacker* to an audience was a person who could be telephoned.

That sentence explains the whole section. The Dobie, Pierson, Orion, Susan Blodgett, and the *Film
Comment* paragraph all belong to the same world: named people made legible decisions.

The Tzioumakis paragraph is the one place where the section loses that clarity:

> The studios did close their specialty divisions, and Yannis Tzioumakis documents it. What his numbers
> refuse is the second half of the easy story, that the streamers then took over. The lowest tier's
> fall from 10.22 to 2.11 percent of theatrical box office is, in his phrase, only "seemingly
> suggesting" a collapse, because he reclassified the largest earners upward himself; and the tier
> above it refilled.

As a general reader, I do not know what the tiers are, why the reclassification matters, or how this
paragraph changes the argument. The sentence after it is the part I can use:

> Theatrical distribution changed shape more than it shrank...

If the paragraph stays, it needs a plain topic sentence that tells the reader what the numbers prove.
Better, cut the numbers and keep the conclusion.

Paste-ready replacement:

> The studios did close their specialty divisions, and Yannis Tzioumakis documents it. But his numbers
> do not support the easy story that streamers simply took over after theatrical distribution
> collapsed. Small-film distribution changed shape more than it vanished - and *Slacker*'s own
> position in 1990, with no other bidder and one modest offer taken on a template, looks more like the
> present than like the boom in between.

### 5. The ending works emotionally. One term still blocks the last turn.

The final paragraph earns its last claim. The chapter has spent enough time with the film for this to
land:

> Its people withdrew from plot, career, and convergence and stayed coordinated anyway - put within
> reach by a cheap and legible city, and shown to us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from
> them except a day, and gave the day back watchable.

The only snag is the sentence before it:

> *Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself on one of
> the oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it: "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same
> thing as apathy."

The quotation is clear. "Oblique-strategies cards" is not. The reader does not need a history of the
deck. A plain noun solves it.

Paste-ready replacement:

> *Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself on one of
> the prompt cards a passerby hands out in it: "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as
> apathy."

## Part 2 - The four bans, and AI-slop audit

### Ban 1 - metaphor doing argumentative work

Mostly clear. "Baton-passing," "daisy chain," "wiggly, knotted string," and "chain" are either quoted
from sources or cashed into literal claims about order, handoff, and connection. "Enclosure" is the
closest call. The section makes the enclosure claim through licensing, prominence, removal, and withheld
reasons, so the heading is not carrying the argument alone. Still, a general reader may expect land,
law, or commons history from that word and may be surprised to get streaming distribution. The heading
can stay, but it is doing more mood work than the other section titles.

### Ban 2 - throat-clearing

Low. The chapter usually states the point directly. Two places still announce the shape of a correction
before making it:

> The difference has to be drawn in the right place, though...

and:

> The questions the film leaves open have answers of unequal quality.

Neither is fatal. The first can be removed if the section 5 opening moves the camera contrast forward.
The second is a useful final turn, but it is still a road sign.

### Ban 3 - meta-commentary / forward-backward reference

Mostly clear. The chapter does not lean on "as I have shown" or section roadmaps. The phrase "and later
Uber" in section 4 is a soft forward reference:

> Some choose who does business with whom - the taxi dispatcher, and later Uber.

It earns its place because the sentence is sorting two types of selector. I would not cut it. The
reader understands why Uber returns.

### Ban 4 - unexplained jargon

This is the remaining ban problem. It is not severe enough to threaten the chapter, but it is the main
general-reader cost. The terms listed in Part 1 are the ones I would insist on glossing or cutting:
"nonterminal," "subject-access data," "take rate," "preliminary injunction," "second window," the
Tzioumakis "tiers," and "oblique-strategies cards." The chapter's own central vocabulary is clearer
than this supporting vocabulary, which means the fixes are small.

### AI-slop audit

The prose does not read as machine prose. Scenes precede claims. Paragraphs do not all open with the
same clipped gesture. The concessions are specific enough to build trust.

The one pattern worth trimming is the corrective sentence ending. The chapter often lands by saying
what something is not:

> These are diagnostics and not a decision procedure...

> He is not a setting.

> The camera does not coordinate the characters.

> Surge pricing is the wrong example here.

> The remedy was disclosure, not reinstatement.

Most of these are useful. In clusters, they give the reader the sense of watching the chapter answer
specialists in the room. Section 5 has the thickest cluster. Moving the film contrast to the opening
will help because the section will start from a positive claim instead of accumulating corrections.

## Part 3 - Line-level revisions (paste-ready)

Add after the Austin/camera/Uber comparison in section 2:

> between them, it is a selector however little it knows. The third question tells what that selector

### 2. Put the camera back into the opening of section 5.

Replace the first paragraph of section 5 with:

> A camera can drop people for good - *Slacker* does it in the first three minutes, twice - and it
> costs the dropped party nothing outside the frame. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take
> you off the street. Uber does not put drivers and riders within reach of each other and leave them to
> it. It chooses which driver gets which ride, and nothing in the design lets the two settle anything
> between themselves. That much has been true since researchers first studied the app.

Then delete the repeated camera sentences from the Cameron paragraph.

### 3. Gloss "subject-access data" and "take rate."

Replace:

> an audit built from drivers pooling their own subject-access data - 1.5 million trips, 258 drivers -
> found that "the price the passenger pays and the fee the driver receives vary independently of each
> other."

with:

> an audit built from drivers pooling the data Uber held about their own trips - 1.5 million trips, 258
> drivers - found that "the price the passenger pays and the fee the driver receives vary
> independently of each other."

Replace:

> Across that sample the median take rate per driver moved from 25 to 29 percent while the mean held at
> 25, the rise concentrated on higher-fare trips.

with:

> Across that sample Uber's share of the fare, its take rate, moved from 25 to 29 percent for the
> median driver while the mean held at 25, the rise concentrated on higher-fare trips.

### 4. Replace "nonterminal."

Replace:

> a summons gets an answer, and the sequence it starts is nonterminal, offering no natural exit.

with:

> a summons gets an answer, and the sequence it starts keeps going until somebody does the work of
> ending it.

### 5. Plain-language the legal posture.

Replace:

> That is a ruling on a preliminary injunction and not a final judgment.

with:

> That ruling came early in the case, before final judgment.

### 6. Cut or compress the Tzioumakis tier paragraph.

Replace the paragraph with:

> The studios did close their specialty divisions, and Yannis Tzioumakis documents it. But his numbers
> do not support the easy story that streamers simply took over after theatrical distribution
> collapsed. Small-film distribution changed shape more than it vanished - and *Slacker*'s own
> position in 1990, with no other bidder and one modest offer taken on a template, looks more like the
> present than like the boom in between.

### 7. Gloss "second window."

Replace:

> The film played three months past Orion's Chapter 11 filing that December, so the second window was
> starved by a division applying a default rather than killed by the bankruptcy.

with:

> The film played three months past Orion's Chapter 11 filing that December, so the home-video window
> was starved by a division applying a default rather than killed by the bankruptcy.

### 8. Make the final card concrete.

Replace:

> on one of the oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it

with:

> on one of the prompt cards a passerby hands out in it

### 9. Lead the film-narrator paragraph with the usable rule.

In section 4, move this sentence to the start of the paragraph that begins "Where I stand is not the
middle":

> When I write "the camera," I mean whatever makes those choices.

Then the Bordwell/Slugan positioning becomes support for a practical usage the reader already
understands.

### 10. Shorten one self-announcing correction.

Replace:

> The difference has to be drawn in the right place, though, because Lindsey Cameron's seven-year study
> of ride-hailing found that...

with:

> Lindsey Cameron's seven-year study of ride-hailing found that...

This works best if the section 5 opening has already made the cut/regime difference explicit.

## Standing author tasks this lens cannot close

- I did not retrieve external sources in this session. I am not asserting factual or citation
  corrections.
- If "oblique strategies" must stay because the object is a specific credited deck, the author should
  supply the short gloss from the source or from the film. A general reader needs one noun of help.
- The Tzioumakis paragraph may carry a field-specific obligation I cannot judge. If it must stay, define
  the tiers in the text or move the technical explanation to a note.

## What this lens is not judging

I am not judging whether the film-theory, sociology, platform-studies, or media-industries citations are
the right authorities. I am not checking the legal or empirical claims against their sources. I am
judging whether a smart reader outside those fields can follow the chapter, feel the film under the
argument, understand the three-question test, and keep reading to the end.
