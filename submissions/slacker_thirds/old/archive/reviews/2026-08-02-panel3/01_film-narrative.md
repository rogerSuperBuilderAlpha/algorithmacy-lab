## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. Bordwell's list is still treated as a concession when it is his argument against a sender

**Verified.** Retrieved the accessible PDF excerpt of Bordwell, *Narration in the Fiction Film*, "Principles of Narration," in this session. The relevant passage reads: "No trait we could assign to an implied author of a film could not more simply be ascribed to the narration itself: it sometimes suppresses information, it often restricts our knowledge, it generates curiosity, it creates a tone, and so on. To give every film a narrator or implied author is to indulge in an anthropomorphic fiction." Two lines later he defines narration as "the organization of a set of cues for the construction of a story" and writes that it "presupposes a perceiver, but not any sender, of a message."

The chapter currently says:

> What he grants in the same account is everything I need. Narration, on his own description, suppresses information, restricts what we know, generates curiosity, sets a tone.

That is too strong and source-unfair. Bordwell grants the functions to narration so he can deny that an implied author, narrator, or sender is needed behind them. The chapter also drops his hedges: "sometimes" suppresses, "often" restricts. A Bordwell reader will stop here.

Paste-ready replacement:

> He grants the selecting and refuses the selector. Narration, on his own description, sometimes suppresses information, often restricts what we know, generates curiosity, and creates a tone; he lists those functions precisely to argue that no implied author or narrator needs to be added behind them.

Then the Chatman sentence can continue.

### 2. The minority-position paragraph needs to pay the price of its own fallback

**Verified in part.** Slugan's Ghent PDF was retrieved. His endnote says the narrator view has "little debate" among film scholars, that Bordwell is "the most prominent exception," and that the dispute is lively among analytic philosophers of film. Thomson-Jones was not retrieved this session, so the quotation from her remains unverified here.

The chapter says:

> So I am siding with a minority inside film studies and taking the fallback the philosophers offer, that a story "is actually told by the author in his or her creation of the work." When I write "the camera," I mean whatever makes those choices.

The move is honest, but it leaves the key term too loose. If the fallback is authorial telling, then the selector in *Slacker* is also Richard Linklater's finished decision. That does not kill the chapter's platform comparison. It sharpens it. The camera's selection is fixed, public, and identical for every viewer; Uber's selection is live, private, and computed party by party. §4 needs that distinction before §5 can lean on it.

Paste-ready replacement:

> I take the philosophers' side, which inside film studies is the minority one: there is no narrator in the film, and a story "is actually told by the author in his or her creation of the work." That has a price, because an author is a person. Linklater fixed the order once, and every viewer since has received the same order. A selector whose choosing is finished, public, and identical for everyone is a different animal from one that runs live and privately, party by party. When I write "the camera," I mean the first kind.

Also change "the prominent exception" to "the most prominent exception." That is Slugan's wording.

### 3. The "string" sentence still lets metaphor do argument

**Unverified for Bordwell 2008.** The *Poetics of Cinema* page itself was not retrieved. Poulaki's UvA thesis was retrieved, and it relays the adjacent Bordwell sentence: "The story action doesn't bring the characters face-to-face, and the narration doesn't bare unexpected connections among them" (her citation is to a 2007 printing, p. 215). The chapter's own quotation of the "wiggly, knotted string" remains plausible but unverified here.

The chapter says:

> His word is string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order.

That asks Bordwell's figure to carry the ordered-walk claim. His "wiggly, knotted string" names a balk at network classification. It is a figure for tangle, not an argument about sequence. The literal claim is stronger without it: *Slacker* has an order in presentation, but that order does not make the characters a network.

Paste-ready replacement:

> Bordwell reaches for *string* because the network he is looking for is not there. What is there instead is a sequence. The film runs two shapes at once.

If the 2008 page is checked, this can be stronger:

> Bordwell reaches for *string* because the network he is looking for is not there. He says so on the same page: "The story action doesn't bring the characters face-to-face, and the narration doesn't bare unexpected connections among them." What is there instead is a sequence. The film runs two shapes at once.

### 4. Poulaki is the right rival, but the answer still does not meet self-organization

**Verified.** Poulaki's thesis was retrieved from UvA-DARE. The "self-reflexive agency of the camera" passage appears in the *Gomorrah* discussion. The catalogue/system sentence appears earlier: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs relations between the elements in order to form itself." The thesis repeatedly treats complex films as systems whose organization emerges from relations among units.

The chapter says:

> Her own floor is the answer: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs relations between the elements in order to form itself." The relations here are the order of the visits, and somebody chose it.

The first sentence is good. The second sentence asserts the point Poulaki would contest. A self-organization account says order can arise from the relations among parts. The answer has to show that *Slacker* has no diegetic machinery capable of producing the order. No character wants the next scene. No object travels. No cause crosses the seam. The sequence is the relation, so it cannot be explained by relations beneath it.

Paste-ready replacement for the paragraph's opening and close:

> Maria Poulaki's is the account that would make a selector unnecessary: a film coheres because its parts form a system, and a system organizes itself. Her version is stranger than that summary.

and:

> Her own minimum for a system is where the answer starts: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs relations between the elements in order to form itself." *Slacker* clears that minimum, because the order of the visits is a relation. What it lacks is anywhere for that order to come from inside the story world. No character wants the next scene, no object travels between them, no cause runs across a cut. A system that organizes itself makes its sequence out of relations among its parts. Here the sequence is the only relation there is, so it arrived from outside.

### 5. The co-presence constraint still overstates after the Gaughen paragraph

**Verified against the chapter.** §3 says Gaughen found a jump of about three miles, "played across a cut as though it were a walk." §4 says:

> The camera can only leave with someone the city has already put within reach of the lens.

The earlier "always" has been softened to "almost every transfer," which is the right direction. This "only" has not been softened. The chapter's own geography says the film can also fake co-presence across a cut. That does not weaken the setting/selector distinction. It gives the distinction a sharper form: the city supplies many proximities; the film can also manufacture a proximity that looks like the same thing.

Paste-ready replacement:

> Usually the camera can only leave with someone the city has already put within reach of the lens. When a cut fakes that reach, it proves the same point from the other side: Austin hosts bodies, and the film manufactures the walk between them.

### 6. §6 overattributes one claim to Smith, Essex, and Bedford

**Verified.** Retrieved the accepted preprint of Smith, Essex, and Bedford, "Are Movies Making Us Smarter?" from UAL Research Online. The chapter's first quoted claim is present in substance and nearly in words: "The cognitive benefits may be specific to the domain of movie narrative comprehension and not transfer to more general applications." The second quoted claim was not found in that text:

> the trials that would settle it "have not been conducted."

The sentence may be the chapter's own inference from the state of the literature. It should not be printed as Smith et al.'s statement unless another source supplies those words.

Paste-ready replacement:

> The default expectation here is against transfer. Smith, Essex and Bedford leave the benefit domain-bound: it "may be specific to the domain of movie narrative comprehension and not transfer to more general applications." The trial that would connect film-editing fluency to algorithm reading still has not been run.

### 7. §1 is now appropriately narrow, with one film-specific task left

The current §1 no longer claims that audiences were never lost. It claims that retrieved critics do not report losing track, while Pierson's thirds keep walkouts and boredom in view. That is the right evidentiary size.

One sentence remains dependent on the film rather than the reviews:

> Of the ninety-eight credited roles, almost none meet twice.

That may be true. It should be checked while the handoff log is made. This lens does not need the sentence cut; it needs a viewing note or a transition log that lets it survive a reader with the disc open.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and AI-slop audit

**Ban 1, metaphor doing argumentative work: one live hit.** The "important property of a string" sentence remains the clear violation. It imports order from Bordwell's metaphor. The ordered-walk claim is literal and should stand on its own.

**Ban 2, throat-clearing: mostly clear.** Earlier throat-clearing has largely been removed. The one nearby substitute is conceptual rather than structural: "What he grants in the same account is everything I need" announces victory before the source has been represented fairly. The fix in Part 1 solves it.

**Ban 3, meta-commentary and forward-backward reference: clear in this lens.** The old "constraint matters later" problem is gone. §4 now mostly proceeds by claim and evidence.

**Ban 4, unexplained jargon: materially improved.** §6 now explains the Kuleshov effect by example, replaces bare "diegetic sound" with "sound that belonged to the world on screen," and explains the 180-degree rule as a line the camera should not cross. Remaining terms are manageable. "self-reflexive agency of the camera" is quoted jargon, but the following clause glosses the operative contrast as describes versus chooses.

**AI-slop audit.** The draft is much cleaner than the prior panel's target. The surviving issue is not machine cadence but a repeated reveal posture in §4: "goes further than," "Where I stand is not the middle," "strongest account against me," "stranger than it looks." Each sentence has a job, but together they make §4 sound more staged than the rest of the chapter. The line fixes above will reduce the pattern without sanding down the voice.
