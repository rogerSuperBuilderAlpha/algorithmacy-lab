# Review — hostile referee / Reviewer 2

**Verdict:** major revisions  
**Target:** `chapter_v3.md` (post-followup; panel 3)

## Step 0 — register

The first person, the direct address to scenes, the contractions, and the author's visible concessions are
part of the chapter's method. They are not defects. This report treats the chapter as a first-person film
essay in the Hansen/Bordwell register and judges whether the argument can survive a skeptical referee.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. Major: the three questions still do not sort the chapter's own cases

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text. No external source is needed.

The current version repairs the bluntest earlier problem by saying:

> "These are diagnostics and not a decision procedure, and they presuppose the two definitions above."

That helps. It does not close the crack. Four lines earlier the chapter still says:

> "Three questions tell them apart."

Both cannot be true in the form printed. If the questions "tell them apart," they must classify the
camera and the landing page. If they only diagnose cases already classified by the definitions, the
definitions are doing the real work and the questions should be demoted openly.

The camera remains the hard case. The chapter says:

> "The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and knows
> nothing about anybody."

That is selector, selector, setting. The chapter then adds:

> "One more thing the questions do not do is demote. A selector that answers no to the third question is
> still a selector..."

This is true by declaration, not by the test. It says Q3 is not classificatory for the camera, after Q3
has just been introduced as one of the three questions that tell settings and selectors apart.

The landing page repeats the problem:

> "A page that chooses what you see next, largely without knowing who you are, is the nearest thing
> streaming has to Linklater's camera."

That object answers the first and third questions like a setting if the relevant coordinatee is the
viewer: it acts on fresh accounts alike, and it does not know who the viewer is. The chapter can call it
a selector, but only because the definition has been broadened to "which one an audience meets next."
That broadened definition is promising. The questions have not been rebuilt around it.

**Why this matters:** the paper's contribution is a distinction and a set of diagnostics. The current
text has a good distinction and weak diagnostics. A hostile reader will say the questions are a local
teaching device, not an analytic instrument. That is survivable only if the chapter says so before the
reader has to discover it.

**Paste-ready repair: replace the paragraph beginning "Three questions tell them apart" through "Uber
picks the pair..." with:**

> Three questions help locate the difference, but the definitions carry the distinction. First: does the
> third act on a whole field, or on a selected path through it? Second: could the encounter have happened
> without this third? Third: does the third know who the parties are and change what it does on that
> basis? Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at most. Uber chooses a
> pair, is the route to that match, and the published evidence for reading the parties sits chiefly on
> the pricing side. The camera is the mixed case that matters here: it chooses the next person the viewer
> meets, but it knows nothing about that person or about the viewer. That does not make it less of a
> selector. It makes it an attentional selector, not a transactional one.

This costs words, but it buys the chapter out of the score-sheet problem.

### 2. Major: the camera coordinates the viewer, and the chapter has not paid the cost of admitting it

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text. No external source is needed.

The best sentence in the current theoretical apparatus is also the one that unsettles the apparatus:

> "The camera does not coordinate the characters. The moon-landing man and his captive coordinate each
> other; the camera coordinates *me*, serially, with one of them after another."

Good. But §2 opens with this premise:

> "A third party can coordinate two people who never deal with each other directly."

That premise fits Uber. It only fits the camera after a shift in object: one of the "two people" is now
a viewer outside the story world, and the other is a character who is not available for social
coordination with that viewer. The relationship is attentional, not social. The chapter knows this:

> "Some choose what an audience sees next... Some choose who does business with whom..."

That distinction arrives late, after the three questions have already done their demonstration work. It
should arrive before the questions or inside them. Otherwise the camera looks like a category error that
the prose has learned to say beautifully.

This also matters for the platform analogy. §5 defines its powers as running:

> "sideways, from a third party across a pair it has itself assembled."

That is not what the camera does. It does not assemble a pair who can bargain, refuse, price, or suffer
sanction. It composes a sequence for a viewer. The chapter should keep the analogy formal and narrow:
camera, landing page, dispatch, and licensing all select, but they select different relations.

**Paste-ready bridge after "Some choose who does business with whom — the taxi dispatcher, and later
Uber":**

> The two jobs should not be collapsed. In the first, the selected relation is between a viewer and a
> presentation. In the second, it is between parties who must live with the transaction. That is why the
> camera can teach the form of selection without sharing the platform's powers.

### 3. Major: the novelty claim is safer, but it still leans on an unverified universal negative

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text for wording. Unverified as a source claim in this
session; Rivera, Feld, Finkel, Halevy, Kalmijn, and Small/Gose were not retrieved here.

The current text has improved substantially. It no longer says nobody has put setting and broker side by
side. It says:

> "Sociologists have reviewed the setting and the broker together before: Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi sort
> the causes of tie formation into assortative, relational, and proximity mechanisms, with Feld and Burt
> in the same reference list. What they sort by is what makes a tie form. What nobody has asked of both is
> the question these three put to them — what does the third know about the two parties it brings
> together?"

This is the right escape route. It is still too exposed. "What nobody has asked" is a universal negative
over at least two literatures, and the note that follows checks only three canonical documents. Three
empty searches do not support "nobody." They support "not in these three statements."

The chapter then weakens itself:

> "Finkel's team draws something close to my distinction inside a single platform, between a site's access
> — exposure to partners you would otherwise not encounter — and its matching, an algorithm selecting
> partners for you. They get there and stop; the distinction stays inside dating."

That concession is good. It means the novelty is not the distinction itself. It is the movement across
film, urban setting, and platform selection, with knowledge as the sorting question. Say that. Do not
make a referee prove that "nobody" is too strong.

**Paste-ready replacement for "What nobody has asked..." through "The disciplinary separation is real
even so":**

> Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi sort these families by what makes a tie form. Finkel's team reaches a close
> version inside online dating, where access and matching name two platform functions. The question this
> chapter adds is different: what does the third know about the parties it joins, and what follows from
> that knowledge? That narrower claim is the one the evidence here can carry. The disciplinary separation
> is still visible in three canonical statements...

This keeps the contribution and removes the invitation to falsify it with one article.

### 4. Major: §5 still calls platform powers "selector" powers when the evidence shows platform powers

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text. External source claims about Uber, Dubal, Binns,
Cameron, and the Amsterdam/Seattle cases were not reverified in this session.

The current §5 is more careful than the earlier version. It now names Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin
and marks the vertical/sideways distinction:

> "They are not the standard list... what follows runs sideways, from a third party across a pair it has
> itself assembled."

That distinction helps. The problem remains that two of the three powers are not entailed by selection.
Pricing a match and removing a party are powers of platform governance, market position, contract, and
law. A selector may hold them. A setting may hold versions of them too. A market can charge stall rent
and expel a vendor. A nightclub door can select and exclude. A bulletin board can charge and ban.

The chapter nearly admits the issue where it says:

> "The evidence for reading sits on the pricing side, which is not the same as the pricing power over
> again. To pay two people differently you have first to tell them apart. What has no evidence behind it
> is reading in order to match."

This is honest, but it undercuts the demonstration in §2:

> "Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both parties."

The chapter cannot first use "reads both parties" to make Uber score cleanly as the paradigm selector,
then later concede that the evidence for reading does not bear on matching. A hostile reader will call
the paradigm case retrofitted.

**Demand:** separate "selection" from "regime." The selector chooses a path or pair. The regime can price,
read, remove, and withhold reasons. Uber is both. The camera is only the first. That is the sharper
contrast.

**Paste-ready replacement for §5's opening paragraph after the first sentence:**

> The selector is only the beginning of the difference. Uber chooses the pair, but the political force of
> the system lies in the regime built around that choice. It can price a match, read parties well enough
> to differentiate them, remove a party, and sometimes withhold the reasons. Those are not the standard
> six functions of algorithmic control that Katherine Kellogg, Melissa Valentine, and Angele Christin
> catalogue from employer to worker. They run sideways across a pair the platform has assembled.

This names the fourth power if §7 needs it and stops pretending all of the later powers are produced by
selection itself.

### 5. Major: §7 still overclaims enclosure and replacement

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text for internal claims. Source details on Bengesser,
Tzioumakis, Amazon, Orion, and Macor were not reverified in this session.

The section title is:

> "Enclosure"

The section does not yet earn the word. It shows narrowing, licensing, prominence, supplier review, and
unappealable decisions. Those are serious. Enclosure requires a stronger claim: an open route closed, a
previously reachable gate made unreachable, or a commons converted into a controlled asset. The current
text gestures toward this but does not state the mechanism.

The first sentence of the streaming comparison is also too strong:

> "What replaced that chain narrows what reaches a viewer twice over, and the two stages are separate."

Two problems follow. First, the section itself says the takeover story is false or at least too easy:

> "What his numbers refuse is the second half of the easy story, that the streamers then took over."

So "replaced" is not safe. Second, the later Amazon paragraph switches the affected party:

> "Here the seat shifts: a landing page selects among titles for a viewer, and what follows is the
> supplier's side of the same platform..."

That sentence is useful because it names the shift. It also exposes the splice. The landing page is the
camera analogue. The supplier portal is the Uber analogue. They sit on the same platform, but they are
not the same relation. The chapter should present that as the point, not pass through it as a transition.

The closing sentence also reintroduces a fixed problem:

> "...put within reach by a cheap and legible city..."

§3 now says:

> "Austin was not cheap..."

The final paragraph cannot call the city cheap after the evidence has been revised away from cheapness.
It should say slack.

**Paste-ready replacement for §7's first streaming paragraph opening:**

> The later chain does not simply replace the old one. It puts two platform decisions where several named
> people once stood. Licensing decides what can be found. Prominence decides what is likely to be found.

**Paste-ready replacement for the final sentence's last clause:**

> ...put within reach by a slack and legible city, and shown to us one at a time by a camera that took
> nothing from them except a day, and gave the day back watchable.

### 6. Serious: §6 now makes the darker argument, but the chapter should own the narrowing

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text. Source claims about the film-cognition literature
were not reverified in this session.

The strongest improvement in the current draft is the ending of §6 and §7. The chapter no longer claims
that cinema trained viewers to contest platforms. It says:

> "Whether that skill transfers to reading an algorithm is unknown in a specific sense: nobody has run the
> study."

And later:

> "What that century taught is a competence at receiving an arrangement... and none of it is a competence
> at answering back."

This is the right argument. It is also narrower than the accepted abstract's line that cinema trained
viewers to read engineering thirds for a century. The abstract is off-limits as a defect, but the body
should show that it has narrowed the claim. The chapter can say cinema trains reception of selection,
not contestation of it. That is sharper and less grand.

**Paste-ready sentence for the end of §6, after the Heiland paragraph:**

> Cinema supplies a habit of reception, not a politics of reply.

This sentence is short, but here the shortness earns its place.

### 7. Serious: the Feld/PNAS point is still hostage to a source the note says was not opened

**Status:** verified against the current chapter text for the disclosure. The Feld article was not
retrieved in this session.

Note 18 now says:

> "I quote Feld's definition at second hand: the article is closed access and I have not been able to read
> it."

Good. But the body still says:

> "Feld's foci are not people."

That is load-bearing because it makes Rosenfeld, Thomas, and Hausen's opening paragraph look like a
category mistake. The note's disclosure means the body should be less categorical until Feld is read.
The prior synthesis says the p. 1016 definition may not decide whether a focus can be a person. If that
remains true, the PNAS paragraph should shift away from "Feld's foci are not people" and toward the safer
point: their figures measure human intermediaries and do not measure the new non-human intermediary.

**Paste-ready safer version:**

> Feld's foci are the organised settings around which activities gather. Friends and family may help make
> such settings work, but every figure in the paper measures a human third, which leaves the arrival of a
> non-human one untouched.

Use only after Feld is checked. Until then, mark the sentence as a claim under verification.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and AI-slop audit

### Ban 1 — metaphor doing argumentative work

**Hits.**

1. **"Enclosure" as a section title.** The section has not yet defined enclosure or shown closure of a
previously open route. The title carries more argument than the body proves.

2. **"its oldest form" in §4.**

   > "*Slacker*'s camera is the first kind in its oldest form..."

   This implies a lineage from camera to landing page. The chapter has shown analogy, not historical
   descent. Replace with "in a simple form" or cut the phrase.

3. **"one route across a field" in §7.**

   > "When its choices stop being one route across a field the parties could cross by themselves..."

   At the point where the chapter defines the threshold between setting and selector, the route/field
   figure should not do the work. Use the literal language from §2: "when it stops supplying reachability
   and becomes the route."

**Clears.** "Daisy chain" is a cited term of art. "Dérive" is glossed immediately. "Chain" in the opening
of §7 is mostly literal because the paragraph names telephoned people in sequence, though it should not
be asked to prove enclosure.

### Ban 2 — throat-clearing

Most of the loud throat-clearing has been fixed. The remaining cases are smaller:

- "What I am claiming is narrower than it first looks..." The paragraph can open on Rivera directly.
- "One more thing the questions do not do is demote." This announces a defense of the test. The defense
  should be integrated into the test paragraph.
- "The strongest account against me is Maria Poulaki's..." This is acceptable in the author's register,
  but the paragraph then spends several sentences explaining why the account is not actually against the
  argument. Consider "Maria Poulaki gives the camera agency directly..." and proceed.

### Ban 3 — meta-commentary / forward-backward reference

The worst forward references are gone. Two backward/meta moves remain:

- "What it adds instead is a fourth thing the three questions do not reach." This is backward reference
  plus enumeration repair in the same sentence. If "withholding reasons" is a fourth platform power, name
  it in §5 and let §7 cash it out.
- "The cost of leaving it there shows up in *PNAS*." This is elegant, but it narrates the move before
  making it. The paragraph can begin with Rosenfeld, Thomas, and Hausen.

### Ban 4 — unexplained jargon

The draft is much cleaner here. Remaining terms likely to snag a cross-disciplinary reader:

- **"subject-access data"** in §5. Gloss as "data drivers obtained about their own trips."
- **"take rate"** in §5. Gloss as "Uber's share of the fare."
- **"assortative matching"** is now glossed as "the tendency of like to pair with like." Clear.
- **"Kuleshov montages"** are now explained in the sentence. Clear.
- **"diegetic sound"** and the 180-degree rule have been removed or glossed. Clear.

### AI-slop audit

The chapter is not slop. It has a voice. The remaining problem is defensive cadence in the back half:

- The current draft still leans on "rather than" when it wants to show care: "market size rather than
  density," "describes rather than chooses," "rather than the act of matching," "changed shape more than
  it shrank," "contestable rather than proprietary," and several others. Keep the ones that carry real
  contrasts. Convert the decorative ones to direct claims.
- The self-narrating rigor is reduced but not gone: "I am not claiming it as one," "What I am claiming is
  narrower," "I will assert the differential pay..." Some of these are useful because the chapter is
  arguing against overclaim. The §1 and §2 versions can be cut or folded into the evidence.
- The short landing sentences still cluster, but less mechanically than before. The strongest are earned:
  "Nobody is owed a return" should stay. The final "Both kinds..." sentence also works. Trim weaker
  short verdicts elsewhere instead of flattening the chapter.

## Standing author tasks this lens cannot close

1. **Rivera, Soderstrom, and Uzzi 2010.** Read the body, not only the abstract or reference list. The
   chapter's novelty claim depends on what their review does and does not ask.

2. **Feld 1981, p. 1016.** Decide whether the body can keep "Feld's foci are not people." The current
   note discloses second-hand use, which is good, but the body still rests a category correction on it.

3. **Finkel et al. 2012.** Check whether "they get there and stop" is fair. The chapter needs Finkel as
   prior art, not as a straw concession.

4. **Bengesser, Tzioumakis, Amazon Prime Video Direct.** Verify the exact scope of the streaming claims
   before keeping "enclosure." If the stronger closure fact is not added, retitle §7.

5. **Disc pass.** A hostile reader will ask how many handoffs actually work by bodily crossing, by object
   transfer, by cut, and by coincidence. The chapter still relies on "about three dozen" and "almost every
   transfer." A timecoded appendix note or private count would harden §4.

## What this lens is not judging

This review does not judge Chicago note hygiene, bibliography sorting, or DOI completeness. It does not
reverify court decisions, census figures, Amazon pages, Uber studies, or film-cognition experiments. It
also does not object to the chapter's first person, its film-forward register, or its concessions. The
problem is architectural: the chapter has a good distinction, but the diagnostic questions, the camera
case, and the platform payoff still do not line up tightly enough for publication.
