## 1. Whole-paper argument map

The chapter argues five things, in this order.

1. *Slacker* has no protagonist, no plot, and almost no character who meets another character
   twice, and audiences in 1991 followed it comfortably anyway. Something other than story held it
   together.
2. Two different things coordinate the people in this film. Austin puts strangers within reach of
   each other and supplies the conventions that let one address another, without arranging any
   particular meeting. The camera chooses which person the viewer sees next. Three questions tell
   the two apart: does it act on everyone or on two particular people; could the encounter have
   happened without it; does it know who these people are and adjust accordingly.
3. The second kind of coordinator is the one a platform occupies. Uber chooses which driver gets
   which ride, and the parties never negotiate with each other. What Uber adds beyond what the
   camera does is the whole political difference: it reads the people it matches, prices each
   match, and can remove a party from the pool.
4. A 1991 audience could follow a film built entirely out of such choices because reading a cut is
   a learned skill and a century of filmmaking taught it. That much is measured. Whether the same
   skill transfers to reading an algorithm is not measured, and the chapter says so.
5. The distribution chain that carried *Slacker* to viewers was made of people who could be
   argued with. The chain that carries films now is a platform that decides what gets bought and
   licensed before any viewer sees a recommendation. The film is worth this much attention because
   it is one of the few places where both kinds of coordination happen in the open, where an
   audience can watch them.

## 2. Terminology conversion

| Current | v3 | Definition to state literally on first use |
|---|---|---|
| hosting third | **setting** | A setting puts people within reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one approach another. It arranges no particular meeting. |
| engineering third | **selector** | A selector chooses. It picks which particular parties come together, or which particular one an audience meets next. |
| the third / structural third | *retained, glossed* | A third party coordinates two others who never deal with each other directly. Simmel's term; explain it, don't lean on it. |
| the two faces of the engineering third | **two kinds of selector** | Some selectors choose what you look at (the camera, the recommendation feed). Others choose who you do business with (the taxi dispatcher, Uber). |
| the three questions / three tests | **three questions**, asked plainly | Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could the encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what it does accordingly? |
| structural hole (Burt) | *retained, glossed* | Two people who would each gain from what the other knows, and who are not connected. Whoever connects them profits from the connection. |
| thick market (Duranton & Puga) | *replaced by its own gloss* | Put enough people in one place and each person's chance of a good match improves, without anyone proposing a match. |
| brokerage | *retained, glossed* | Standing between two parties and doing something to their relationship. |

The architectural resolution from 2026-07-03 (Option 3, the doubling) survives the rename and gets
easier to state: the objection was that the camera coordinates the viewer, not the characters, and
so looks unlike Uber. In the new terms the answer needs no figure. Selectors do two different jobs.
Some choose what an audience sees. Some choose who transacts with whom. *Slacker*'s camera does the
first; Uber does the second; the recommendation feed does the first at industrial scale. The
chapter's subject is the first, and the second is where the comparison's stakes show.

**Cost of the rename, for the editor query.** The accepted abstract uses "hosting" and "engineering"
five times between them. An editor holding the abstract will not find those words in the chapter.
§1 needs one sentence tying the two vocabularies together, and the submission needs a note.

## 3. Section by section

### §1 — The coherence puzzle (v2 lines 5–51, ~950 words)

**Claim.** *Slacker* refuses every device that normally makes a film followable, audiences followed
it anyway, and the existing explanations name what came loose without naming what held.

**Moves.**
1. Linklater's taxi monologue, and the fact that the film abandons him (lines 7–17).
2. The refusal, stated: ~100 characters, no protagonist, no return, no convergence; production and
   reception facts; Canby and Ebert (lines 19–26).
3. The puzzle sharpened: audiences were not lost (lines 28–29).
4. The rival answers — Rosenbaum, Pierson, the 1991 lineage critics, Stone (lines 31–39).
5. Macor's "least auteur film ever made" set beside Rosenbaum's tight structure, to get from
   *person* to *position* (lines 41–46).
6. The chapter's answer, announced (lines 48–51).

**Evidence audit.**

| Rosenbaum 2001 | The strongest rival: structure is there, and it's a portrait of chaos | No — must be answered |

### §2 — The third that coordinates (v2 lines 53–91, ~800 words)

**Claim.** A third party can coordinate two others who never deal with each other directly, and it
can do so in two different ways, distinguishable by three questions.

**Moves.**
1. Simmel: adding a third position changes the form (lines 55–60).
2. Brokerage after Simmel: Burt, Obstfeld, and the shared assumption that a broker works on a pair
   (lines 62–66).
3. The other way: Feld's focus, renamed *setting* (lines 68–74).
4. The three tests, and the concession that these are corners of a space, not a binary (76–83).
5. Neighbours defused: Oldenburg's third place, Lofland (lines 85–89).
6. The claim that the film holds both (line 91).

**Evidence audit.**

| Source | Job | Survives removal? |
|---|---|---|
| Simmel 1908/1950 | The origin of the third position | No |
| Burt 1992 | Brokerage as profit from an unconnected pair | No |
| Obstfeld 2005 | The friendlier broker; shows the pair assumption is general, not Burt's quirk | Yes, but cheaply |
| Gould & Fernandez 1989; Stovel & Shaw 2012 (note 13) | The formal typologies; all five mediation types are pair-brokering, which is what makes the setting an unoccupied cell | No — this note carries the novelty claim |
| Feld 1981 | Names the mechanism the chapter calls a setting | No |
| Small 2009 (note 13a) | The nearest rival; treats settings as brokers, which the chapter must distinguish itself from | No |
| Oldenburg 1989 | Defuses the "third place" collision | Yes, but the collision is real and a reader will make it |
| Lofland 1973, 1998 | Closest predecessor on method | No |
| Anderson 2011 (note 15) | Related bounded-setting work | Yes |

**Weak points.** This is where the panels did the most damage and the record must be respected.
Feld 1981 pre-names the mechanism, so the chapter cannot claim to have found it — the surviving
claim is that the setting has never been set beside the broker and held to one test. Small 2009
narrows it further: he treats settings as brokers, folding the two together, where the chapter
holds them apart. The formulation that survived the July purge is "never quite did… hold them
apart." The v3 rebuild must not let the rename quietly re-inflate this claim, and §2's deep research
has an explicit falsification probe for exactly that.

Second weak point: the three tests must be applied visibly to the cases, or they are ornament. In
v2 they are stated once and never run on anything except in passing. The rebuild states each
question and answers it for Austin, the camera, the club door, Uber, and the feed.

**Ban inventory.**

| Quote | Fault | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| "sociology has a name for the mechanism if not for the seat" | Metaphor ("seat") + meta-commentary | "Scott Feld named the mechanism in 1981." |
| "What the broker literature never quite did was… That's the move here." | Meta-commentary | State the distinction; let it be the move |
| "an empty space between two disconnected people is worth money to whoever stands in it" | Metaphor standing in for the definition | Define a structural hole literally, then say what Burt showed |
| "Those are two corners of a larger space, not a tidy binary" | Spatial metaphor doing conceptual work; antithesis | "Neither is a pure type. A matchmaker picks pairs and reads people but is easy to go around." |
| "before long we'll meet a feed that reads everyone while brokering no one" | Forward reference / roadmap | Delete; introduce the feed where it arrives |
| "because a seat is the one thing you can also put a camera in" | Metaphor carrying the argument | Dies. The literal point: the same position can be held by a person, an institution, or a machine — including a camera |
| "Others have mapped nearby ground." | Metaphor + vague attribution | Name Oldenburg and Lofland |
| "I'll call the setting-as-third a *host*, and the pair-brokering third an *engineer*." | The apparatus being replaced | The two definitions, plainly |

**Verification debt.** Simmel's Wolff page range is a standing LIBRARY CHECK. Small's treatment
of settings was verified against the OUP/author page in July, not against the book — the deep
research re-checks it. Gould & Fernandez's five roles need the gloss the network reviewer asked
for: two of the five turn on the two parties' co-membership, not the broker's.

### §3 — Austin hosts (v2 lines 93–137, ~800 words)

**Claim.** Austin in 1990 made encounters between strangers possible in bulk, by density,
convention, and cheap rent, and arranged none of them.

**Moves.**
1. Two scenes that play as plausible: the moon-landing monologist, the pap-smear seller (95–99).
2. The mechanism: Lofland's appearances-and-locations, the cost of closing an opened encounter,
   the exclusion caveat, Milgram's familiar stranger (101–111).
3. The material base: the 1986 oil crash, rents, the 1990 census, the university (113–119).
4. The economics: agglomeration as improved odds without proposals (121–123).
5. Two facts almost too good: the Live Music Capital resolution eight weeks after release; "Keep
   Austin Weird" as resistance to enclosure (125–129).
6. The complication: Gaughen's geography — the film's Austin is not a real walk (131–137).

**Evidence audit.**

| Source | Job | Survives removal? |
|---|---|---|
| Lofland 1973 | The core mechanism: a city orders strangers by appearance and location | No |
| Goffman 1963 | Civil inattention, cited to say the opposite is at work here | No — but the citation is doing negative work and must stay precise |
| Milgram 1977 | The familiar stranger as resting state; crisis as what breaks it | Yes, but it earns its line, and the film opens on a body in the street |
| Texas Tribune 2020; Boyd 2014; Long 2010 | The 1986 crash, the $515→$350 rent, the milieu | No |
| TSLAC 1990 census | 465,622; scale | Yes |
| Duranton & Puga 2004 | Agglomeration: better odds, no proposals — the setting's defining property | No |
| KUT 2016 | The Live Music Capital resolution date | Yes, but it is the section's best fact |
| Gaughen 2014 | The geography is fictional; the city hosts the milieu, not the map | No |
| Linklater 1991 | "a 100 percent Austin product" | Yes |

**Weak points.** The urban reviewer's standing objection is unresolved and the author was asked to
decide it: is the setting a neutral improver of odds, or a sorting mechanism? The draft handles it
in one sentence ("the same code that lets a white twenty-something pitch Madonna's pap smear on the

"hosting" launders exclusion as ambience. In v3 the word "setting" makes this sharper, not softer:
a setting that supplies conventions supplies conventions about who may speak to whom. This is a
real gap in the argument, not a political add-on, and the rebuild should spend words on it. The
July triage also parked "a mile east the film hosts no one at all" as unverified; the current
hedge is "the film scarcely goes a mile east."

### §4 — The camera engineers (v2 lines 139–211, ~1,200 words)

### §6 — Cinema as training (v2 lines 260–316, ~950 words)

**Claim.** Reading a cut is a learned skill, filmmakers spent a century tuning cuts to how attention
works, and by 1991 that skill could carry a film with no plot underneath it. Whether the same skill
helps someone read an algorithm is unknown.

**Moves.**

2. Benjamin's training claim and its hopeful valence; Hansen's negotiation (265–274).
3. Kuleshov, honestly stated; Ildirar & Ewing's first-time viewers (276–282).
4. Smith's eye-tracking: continuity editing rides attention; edit blindness (284–288).
5. *Slacker* strips the alibi; Linklater on channel-surfing (290–295).
6. The film's own media theorist, the Video Backpacker (297–300).
7. Beller set aside (302–305).
8. The limit: the transfer is unproven, and what would settle it (307–311).

## 4. Cross-cutting findings

**The prose argues by figure more than the panels registered.** Both panels were hunting AI slop
and both certified the body clean by that standard — antithesis 2.2–2.6 per 1,000 words, no fillers,
healthy rhythm. They were not asked whether a metaphor was standing where a claim should be, and
several of the sentences they explicitly *protected* are exactly the ones the new bans remove: the
§3→§4 hinge epigrams, the gerund cluster ("the taking, the carrying, the welding") cleared as
"thesis-enacting signature," "one seat, three occupants," and the "almost too good, but they're
true" aside cleared as register. The rebuild overrides those clearances, which is the point of
rebuilding.

**Count.** 41 items in the ban inventory above: 14 metaphors carrying argumentative weight, 11
throat-clears, 10 meta-comments, 6 antithesis-machine constructions. Four terms need a plain
definition they currently lack: structural hole, thick market, brokerage, apparatus.

**What the rename actually buys.** Three of the chapter's hardest moments get easier to state
literally: the two-kinds-of-selector resolution, the dependency of the camera on the city, and the
enclosure claim in §7. One gets harder: §2's novelty, which was partly carried by the act of naming
a cell nobody had named. With "setting" as a plain word rather than a coinage, the novelty must rest
entirely on the three questions and on holding the setting apart from the broker against Small. That
is the honest place for it to rest.

**What must not be lost in the rebuild.** The concessions. Feld pre-names the mechanism. Small
folds setting into broker. The camera coordinates the viewer, not the characters. Kuleshov is
contested. The transfer from cut-reading to algorithm-reading is unmeasured. Viewers don't trust
recommendations. Cinema's aims were economic too. Every one of these was won by a reviewer catching
an overclaim, and each one makes the chapter better. A rebuild from research is exactly where they
would quietly disappear.
