# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

The film opens on a face that talks for a stretch and then is gone, and another face arrives. In 2011 Richard Linklater told Marc Savlov he saw that drift as "either channel surfing or … going in and out of different movies," a "primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."[^1] Maria Teresa Soldani makes the resemblance a matter of form. *Slacker*, she writes, is a twenty-four-hour urban narration whose camera "drifts along the street space, from character to character and from place to place."[^2] What interests me in that sentence is less the single drift than the two jobs inside it: the city holds the next face somewhere on the sidewalk, and the camera chooses which one we see.

A platform does that choosing without a face: it holds strangers available to one another and arranges none of their meetings. It is a self-less third, and still an agent — it charges, lists, refuses, withholds a reason. Whether that agent is a person or an algorithm I leave open. Georg Simmel named a third that never comes between an identified pair, then postponed the analysis. Josiah Royce defined community so that membership waits for each person's yes. Charles Reich described access held as a grant, issued and taken back. Linklater films a day in which those three descriptions are true of one sidewalk: a city that holds without introducing, a shared day nobody claims, access still spoken to a person who can answer.

In 2020 David Stark and Ivana Pais described platform owners who co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^3] *Slacker* already has contribution without a contract on the street, and a third that still has a face. The day lets us watch people argue with that third. I am using algorithmacy here as a name for fluency with determinations that arrive without reasons and without anyone to answer them, although I cannot develop that fluency here.[^4] What the film can show is the competence those platforms replaced: how to argue with a door, copy a stamp, say a join out loud.

It is not my intention to contest Bordwell's warning against treating multi-protagonist films as symptoms of the internet; network narratives do solve storytelling problems and predate the technologies invoked to explain them.[^5] My interest is rather in what the film still makes visible. The arrangement it films was already on the sidewalk, and the platforms later ran it without a face.

## Philosophical Origins in Simmel's Postponement and Royce's Canoe

The film opens on a taxi and a woman struck in the street, and what they share is a city. Simmel had a name for a third that never stands between a known pair, and he set the analysis aside.

In his triad chapter the non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties. The *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits by standing between them. *Divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets them against one another in order to rule both.[^6] Each already knows the pair. On the opening page of that same chapter he writes of a third that unifies without standing between anyone, then sets the thought aside. Kurt Wolff translates the observation as "it is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them." He offers two examples: an alliance against a common enemy, and the invisible church, which joins all believers through their equal relation to the one God. Then comes the postponement: "The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context."[^7]

He sets it aside because of distance. The third stands so far from the other two that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three alike. What remains are pairs: either the relation among those who join, or the relation between them as a unit and the outside center of interest. God and two believers do not constitute one three-way field, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. The chapter requires three elements close enough to form a group, so the distant case falls outside its concern. I will call what he set aside the deferred third: a third so far off that it never comes between an identified pair. Simmel's examples unify — an enemy, a God — and Austin does not: it holds the man in from the bus station and the people standing around a woman struck in the street in one city, and it introduces nobody. I take the postponement as an ancestor of that distance.

A more specific usage of the postponed phrase occurs at that one place in the 1908 *Soziologie*. When the theme returns some two hundred pages later, in the chapter on conflict, the very distance that had been his ground for deferring becomes a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them turns out to be an especially favorable constellation for the bond. Both of the original examples come back on that page: the league formed against a common enemy, and the God whose infinite remoteness is exactly what lets him gather the most heterogeneous peoples into one church. Simmel indexed the passage himself, and the alliance half recurs a dozen pages earlier still.[^8] If the former return is a promise kept, the latter is a third that never returns as a third. The theme comes back in the dyadic register the postponement had itself predicted: unification described from the side of the parties who are unified, with the distant unifying element analyzed as a condition of their bond rather than as an element of a triad.

Christoph Neuberger has taken that postponed mediating function to journalism and to platforms. Network-brokerage research took the pair-brokering thirds from the triad chapter instead. Ronald Burt's structural-hole broker and David Obstfeld's *tertius iungens*, the third who joins, each know which two parties they stand between. The integrative review that defines the field treats broker and brokered as network actors, and it excludes non-human networks and networks outside a work or professional context.[^9] Austin's sidewalk and Linklater's cut fall outside those instruments before any measurement begins, and the taxi is already moving before anyone in *Slacker* has met anyone.

The question of how the holding becomes membership returns us to Royce. In *The Problem of Christianity* he asks what makes a community out of people who need not have met, and he answers with a definition. A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation by the same relation to shared future events.[^10] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^11] He permits aloofness, enmity, and the absence of any meeting. What he does not permit is a community of people who have not each, individually, claimed the same past. Royce lists three conditions, and the scholarship reads them as cumulative: the self's power to extend itself ideally in time; a plurality of distinct selves capable of social communication and in general engaged in it; and at least one identical event included in the ideally extended lives of all.[^12] Without the second condition there is no community at all. Acceptance is written into the definition itself, which a day of unclaimed strangers cannot meet.

Simmel gives the distance: a third that does not enter the pair. Royce gives the membership that distance refuses — no yes, no community. Reich gives the form the distance takes when a face still has to administer it, a grant spoken at a door. One afternoon, three refusals of the same arrangement: nobody introduced, nobody claimed, nobody given a reason, and somebody still there to be asked. The film moves from a taxi and a body to a door to a missing friend.

## 'You seen Gary around?' – The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

I begin with a stretch that takes us back to the first five minutes, where the deferred third is already the holding. I am using selection here as a name for the cut's choosing of the next face, a job the city does not do. Linklater himself, credited as Should have stayed at bus station, rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a silent driver. His subject is a dream in which nothing happened, and then a theory that every unchosen path continues somewhere as its own reality. At the station he had almost skipped the cab, and in that other life a woman picks him up and he moves in with her. The driver never answers and he never seems to notice. He gets out on "Man, shit. I should've stayed at the bus station."[^13]

Location notes put the drop on 24th Street, between San Antonio and Nueces, near the Castilian, and say the passenger witnesses a hit-and-run there.[^14] What the stretch still shows, even if he walks into the next street, is that Austin holds both beats and introduces nobody. A famous case in point is the stretch after the drop, in which a woman lies struck, a jogger jogs in place and barks at no one to touch her, somebody goes for help, and a man who has pulled over asks whether he is needed, hears that he is not, and presses his card on the jogger anyway — "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it" — before driving off ahead of the police. The camera then leaves the body and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother.

In the taxi the man had said there is always someone getting run over. The next stretch of the film is a son who has run over his mother. The rhyme is in the lines, and the city holds both without introducing them.

Down at the Blue Bayou on South Congress a man credited as S-T-E-V-E with a van has spent the afternoon telling a friend about the night ahead: his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he had made the roommate write. At the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them. The doorman charges him three dollars. He spells his way onto a list he believes he owns, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and is refused on it. The door answers, "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you," and Steve supplies the gloss: "Fuck. I'm not on the list."

Steve is refused, and the only reason the door will give is that it does not see him on its list. Wealth of the modern kind, Charles Reich argued in 1964, is increasingly held not as property but as largess, a grant, whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^15] The Blue Bayou's door does that work in miniature. It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum, and will not say why the list Steve believes he owns does not have him. I am using four powers here as a technical list — price, evaluate, remove, withhold the further reason — that is this chapter's, not Reich's. It is not my intention to rest the case on his closing demand; I lean on his diagnosis of the dependence, not on his list. Reich also demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated." The door never reaches that sentence. The demand stays a hope appended to the description.[^16]

The film adds the face to that diagnosis. Later, at another door, a man offers a hand stamp — "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in" — and copies it by licking wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that does not stop anyone, and the two of them walk in free. They can copy the stamp because a stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers. The guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it. Later at closing someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it — "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Even that peremptory sentence is addressed to people who could in principle argue back. The platforms that inherit this arrangement keep the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason. They drop both the person who can state the rule and the stamp that strangers could copy among themselves.

Gary is missing, and nobody in the room can answer for him. In a café later identified as Captain Quackenbush's on the Drag, a passerby breaks in to ask after a man who is not there — "You seen Gary around?" — and nobody has seen him for months, and the asker leaves without an answer. Outside, a man who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited. The asker heads off toward a friend's place, and the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed before stopping a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" and then waving him along — "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." But Gary never appears. The sidewalk had held both men within reach of one another and the cut selected their meeting, and no one introduced them.

They are contemporaries, and they are in communication, so Royce's second condition holds on the street in a way it does not hold for most of the people the film shows us. The third condition fails, and the acceptance built into it. Neither man takes Gary's absence into his own life as the same past event. If the former is looking for a friend, the latter converts the absence into evidence for a conspiracy. The day they share remains a day neither of them claims. Sharing a city is not sharing a past. On Royce's own terms these people are not members of anything, and Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

Through the rest of the day the holding keeps working, and two kinds of join take turns. Early on the cut places the taxi and then the body in one sequence for us. A missing-friend question pulls a conspiracy into the frame. A friend breaking off a voting rant to say that Paul has vanished follows it with "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle of the day the people on screen close meetings themselves, with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at a door, a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud: shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Those are people using the city's reach. The cut still chooses which of those uses we watch. The doorman, the same afternoon, administers a grant — a list, a refusal, a face — which is not the camera's job and not the city's holding either. By dawn the cut is joining scenes again, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a camera thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell. When the cut is doing that joining there is nothing handed over and nobody addressed.

The same day shows labor without a contract, such as kids hawking Cokes, a man selling T-shirts, and a hitchhiker coming back from his stepfather's funeral who takes a lift into town, says the man "was a serious fuck-up," and asks for a cigarette. He has nothing to say about work at all. Outside Les Amis a different man, credited as Hitchhiker awaiting "true call," tells a camera crew he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and when they ask what that is he says he will know it when he hears it. The city holds him within reach and hires him for nothing. He withholds the specification, and he offers that withholding to people standing there to be asked. Stark and Pais call the later version co-optation without delegated authority. On the sidewalk in 1990 there is always somebody standing there to be asked. Contribution without employment, with a person still available to answer for it, is still legible.

Amazon's Prime Video licensing page says that, unless otherwise indicated, removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection sits at the company's sole discretion, and that it "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^17] Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus, surveying 810 California drivers, found two-thirds reporting deactivation and thirty percent of those given no reason at all.[^18] Drivers contribute without being hired, as the people at that party did, and the counterparty that removes them has removed anyone who could be argued with. A famous case in point is the Video Backpacker, who explains that a video image is more powerful than an actual event because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind," can't pause it, can't fix the hue. A driver cannot replay an allocation once the queue has moved on. Steve's list failed in public and the group renegotiated on the sidewalk. An app assigns a queue position whether or not the driver assents to it, and the inclusion waits for nobody's yes. As early as 1992, Reuters Dealing 2000-2 and then EBS were matching professional traders without themselves buying or selling anything, and later rulebooks still give reasons only if asked.[^19][^20]

Because Royce makes acceptance constitutive, the app's assignment is not membership. Calling it one would spend his term twice and in opposite senses. Salomé Viljoen describes the mechanism: a horizontal, population-based data relation includes a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he has in common with others rather than anything he has claimed.[^21] Enrollment of that kind is the platform's answer to Royce's question, and the answer is that it has stopped asking. Rideshare Drivers United exists because drivers organized to compel an answer from a third that will not give one.

## Reach, Selection, and the Refusal to Resolve

If, however, we consider the film's division of labor between reach and selection, the city and the cut do not swap jobs. People use the city's reach: a lift, an address, a stamp. The doorman administers a grant inside that arrangement. That is not the cut taking the city's job.

In 1981 Scott Feld asked how people come to know one another without a matchmaker. His answer was the focus: a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized (a workplace, a voluntary organization, a hangout, a family), such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^22] Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organized. But the people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster. The shared activity that produces Feld's ties is missing, and what remains is the bare holding I have been calling reach. Sarah Barns describes digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange with "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom, which is nearer to selection, and to what the camera does when it chooses the next face.[^23] The city supplies the one, the cut supplies the other. The door, the same afternoon, still has to say the grant out loud.

Michael Warner's account of a public constitutes strangers as a collectivity precisely through their common relation to something outside them, with no interaction among them required and membership arriving by mere attention.[^24] Warner's public is addressed. It does not have someone choosing which two of its members are put in front of each other next. That choosing is the cut's job, and I have been calling it selection. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga already split match quality from match probability in their account of what cities do for the people in them, a precedent for treating reach and matching as separable.[^25] They split the same two functions in economic form, and the film shows them performed by visibly different agents.

But what about the questions the film will not close? Linklater does away with the closure a film of about a hundred people would demand. To produce Gary, or to let the man at Les Amis specify the true call, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk, would be to supply from outside precisely the introductions the arrangement does not make. The film's recurring question — asked of Gary, and of Paul, and implicitly of everyone who leaves the frame and does not return — is a question the city is not built to answer. A door still has to say the rule out loud, and a camera still has to choose the next face without telling anyone why.

## Conclusion

The taxi and the struck woman share a city, and Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away, and give no further reason. Gary is asked after and never produced. Linklater leaves those questions hanging so that the form of the arrangement stays in view. Simmel describes the distance, Royce the membership test, Reich the grant, Stark and Pais the co-optive enrollment; none of them has to name the leftover the day still shows, a third someone can still argue with.

The platform keeps the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason, and withdraws the person who had to say them out loud. Now that the face sits behind a rule that does not have to answer, we can still hear the people on screen ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
