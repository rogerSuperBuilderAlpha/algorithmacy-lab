# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

This chapter sets out to read Richard Linklater's *Slacker* (1990) as a film about a third that holds strangers available to one another and does not arrange who meets whom. The story, such as it is, is one day in Austin, Texas, among roughly a hundred people. Someone talks for a stretch and is gone, and the next face is simply whoever the camera finds, with no plot that collects them and no character who walks us from one to the next. The twin aspects of that day, the city that holds people in one another's way and the camera that chooses which of them we see next, form the basis of this chapter's investigations.

I hope to show that this arrangement was already thinkable, in pieces, before anyone shot the film. Georg Simmel, in 1908, saw that isolated people can be unified by their common relation to something outside them, a third so distant that it does not stand between a known pair. He thought the insight sociologically significant, and then postponed it. Josiah Royce, five years later, asked when strangers count as members of a community. Sharing a relation to an outside past counts only if each person takes that past as part of a life, he said; they may keep aloof, or even be enemies. Charles Reich, in 1964, described access held as a grant, which a grantor can issue and take back. What Linklater shoots is a day in which those three problems are still visible: a city that unifies without introducing, a shared day nobody takes as theirs, and access still spoken to a person who can answer.

David Stark and Ivana Pais, writing in 2020, give us a later vocabulary for the ground that day was breaking. Platform owners, they argue, co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^1] Nobody had that description in 1990. Their terms help us see what the film already shows: people contributing without a contract, a third that still has a face. Co-optation is their 2020 verb.

Scott Feld, writing in 1981 on the focused organization of social ties, was interested in how people come to know one another without a matchmaker. A focus, he says, is a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized: a workplace, a voluntary organization, a hangout, a family. Because people share the activity, they "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster." The shared activity produces the ties.[^2] Austin in *Slacker* looks, at first, like that kind of entity, a place around which a day of talking and walking is organized. The people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster. I will call that weaker holding reach: the city keeps them available to one another without the joint activity that, for Feld, produces the tie.

A focus, even a strong one, still does not choose which two people meet. The cluster forms among whoever shares the activity. Sarah Barns, looking at digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange, writes of "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom. That is closer to what the camera does in *Slacker* when it chooses the next face, and I will call that choosing selection.[^2]

The camera's choosing is a philosophical problem. Royce held that interpretation is always a relation among three terms, and that all three are selves. The camera that finds the next face takes one person's situation as its object and addresses a viewer who never meets them. The camera occupies the interpreter's place. Royce required a self there. The grant Reich described, access a person can issue and take back, is still attached in this film to a face: someone who can charge a cover, check a list, turn a person away, and give no reason.

## Philosophical Origins in the Opening Cut and the Club Door

The film opens with Linklater himself, credited only as the man who should have stayed at bus station, riding in from the overnight bus. In a taxi he talks through a dream where nothing happened, then a theory that every thought of an unchosen path keeps going as its own reality. At the station he almost skipped the cab; in that life a woman picks him up and he moves in. The driver never answers, and he never seems to notice. He ends with "Man, shit. I should've stayed at the bus station."[^3] Then he is on a street where a woman lies struck. A jogger, still jogging in place, barks not to touch her. Someone goes for help. A man who pulled over asks if he is needed, hears no, then presses his card on the jogger anyway: "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it." She does not take it. He drives off before the police arrive. The camera leaves the body and follows the driver who did it into a house, where officers take him for running over his mother.

There is no spoken bridge between those beats. Austin has them both in it, and the cut decides which of them we see. In the taxi he had said there is always someone getting run over; the next scene is a son who ran over his mother. No character connects the two. I think this is already the arrangement we now call a platform: a third that holds strangers available and arranges none of their meetings. A camera that sequences those strangers for a viewer occupies an interpreter's place. It takes one party's situation as its object and addresses another party, the viewer, with a next encounter. Royce held that interpretation always involves three terms, "in the technical phrase, a triadic relation," and that two relations laid end to end are not a triad.[^4] [^5] For him all three terms are selves.[^6] I read the camera as interpreter, beyond Royce.[^7] I do it because the cut already selects who comes next for a viewer who never meets the people on screen. That is Royce's problem, still visible here, with something other than a self in the interpreter's place.

The non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits from standing between them; *divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets them against each other in order to rule both.[^8] The taxi-to-street cut has no such pair. Austin holds the man from the bus and the people around the body in one city and introduces neither to the other. On the opening page of the same chapter, though, he writes a sentence he then refuses to follow. Kurt Wolff translates: "It is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them." The examples are an alliance against a common enemy and the invisible church. Then: "The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context." In the German: "Diese gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung eines dritten Elementes ist indes in späterem Zusammenhang zu behandeln."[^9] He postpones it because the third stands at too great a *Distanz*. Sociology, as he is practicing it in that chapter, cannot treat all three as occupants of one field. Pairs remain: the people who join one another, or those people taken together against the outside thing they share. God and two believers do not form one three-way field, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. Simmel's chapter wants three elements close enough to constitute a group. The distant case falls outside that concern, so he sets it aside for later, and across the 1908 *Soziologie* the phrase never comes back. I call what he postponed the deferred third. Austin in these opening minutes is that postponed case: people unified by a city they share, never introduced as a known three. Simmel saw the holding in 1908 and set it aside. Linklater puts it on a street.

The man from the bus and the strangers around the body share Austin as that deferred third. Simmel later treats conflict itself as a form of association, even a combat-game under shared rules, and a common enemy as the cement of a group.[^9] Those cases still have a known opposition. The film's strangers share no common foe. Brokerage research followed the pair-broker thirds and left this case aside: Burt's structural-hole broker and Obstfeld's *tertius iungens* still know which pair they stand between, and the reviews that define the field drop non-human networks and networks outside work, so Austin's sidewalk and the film's cut fall outside those instruments by construction.[^10] Other writers describe absent and institutional thirds. Austin holds people as a platform does: available, unintroduced, with no identified pair between them.[^11] Stark and Pais, much later, give us terms for owners who co-opt without delegated authority.[^1] Those terms help us read what is already on screen: people held available, meetings unarranged, no owner yet.

At the Blue Bayou on South Congress, meanwhile, a man credited as S-T-E-V-E With A Van has already told a friend about the night: his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he made the roommate write. At the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight." Then the doorman charges him: "Three dollars." He spells it—"S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four"—and the doorman refuses him on the list he thought he owned.

> "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you."
>
> "Fuck. I'm not on the list."
>
> "Figures."

Steve has been refused without a reason. That dependence is already a platform's access, spoken by a person on a sidewalk in 1990. Charles Reich described it twenty-six years earlier as a grant: a grantor issues permission and can take it back.[^12] "The granting, regulation, and revocation of government largess," he wrote, "is carried on by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^12] Denials without disclosed grounds, he said, "should no longer be tolerated."[^13] Steve has just received that denial. Reich later argued that large private organizations raise the same issues; the Blue Bayou is already that private grantor, with a face Steve can still speak to.[^14] Austin holds the two of them in reach and arranges no meeting as a known pair.

Outside, a man offers a hand stamp—"I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in"—and copies it wrist to wrist.[^3] "No, man. I don't want him licking my arm." They do it anyway and walk in free. They can copy access because the stamp is property they can show each other. The guest list was a grant only the door's record could confirm. A platform that still has a face still lets strangers transfer access among themselves. At 2:00 a.m. someone says the rule out loud and empties the room: "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Later platforms keep the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason, and they drop the person who could say the rule and the stamp strangers could copy. Stark and Pais describe that later capture as co-optation without delegated authority.[^1] The film still has someone to answer.

## 'You seen Gary around?' – The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

A passerby breaks into a café later identified as Captain Quackenbush's on the Drag and asks after someone who is not in the room.

> "You seen Gary around?"

No one has seen him for months. The asker leaves without an answer. Outside, another man has overheard. He latches on: suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that leaves "perfect employees," Gary maybe already recruited. The asker leaves toward a friend's place. The talker waits outside to make sure they were not followed, then stops a second stranger—"Just a little security check. There've been some people missing"—and waves him on: "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears.

The sidewalk held both men in reach and the cut selected their meeting. Nobody introduced them. That is the platform the opening cut already put in front of us, now spoken as a question the city cannot answer. Royce asked when strangers count as members of a community. A community of memory, he says, takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts"; a community of expectation, by the same relation to shared future events.[^15] His example is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They form a community on that past even if, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^16] He also requires contemporaries who are, in general, in communication.[^17] The asker and the talker are contemporaries in communication. Neither takes Gary's absence into his own life. The talker turns it into a conspiracy. Sharing Austin leaves the day unclaimed. That is membership under a deferred third. The platform holds people and claims none of them.

Through the day that holding keeps working. Early on, the cut does the joining. The missing-friend question pulls a conspiracy into the frame. A friend breaks a voting rant to say Paul vanished, then invites "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." In the middle of the day the people on screen take over the joins with things they can hand over and say aloud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at the door, a PixelVision camera passed around for "a project." Those handoffs are still the platform. Austin made the meetings possible; the people say the join out loud; the city still pairs no one in advance. By dawn the cut resumes with "Wake up, America" from a car, a tape weapons rant, then a wordless Super-8 toss at Mount Bonnell. When the cut joins scenes, there is nothing to hand over and no one to address. Austin's reach made both kinds of join possible. The camera's selection gives us one of them next.

The sidewalk holds people available and hires none of them. A silent taxi driver carries Linklater's monologue. Kids hawk Cokes. A man sells T-shirts. Officers arrest the hit-and-run son. At the party someone hands off the PixelVision with terms spoken aloud: shoot whatever you want, pass it around, but I want it back. The owner can still enforce face to face. Steve had no contract with the Blue Bayou door, and no one hired him to stand there. The doorman still charged him, checked the list, and turned him away. Stark and Pais write that platform owners co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^1] Nobody had that description in 1990. What Linklater puts in front of us is contribution without employment, with someone still there to answer.

A licensing page keeps that contribution and removes the person. Amazon's Prime Video page says removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection is at Prime Video's sole discretion, and that the company "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^18] Prime Video issues a determination and withholds its grounds. Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus surveyed 810 California drivers. Two-thirds reported deactivation, and 30 percent of those received no reason.[^19] Drivers on the app contribute without being hired, as people at that party did, and the platform has removed anyone who could answer. The four powers attach by rule.[^23] In the three years after the film's release, Reuters Dealing 2000-2 and EBS matched professional traders without themselves buying or selling.[^20] [^21] Dealers also traded directly, so those screens join people who already meet.[^22] Formal models of a third that sits between banned parties ask whether the third would still be needed if the ban were lifted.[^24] Austin's strangers were never banned from meeting. They simply never met. That is the deferred third, the platform's holding before any owner appears.

Outside Les Amis a hitchhiker fresh from his stepfather's funeral faces a camera crew and will take a job only "when I hear the true call," then refuses to say what that is. The city holds him in reach and hires him for nothing. He withholds the reason, as the door withheld Steve's. Royce's memory community needs selves that extend past the present, communication among contemporaries, and shared past events each member takes as part of a life. Loyalty makes a shared past *mine*.[^25] Sharing Austin for a day leaves that loyalty unmade.

The Video Backpacker, in a room of sets he "rotates," argues that a video image is more powerful than an actual event: he once saw a stabbing and now "can't press rewind," can't pause, can't fix the hue. A later platform keeps the past that members once kept themselves. A driver cannot replay an allocation once the queue has moved. Steve's guest list failed in public, and the group renegotiated on the sidewalk, so refusal still required a spoken response. An app assigns a queue spot whether or not the driver assents. Enrollment there waits for no one's yes. Rideshare Drivers United organized drivers to answer a third that will not explain itself.

Linklater leaves the day hanging on that same form. At the last door the doorman states the rule out loud and empties the room into the street. Someone can still say the cover, show a stamp, or spell a name to the person holding the list. The camera already sequences the next encounter without a spoken handoff. By dawn the cut is joining scenes again. The people keep walking into one another's reach, and the camera keeps deciding which of them we see. That is Austin as a platform. It holds strangers available, arranges none of their meetings, and still has a face.

## Conclusion

The opening cut and the club door, and then the day walked under them, show the same third. It holds strangers within reach and arranges none of their meetings. The taxi and the struck woman share a city and a cut. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away, and give no reason. Gary is asked after and never appears. The hitchhiker will not say what the true call is. The title's question hangs the same way: someone asks after Gary, and the film never produces him. Linklater leaves those questions hanging, which is how the form stays visible: a door still has to say the rule out loud, and a camera still has to choose the next face without telling anyone why.

The people on screen contribute without being hired, and they still have someone to argue with. Stark and Pais describe a later capture of that arrangement, when the same powers sit behind a screen. Between those two moments the film shows the form in the open, with reach still a sidewalk and selection still a cut.

I call fluency with determinations that arrive without reasons, and without anyone to answer them, algorithmacy.[^26] Simmel, Royce, Reich, and Stark and Pais describe structure, membership, grant-access, and co-optive enrollment. They leave unspoken the competence those arrangements demand once reasons stop arriving with the determinations. The people on screen still know how to argue with a door.
