# The Setting and the Selector: *Slacker* and Two Ways to Coordinate Strangers

Roger Hunt, Bentley University

*Long draft, 2026-08-01. Written from `outline_v3.md`; every citation verified against the version of
record this pass. To be compressed to 5,000 words with a cut ledger.*

## 1. The coherence puzzle

The first face in *Slacker* belongs to the director. Richard Linklater gets off a bus, climbs into a
taxi, and talks the whole ride about a dream in which nothing happened, and about how every road you
don't take splits off and gets lived out by some other version of you. The driver says nothing. Then
Linklater pays, walks a block, passes a woman lying in the road where a car has hit her, and the film
leaves him for good. The end credits list him as "Should have stayed at bus station."[^1]

What the film does next it does about three dozen times. It follows someone until a stranger crosses
the frame, then leaves with the stranger. There is no protagonist. Of the ninety-eight credited roles,
almost none meet twice.[^2] Linklater shot it on 16mm in the summer of 1989 for about $23,000, opened
it at Austin's Dobie Theatre in July 1990 where it ran eleven weeks, and when Orion Classics released
it nationally the following July it took in something over a million dollars on a top of sixteen
screens.[^3]

By every rule of screenwriting I know, a hundred minutes of that should be unfollowable. So I want to
know what people were following.

The reviews support something narrower than the claim usually made for this film. In
the notices I have been able to retrieve — Ebert, Hinson, Howe, Turan, *Variety*, Canby, Jonathan
Rosenbaum — no critic, friendly or hostile, reports losing track of who was on screen or why the film
had moved on. The complaints are boredom, tedium, monotony, pointlessness. The most useful of them is
Kenneth Turan's, because he disliked the film and described its mechanism exactly anyway: you "follow
each particular slacker … for a few seconds or a few minutes until another member of the species
wanders somnolently into the frame."[^4] Hal Hinson, who liked it, put the same observation the other
way up: "Linklater moves from one exchange to the next with miraculous ease… Linklater's control seems
all but invisible here. But this kind of stylistic lucidity can only be the result of determined
calculation and planning."[^5]

That is a narrower claim than "audiences were never lost," and it has to be, because at the Sundance
premiere, John Pierson reports, "the audience broke into thirds: one-third loved it, one-third walked
out, and one-third fell asleep."[^6] People walked out. But walking out on a film you find pointless
is a different act from losing the thread, and the complaint Pierson kept from the Dobie run says which
one it was: "Why are the lives of these unproductive, pretentious, and boring people documented on
film? The movie does not mean anything."[^7]

Whatever was carrying viewers through, it was built on purpose. In 1991 Linklater described explaining
the plan to his cameraman and his dolly grip: "They were like 'What?!' And I was like, 'No, no, no.
We'll hold {the viewer's} hand. We'll have these transitional characters. … It won't make any sense on
paper, but will be kind of easy to watch in its own kind of weird way.'"[^8]

Three published readings have already located that hand-holding in the camera. Alison Macor writes that
Linklater replaced *La Ronde*'s narrator with "the fluid camera and a kind of 'baton-passing'," under a
chapter section headed "Chaos by Design."[^9] Jeroen Boom describes a meandering camera "weaving
together the lives of eccentric characters," and reads the weaving as a love of place.[^10] Enrique
Ramirez has the camera marking "its dérive" through Austin — a drift, which is movement without a
chooser.[^11] So the camera's linking work is not a discovery, and I am not claiming it as one. My
question is what kind of coordinator a camera is. Two things coordinate the strangers in this film,
they work differently, and telling them apart turns out to matter well past the movie.

## 2. Two ways to coordinate strangers

A third party can coordinate two people who never deal with each other directly. Georg Simmel saw a
century ago what the step from two to three does: add a third position to a two-party relationship and
the social form changes in kind, because now there can be mediation, coalition, a politics. He
catalogued three ways to hold the position — the non-partisan who "produces the concord of two
colliding parties"; the *tertius gaudens*, who turns the quarrel of two others to his own account; and
the third who sets them against each other deliberately, *divide et impera*.[^12]

On the first page of that chapter Simmel names a fourth kind and puts it aside. "It is sociologically
very significant," he writes, "that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a
phenomenon which lies outside of them." His examples are an alliance of states against a shared enemy
and the invisible church that unites the faithful through their equal relation to one God. He calls
that a group-forming mediation, says it belongs to a later discussion, and gives his reason: here the
third stands at such a distance that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three
elements alike.[^13] He saw that not every third is a broker of pairs, and he moved on.

Sociology after him did not move on. It built out the broker. Ronald Burt showed that two people who
would each gain from what the other knows, and who are not connected, are worth money to whoever
connects them and passes things across.[^14] David Obstfeld described a friendlier version, the third
who introduces the two rather than playing them off.[^15] The assumption underneath is constant and the
field states it plainly: brokerage happens "when one actor (the broker) is connected to two other
actors (alters) who are not themselves connected."[^16] Roger Gould and Roberto Fernandez built the
formal apparatus on an ordered triple of actors, and every measure that came out of it — betweenness,
constraint, role counts — is a property of a node in a network.[^17]

There is another way to hold the position, and Scott Feld named the mechanism in 1981. A focus, in his
term, is a person, place or activity that people organise around, so that ties form among them as a
side effect.[^18] I am going to call it a setting, because that is what it is. **A setting puts people
within reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one approach another. It arranges no
particular meeting.** Against it, **a selector chooses: which two parties come together, or which one
an audience meets next.** Austin in 1990 is a setting. Linklater's camera is a selector.

Three questions tell them apart. Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could
the encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what
it does accordingly? Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at most.
The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and — this
matters later — knows nothing about anybody. Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and
reads both parties in detail.

The three questions sort by reach, by counterfactual and by knowledge. They do not sort by whether a
third adds or subtracts encounters, and they are not tests of harm. Simmel's own case makes that plain.
His simplest example of *divide et impera* is a superior who forbids by general prohibition
associations that "are merely possible and very often do not exist even as wishes of the elements
separated by the prohibition."[^19] That third acts on everyone and knows nothing about particulars,
so on two of my three questions it scores as a setting — while destroying reachability rather than
supplying it. The questions tell you what kind of third you are looking at. What it can do to you is a
separate matter.

What I am claiming is narrow. The setting was named in 1981 and has been studied since; the broker has
a literature of its own; nobody has set the two side by side and held both to one test. The warrant is
documentary, and anyone with three PDFs can check it. The *Academy of Management Annals* review of
third-party influence, which announces that it "intentionally took an inclusive approach," contains no
occurrence of Feld, foci, focus theory, homophily or propinquity across its whole text and reference
list. Matthijs Kalmijn and Henk Flap's leading study of organised settings and partner choice contains
no occurrence of broker, brokerage, matchmaker, intermediary or third party. Eli Finkel and colleagues'
review of online dating contains no occurrence of Feld, foci, focus theory or Simmel.[^20]

Two qualifications, both real. At the level of whole traditions the separation has been breached: Mario
Small and Leah Gose call routine organisations brokers outright, though what they list as the
conditions of successful brokerage are all properties of institutional norms — interaction that is
"frequent, long-lasting, focused on others, and centered on joint tasks" — and not one of them requires
the third to know which two people are involved.[^21] And Finkel's team draws something close to my
distinction inside a single platform, between access, "users' exposure to and opportunity to evaluate
potential romantic partners they are otherwise unlikely to encounter," and matching, "a site's use of a
mathematical algorithm to select potential partners for users."[^22] They get there and stop; the
distinction stays inside dating.

The cost of leaving it there shows up in *PNAS*. Michael Rosenfeld, Reuben Thomas and Sonia Hausen
report that online dating has displaced friends and family "from their former roles as key
intermediaries in the formation of new unions," and name the pattern disintermediation, "the removal or
subordination of the human intermediary between two parties." Their numbers are good: among couples who
met online, the share previously strangers rose from 81.0 to 89.5 percent between 2009 and 2017, while
the share brought together online by a third person fell from 11.2 to 3.7.[^23] In the opening paragraph
of that paper, one sentence calls friends and family "the intermediation of friends" and the next calls
them "the essential network foci that enable connections to other people," citing Feld.[^24] Feld's
foci are not people. Every figure in the paper measures a human third, and the authors say so — *human*
is their word — which leaves the arrival of a non-human one entirely untouched.

## 3. Austin as setting

A man leaves a coffee shop and a stranger falls into step beside him, delivering an unbroken monologue
about the moon landings that he cannot easily shake. A woman works the sidewalk selling what she swears
is Madonna's pap smear, and gets a hearing. What makes these scenes play as oddly plausible is the
city, and Austin stages neither of them.

The mechanism is not politeness. Erving Goffman's civil inattention — the glance and withdrawal by
which strangers in public leave each other alone — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not
speak.[^25] What licenses the monologue is the opposite rule, and Emanuel Schegloff describes it: an
opened exchange is hard to close. A summons gets an answer, and the sequence it starts is nonterminal —
it does not offer a natural exit.[^26] The moon-landing man exploits that. I should be careful with
Schegloff, though, because he also says the escape route is closed at the other end: in co-presence a
member cannot simply decline to answer, since the excusing inference is unavailable and
cold-shouldering gets read in instead. Both sides of an opening cost something. That the cost falls
harder on the person who wants out is my claim, not his.

The material base was ordinary and it was not romantic. Austin overbuilt through the mid-eighties and
was still absorbing the surplus at the end of the decade: housing stock rose 48.0 percent between 1980
and 1990 while population rose 34.6, a net addition of 70,427 units inside city limits that were
themselves moving outward.[^27] In December 1989 the Austin metropolitan area issued no multifamily
building permits at all, against 632 in Houston and 260 in Dallas.[^28] Median contract rent on census
day 1990 was $346 and the rental vacancy rate was 11.6 percent.[^29] Cheap space buys unstructured
time, and unstructured time is what this film is about. I should not overstate it, though — Dallas that
year sat at 15.3 percent vacancy and Houston at 15.8, so Austin was the tighter market of the three.
What Austin had was a great deal of room and 50,245 students at the university in the middle of
it.[^30]

Economists have a name for what a place like that does. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga set out how
agglomeration works, and the driver they identify is market size rather than density as such: a larger
market improves both the chance of finding a match and the quality of the match you find.[^31] Better
odds, and no proposals — which is what a setting does. I will not attach a number to it, because
Duranton and Puga are explicit that sharing, matching and learning produce observationally equivalent
effects, so no measured agglomeration elasticity can be assigned to matching alone. Giordano Mion and
Paolo Naticchioni sharpen the mechanism and complicate it in the same breath: in larger markets general
attributes count for relatively less and specific ones for more, while assortative matching runs
*negatively* with local market size.[^32]

A setting that supplies conventions about who may approach whom is also supplying conventions about who
may not, and the American law of the street is where those conventions were enforced. Until 1972 a
vagrancy ordinance of the kind struck down in *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville* reached "poor
people, nonconformists, dissenters, idlers," and the Supreme Court's objection was precisely that it
put the choice in an officer's hands. Justice Douglas defended wandering and strolling as "amenities of
life as we have known them," and conceded in the same breath that they "are not mentioned in the
Constitution or in the Bill of Rights."[^33] A code that reads types rather than persons is not a
neutral improver of odds. It licenses the white twenty-something pitching Madonna's pap smear on the
Drag and it has always been available against somebody else, and the discretion is the point rather
than a defect. Reuben May's audit work on exclusionary practices in urban nightlife, Austin included,
finds the same discretion at work in commercial gatekeeping thirty years later.[^34]

One thing the city did not do is join the scenes. Brendan Gaughen retraced the film's locations and
found a jump of about three miles — the Old Anarchist's house to Thirty-fifth and Mopac — played across
a cut as though it were a walk. His survey is non-exhaustive and covers outdoor locations only, and he
says so, so I want one instance rather than a pattern.[^35] One instance is enough. Austin put these
people into the same day. It did not decide which of them the film would follow.

## 4. The camera as selector

Watch what happens after the taxi. Linklater's talker walks away and the film hands you a ready-made
plot: a woman lies in the road, run down by a car, and the driver is her son. Matricide, arrest, grief.
A conventional film lives there for ninety minutes; this one gives it about two, then leaves the son
mid-arrest for a bystander who happened to watch. Writing in the *Austin Chronicle* in July 1990, before
the film had left Texas, Chris Walters described the move as it happened: "The camera eye slowly pulls
back until it sees another nameless guy … and follows him upstairs into another story already in
progress." He called the resulting structure a daisy chain, sixteen years before the term entered film
studies.[^36]

Two abandonments in the opening minutes — the director, then a tragedy — teach the rule the rest of the
film runs on. Nobody is owed a return.

What connects one scene to the next is worth stating exactly, because it is almost nothing. The
characters share no history, no reunions, no coincidences that pay off later. No character introduces
another. Pierson's craft note is right that there is "not a single jump cut, and the passage from one
scene to the next is always in a flow" — he does not mean there are no cuts, since the arrest itself is
cut together, but that no cut breaks the movement.[^37] The transfer is always the film following one
body until another body crosses it. Strip out every durable tie between characters, which in this film
is easy because there are none, and the only thing left holding scene to scene is the film's decision
about whom to follow next.

That decision is constrained, and the constraint matters for what comes later. The camera can only
leave with someone the city has already put within reach of the lens. Its choices are drawn from
whoever the street assembled. A dispatch system operates on the whole city's queue and does not wait
for anyone to walk past.

Whether a camera can be said to choose anything is a real question and film scholars have fought over
it. David Bordwell's objection goes further than the one usually attributed to him: he denies not just
the personified narrator but any sender at all, on the ground that film narration "presupposes a
perceiver, but not any sender, of a message." What he grants in the same account is everything I need.
Narration, on his own description, suppresses information, restricts what we know, generates curiosity,
sets a tone.[^38] Seymour Chatman answered him with a narrator already stripped of personality, "the
composite of a large and complex variety of communicating devices."[^39] So the argument is not about
whether a camera has intentions. It is about whether an impersonal system counts as an agent.

I should say where I stand in that argument, because it is not the middle. Mario Slugan reports that
there is "little debate on this matter among film scholars in general," among whom the narrator view
predominates and Bordwell is the prominent exception, while the dispute runs hot among philosophers of
film.[^40] I am siding with a minority inside film studies, and taking the fallback the philosophers
offer, which is that a story "is actually told by the author in his or her creation of the work."[^41]
When I write "the camera," I mean whatever makes those choices.

When Bordwell reaches *Slacker* he cannot fit it into his own category: "Another borderline case, I
think, is *Slacker*. … This isn't a network so much as a wiggly, knotted string."[^42] His word is
string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order. The film runs two shapes at
once. Among the characters it is a bare chain, each one tied only to whoever shares their sidewalk for
a few minutes — the "daisy chain plot" that Charles Ramírez Berg names with *Slacker* as an
example.[^43] In presentation it is an ordered walk: scene one, then scene two, in that sequence,
chosen. No taxonomy in this literature sorts films by how scenes connect in presentation order, and the
graph tradition dropped order deliberately. Franco Moretti says so in as many words: his edges "have no
'direction'," because he "just couldn't find a non-clumsy way to visualize weight and direction," and in
the diagram that results "Time turned into space."[^44]

The camera does not coordinate the
characters. The moon-landing man and his captive coordinate each other; the camera coordinates *me*,
serially, with one of them after another. That is a real difference from Uber, and it is the difference
worth keeping, because selectors do two different jobs. Some choose what an audience sees next — the
camera, and later the landing page of a streaming service. Some choose who does business with whom —
the taxi dispatcher, and later Uber. *Slacker*'s camera is the first kind in its oldest form: it
arranges what reaches you and knows nothing whatever about you.

The strongest account against me is Maria Poulaki's, and it is stranger than it looks. She does not
explain coherence without any coordinating agent — she gives the camera agency directly, writing that
the movement from one story to the next runs on "the self-reflexive agency of the camera."[^45] But her
camera describes rather than chooses; it drifts without motivation, and the sentence is about
*Gomorrah*. So I can grant her the drift and keep the choosing. Two further things in her account are
worth having. What links *Gomorrah*, on her reading, is "primarily the setting … and the character of
Maria" — a setting and a selector, side by side, inside her own analysis. And she treats character
interactions as "primarily informational and secondarily anthropomorphic/characterological," which
means the obvious rebuttal — nobody in *Slacker* meets twice — does not touch her. Her own floor is the
answer: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs relations between the
elements in order to form itself."[^46] The relations in this film are the order of the visits, and
somebody chose it.

## 5. The new selectors

Uber does not put drivers and riders within reach of each other and leave them to it. It chooses which
driver gets which ride, and nothing in the design lets the two settle anything between themselves. That
much has been true since researchers first studied the app.[^47] What a decade of subsequent work adds
is a precise account of what a selector can do that a camera cannot, and it comes to three things.

The first is that it prices each match. Since the first quarter of 2023 in London, on the evidence of a
participatory audit of 1.5 million trips by 258 drivers, "the price the passenger pays and the fee the
driver receives vary independently of each other." The driver cannot see the passenger's fare in the
app, and Uber bars him from asking the passenger directly. Across that sample the company's median cut
rose from 25 to 29 percent while the mean held at 25, the increase concentrated on the highest-fare
trips.[^48] A price computed twice, per match, and disclosed to neither party in full.

Surge pricing is the wrong example here, and saying why sharpens the distinction rather than blunting
it. In the economics, surge is a price attached to a place and a time whose designed effect is to move
drivers across a map: Omar Besbes, Francisco Castro and Ilan Lobel model a platform that "selects prices
for different locations" and find an optimum that induces overcongestion in some places and prices
others out.[^49] That acts on everyone in a zone. It behaves like a setting the platform builds — and I
should be careful to say only that surge has not been shown to know who the two parties are, not that
it has been shown not to, since a model that abstracts away from identity cannot establish anything
about identity.

The second thing is that it reads the parties. Veena Dubal states flatly what the record supports:
"people who are doing the same work, with the same skill, for the same company, at the same time may
receive different hourly pay."[^50] She goes further, and marks it as inference when she does — the
wage manipulators "appear to be personalized based on what Uber's machine learning systems know about
the habits, practices, and income targets of individual workers," reasoning by analogy from consumer
pricing. Uber denies it, and Dubal's own footnote says "We have no way to judge the accuracy of this
statement."[^51] I will assert the differential pay and leave the personalisation where she leaves it.
The best evidence for this power is not where a reader would expect it. No
published engineering account describes ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver identity in order
to choose the pair. Uber's and Lyft's own descriptions run on collective wait time and marketplace
value. The evidence for reading the parties sits on the pricing side.

The third thing is that it can remove a party, and that power has recently been litigated from both
directions. In April 2023 the Amsterdam Court of Appeal held that Uber's deactivation of three drivers
had been taken by automated means. Notes written in Kraków by staff whose qualifications Uber never
disclosed, about drivers who were never heard, "in no way show that this act was much more than a
purely symbolic act." For a fourth driver the court found the opposite, because Uber stated, and nobody
contested, that a personal conversation had preceded the decision.[^52] The remedy was disclosure, not
reinstatement; no deactivation was undone. From the other side, in March 2026 a Ninth Circuit panel let
Seattle's deactivation ordinance stand, holding that "the conduct that draws the remedy is the
unwarranted deactivation of worker accounts. When conduct is nonexpressive, as is the case here, it is
not subject to First Amendment scrutiny."[^53] That is a preliminary posture rather than a final
judgment. It is still a court saying that removing a worker from a platform is conduct a city may
regulate.

A camera can pass you over. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take you off the street. That
is the whole difference, and I want to draw it in the right place, because Lindsey Cameron's seven-year
study of ride-hailing found that "many workers report liking and finding choice while working under
algorithmic management," with the choices made frequent and narrow, and the resulting consent apt to
"mask the more-structurally problematic elements of the work."[^54] Her subject is the management
regime — when to work, which nudges to take, how to game the inputs — rather than the act of matching.
So the contrast I want is not between a cut and a match. It is between a cut and a regime that prices
and removes.

## 6. Cinema as training

Where does a 1991 audience get the skill to follow a hundred minutes of somebody else's choices, and
enjoy it? Hugo Münsterberg had an answer in 1916, in the first book of film psychology: "our attention
is constantly drawn in accordance with the intentions of the producers," and in the cinema "voluntary
attention is eliminated from the sphere of art."[^55]

Walter Benjamin put the strong version twenty years later. "The function of film," he wrote, "is to
train human beings in the apperceptions and reactions needed to deal with a vast apparatus whose role
in their lives is expanding almost daily."[^56] His bet was hopeful; the training was to produce a
public equal to the machinery. Miriam Hansen keeps that half in view, describing cinema as "the single
most inclusive cultural horizon in which the traumatic effects of modernity were reflected, rejected or
disavowed, transmuted or negotiated."[^57] Her frame is narrower than mine, though, and I should not
borrow more of it than she offers: she bounds herself to "roughly from the 1920s through the 1950s" and
to mainstream Hollywood, and *negotiated* is the last of four verbs in a list that opens with rejected
and disavowed.

What is actually measured is this. Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing ran the classic Kuleshov montages —
a neutral face cut against a gravestone, a bowl of soup, a child — for viewers who had never watched a
film. The first-time viewers understood each shot perfectly well and did not connect them: they
"perceived them to be wholly separate from each other and did not relate them spatio-temporally."
Experienced viewers did connect them, though not unanimously. On the gravestone sequence all twenty
made the spatio-temporal link and eleven of the twenty read sadness into the face; on the soup sequence
the figure was 30 percent, and on the child sequence nobody showed the effect at all.[^58] Eleven of
twenty against none is still the cleanest evidence available that reading a cut is learned rather than
given.

The same research team then complicated their own result, and the complication belongs here rather than
in a footnote. When they added diegetic sound to the clips, first-time viewers managed transitions that
had defeated them in the silent studies — 100 percent on a cut from a man calling outside a house to a
woman inside it, and 100 percent across cuts that violate the 180-degree rule. Even then they failed on
unfamiliar environments, at 25 percent on one clip and none at all on another.[^59] So what the silent
studies establish is narrower than "reading cuts is learned": it is that bridging shots *without an
audio scaffold* is learned.

The best account of what experienced viewers have comes from the same paper, and it names an agent.
They "understand that views are arranged in the service of telling a story, and that in cases where the
story is not initially evident, some effort at problem-solving may be rewarded," and that
problem-solving "likely involves assumptions about something akin to directorial intent."[^60] The
authors propose this rather than show it — it sits in a discussion section and was never manipulated.
Proposed or not, it is the posture exactly: attributing intent to a hidden arranger, and working to
meet it.

On that learned habit filmmakers built a craft aimed at the eye. Tim Smith and John Henderson found
that about a quarter of the cuts joining one view of a scene to the next go unnoticed altogether.[^61]
Lester Loschky and colleagues, tracking gaze through a film clip, concluded that "the filmmakers
influenced when and where viewers attended, irrespective of their viewing condition or whether they
made the critical inference."[^62] Taking a stranger's run of choices for a world went invisible as
convention.

*Slacker* takes away the alibi. In an ordinary film the cut serves the plot, so a viewer credits the
plot. Remove the plot and the choosing is the only thing left to notice, and by 1991 a trained audience
found it followable — Hinson's "miraculous ease" is a description of a skill meeting a craft.

Whether that skill transfers to reading an algorithm is unknown, and I want to be exact about the
sense in which it is unknown: nobody has run the study. Five independent searches of the relevant
literatures turned up no work relating fluency with film editing to comprehension of a recommender or a
dispatch queue. Two field-defining reviews of algorithmic literacy — one a systematic review of 169
studies — contain no occurrence of film, cinema, montage or shot, and the field says of itself that
"much remains unknown, such as what predicts algorithmic literacy."[^63] The closest available test
measured general media literacy and algorithm awareness in the same students and found them only
inconsistently related.[^64] The default expectation in this literature is against transfer, and Tim
Smith and colleagues state it: the cognitive benefits of screen experience "may be specific to the
domain of movie narrative comprehension and not transfer to more general applications," and the trials
that would settle it "have not been conducted."[^65]

What workers build instead is not simply the second skill. Heiner Heiland compared what food-delivery
riders believed about two dispatch algorithms against what the algorithms did. On speed, the parameter
they cared most about, the shared theory was wrong — former managers confirmed it and an A/B test bore
it out — and the wrongness disciplined them into riding faster. On proximity to restaurants they were
right, and exploited it. His conclusion about the half that was wrong: "these obvious misunderstandings
work in the interest of the platforms and are not corrected by them."[^66] Pooled sensemaking converges
unevenly, and nobody has any reason to correct the flattering half.

## 7. Enclosure

Every link in the chain that carried *Slacker* to an audience was a person who could be telephoned.
Pierson, a producer's representative whose job was introducing films to buyers, took it to them. The
Dobie booked it on its own judgment and held it eleven weeks. Orion Classics bought it and released it
nationally, on a top of sixteen screens. Sixteen bookings is not a national audience reached; it is
sixteen placements argued for one at a time, which is the scale worth keeping in mind before comparing
any of this with a recommender.

Address was possible, and refusal was attributable. Both halves of that matter, because the arguing did
not always work. Linklater and Pierson took the video release to a named vice-president at Orion and
lost: the company shipped 7,000 cassettes, its foreign-language quantity, against a tie-in book that
sold 20,000.[^67] The film played three months past Orion's Chapter 11 filing in December 1991 and the
video shipped the following summer, so the second window was starved by a division applying a default
rather than killed by the bankruptcy.[^68] One gate refused them. The gate had a name, and the earlier
gate had responded — a paragraph in *Film Comment* reached a buyer, who telephoned relatives in Austin
to ask whether the Dobie sellouts were real.

What replaced that chain narrows what reaches a viewer twice over, and the two stages are separate.
Cathrin Bengesser, Matthew Hilborn and Jeanette Steemers measured both: 15,584 British titles in
European video-on-demand circulation become 1,165 in Netflix's European catalogues, and inside a
national catalogue fewer than one title in ten reaches the landing page.[^69] Licensing decides what
can be found. Prominence decides what is found. Their landing-page figures are evidence about editorial
promotion rather than personalisation — they cleared cookies daily and used fresh accounts precisely to
strip personalisation out — which makes the point sharper rather than weaker. A page that chooses what
you see next, without knowing who you are, is the nearest thing streaming has to Linklater's camera.

I should resist the easy version of this story, which is that the studios closed their specialty
divisions and the streamers took over. Yannis Tzioumakis's own numbers refuse it. The third tier's fall
from 10.22 to 2.11 percent of the market is, in his phrase, only "seemingly suggesting" a collapse,
because he reclassified the largest earners upward himself; and the middle tier refilled, with late
indies averaging 6.46 percent against the vanished divisions' 6.91.[^70] The acquisition market changed
shape rather than shrinking. *Slacker*'s own position in 1990 — no other bidder, one modest offer taken
on a template — looks more like the present than like the boom in between.

What has changed is what a party is told. Amazon publishes its own account of how titles are licensed
for Prime Video, and three of the powers described in §5 are in it. It reads: "we continuously review
performance metrics and viewing behavior." It removes: titles may be taken down "at any time at the
sole discretion of Prime Video." And it declines to explain: a rejected title "may not be resubmitted
or appealed," because "we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not
selected for licensing."[^71] The fourth power is missing from the page, and I am not going to supply
it: Amazon publishes nothing about pricing, and a four-item list with one unsourced item is not worth
having.

That sentence — we cannot provide additional details about why — is the exact modern counterpart of the
Orion vice-president who said no. She said no, and Linklater knew whom to ask.

So the questions the film leaves open have answers of unequal quality. When does a setting become a
selector? When its choices stop being one route across a field the parties could cross by themselves,
and start being the only route — when it picks the pair, reads them, and admits no way around. What
does collective navigation of a selector require? More than the century of cinema supplied, on the
evidence: audiences learned to follow a hidden arranger and learned nothing about questioning one, and
what riders build in its place is half accurate and half folklore the platform is content to leave
standing. What would make selectors contestable rather than proprietary I cannot answer. Reading them
is the precondition, and reading is the part that has a century of teaching behind it.

*Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy at the time, and the film headed the charge off itself,
on one of the oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it. Its people withdrew from plot,
career and convergence and stayed coordinated anyway — put within reach by a cheap and legible city,
and shown to us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from them except a day, and gave the day
back watchable. That is what the comparison is for. Both kinds of coordination are doing their work in
the open here, in a film anyone can watch, which is the rarest thing about it.
