# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

The 1991 film *Slacker* (Richard Linklater), aside from being a founding document of American independent cinema, can also be considered a coordination puzzle: it depicts, as its first reviewer wrote, "no plot, no major characters, no suspense: just fleeting glimpses of bohemia in its twilight phase."[^1] In this chapter I use *Slacker* as an illustration and as a limit case of the multi-character film, particularly when it comes to the issue of coordination. I describe the arrangement through which the film holds roughly one hundred unacquainted characters in one legible day, and argue that this arrangement consists of two jobs performed by two agents: the city of Austin, which holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody, and the camera, which selects which stranger the viewer sees next.

The film opens with the arrangement already at work. A man played by Linklater himself, credited as "Should have stayed at bus station," rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a taxi driver who never once answers him.[^2] His monologue proposes that every road not taken continues somewhere as its own reality, and he gets out on the line that gives him his credit. Moments later a woman lies struck in the street, a jogger barks at bystanders not to touch her, and a motorist who has pulled over presses his business card on a stranger, "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it," before driving off ahead of the police.[^3] The camera then abandons the victim and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother. In the taxi the passenger had remarked that there is always someone getting run over. The viewer soon learns to anticipate such hand-offs: a character walks out of one scene and into the next, and the film goes with whichever body the cut prefers.

The scholarship on *Slacker* has answered the coordination question mainly by locating a property that all the scenes share. The characters "unite by unanimously doing nothing," so the film coheres as a common ethic of refusal.[^4] Alternatively, "conversation, and ideas themselves, become the determining force of the narrative," so the film coheres as one continuous exchange of talk.[^5] These observations are valid in the sense that the film's tone and its talk are remarkably consistent from scene to scene. However, a property that every scene possesses cannot join any two of them, and the film's monologues do not in fact travel: each dies with its speaker, unanswered by the scene that follows.

A second line of interpretation attributes the continuity to drift. The camera "drifts along the street space, from character to character and from place to place," revealing a generation in the act of performing itself;[^6] "the movement of these films is the movement of the characters therein," walking raised to a philosophical practice;[^7] the scenes flow into one another until they create "the sense of a seamless urban tissue."[^8] The drift readings are valid as far as the camera's path is concerned. However, drift is mostly negatively defined, naming the absence of a destination, and it does not elaborate on the one act the film performs at every juncture: the selection of a single passerby from several available, a selection no character makes and the film never revokes.

The formal literature names the structure and stops there. In Charles Ramírez Berg's taxonomy of alternative plots, *Slacker* defines the daisy chain, in which the story jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit any of them.[^9] The strongest recent position goes further and holds that the missing plot is itself the point: the film has "no protagonist, no story, no thread linking individual events," and its refusal of narrative protects its characters from the stories told about their generation.[^10] The refusal reading is valid at the level of plot, where nothing accumulates and nothing resolves; one commentator states the consequence plainly, that "the frustration that viewers experience in the film's refusal to provide a coherent plot is the point."[^11] However, the reading proves too much about the film as an experience. A genuine refusal of coordination would play as shuffle, and *Slacker* is legible at every hand-off; viewers who cannot say what the film is about can always say where they are. If neither a shared ethic, nor the circulation of talk, nor the camera's drift, nor the refusal of narrative accounts for the hand-offs, does coordination play any role in *Slacker* at all? And if so, what performs it, and what form does it take?

Theories of the network narrative, where the question might be expected to find an answer, require materials this film withholds. Their accounts of multi-character coherence run through convergence: plotlines that intertwine, consequences that propagate across strands, strangers whose paths finally cross.[^12] *Slacker* appears on the network filmographies and supplies none of these; no line re-crosses, and nothing propagates. The film is what that literature would have to call a borderline case, the film that holds together after every binding agent the theory recognizes has been removed. I would argue that the film's coordination is real and external to its story. Austin holds the day's strangers within reach of one another and introduces none of them. The camera selects, at every juncture, which of them we see. And at the doors where the day's access is priced, listed, and refused, a person still pronounces the refusal and can still be argued with. The platforms that later operated this same arrangement retained the holding, the selecting, and the grant, and withdrew the person. The film will ask after a man named Gary and decline to produce him, and the argument of this chapter is that the two refusals are the same refusal.

## Simmel's postponed third

The sociology of the third begins in Simmel's triad chapter, and the chapter is best known for the thirds who stand between a pair. The non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits from the position between them; *divide et impera* sets the two against one another in order to rule both.[^13] Each of these figures presupposes an acquaintance: the third works on a pair it already knows. The taxi passenger and the woman in the street have no such third and no such acquaintance. What they share is a city, and nobody introduces them.

On the chapter's opening page, however, Simmel registers a third of another kind, and the passage rewards quotation at length:

> [I]t is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them. … The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context.[^14]

Between those two sentences stand his examples: an alliance formed against a common enemy, and the invisible church, which joins all believers through their equal relation to the one God. The postponement itself was already old. In the chapter's first English serialization, in 1902, the same sentence reads, "This society-constructing mediation of a third element is, however, to be treated in a later connection."[^15] The triad chapter never redeems the promise.

His reason for deferring is distance. The third stands so far from the other two that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three alike; what remain are configurations of two, either the relation among those who join, or the relation between them as a unit and the outside center of interest.[^14] God and two believers do not constitute one three-way field, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. I call the figure he set aside the deferred third: a third so distant that it never comes between an identified pair. The definition does half of this chapter's work, and the film's city does the rest, because Austin departs from Simmel's examples at the instructive point. An enemy unifies, and a God unifies. Austin does neither. It holds the man from the bus station and the people around the struck woman in one city, all day, and introduces none of them.

When the theme returns, some two hundred pages later in the chapter on conflict, the distance that had been grounds for deferral has become a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them is an especially favorable condition of the bond, and both of the original examples come back on that page.[^16] The third returns as the condition of a bond rather than as the member of a triad. Austin's sidewalk is distance of that kind. It is worth noting that where platforms have entered Simmel's triad at all in the recent literature, they have entered it as thirds of the between, brokers profiting from the parties on either side.[^17] The deferred third has remained where Simmel left it.

## Royce's test for membership

Holding, however, is one thing, and belonging is another, and the question of what would convert a shared city into a shared membership has an exact answer in Royce. A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation stands in the same relation to shared future events.[^18] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^19] The definition is permissive at every point where one might expect strictness, since it tolerates aloofness, enmity, and the absence of any meeting. It is strict at a single point instead. There is no community of people who have not each, individually, claimed the same past. The scholarship reads his three conditions as cumulative: the self's power to extend itself ideally in time; a plurality of distinct selves capable of social communication and in general engaged in it; and at least one identical event included in the ideally extended lives of all.[^20] Acceptance is written into the third condition, and without the second there is no community at all.

The test matters here because the film keeps administering it. A day of unclaimed strangers cannot pass it; sharing a city is not sharing a past. The film will ask after Gary and decline to produce him for precisely this reason.

## The two jobs, with someone still there to be asked

Down at the Blue Bayou on South Congress, a man credited as S-T-E-V-E with a van has spent the afternoon describing the night ahead: his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he made the roommate write. At the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them. The doorman charges him three dollars. He spells his way onto a list he believes he owns, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and the door answers, "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you," and Steve supplies the gloss himself: "Fuck. I'm not on the list." The scene lasts a minute and contains a complete jurisprudence. Wealth of the modern kind, Reich argued in 1964, increasingly takes the form of largess, a grant, whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^21] The Blue Bayou's door performs that account in miniature. It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum, and declines to say why the list Steve believes he owns does not have him. Reich also demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated."[^22] The door never reaches that sentence, and the demand stays a hope appended to the description. What the scene preserves, and what the platforms will later discard, is the grant's address. The grant is spoken to a person. Steve can answer the door even if the door will not answer him.

Later, at another door, a man offers a hand stamp, "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in," and copies it by licking wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that stops nobody, and the two of them walk in free. The pair of doors is as close as the film comes to a controlled comparison. A stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers on their own initiative. The guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it. At closing time someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it, "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Even the eviction is addressed to people who could in principle argue back.

Gary is missing, and nobody in the room can answer for him. In a café on the Drag a passerby breaks in to ask after a man who is not there, "You seen Gary around?," and nobody has seen him for months, and the asker leaves without an answer. Outside, a man who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited. The asker heads off toward a friend's place; the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed, stops a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing," and waves him along, "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears. The sidewalk had held both men within reach of one another, the cut put their meeting on screen, and no one introduced them. On Royce's terms the two are contemporaries in communication, so his second condition holds on this sidewalk in a way it holds almost nowhere else in the film. The third condition fails, and the acceptance built into it fails with it. Neither man takes Gary's absence into his own life as the same past event: one is looking for a friend, and the other converts the absence into evidence for a conspiracy. The day they share remains a day neither of them claims. Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

Through the rest of the day the holding keeps working, and two kinds of join take turns. Early on, the cut does the joining for us, placing the taxi and then the body in one sequence, pulling a conspiracy into the frame behind a missing-friend question, following a friend who breaks off a voting rant to say that Paul has vanished with "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle of the day the people on screen close meetings themselves, with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at a door, and a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud, shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Those are people using the city's reach. The cut still chooses which of those uses we watch. The doorman, the same afternoon, administers a grant, which is neither the camera's job nor the city's holding. By dawn the cut is joining scenes again, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a camera thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell. When the cut does that joining, nothing is handed over and nobody is addressed.

The same day shows labor without a contract: kids hawking Cokes, a man selling T-shirts, a hitchhiker back from his stepfather's funeral who takes a lift into town, says the man "was a serious fuck-up," and asks for a cigarette. Outside Les Amis a different man, credited as Hitchhiker awaiting "true call," tells a camera crew he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and when they ask what that is he says he will know it when he hears it. The city holds him within reach and hires him for nothing. He withholds the specification, and he offers that withholding to people standing there to be asked. The verb for what the city is doing to him arrived later, in the literature of platforms: co-optation, the enrollment of providers and users in practices of algorithmic management "without managerial authority having been delegated to them."[^23] On the sidewalk in 1990 there is always somebody standing there to be asked.

## The same jobs without an address

Linklater glossed the structure, twenty years on, as "either channel surfing or … going in and out of different movies," a "primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."[^24] The comparison can be taken more literally than he offered it, because the platforms that came to operate the film's arrangement are documented in their own words. Amazon's licensing page for Prime Video states that, unless otherwise indicated, removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection sits at the company's sole discretion, and that it "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^25] Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus, surveying 810 California drivers, found two thirds reporting deactivation and thirty percent of those given no reason at all.[^26] The four acts at Steve's door are all present: the price, the record, the refusal, the withheld reason. The person is what is missing. Drivers contribute without being hired, as the people at that party did, and the counterparty that removes them has removed anyone who could be argued with. Steve's list failed in public, and the group could renegotiate on the sidewalk; an app assigns a queue position whether or not the driver assents, and the inclusion waits for nobody's yes. The film supplies its own gloss on the irreversibility. The Video Backpacker explains that a video image is more powerful than an actual event because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind." A driver cannot rewind an allocation once the queue has moved on.

Because Royce makes acceptance constitutive, the app's assignment is not membership, and calling it one would spend his term twice and in opposite senses. Salomé Viljoen describes the mechanism: a horizontal, population-based data relation includes a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he has in common with others rather than anything he has claimed.[^27] Enrollment of that kind is the platform's answer to Royce's question, and the answer is that it has stopped asking. Rideshare Drivers United exists because drivers organized to compel an answer from a third that will not give one. What it took to live with a grant while a person still pronounced it is what the film shows. What it takes when nothing pronounces it is a further question, and this chapter's task ends where that question becomes askable.

## Reach, selection, and the refusal to resolve

The city and the cut do not swap jobs, and the film's doors mark the boundary between them. People use the city's reach: a lift, an address, a stamp. The doorman administers a grant inside that arrangement, and neither act is the cut taking the city's job.

In 1981 Scott Feld asked how people come to know one another without a matchmaker. His answer was the focus, a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized (a workplace, a voluntary organization, a hangout, a family), such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^28] Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organized. However, the people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster, and the shared activity that produces Feld's ties is missing. What remains is the holding I have been calling reach. The newest scholarship on the film reads its Austin as a myth, the emblem a generation uses to mourn a city that has since become something else; the reading is right about the film's afterlife, and the arrangement described here is the thing the myth mythologizes.[^29] Sarah Barns describes digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange with "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom, which is nearer to selection, and to what the camera does when it chooses the next person.[^30] The city supplies the one, the cut supplies the other, and the door, the same afternoon, still has to say the grant out loud.

Michael Warner's account of a public constitutes strangers as a collectivity precisely through their common relation to something outside them, with no interaction among them required and membership arriving by mere attention.[^31] Warner's public is addressed. It does not have someone choosing which two of its members are put in front of each other next, and that choosing is what I have been calling selection. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga already split match quality from match probability in their account of what cities do for the people in them, a precedent for treating reach and matching as separable functions.[^32] They split the two in economic form. The film shows them performed by visibly different agents.

The film will not close the questions the city is not built to answer. To produce Gary, or to let the man at Les Amis specify the true call, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk, would be to supply from outside precisely the introductions the arrangement does not make. The film's recurring question, asked of Gary, and of Paul, and implicitly of everyone who leaves the frame and does not return, stays open. A door still has to say the rule out loud. A camera still has to choose the next person without telling anyone why.

## Conclusion

The taxi and the struck woman share a city. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away, and give no further reason. Gary is asked after and never produced. Linklater leaves those questions hanging so that the two jobs stay in view. Simmel describes the distance. Royce describes the membership test. Reich describes the grant. Stark and Pais describe the enrollment that later withdrew anyone who could be asked.

The platform keeps the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason, and withdraws the person who had to say them out loud. Now that no one has to answer, we can still hear the people on screen ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
