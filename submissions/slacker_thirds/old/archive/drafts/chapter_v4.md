# Reach and Handoff: *Slacker*'s Thirds

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

## 1. The problem, and what I do about it

*Slacker* moves through about a hundred people in a single day in Austin and gives none of them a story. They talk, walk out of the frame, and do not come back. Most never meet each other. Nothing returns and nothing converges, and audiences follow it without much trouble. Every published account of why says the same thing: the camera joins what the characters do not. That is true, and it stops one question short. It names the joiner without saying what kind of coordinator a camera is.

Sociology has a name for a party who coordinates people who never deal with each other. Simmel called it the third. The case that matters here is the one he set aside mid-sentence, promising to return to it. What grew from him instead was brokerage, and a broker knows both parties and stands between them. A sidewalk knows nobody. Neither does the cut that moves *Slacker* from a taxi ride to a street accident. Both coordinate strangers, and neither of them brokers anything.

The gap is not only a film problem. When a platform sequences strangers, putting a driver with a rider or a viewer with a title, the usual move is to say it does industrially what a camera has always done. The resemblance is real and narrower than it looks, and taking it for more gets the politics wrong. A camera hands you the next scene. It cannot price your passage, rate you, or take you off the street. The doorman in *Slacker*'s last hour can do all three, and he arranges nobody's evening at all.

So I ask two questions of anything standing between strangers, and keep them apart. Does it hold people where they can summon one another, without naming which two? I call that **reach**, and 1990 Austin has it: a man asks a café table after a missing friend, and the stranger who answers him is standing somewhere else entirely. Does it decide which particular party comes next, for a particular participant, in sequence? I call that **handoff**, and the film's cut has it absolutely, since every viewer since 1990 has met these hundred people in the same order. The questions are independent, so they sort into four cells rather than two kinds of third. Crossing all four is a separate register of capacities. A third can price an exchange, evaluate a party, remove a party, and withhold its reason, and none of those follows from either question. To put the pair to work, I counted: two coders, each blind to the other's sheet, asking both questions of all thirty-four transitions between the film's segments.

Three things came out. The joining migrates: the cut carries the first third nearly alone, the characters take over in the middle, and at dawn the cut takes it back. Only six of the thirty-four handoffs are anybody's deliberate act. Second, a film cut and a dispatch queue land in the same cell and differ entirely in what they can do to the people they sequence, which is where the analogy earns its keep and where it stops. Third, what makes a coordinating third arguable turns out not to be whether it explains itself, because a cut never explains itself either. It is whether the instrument can change hands.

## 2. What joins them

A man fresh off an overnight bus rides into Austin telling his taxi driver about the lives he didn't choose. Every alternative he turned down back at the station, he explains, split off and continues somewhere as its own reality, one of them with a beautiful woman and her great apartment. The driver never answers him. In the film's next segment a woman lies struck in the street, and the strangers who gather manage the emergency entirely out loud. One calls for an ambulance, one reaches her son by telephone, and one presses his business card on a witness — "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it."[^1] The offer produces nothing; he leaves before the police arrive, and a bystander assembles the story the scene has been withholding: "Looks like some guy, uh, ran over his mother." Nothing audible joins the taxi to the accident. The film simply cuts, and two strangers who will never learn of each other stand a scene apart.

Two segments later the film makes a join that nobody in it intended. In a café a man is dictating aphorisms (he is Dostoyevsky, his companion is Anna, they are writing *The Gambler*) when a passerby interrupts the dictation to ask after a friend: "You seen Gary around?" Nobody has seen Gary for months. The passerby moves on, and the next segment's second line belongs to a different man entirely, a stranger who says he overheard that a friend has gone missing. No one made it on purpose. The asker asked one man, and another man answered. What follows runs about a hundred and thirty lines of transcript: a suppressed moon program, colonies on Mars since 1962, a rain-forest drug that leaves "perfect employees."

Answering a stranger costs you the next few minutes, and this film's talkers know it. Emanuel Schegloff, working from tape recordings of calls to a police complaint desk in a middle-sized Midwestern city, found that a summons binds whoever takes it up. The answerer "commits himself to staying with the encounter," and a member of society "may not 'naively choose' not to answer a summons," because silence gets read, as a cold shoulder among other things. Schegloff extended the rule himself from telephones to strangers standing near one another, counting a courtesy phrase and a tap on the shoulder as summonses.[^2] The man who answered keeps his listener that whole time and passes him on to nobody. Then he stops a second man on the street, "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing," and releases him with a courtesy of his own: "you're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." The street supplied the opening twice. It arranged nothing.

Density alone will not deliver reach, which is what makes the claim falsifiable. Exposure segregation — how rarely people of different incomes actually cross paths — runs 67 percent higher in the ten largest American metropolitan areas than in the smallest,[^3] and at street level the concentration of passersby explains 26 percent of observed social mixing while adjacent amenities, residential diversity, and income levels account for 44 percent.[^4] A big legible city can fail at this. What reach buys beyond "dense downtown" is Schegloff's rule, a mechanism operating at the scale of a single exchange.

And the Austin doing the reaching is the edited one. Brendan Gaughen, walking the locations decades later, found exactly one covert jump. The cut treats about three miles, the Old Anarchist's place down to a bridge at 35th and Mopac, as one burglar's walk, and he flags the survey as non-exhaustive and outdoor-only.[^5] Austin viewers complained about the geography anyway in 1990, and Linklater's answer conceded the map while keeping the grammar: "If you didn't know Austin, the film makes sense. … They're still walking right to left. Syntactically, it works."[^6] The city of 1990 enters this chapter as a condition the fiction drew on, never as evidence that anything was coordinated.

## 3. The joining migrates

Across its running order the film changes its mind about who does the joining. Of its first fifteen transitions, four or five carry an audible link. Of the middle fifteen (16→17 through 30→31), eleven do. Of the last four, none the transcript can show. The early bridges are the missing-friend question at 4→5 and, at 6→7, a report that a housemate named Paul has vanished, closed with an invitation — "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." Then the middle of the film stacks them: a lift into town asked for and granted at 18→19, an address given to a reluctant driver at 23→24, a guest list and a van at 27→28, a stamp at 29→30, a camera handed to a stranger at 30→31. Then, from 31→32 to the end, nothing audible crosses a seam at all.

Those figures come from a census. The film runs thirty-five segments, thirty-four of them with dialogue, and thirty-four transitions between them. Two coders, each working from the same transcript without sight of the other's work, put two questions to every transition. Does anything audible cross the seam (a line spoken across it, a word or a topic answered on the far side)? And does an act aimed by one character at another produce the next encounter? The codings agree on 82 percent of the first question (κ = 0.75) and 91 percent of the second (κ = 0.84).[^7] Fifteen or sixteen transitions carry an audible link; four or five are equivocal. The instrument is a transcript of the film's subtitle track, and subtitles compress and paraphrase, so an afternoon with the disc may move these numbers.

Six transitions, identical in both codings, are deliberate, five of them in the film's middle third. Deliberate means an act aimed at another person that intends the next encounter, whether an offer, an invitation, an address, or an object handed over with a purpose. A character who announces his own next move and goes there alone doesn't qualify. The six, in order: the invitation to come look at Paul's emptied room; the lift into town ("How about a lift back into town?" — "Yeah"); the address pressed on a driver who wants no part of the trip ("The address, man. Take me there. Drop me off"); the guest list and the van offered to two women who accept ("Do I have a car? I have a van"); the stamp offered at one door, demonstrated, and claimed again at the very next one; and the camera handed off at a party — "Shoot whatever you want. Pass it around … But I want my camera back, all right?"

The census's own finding is an asymmetry. No transition in either coding pairs a silent seam with a deliberate act. Every handoff anyone in this film intends is also audible where it happens. The reverse is everywhere. Among the seams the codings leave bare, five hold a checkable offer, invitation, or destination the film declines to follow: the ride and the card at the accident, both refused; two exit lines from the pap-smear pitch (a collector named Chico on the east side, Stephanie's Friday guest list), neither pursued; the Video Backpacker's standing "Sure. I'll be here"; a bar errand ("I'll buy this round if you, uh, score me a pack of smokes, man") that dispatches a man the film never follows; and a recommendation to catch *Blow-Up* at 4:00, declined in the dialogue itself. The film hears its offers; it just doesn't take them.

Then dawn, and the pattern falsifies itself from inside. Segments 33 and 34 are address with no addressee — a recorded voice meditating on mourning and freedom, a hail into the street ("Wake up, America"), a man ranting a weapons give-away program at nobody. The four transitions that carry them carry nothing audible at all, and the cut has the film back.

Five seams stay equivocal on at least one of the two codings, and the disc could settle each one. The list, with what each turns on, is in the notes.[^7a] One of them, at 22→23, is why the figure is a band and not a number: an object changes hands unnamed, and if it is the rare Marquis de Sade the burglars admire two lines later, the seam is a bridge. If not, it is bare [DISC:T6]. Three pairs of segments also share overlapping line ranges and several boundaries fall inside continuous exchanges, so a few codes describe where I drew a seam rather than where the film cut. They stay in the count, flagged, because deleting any of them would manufacture the point estimate the band exists to refuse.

## 4. Two questions, four cells

Back on the sidewalk in segment 8, a man recognizes Stephanie, just back from a hospital stay in Dallas, and gets an offer inside two minutes of talk. Her band plays Friday, and "If you wanna come, I can put you on the list." A third arrives mid-story — an old man came down the freeway from San Antonio at a hundred miles an hour, waving a gun and laughing, chased by squad cars, dead by his own hand on the median. From that he pivots straight to a lab slide he calls a Madonna pap smear: "Do you think maybe, uh, you'd be interested in buying something like this?" Nobody buys, and he takes the refusal in character: "It's a material world and I'm a material girl."

Put the two questions to that sidewalk and they answer opposite ways. It has reach: the recognition, the gun story, and the sales pitch all happen because the street held these people within speaking distance of one another. It has no handoff. Everything said is addressed to whoever happens to be standing there, and nobody assigns anyone to anybody. Because the questions come apart like that, the cases fall into four cells. The club door late in the film answers no to both, subtracting encounters and arranging none. The film's own cut answers no and yes. It holds nobody in talking range and hands its viewer a specific next scene, every time. Uber's dispatch answers the same way, over people who then have to live with the pairing. So does Amazon's submissions page, over films. Four cells, not two kinds of third.

Handoff also comes in degrees, which is why a cleaner test — does a film or a novel select, or doesn't it — would miss the only thing here worth measuring. This film's own degree swings across its runtime. Four or five audible links in the first fifteen seams, eleven in the next fifteen, none the transcript can show in the last four. And a residual stays open once the sorting is done. The cut, the dispatch queue, and the submissions page share a cell, and the sorting by itself says nothing about what else each of them can do to the parties it sequences.

What the characters arrange, the film sometimes honors. The invitation and the van in segment 27 produce segment 28's ride, one of the six deliberate handoffs, kept exactly as spoken. Then the door refuses the very name the arrangement was made in: "The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E." — "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." An arrangement and its outcome are two different claims, and this film keeps them apart with something close to rigor: the ride happens because someone said it would, and the entry fails although someone said it wouldn't.

The distinction has ancestry, and some of the ancestry pushes back. Simmel, whose triad opened this line of thought, set the question aside in the middle of drawing it. Elements unified "by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them" (his examples are an alliance against a common enemy and the invisible church) are, he writes, a mediating function "to be discussed in a later context."[^8] He promised a return, and the promise marks the gap this chapter works in. The German *Theorien des Dritten* debate names a related pair, which I report at second hand: *konkreter* versus *abstrakter Dritter*, the concrete third who stands between identified parties and the abstract third who never appears.[^9] The platform literature mostly runs the other way. Meyer and colleagues, from whom I cite the published abstract and no more, divide matching mechanisms from attention mechanisms and find both "coexist on most platforms";[^10] O'Reilly, Strauss, and Mazzucato (whom I also know only at second hand) fold everything into one function. Each dominant platform is "an attention gatekeeper of one kind or another."[^11] That unification is the strongest complication on record, and the grid concedes it by construction. The axes are independent, so a single platform can occupy more than one cell at once. What the grid refuses is only the inference from one cell to another, from where a third sits to what it can do.

## 5. What a third can do to you

"What's up, dude? I'm on the guest list." The door's answer prices him instead: "Three dollars." He spells his own name at the doorman — "The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four" — and gets it handed back: "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." He argues the one fact he has, that the band on stage is his roommate's. The door does not move. Four capacities are on display around that door across the film's last stretch, and I want them on the table by name: a third can price an exchange, evaluate a party, remove a party, and withhold the reason. The fourth is its own power and no version of the other three, because a contested party needs the reason first, before any argument about the price, the verdict, or the removal can begin. None of the four follows from choosing which two parties meet.

Work the door through the register. It prices. A cover is named out loud at five dollars, at the door in segment 29 and again at the head of segment 30. It evaluates. A name offered in good faith is checked against a list and refused. It removes. At two in the morning the room is cleared with the reason said aloud and no destination named — "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." And through all of it the door arranges nothing; it never chooses which two people will meet, and the one deliberate handoff the census records at that door is a stranger's, not the doorman's. So a concession is unavoidable. Pricing a passage and expelling a party are powers that owe nothing to selection. The register cuts across the grid, which is why the two stay separate. Choosing which two parties come together entails no right to charge either of them, and a market square, the least selective third there is, can still charge rent and call the police.

The platforms hold the fourth capacity in writing. Amazon's licensing page for Prime Video, a living page quoted as retrieved, reads: "Unless otherwise indicated, removed titles (or titles not selected for licensing) may not be resubmitted or appealed. All content is selected at the sole discretion of Prime Video, and we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected for licensing." The same page promises visibility in a rights holder's portal when a title isn't selected, so the verdict arrives; the reasons don't.[^12] Evaluation is on the page too. Once a title is live, "performance metrics and viewing behavior" (buy and rent transactions, unique customer streams, total hours) decide which content "is resonating," and content that fails the engagement standards "may be subject to removal." Price, verdict, removal, and a stated policy of unstated reasons, all four capacities in one document. On Uber's side the evidence is narrower than the folklore, and I'll keep it that way. The survey is a convenience sample, 810 California drivers recruited through a drivers' union's networks between April and July 2022, and its own authors caution that people wanting to document a deactivation were likelier to respond. Two-thirds reported having been deactivated at some point, temporarily or permanently, and 30 percent of those were given no reason at all.[^13]

The film's cut is the boundary case, because a cut never explains itself either, and a viewer has no decision to appeal. The difference sits in what can be revisited. The Video Backpacker, surrounded by his running televisions in segment 24, complains that a real death he once witnessed left him nothing to consult — "I have no reference to it now. I can't refer back to it. I can't press rewind. I can't put it on pause." A recorded image can be looked at again, which is what an appeal would need. And the doorman, unlike the cut, explains — the hour, the list, his own wish to go home — and explaining is exactly what makes him arguable.

## 6. The queue and the cut

An Uber driver waiting for airport work joins a queue and watches a number. The app shows a dynamic estimate of the wait until the next offer, updated on three stated inputs: how many riders are requesting trips, how many drivers are ahead, recent flight arrivals and cancellations. The company's own help page names position as "a priority factor for trip matches."[^14] Position is also the lever. Refusing a request outright, or canceling one after accepting it, "could result in your spot in the queue resetting"; a rider's own cancellation doesn't cost the driver his spot. And the exchange the queue delivers is priced around him. Since early 2023 in London, the fare a passenger pays and the fee a driver receives vary independently, the passenger's number never appears in the driver's app, and Uber bars him from asking for it. The findings come from an audit of 1.5 million trips donated by 258 UK drivers, a self-selected sample the researchers describe as exactly that.[^15]

Set the queue beside the cut and the handoff question can't tell them apart. Each hands a specific party the specific next thing, in sequence, no negotiation. The capacities differ. The queue prices the exchange it arranges; the cut hands a viewer the next scene and prices nothing. The film's own version of an arranged next party is the guest list — "I made him put me on the guest list, and I'm on the guest list" — an arrangement with a name on it, made out loud in front of witnesses, checkable at the door. Everything about it can be seen by everyone standing there, and when it fails, it fails in public too — "Fuck. I'm not on the list." "Figures." — with the whole group left on the sidewalk to renegotiate the evening out loud. The queue's arrangements happen to one driver at a time, inside an app, on inputs only partly stated, and its failures arrive the same way.

Enclosure is a strong word, and I rest it on a single dated pair. When Roderik Smits did his fieldwork on film-distribution gatekeeping in 2017, Amazon's route stood open. Rights holders could submit films directly, and the process was "unfiltered, with no gatekeeping restrictions to making such films available."[^16] The same company's page, retrieved 1 August 2026, reads: "Access to self-service submissions is by invitation only. We are not currently accepting new content submissions for rent or buy from content providers that do not already have an account."[^17] One channel tightened, in writing, inside a decade — and it's the channel a film shot for $23,000, as *Slacker* famously was, would have used.[^18]

In domestic theatrical distribution the middle tier, the specialty distributors sitting between the major studios and the smallest outfits, refilled. The "late indies" of 2009–2019 averaged 6.46 percent of the market against the 6.91 percent the vanished studio specialty divisions had held in 1998–2008, even as the surviving divisions themselves fell to 3.11.[^19] And Waldfogel (on his published abstract, at second hand, since I haven't opened the body) documents "substantial growth in the number of movies brought to market" in the digital era.[^20] So no general gate slammed on independent film. The claim narrows to one named channel on one dated page.

What the cut allows that the queue doesn't is re-inspection, and the film states the point itself. The Video Backpacker's whole case for the recorded image over the actual event is that the event can't be consulted afterward: no rewind, no pause, no slow motion for "all the little details." A film, whatever else its cut withholds, can be watched again; the handoffs this chapter counts are countable because anyone with the disc can recount them. A driver can't replay an allocation. The offer expires, the queue moves, the fare behind it stays sealed, and the one party who could describe the pairing from the other side is the party he's barred from asking. That gap between the two, replayable against sealed, is a capacity difference, and nothing in the cell they share predicted it.

## 7. What cuts taught

The film's first rhyme belongs to nobody in it. Riding in from the bus, the taxi passenger muses that in dreams "there's always someone getting run over or something really weird"; twenty-nine lines into the next segment, a bystander at the accident works out that the driver ran over his own mother. Both codings record the echo, and both record its agency as none. No character makes the connection, notices it, or acts on it. The man who said the line has left the film for good before the line comes true. His other idle image lands the same way: "you're probably giving someone else a ride, you know?" he tells the driver, and one segment later a stranger is offering a ride at the roadside. The joining here is entirely the film's. The following of it is entirely the viewer's, and following it is a skill with a measurable edge.

The measurements exist, and they're narrower than the folklore about a trained century of viewers. Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing showed edited sequences to forty adults from mountain villages south of Isparta, Turkey: twenty with no prior experience of film or television, from a district only recently connected to the electrical grid, and twenty from nearby with some television, a younger group with more schooling, in a small, elderly, precisely described sample. On the gravestone sequence, all twenty experienced viewers linked the shots in space and time, and eleven of them (55 percent) read the man's face as sad, the classic Kuleshov reading. On the child sequence, none showed the effect at all. The twenty first-time viewers, able to describe every shot, "perceived them to be wholly separate from each other."[^21] In a companion study, sound helped. Dialogue and salient noise carrying across a cut let first-time viewers connect shots, though not always, and one unfamiliar sequence integrated for none of them.[^22] Shot-joining is learned, cheaply and incompletely, and the audible link is the easiest join there is. A census of what can be heard at this film's seams is a census of its lowest rungs.

Does the trained competence generalize? The one study on record that asks reports that the evidence "does not support the hypothesis" that more exposure to faster-cut movies, television, or touchscreens brings a general IQ advantage, and offers, introduced in its own words as "One explanation could be," that the benefit "may be specific to the domain of movie narrative comprehension."[^23] That's what was measured, and I'll stop where it stops.

The strongest positive case for a competent audience is this film's own, and it cuts a precise shape. In 1990, Austin viewers audited the handoffs against a city they knew and told Linklater to his face: "It doesn't make sense!" They'd caught characters rounding a corner from Sound Exchange straight into Half Price Books.[^24] His defense was syntax over geography. Those viewers contested a handoff's content, line by line, corner by corner. Whether an audience trained on a century of cuts can contest the order itself — why this stranger next and not that one — is a different competence, and nothing measured shows it.

## 8. Instruments you hand on

A card changes hands on the street. The woman handing them out in segment 25 attaches no instruction and no reason: "No two cards are alike. Think about whatever you want and take a card." A passerby takes one and, asked which he got, reads out the line the film is best remembered by:

> "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy."[^25]

Another man at the bar takes his card, "It's not building a wall, but making a brick," as confirmation of the "total recalibration" he has been announcing all day: "There is no structure. The underlying order is chaos."[^26] The instrument arrives blind and works anyway, because it can be read aloud, asked about, drawn again ("Could I have another one? Is that allowed?"), and handed to the next table.

The stamp is the same kind of object with a cover charge attached. Outside one door a man hears the cover is five bucks and makes his offer: "But, uh, I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in or something." He demonstrates the transfer: "Here, watch. It's easy. Watch. You just do this" — whatever exactly *this* is [DISC:S5]. At the very next door, against a five-dollar cover, the claim is made again by the group he made it to. A rule that can be said can be copied, and copied by strangers who met its owner two minutes ago.

The camera is the instrument at its most explicit. At the party in segment 30 a man names it aloud, "it's my PixelVision camera," and gives it away with terms attached: shoot whatever you want, pass it around, give it back. Ten segments earlier the film held the mirror case, a camera already inside the film, interviewing a man on the street who refuses its terms. He'll take a job "when I hear the true call," he won't say what the true call is, and he closes the frame himself: "End of interview."

That's where the chapter's open question lands, and it isn't the one I expected to end on. The obvious question, whether the coordinating third explains itself, turns out to sort almost nothing, because a cut never explains itself and neither does a deactivation, and only one of the two feels like an injustice. The usable question is about the instruments. The club's rule is sayable and its stamp is copyable; the guest list has a name on it that can be spelled at a doorman. A queue position can't be held, read, or passed to someone else, and the reason for a removal is, in writing, the thing not provided. What would make the new engineering thirds, the platforms that broker each match, contestable is what the film's coordinating objects already have: a form that can change hands. At the door in segment 31 the rule arrives out loud — past 2:00, everybody out, you gotta be somewhere and you can't be here — and the room empties into the street, where the film's last talkers are still going. One gate refused them.
