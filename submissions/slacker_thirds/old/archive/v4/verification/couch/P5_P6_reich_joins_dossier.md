# Deep research: P5–P6, Reich reception and the chapter's join claims

Run 2026-08-12 as a targeted source-retrieval and falsification pass. Scope: `A-C1`–`A-C8`,
`B-C1`–`B-C4`, `A-S8`, `A-S9`, `A-R4`, and `A-R8`. The run tested two propositions:

1. whether contemporary reception treats Charles Reich as having named a legal form of dependence,
   rather than only having made a normative demand; and
2. whether anyone has already made the chapter's joins among city, camera, deferred third,
   membership-without-meeting, grant, and platform.

The result is asymmetrical. The Reich side has a substantial couch, but it requires a correction:
reception treats *The New Property* as both diagnosis and prescription. Reich's diagnosis is the
growth of government-created wealth held on terms fixed by the grantor; his prescription is to
convert essential forms of that wealth from gratuities or privileges into protected entitlements.
The sentence about undisclosed reasons is part of the prescription, not a fourth item in a received
taxonomy of grantor powers. The private-platform side has strong functional analogues, but the
verified platform texts usually do not cite Reich. The join side is mostly absence. No source found
narrates the 1908–1913–1964 sequence or defines platform as grant plus Roycean
membership-without-meeting plus Simmelian *Distanz*.

This was not a three-agent run, so no fictive 3–0 votes are printed. Each finding instead records
read depth and provenance. Provenance follows the project ladder: `A` = retrieved full text with
verbatim evidence; `B` = convergence-verified; `C` = metadata, abstract, snippet, or one-lens
evidence; `D` = needs a person or library.

## Finding 1 — Reception treats Reich as diagnosis plus demand, not form instead of demand (high; A)

The reception does not support a clean choice between “named a form” and “made a demand.” It
consistently attributes both moves to him. The form is a relation of dependence on
government-created wealth—benefits, jobs, licenses, contracts, franchises, subsidies, services—held
subject to public power. The demand is that removal of those interests carry substantive and
procedural protections and, in Reich's strongest formulation, that essential forms cease to be
gratuities and become rights.

Kali Murray supplies the most concise contemporary digest. She says that Reich's claim was that
“certain procedural and constitutional rights should accompany the removal of entitlements,” and
defines the new property as government-created wealth including “income and other benefits, jobs,
occupational licenses, franchises, contracts, subsidies, use of public resources, and services”
(2020, 714–15). She then quotes *Goldberg v. Kelly*'s reception of Reich: “It may be realistic
today to regard welfare entitlements as more like ‘property’ than a ‘gratuity’” (397 U.S. 254,
263 n.8).

Paul Verkuil's twenty-five-year retrospective calls *The New Property* a “new synthesis” and “the
primary technique for converting the due process clause into a tool of civil justice” (1990, 365).
He says the *Goldberg* majority “virtually adopted *The New Property* for its analytical framework”
and “constitutionalized a judicial-like procedure for deciding welfare cases” (367–68). His
criticism is not that Reich offered no form, but that Reich's full-adjudication prescription
protected insiders, could delay access for outsiders, and did not design a workable mass
administrative system (366–70).

Thomas Merrill describes the post-*Roth* settlement more narrowly. For procedural due process,
property became a threshold interest whose source and dimensions are supplied by
nonconstitutional law. He calls the resulting approach “pure positivism” and proposes
“property-as-entitlement”: an interest conferred by nonconstitutional law, with monetary value,
terminable only when a specified condition is satisfied (2000, 916–17, 960–68). This is a
doctrinal descendant of Reich, but not Reich's full claim that economic security should be made
property.

**Verdict:** BOTH. `A-C1` and `A-C5` must be reframed. Reich named a form of dependence and argued
for its conversion into protected entitlement. He did not name “withholding undisclosed reasons”
as one received power in a structural taxonomy.

## Finding 2 — *Goldberg* adopted the entitlement frame; *Roth* relocated its source (high; A)

The standard reception sequence is stable. *Goldberg* used Reich to reject gratuity language and
attach pretermination process to welfare benefits. *Roth* did not simply “overrule Reich.” It made
property a threshold category and located property interests in “existing rules or understandings”
from an independent source such as state law. That move narrowed the constitutional project by
making the grant's source define whether an entitlement exists.

Murray states that *Goldberg* “cited Reich's theory of new property entitlements with approval”
(2020, 714–15). Verkuil says the majority “virtually adopted” Reich's framework but exposed an
inside/outside problem: process for current recipients can make entry harder for applicants
(1990, 367–69). Merrill calls the two-year period from *Goldberg* to *Roth* a movement from an
“important interest” approach to an insistence that the claimant first identify property; he then
tracks the “positivist trap” created when the same nonconstitutional source defines both the
entitlement and its dimensions (2000, 887–90, 916–26).

Van Alstyne, Monaghan, Mashaw, *Arnett*, and *Loudermill* belong in the doctrinal history, but this
pass did not obtain clean full-text evidence from each. Merrill's retrieved article maps that
literature and identifies the “bitter with the sweet” controversy as settled by *Cleveland Board of
Education v. Loudermill*, 470 U.S. 532, 539–41 (1985) (Merrill 2000, 890–91 n.16). Do not assign
unopened authors a more specific thesis from this run.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTS a qualified doctrinal history for `B-C1`–`B-C3`; COMPLICATES any sentence
that says *Roth* simply killed Reich. *Roth* retained entitlement analysis but made its source
positivist.

## Finding 3 — Reich himself carried the problem to large private organizations (high; A)

The private extension is not merely latent in the last page of the 1964 article. In his
twenty-five-year retrospective Reich made it explicit. He opens with the conflict between democratic
ideals and large institutions that are “authoritarian, not democratic,” in which “[t]here is also
little concept of due process or the rule of law” (1990, 295). He defines sustaining wealth as
“the jobs we hold, plus benefits, credentials, licenses, public welfare and all of the other kinds
of valuables that come from large organizations and government” (295).

The decisive passage is at pp. 300–01:

> “As we look at these examples, we see that the public and private sectors are not so different.
> There is less and less reason, it seems to me, to treat a large private institution any
> differently than the government with respect to new property issues.”

He illustrates the point with the NCAA's suspension of a coach and argues that large private
organizations can impose catastrophic job forfeitures “with no due process whatsoever” (300–01).
This is not yet a platform theory, but it closes the factual premise that Reich wrote only about
government and never carried the structure to private doors.

**Verdict:** `A-C7` is FOUND but must be rewritten. The *platform* extension is the chapter's;
the *private-organization* extension is Reich's own by 1990. `B-C4` is FOUND.

**Source:** Charles A. Reich, “The Liberty Impact of the New Property,” *William & Mary Law
Review* 31 (1990): 295–306. Full text retrieved from the publisher repository.

Cohen is the closest functional bridge. At pp. 136–39 she describes platforms as sites of
algorithmically mediated encounter that combine access and legibility. At p. 139 she writes that
access to Amazon, Google, payment systems, and mobile operating systems requires assent to complex
legal and technical protocols and is “increasingly essential to reaching any customers at all.”
At pp. 144–45 she calls platforms strategies for bounding networks and controlling infrastructures:
their protocols “afford[] access but also points of contact for exercises of technological and
political authority.” At pp. 146–47 she adds that a platform forms separate relationships with
each side, can define the terms differently, and controls matching and preferential placement.

Rahman supplies a parallel public-utility genealogy, not a Reich genealogy. His platform powers are
gatekeeping, transmission, and scoring. Van Loo supplies procedure for private platform disputes.
Citron supplies the automated-process framework, but for public agencies. Their failure to cite
Reich is itself useful: the same institutional problem has been reconstructed through platform,
utility, and administrative-process vocabularies without a shared “new property” lineage.

For Anderson, Pasquale, Klonick, Radin, Dubal, and Rosenblat, named-work searches did not produce a

## Finding 5 — Digital access is routinely a revocable, nontransferable license, not a transferable entitlement (high; A)

The strongest couch for `A-C6` comes from the property/contract interface literature, not from
Reich reception.

Christina Mulligan shows that end-user licenses govern whether software and digital media may be
transferred and that EULAs combine contract-like and property-like features (2018, 1073–76).
She calls the many-users/one-licensor relation “compound-paucital”: one identified rights-holder
faces many identified duty-holders. The single rights-holder determines the terms, has aggregated
incentives to favor itself, and the many users are not positioned to negotiate (1082–86). Her
article separately treats restraints on alienation, the license/sale distinction, and revocation
(1094–1119).

Justin Tzeng states the digital item form with unusual precision. In virtual worlds, EULAs replace
property defaults with “revocable, non-transferable license grants to access” in-game items and give
developers discretion to modify or terminate them (2022, 227–28). He argues that this plenary
discretion exceeds what developers need and that selective commitments to ownership or disposition
could create value (242–66).

These sources establish the transferable/nontransferable distinction the guest-list/stamp allegory
needs:

- transferability and alienability are incidents associated with sale/property;
- account-bound access is commonly drafted as personal, revocable, and nontransferable;
- the grantor retains authority to alter terms or terminate access;
- calling the transaction a “license” does legal and distributive work.

They do **not** establish that all platform participation is legally a license or that Reich's
government-largess doctrine controls private user accounts.

**Verdict:** `A-C6` is REFRAME. The exact film-to-platform inheritance remains the author's, but
the grant-versus-property distinction now has a verified digital-law couch.

**Sources**

## Finding 6 — The four-power taxonomy is the chapter's; no received Reich taxonomy matches it (high; A/B)

No retrieved reception source organizes Reich into the chapter's four powers—price, evaluate,
remove, withhold. The received categories are different:

- Reich: rights of holders, powers of government, and the procedure adjusting them;
- Murray: entitlement removal plus privacy, equality, dignity, and vulnerability;
- Merrill: entitlement, monetary value, and for-cause termination;
- Cohen: access, extraction, immunity, and interdiction;
- Rahman: gatekeeping, transmission, and scoring;
- Van Loo: platform dispute procedure.

The chapter can use Reich to identify dependence on a grantor and the need for protection against
arbitrary removal. It can use Cohen, Rahman, and the platform-work literature for powers exercised
by private platforms. It cannot present the four-item list as a secondary digest of Reich.

The same limit applies to `A-C4`. “Counterparty writes and changes terms, decides disputes in its
own forum, and owes no procedure” is a synthesis of (a) Reich's largess relation and
(b) contemporary private-platform governance. It is not a standard page-pinned paraphrase of
*The New Property* located in this run.

**Verdict:** `A-C2` is OWN-AS-AUTHOR; `A-C3` and `A-C4` are REFRAME.

## Finding 7 — The city/camera join has close neighbours, but no Simmelian deferred-third claimant (high; A)

Maria Teresa Soldani is the closest *Slacker*-specific source. She describes the film as a
twenty-four-hour urban narration with no main character, a “collective urban tale” that maps Austin
through multiple trajectories, and a chain in which each scene flows into the next (2017, 72–75).
She reads the Austin scene as a “circumscribed unit of time and space,” describes the camera
drifting “from character to character and from place to place,” and says that the film converts the
local scene's collective body into a representation of a generation (79–85).

That is genuine prior art for the day/city/camera architecture. It is not prior art for the
deferred-third claim. Soldani's terms are city symphony, scene, synecdoche, *dérive*,
time-image, chronotope, and generation. Her article contains no Simmel, deferred third,
*Distanz*, Reich, or Royce.

Scott McQuire's *The Media City* offers a broader neighbour: modern spatial experience is
co-constituted by architectural structures, urban territories, social practices, and media
feedback, and the contemporary city is a “media-architecture complex.” Sarah Barns gives the
platform-urbanism neighbour: digital platforms act as infrastructures of urban exchange and
institute “basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects” (2020, 1). Neither source
makes the chapter's city-as-reach/camera-as-selection division.

Feld and Small pre-name hosting mechanisms, not cinematic selectors. The prior verified S2 dossier
already establishes that Feld 1981 was not opened and that *Unanticipated Gains* was not opened.
This pass retrieved Small's author page and a book review, which confirm that childcare-centre
rules shape unanticipated ties among mothers. That is not enough to make a new page-pinned claim
from the book. Keep the project's ban on citing *Unanticipated Gains* as if read.

**Verdict:** `A-S8` is OWN-AS-AUTHOR with Soldani as adjacent couch. `A-S9` is REFRAME: Cohen and
Barns verify access/matching and Soldani verifies city/day/camera organization, but “city = reach,
camera = selection” remains the chapter's explicit analytic division.

## Finding 8 — Place-myth and memory scholarship does not make the Roycean join (medium-high; A/C)

Erickson and Long support a claim about Austin place-myth and the film's afterlife. Soldani supports
a related movement from local scene to generational identity. A separate urban-memory literature
shows that films can become commemorative objects and experiences, and that urban myths can bind
collective identity to territory. None of the retrieved sources uses Josiah Royce to analyze
*Slacker*, Austin's place-myth, or later audiences' adoption of a filmed past.

The nearest falsification result came from exact and paired searches for `Royce`, `"community of
memory"`, `Slacker`, Austin, place myth, film audience, and city. They returned Roycean reference
works on community and unrelated urban collective-memory studies, but no source joining the two.
The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy also states the limiting condition that matters: for Royce,
community requires communication and some congruence of feeling, thought, and will. Thus merely
being represented by the same film or city is not enough.

**Verdict:** `A-R4` and `A-R8` are OWN-AS-AUTHOR. If retained, the chapter must say explicitly that
it is applying Royce's conditions to a reception phenomenon described by Erickson/Long, and must
state the communication/appropriation condition rather than treating shared representation as
automatic membership.

The nearest real architectures remain separate:

- Simmel: a common outside relation can unify isolated elements;

- Reich reception: essential grants create dependence and call for protection;
- Cohen/Barns: platforms combine access, legibility, matching, and authority;
- Mulligan/Tzeng: digital access is often revocable and nontransferable.

Stacking these as prerequisites is the chapter's theoretical operation.

## Short drafting consequences

1. Replace “Reich named access as grant and whose reasons the grantor can withhold” with a two-step
   sentence: Reich diagnosed dependence on organizationally created wealth; he demanded rights and
   reasons against arbitrary withdrawal.
2. Credit Reich, not the chapter, with extending the issue to large private organizations by 1990.
   Credit the chapter with the narrower move from those organizations to platform access.
3. Introduce the four powers as the chapter's analytic taxonomy and source each power independently.
4. Use Mulligan and Tzeng for grant versus property in digital access; do not imply that
   nontransferability alone makes an account Reichian new property.
5. Present city = reach, camera = selection; Roycean place-memory; and the 1908–1913–1964 stack as
   explicit authorial joins. Soldani, McQuire, Barns, Cohen, Erickson, and Long are neighbouring
   literatures, not prior claimants.
