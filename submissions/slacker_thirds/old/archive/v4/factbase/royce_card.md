## The passages, verbatim, with locators

Format: 1913 Macmillan volume and page **/** 1968 Chicago page. Quotations follow the 1913 setting; the 1968 reset was checked against each and is verbally identical throughout, with the sole change that its running heads carry the lecture title.

### 1. Interpretation as a triadic relation

Lecture XI, "Perception, Conception, and Interpretation," vol. II, pp. 107–164 / 1968 pp. 277–296. The passage the chapter needs is in section XI of that lecture. The two quotations below drawn from pp. 188 and 211 fall in the following lecture, XII, "The Will to Interpret," vol. II, pp. 165–222 / 1968 pp. 297–320.

> "Interpretation always involves a relation of three terms. In the technical phrase, interpretation is a triadic relation. That is, you cannot express any complete process of interpreting by merely naming two terms, — persons, or other objects, — and by then telling what dyadic relation exists between one of these two and the other."
> — **II, 140 / 1968, 286**

The three terms, named:

> "Thus an interpretation is a relation which not only involves three terms, but brings them into a determinate order. One of the three terms is the interpreter ; a second term is the object — the person or the meaning or the text — which is interpreted ; the third is the person to whom the interpretation is addressed."
> — **II, 142 / 1968, 287**

The dyadic contrast, stated on the same page:

> "You may observe that, when a man perceives a thing, the relation is dyadic. A perceives B. A pair of members is needed, and suffices, to make the relation possible. But when A interprets B to C, a triad of members (whereof, as in case of other relations, two or all three members may be wholly, or in part, identical) must exist in order to make the interpretation possible."
> — **II, 142 / 1968, 287**

**Does Royce state the irreducibility, or do commentators infer it?** He states it, twice, in his own words, and the sentence at II, 140 is the explicit form: you *cannot* express a complete interpretation by naming two terms and a dyadic relation between them. He also blocks the obvious reduction in advance. Two lectures' worth of argument (II, 117–125 / 1968, 277–80) establishes that philosophy has run on a "dual classification of our cognitive processes," that Bergson, Kant, James and Russell are "in this respect, at one" (II, 119 / 1968, 278), and that the standard escape — calling knowledge a synthesis of perception and conception effected by an act of will or attention — does not produce a third type:

> "Yet such a recognition of an active synthesis of perception and conception does not by itself enable us to define a genuinely triadic classification of the types of knowing processes."
> — **II, 124 / 1968, 279**

And the flat statement of the claim the chapter wants:

> "But a dual antithesis between perceptual and conceptual knowledge is once for all inadequate to the wealth of the facts of life. When you accomplish an act of comparison, the knowledge which you attain is neither merely conceptual, nor merely perceptual, nor yet merely a practically active synthesis of perception and conception. It is a third type of knowledge. It interprets. It surveys from above."
> — **II, 188 / 1968, 306**

Royce also gives three psychological differences between interpretation and the dyadic modes, and the first of them constrains any use of him:

> "First, interpretation is a conversation, and not a lonely enterprise. There is some one, in the realm of psychological happenings, who addresses some one. The one who addresses interprets some object to the one addressed."
> — **II, 148 / 1968, 289**

> "Thus interpretation is not only an essentially social process, but also a process which, when once initiated, can be terminated only by an external and arbitrary interruption, such as death or social separation. By itself, the process of interpretation calls, in ideal, for an infinite sequence of interpretations."
> — **II, 150 / 1968, 290**

Attribution note that must travel with the quotation: Royce credits the triadic thesis to Peirce and says so on the page — "The observation of Peirce that interpretation is a process involving, from its very essence, a triadic relation" (II, 148 / 1968, 289). The metaphysical generalization to the time-process is Royce's own, and he flags the seam: "For this generalization I cannot cite the authority of Peirce. I must deal with just this aspect of the matter in my own way" (II, 144 / 1968, 288).

### 2. The community of memory — **wording in the prompt is slightly off**

> "A community constituted by the fact that each of its members accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts, may be called a community of memory. Such is any group of persons who individually either remember or commemorate the same dead, — each one finding, because of personal affection or of reverence for the dead, that those whom he commemorates form for him a part of his own past existence."
> — **II, 50 / 1968, 248**

The prompt's version reads "accepts as *a* part of his own individual life." Royce has no article: "accepts as part of his own individual life and self." Both 1913 scans and the 1968 reset agree. The three words "and self" are also load-bearing and are frequently dropped in circulation.

### 3. The community of expectation, or of hope

> "A community constituted by the fact that each of its members accepts, as part of his own individual life and self, the same expected future events that each of his fellows accepts, may be called a community of expectation, or upon occasion, a community of hope."
> — **II, 51 / 1968, 248**

Note the asymmetry in Royce's own text: the memory sentence says "fellow-members," the hope sentence says "fellows." The commas around the middle clause are present in the hope sentence and absent in the memory sentence. Reproduce them.

Ten pages earlier Royce draws the practical consequence the chapter will want:

> "We also see that, if great new undertakings enter into the lives of many men, a new community of hope, unified by the common relations of its individual members to the same future events, may be, upon occasion, very rapidly constituted, even in the midst of great revolutions."
> — **II, 52 / 1968, 249**

### 4. "Congruence of feeling, thought, and will" — **not Royce. `unretrievable` as a quotation.**

The word "congruence" occurs nowhere in either volume of *The Problem of Christianity* and nowhere in *The Philosophy of Loyalty*. Searched: exact string and OCR-tolerant variants (`congru`, `congm`, `congr`) across all four 1913-setting scans and the 1968 reset; also "feeling, thought," "thought, and will," "feeling, and will," "in communication with one another," "communication with one another." No hits.

The formulation is the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on Royce, §2.a, in the encyclopedist's own voice with no citation attached: "communities can only exist, in Royce's view, where individual members are in communication with one another and there exists to some relevant way a congruence of feeling, thought and will among them" (https://iep.utm.edu/roycejos/, read 2026-08-11). The version circulating with "to some extent and in some relevant respect" is a further smoothing of that sentence. **Do not put it in quotation marks and attribute it to Royce.**

Royce's own definition of a community, which is what that sentence paraphrases, is this:

> "And further, let the various ideal extensions, forwards and backwards, include at least one common event, so that each of these selves regards that event as a part of his own life. Then, with reference to the ideal common past and future in question, I say that these selves constitute a community. This is henceforth to be our definition of a community."
> — **II, 59–60 / 1968, 252**

And the three conditions, each stated by Royce as a numbered condition:

- First: "The first condition upon which the existence of a community, in our sense of the word, depends, is the power of an individual self to extend his life, in ideal fashion, so as to regard it as including past and future events which lie far away in time, and which he does not now personally remember." — **II, 60–61 / 1968, 253** (the sentence runs over the 1913 page break, breaking after "so as to regard").
- Second: "there are in the social world a number of distinct selves capable of social communication, and, in general, engaged in communication" — **II, 67 / 1968, 255**.
- Third: "the ideally extended past and future selves of the members include at least some events which are, for all these selves, identical" — **II, 68 / 1968, 256**.

### 5. Loyalty

Preliminary definition, Lecture I, section III, the sentence running over the 1913 page break:

> "Loyalty shall mean, according to this preliminary definition : The willing and practical and thoroughgoing devotion of a person to a cause."
> — ***The Philosophy of Loyalty*, 1908 Macmillan, pp. 16–17**

Royce's own recap of it at the head of Lecture II drops "practical," and the drop is his, not a transcription error: "By loyalty, as you remember, I mean in this preliminary view of loyalty, the willing and practical and thoroughgoing devotion of a person to a cause" (p. 51) keeps it; the Lecture VIII recap reads "Loyalty, so we said at the outset, is the willing and thoroughgoing devotion of a person to a cause. We defined a cause as something that unifies many human lives in one" (p. 351). Quote from pp. 16–17 for the definition and cite p. 351 for the gloss on "cause."

**"Loyalty to loyalty" — the prompt's likely wording is wrong.** The maxim reads:

> "In so far as it lies in your power, so choose your cause and so serve it, that, by reason of your choice and of your service, there shall be more loyalty in the world rather than less. And, in fact, so choose and so serve your individual cause as to secure thereby the greatest possible increase of loyalty amongst men. More briefly: In choosing and in serving the cause to which you are to be loyal, be, in any case, loyal to loyalty."
> — **p. 121** (Lecture III, "Loyalty to Loyalty")

The widely quoted "be, in a word, loyal to loyalty" is not in the text. Both scans read "be, in any case, loyal to loyalty."

The criterion behind it, three pages earlier:

> "And so, a cause is good, not only for me, but for mankind, in so far as it is essentially a loyalty to loyalty, that is, is an aid and a furtherance of loyalty in my fellows."
> — **p. 118**

Final, metaphysical definition, for completeness, since it changes what loyalty is by the end of the book:

> "Loyalty is the will to manifest, so far as is possible, the Eternal, that is, the conscious and superhuman unity of life, in the form of the acts of an individual Self."
> — **p. 357**

**"My life means nothing…" — real, and confirmed.** It is not in *The Philosophy of Loyalty*. It is in *The Problem of Christianity*, Lecture XIV, "The Doctrine of Signs":

> "My life means nothing, either theoretically or practically, unless I am a member of a community."
> — **II, 313 / 1968, 357**

The two sentences that precede it carry the argument and are worth quoting with it: "Practically I cannot be saved alone ; theoretically speaking, I cannot find or even define the truth in terms of my individual experience, without taking account of my relation to the community of those who know. This community, then, is real whatever is real. And in that community my life is interpreted" (II, 312 / 1968, 357).

## The three questions the chapter depends on

### Does Royce require contact between members?

He requires communication, and he does not require meeting. Both halves are in the text and the chapter must carry both.

The requirement, stated by Royce as the second of the three conditions on the existence of a community:

> "The second condition upon which the existence of a community depends is the fact that there are in the social world a number of distinct selves capable of social communication, and, in general, engaged in communication."
> — **II, 67 / 1968, 255**

The qualifier "in general" is Royce's. Capacity for social communication is the hard requirement; actual engagement in it is the normal case rather than the condition.

The passage that comes closest to the chapter's claim is the Maori canoe case, and it is stronger than a hedge. Two tribesmen who each say "I came over in the canoe Tai-Nui" constitute a community with respect to that past, and Royce is explicit that neither acquaintance nor goodwill is needed:

> "Now the belief in this identity of the past self of the ancestor of the canoe, belonging to each of the two New Zealanders, does not in the least depend upon ignoring, or upon minimizing, the present difference between these two selves. The present consciousnesses do not in the least tend to interpenetrate. Neither of the two New Zealanders in question need suppose that there is now any compounding of consciousness. **Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies.** But each has a reason, and an obvious reason, for extending himself into the ancestral past."
> — **II, 46 / 1968, 247** (bold added here, not in Royce)

He then generalizes from it: those who share such a past "constitute, with reference to this common past, a community" (II, 47–48 / 1968, 247–48).

**What the chapter may say.** Royce's community of memory does not require that its members meet, associate, cooperate, or feel warmth toward one another; enmity and mutual aloofness are compatible with membership. **What it may not say.** That Royce dispenses with communication. He names social communication as a condition, and interpretation — the process by which a community of interpretation is formed at all — is "a conversation, and not a lonely enterprise" (II, 148 / 1968, 289). The honest form of the chapter's sentence is that Royce constitutes a community out of a shared ideal extension of the self rather than out of encounter, while holding that the selves so joined must be the kind of beings who can address one another.

One further constraint, easy to miss. Royce frames the question as one about contemporaries: "Can many different selves, all belonging to the present time, possess identically the same past as their own personally interesting past life?" (II, 44 / 1968, 246), and the definition proper is stated of "many contemporary and distinct individual selves" (II, 49 / 1968, 248). The dead in the community of memory are what is commemorated. The members are the living commemorators.

### Is the interpreter a person, or can it be an impersonal process?

A mind, necessarily. The chapter must not seat a camera or an algorithm in Royce's interpreter position.

The direct evidence. Interpretation is "a mental act" whose product is itself a Sign (II, 148–49 / 1968, 289). The interpreted object "is itself something which has the nature of a mental expression" (II, 148 / 1968, 289). All three terms of the Community of Interpretation are selves: "we three, — you, my neighbor, whose mind I would fain interpret, — you, my kindly listener, to whom I am to address my interpretation, — we three constitute a Community. Let us give to this sort of community a technical name. Let us call it a Community of Interpretation" (II, 211 / 1968, 315). And the interpreter is characterized by an office and a self-surrender no mechanism performs:

> "The interpreter appears, then, as the one of the three who is most of all the spirit of the community, dominating the ideal relations of all three members. But the one who is, in ideal, this chief, is so because he is first of all servant. His office it is to conform to the mind which he interprets, and to the comprehension of the mind to which he addresses his interpretation."
> — **II, 216 / 1968, 317**

The one place Royce appears to go impersonal is the Colorado Canon, and reading it settles the question the other way. The general claim is stated in the subjunctive and routed through an observer:

> "…that an intelligent observer who happened to be in possession of the facts could, were he present, interpret to a possible future observer the meaning of the past."
> — **II, 145 / 1968, 288**

> "The erosions and the deposits of a present geological period lay down the traces which, if read by a geologist, would interpret the past history of the planet's crust to the observers of future geological periods."
> — **II, 146 / 1968, 288**

And the summary formula keeps the modal: "we may say that (at least in these parts of the world) **the present potentially interprets** the past to the future" (II, 146 / 1968, 288, emphasis added here). Royce defines the time-order "as an order of *possible* interpretation" (II, 147 / 1968, 289). A record that no mind reads is a sign awaiting an interpreter, not an interpreter.

**What the chapter may say.** A recording, an archive, a stratum, a film can occupy the *second* position in Royce's triad — the sign, the object interpreted. That position is fully impersonal in Royce, and a camera fits it exactly. **What it may not say.** That the interpreter can be an apparatus. If the chapter wants a technology in the interpreter's seat it is extending Royce, and it must say so in its own voice.

### What does loyalty do — constitute a community, or sustain one?

Royce makes it do both, and the constitutive job is the one that damages the chapter's thesis.

Loyalty as Royce defines it in *The Problem of Christianity* presupposes the community whose interest the cause is:

> "Loyalty, as I have elsewhere defined it, is the willing and thoroughgoing devotion of a self to a cause, when the cause is something which unites many selves in one, and which is therefore the interest of a community."
> — **I, 68 / 1968, 83**

That reads as sustaining. But Lecture X sets up the argument the chapter has to answer, and there loyalty is constitutive of the one thing that makes a *community* out of a merely cooperating group. Royce's sequence:

1. Cooperation is not community. "Men do not form a community, in our present restricted sense of that word, merely in so far as the men cooperate" (II, 85 / 1968, 263). A community also requires that each cooperator take the common work into his own ideally extended life.
2. Scale destroys the condition. "It is the original sin of any highly developed civilization that it breeds cooperation at the expense of a loss of interest in the community" (II, 85 / 1968, 262). Modern cooperation outruns "the power of any individual man's wit to understand its intricacies," so that "Most individuals, in most of their work, have to cooperate as the cogs cooperate in the wheels of a mechanism" (II, 87–88 / 1968, 263–64).
3. Love is what is left. "And the more complex the social order grows, the more all this cooperation must tend to appear to the individual as a mere process of nature, and not as his own work, — as a mechanism and not as an ideal extension of himself, — **unless indeed love supplies what individual wit can no longer accomplish**" (II, 91 / 1968, 265, bold added here).
4. And it does the constituting: "then indeed love furnishes that basis for the consciousness of the community which intelligence, without love, in a highly complex social realm, can no longer furnish. **Such love — such loyalty** — depends not upon losing sight of the variety of the callings of individuals, but upon seeing in the successful cooperation of all the members precisely that event which the individual member most eagerly loves as his own fulfilment" (II, 92 / 1968, 265, bold added here).
5. Royce names the role in so many words: "No one understands either the nature of the loyal life, or **the place of love in the constitution of the life of a real community**, who conceives such love as merely a longing for the mystical blending of the selves" (II, 95 / 1968, 267, bold added here).

In *The Philosophy of Loyalty* the direction of dependence runs the same way. The cause "links several human selves, perhaps a vast number of selves, into some higher social unity," and "Where there is an object of loyalty, there is, then, an union of various selves into one life" (pp. 52–53). Loyalty is what the union of selves is *for* the individual who serves it, and serving it is what keeps the union alive: "in being loyal myself, I not only get but give good; for I help to sustain, in each of my fellow-servants, his own loyalty" (p. 118).

**The plain answer.** Loyalty does not create the sharing of a past or a hope — the ideal extension of the self does that, and the three conditions at II, 60–68 make no mention of loyalty. What loyalty does is keep a community *conscious of itself* once its cooperations have grown too complex for any member to comprehend. That is exactly the case the chapter is about, and Royce's verdict on it is the opposite of the chapter's.

## What it argues

*The Problem of Christianity* takes three Christian ideas — the community, the moral burden of the individual, atonement — and rebuilds them as philosophy. Volume II supplies the machinery. Lecture IX defines a community without appeal to any group mind: a set of selves each of whom extends his own life ideally backward and forward and finds in that extension at least one event that every other member also claims as his own. Communities of memory and of hope follow from that definition, and the definition is deliberately thin enough to cover a tribe, a church, a nation, or a set of enemies who share an ancestor. Lecture X adds the three conditions and then spends its second half on the disease of modern cooperation: organized work outruns comprehension, cooperation degenerates into mechanism, and only love of the community holds the community's consciousness of itself together.

Lectures XI and XII do the epistemology. Philosophy has always sorted knowing into perception and conception; Royce, following Peirce, says the pair is not enough, and that interpretation is a third irreducible kind, defined by a non-symmetrical triadic relation among an interpreter, an object, and an addressee. He is precise about why the pair cannot be patched: adding an act of synthesis to a dyad yields a dyad plus an act, not a triad. Every interpretation, being addressed, calls for further interpretation, so the process is in ideal endless. The set of three selves so related is a Community of Interpretation, and Royce ends the argument by identifying the Beloved Community with it.

*The Philosophy of Loyalty* is earlier and simpler. Loyalty is willing, practical, thoroughgoing devotion of a person to a cause; a cause is something that unifies many human lives in one; the criterion for judging causes is whether serving them increases or destroys loyalty elsewhere, which yields the maxim "be, in any case, loyal to loyalty." The last lecture converts the ethical definition into a metaphysical one.

## Relation to the argument

Three things in Royce do work for the chapter, and each has a boundary written into the text.

The **community of memory** is exactly the construct the chapter needs at §2, and the Maori canoe passage is the citation that carries it. Royce constitutes a community out of a shared claim on the same past, with no encounter, no cooperation, no affection, and explicitly with the members possibly "enemies" (II, 46 / 1968, 247). A hundred thousand people who take *Slacker*'s Austin as part of their own past are a community of memory on Royce's definition, and the counter-source in `austin_1990.md` becomes an instance rather than an objection. The boundary is II, 67 / 1968, 255: the selves must be capable of social communication.

The **triad** gives the chapter a philosopher who states, in his own words and about cognition rather than about groups, that three terms do what two cannot. That is the structural claim the chapter's group-forming third rests on, and Royce's version comes with an argument against the obvious reduction rather than an assertion. It sits alongside Simmel's triad without duplicating it: Simmel's third is a social position, Royce's is a term in a relation of knowing. The boundary is that all three of Royce's terms are minds, and the sign — not the interpreter — is the position an apparatus can hold.

**Loyalty** is where the chapter should be most careful, and the relation is adversarial rather than supporting. See the Caution.

## Caution

**The single largest risk: Royce says the opposite of the chapter's thesis about loyalty and technology.** The chapter's claim is that technology replaces loyalty while the logic of community survives. Royce's Lecture X argues that as cooperation grows complex the logic of community *fails* — cooperation "must tend to appear to the individual as a mere process of nature, and not as his own work, — as a mechanism and not as an ideal extension of himself" — and that the one thing that arrests the failure is love, "such love — such loyalty" (II, 91–92 / 1968, 265). Loyalty is not a decorative sustainer that a coordinating technology can take over. It is Royce's answer to precisely the situation in which a technology coordinates people who cannot comprehend the coordination. A referee who knows the book will reach for II, 84–95 the moment the chapter says "technology replaces loyalty," and the passage is four pages long and unambiguous. Two ways out, both honest: argue *against* Royce here and say so, or narrow the claim to the community of memory, which Royce constitutes without loyalty and whose three conditions (II, 60–68 / 1968, 252–56) never mention it. The second is cheaper and the citations already support it.

**Do not put the IEP sentence in Royce's mouth.** "Congruence of feeling, thought, and will" is an encyclopedist's paraphrase. The word does not occur in either book. Anyone who checks will find the chapter quoting a tertiary source as a primary one, and the rest of the Royce section will be read in that light.

**Two circulating misquotations, both corrected above.** "Be, in a word, loyal to loyalty" is not the text (p. 121 reads "in any case"). "Accepts as *a* part of his own individual life" drops Royce's actual "accepts as part of his own individual life *and self*" (II, 50 / 1968, 248). Both are common in secondary literature, which is presumably where they were picked up.

**Pagination.** The 1913 Macmillan and 1968 Chicago settings differ by roughly 198 pages in volume II and are not related by a constant offset, because the 1968 reset runs both volumes continuously and more densely and carries an added Smith introduction. Vol. I p. 68 is 1968 p. 83; vol. II p. 50 is 1968 p. 248; vol. II p. 313 is 1968 p. 357. Pick one edition for the whole chapter.

**Royce's own community is metaphysical and religious, and the chapter's is not.** The Community of Interpretation terminates in "God the Interpreter" (II, 221 / 1968, 319) and the Beloved Community is the Church Universal. Nothing in the chapter's use requires that apparatus, but a reader who follows the "Community of Interpretation" citation at II, 211 to the end of the same lecture reaches it ten pages later. Say once, in the chapter's own voice, that the structural claim is being taken without the metaphysics.

**Bergson is Royce's foil, and the quotations are Royce's translations.** The "bank-note and cash" and "conceive is a makeshift" passages at II, 118 / 1968, 277 and II, 124 / 1968, 280 are Royce quoting Bergson's 1911 Oxford lectures. If the chapter uses them, attribute them to Bergson via Royce; Royce gives no citation beyond "his Oxford lectures."
