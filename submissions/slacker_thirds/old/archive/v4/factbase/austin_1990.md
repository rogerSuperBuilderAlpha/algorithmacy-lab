# 1990 Austin — the material base, and the counter-sources

Staged for v6 §3, whose claim is narrowed to something evidence can carry: **1990 Austin
coordinated its inhabitants without coordinating their work.** It supplied encounter and
unstructured time and imposed no common production. That is the condition under which a
group-forming third does all the joining.

All six figures below were verified for v3 and dropped from v4 and v5. Their notes carry
across verbatim; the caveats are part of the evidence and travel with them.

## The counter-sources, cited against the chapter and then converted

Both are verified in `research/gap_place_indie.md`. The plan's instruction is to cite them
*against* the chapter first, because a §3 that ignores them reproduces exactly the
myth-making one of them anatomizes.

**Erickson, "'I Think I Still Qualify as a Slacker…': The Myths of *Slacker*, Austin, and
Richard Linklater," in Wilkins & Vermeulen, eds., *ReFocus: The Films of Richard
Linklater* (Edinburgh UP, 2022), 15–33.** Verified via Cambridge Core. Argues the film
became emblematic "of a generation, a city, a mindset" beyond its observational origins,
and that Austin's transformation drives *nostalgic reinterpretation* — audiences now watch
the film through what Austin lost.

**Long, *Weird City: Sense of Place and Creative Resistance in Austin, Texas* (UT Press,
2010).** Treats the film as constitutive of Austin's place identity: "Set on the streets of
late 1980s Austin, *Slacker* came to define the subculture of overeducated,
nonparticipant weirdos so prevalent in Austin." Search-inside, Google Books ebook
pagination ~p. 70 — **confirm against print before quoting.**

**The caveat that decides how to use Long, and it works in the chapter's favour.** Long's
subject is the 2000s enclosure of weirdness, not the 1980s post-bust ecology. "Oil bust"
returns zero search-inside hits. He is the *after* story, so he cannot refute a claim about
1990, and the 1990 material still rests on the census figures and on Macor and Pierson.
Busch's *American Quarterly* review (63.2, 2011, 399–408) adds that Long focuses on
middle-class South Austin while overlooking east-side gentrification — a caveat §3 should
carry, since it bears on who the reaching city reached.

**The conversion.** A myth that a hundred thousand people accept as part of their own past
is not an objection to the chapter's Royce section. It is an instance of it: a community of
memory constituted by members taking the same past events as part of their own life. The
strongest counter-source becomes the §2 payoff.

## The falsifier, to be stated in the chapter

If the film showed its people at work, or if the 1990 figures showed a tight housing market
and a production boom, the claim fails. The money census tests the first half directly.

## One definitional obligation

"Platform" in §3 means the chapter's own defined object — a third that holds parties within
reach and arranges none of their meetings — not a two-sided market. Say so once. Without
that sentence, "decentralized platform" is a metaphor and a referee will say so.
