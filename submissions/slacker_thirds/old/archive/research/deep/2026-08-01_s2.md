## Finding 1 — Simmel names a third of a different kind on the first page of the triad discussion, and defers it (high on the text, contested on the inference; 2-1) [C1]

On the first page of "The Triad," under the heading that introduces the non-partisan and the mediator,
Simmel names a third that is not a pair-broker: a power outside the parties, to which all stand in the
same relation, whose common relation establishes a unification among them. His examples are an alliance
of states against a common enemy and the invisible church that unites the faithful through their equal
relation to one God. He calls that mediation *gesellschaftsbildend* — Wolff renders it "group-forming" —
and says it will be treated in a later context, because here the third stands at such a distance that no
properly sociological interaction encompasses all three elements alike. Two lenses read this as the
chapter's own setting/selector line, drawn by Simmel and set aside. The third lens refused that reading
on three grounds and the refusal is persuasive: Simmel says he will return to the case, not that he
abandons it; his criterion is distance and the absence of genuine three-way interaction, which is a
question about whether this is a triad at all, not the chapter's scope/counterfactual/knowledge test;
and his deferred third *unifies* the parties, where the chapter's setting explicitly arranges no
meeting. What survives, and it is enough to change the section's opening move, is that Simmel noticed
not every third is a pair-broker and said so in the same breath as introducing the mediator. BOTH.

## Finding 2 — Simmel's own thirds already scale past the pair, and one of them breaks the chapter's test (high, 3-0) [C2]

Simmel's tertius gaudens "on the largest scale" is not a person standing between two people: it is the
buying public under free competition, with the producers' side treated as a collectivity whose coalition
would invert the relation. His simplest case of *divide et impera* is a superior who forbids
associations that do not yet exist, by general legal prohibition — a third that acts on a whole
population and cannot know which particular parties it is separating, because, in Simmel's own words,
the prohibited associations "are merely possible and very often do not exist even as wishes of the
elements separated by the prohibition." Apply the chapter's three questions to that prohibition and it
scores as a SETTING: it acts on everyone at once, and it is ignorant of particulars. Yet it does the
opposite of what a setting does — it destroys reachability rather than supplying it — and the third
doing it is a deliberate strategic agent. The test as currently written sorts by scope and by knowledge
of particulars, and those two dials do not track whether a third adds or subtracts possible encounters.
COMPLICATES, and this is the most damaging finding in the set.

## Finding 4 — Brokerage research scopes the non-person third out of existence, in its own words and in its own measures (high, 2-1, with one conjunct of the original claim refuted) [C7]

The field's most recent full-scale review — 170 studies, *Journal of Management* 2020 — screens out
articles that treat networks as collections of dyads "thereby neglecting the role of third-party," and
defines brokerage as one actor connected to two other actors. It also screens out, by criterion (6),
social networks outside a work or professional context, which deletes *Slacker*'s Austin by
construction. Digital platforms are named once in the whole review, in a future-research paragraph, as a
possible future *antecedent* of human brokerage. The *AoM Annals* review defines brokerage as a
structural position and brokering as behaviours individuals pursue. And the formal apparatus is
pair-scoped at the root: all five Gould–Fernandez roles are defined over one ordered triple of actors,
and every operational measure found is a property of a node — betweenness centrality, Burt constraint,
Gould–Fernandez role counts. A setting has no node, so it cannot receive a score. SUPPORTS the section's
core warrant, in the strongest available form.

One conjunct of the original claim is refuted and must not be printed. The review does *not* screen out
non-human intermediaries. Criterion (5) reads "focused on non-human social networks or relied on
computer simulations," and the appendix spells it out as "animal societies, molecular systems" and
"computer simulations of human networks." That excludes non-human *networks*, not non-human *brokers*; a
study of a platform brokering between humans would pass it. Since the claim's whole selling point is
that the exclusion is in the field's own words, getting the words wrong is exactly the failure a referee
would find. Rest the warrant on scoping and measurement.

NAMING TRAP, worth printing: Gould and Fernandez's "coordinator" is not the chapter's setting. It is a
within-group pair-broker, and their example is a Federal Reserve Bank in a major city serving as a
clearinghouse for all the private banks in its area, through which transactions among the smaller banks
are conducted.

## Finding 8 — A PNAS-sized counter-frame says online dating removes a third rather than adding one, and performs the chapter's target conflation in one sentence (high, 3-0) [C10]

Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen argue that internet dating is *dis*intermediation: friends and family
displaced from their roles as key intermediaries, with "the removal or subordination of the human
intermediary between two parties" named as a fundamental social outcome of the internet. They bring
numbers: among couples who met online, the share who were previously strangers rose from 81.0 percent in
2009 to 89.5 percent in 2017, while the share brought together online by a third person fell from 11.2
percent to 3.7 percent. In the same paper's opening paragraph they call friends and family "the
essential network foci" citing Feld 1981, one sentence after calling the same people an
"intermediation" — the exact conflation section 2 exists to undo, in a journal with a very large
megaphone. COMPLICATES a load-bearing move, and supplies the section's best exhibit.

**Sources:** Michael J. Rosenfeld, Reuben J. Thomas, and Sonia Hausen, "Disintermediating your friends:
How online dating in the United States displaces other ways of meeting," *PNAS* 116, no. 36 (2019):
17753–17758. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1908630116.
https://web.stanford.edu/~mrosenfe/Rosenfeld_et_al_Disintermediating_Friends.pdf ; Michael J. Rosenfeld

**Evidence:** The conflation, opening paragraph, verbatim: "One's close friends and family have,

## Do NOT use (refuted or unconfirmed this pass)

**The Small 2009 lexical claim as originally framed (C4, 0-3).** The claim held that in *Unanticipated
Gains* the word "broker" never names the parent-to-parent tie-formation mechanism, which is confined to
Part II, chapter 3. All three lenses refused it, and for the same reason: nobody opened the book.
academic.oup.com/book/32855 returns 403 behind Cloudflare and the Google Books API was over quota, so a
negative lexical claim about 220 pages rests on chapter titles and a review. The Van Eijk review (IJURR
34, no. 1: 231–232, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2427.2010.00946_4.x) is bibliographically real but paywalled with
no OA copy, so the "complex broker" sentence attributed to Small at p. 153 is second-hand at best. Two
structural errors were also caught. The locator caution about hardcover/paperback repagination is a
false alarm: Crossref puts chapter 6, "Ties to Other Organizations," at pp. 129–156, so Van Eijk's p. 153
and Spinuzzi's p. 135 both fall inside one chapter. And tie formation among mothers is not confined to
chapter 3 — chapter 4 is "Weak and Strong Ties" (84–106) and chapter 5 "Trust and Obligations"
(107–126).

WHAT SURVIVES FROM C4 AND MAY BE USED. The structural point, stated only to what Crossref's chapter list
supports: in *Unanticipated Gains*, tie formation among mothers is developed across chapters 3–5
(51–126), while the childcare centre is called a broker in chapter 6, "Ties to Other Organizations"
(129–156), where it mediates between a parent and other organizations' resources. And the real find,
verified three ways: Small and Gose 2020 call the setting a broker in Small's own voice while listing
only setting properties — "successful brokerage will depend on the degree to which an organization's
institutional norms render interaction among participants frequent, long-lasting, focused on others, and
centered on joint tasks." All four conditions are properties of institutional norms; none requires the
third to know which two people are involved. That is the section's nearest rival, and it is stronger
stated correctly.

**The Hagiu-and-Wright reading as originally framed (C9, 0-3).** The claim held that the canonical
definition of a multi-sided platform is a setting definition, so "platforms are selectors" is false as
stated. All three lenses refused it, and lens 3's reason is decisive: Hagiu and Wright's "direct
interaction" condition governs who holds residual control over the *terms* of the interaction — they
list "the pricing, bundling, marketing and delivery of the goods or services traded, the ability to
determine the nature and quality of services offered, the terms and conditions" — and is silent on who
chooses whom. Their §4 treats the boundary as a continuum, with a shopping mall remaining an MSP "even
though it may place contractual restrictions on the goods sold by retailers." A dating app that
algorithmically selects who meets whom, while the two sides retain control over whether and how they
interact, is an MSP and a selector at once. Two further failures: the Roth locators were wrong (p. 6 not
5, p. 10 not 9) and the clearinghouse sentence was cut mid-sentence and closed as if complete; and the
Fang, Wu and Clough passage — the only leg that would show platforms manufacturing Feldian foci — could
not be retrieved by two of three lenses (HBS 404, Wiley 403, OpenAlex reports no repository full text).

WHAT SURVIVES FROM C9 AND MAY BE USED. The pricing point lands: pricing is named among the key terms, so
a platform that prices each match takes control of a term Hagiu and Wright reserve to the sides, and the
affiliation condition licenses removal. Roth is the better source for the real complication: thickness
(attracting participants "to come together ready to transact with one another") and the centralized
clearinghouse (determining who is matched with whom) are separable, and Roth's own framing is that a
clearinghouse can be built to deliver the thickness — "such a clearinghouse can persistently attract the
participation of a high proportion of the potential participants, and when it does so it solves the
problem of establishing a thick market" (p. 10). Do not print the Fang, Wu and Clough quotation until
someone reads the article. If it is later verified, the citation is Fang, Tommy Pan, Andy Wu, and David
R. Clough, "Platform Diffusion at Temporary Gatherings: Social Coordination and Ecosystem Emergence,"
*Strategic Management Journal* 42, no. 2 (2021): 233–272, DOI 10.1002/smj.3230 — the first author is
Tommy Pan Fang, not "Tiona P. Fang," and the source reads "users of e-commerce platforms like Craigslist,
often meet in person," "Digital ride-sharing platforms," and carries a "(Skahill, 2015)" citation the
run's evidence dropped. The Feld definition inside that passage also continues "(e.g., workplaces,
voluntary organizations, hangouts, families, etc.)," which the run silently cut.

**The three-rivals framing as originally written (C11, 0-3).** The rivals are real and must be cited;
the characterization of Lazar is not. The claim said Lazar "explicitly declines to sort algorithmic
intermediaries by what they select," and the passage quoted to support it does the opposite once the
ellipsis is opened: platform companies "typically mediating between an audience and a set of companies
providing services"; "some messaging apps, like Signal, strictly intermediate between users, not between
users and companies"; "some apps, like Tinder, algorithmically match users, rather than connecting users
and companies." That is a taxonomy built on whom the intermediary connects. Two further overreaches:
Lazar's structural intermediary power is introduced through laws, norms, institutions, economic systems
and *language* ("Language makes some intentions unsayable, some actions unthinkable"), a constraint on
what is possible rather than a power that puts strangers within reach; and his agential-power sentences
describe human intermediaries historically, prefaced by "intermediary power is not new to the Algorithmic
City… such as negotiators, bureaucracies, brokers, curators, critics, media companies and matchmakers."
The Neuberger gloss "never as a setting" also sits badly with the body sentence quoted in its support —
"Besonders Plattformen ermöglichen anderen Akteuren Partizipation und Interaktion" describes an enabling
function — and that body sentence is unverified (Springer 303s to an auth wall; only the abstract is
confirmed). And the Finkel locators were wrong twice: the access/communication/matching definitions are
at p. 3 by two lenses' header count (a third lens says p. 4; settle it), with a restatement at p. 6
reading "partners whom they are otherwise unlikely to encounter"; and the supermarket line is at p. 39,
not p. 55, and was cut mid-sentence.

WHAT SURVIVES FROM C11 AND MAY BE USED. Cite Lazar for what he says, which is a better concession than
the one drafted: he distinguishes structural intermediary power — it "imbues social structures like laws,
norms, and institutions, is never within the voluntary or unilateral control of any particular agent,
and is hard to update (it is a 'precipitate of the past')" — from agential intermediary power, which is
"adaptive, individualised," monitoring "the behaviour of their mediatees," and then argues that
"[a]lgorithmic power stands apart for how it combines these two dimensions of intermediary power. It is
both structural and agential." That combination thesis is the real challenge to any binary sort. Cite
Seth Lazar, "Governing the Algorithmic City," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 53, no. 2 (2025): 102–168,
DOI 10.1111/papa.12279, and note that all quotations come from arXiv:2410.20720, titled "Lecture I:
Governing the Algorithmic City" and running 46 pages against the article's 67 — cite the Wiley version of
record for anything page-anchored. Cite Christoph Neuberger, "Journalismus und Plattformen als
vermittelnde Dritte in der digitalen Öffentlichkeit," *KZfSS* 74, Suppl. 1 (2022): 159–181, DOI
10.1007/s11577-022-00832-9, from its abstract only. And cite Finkel et al.'s access/communication/matching
split, which is the nearest published statement of setting versus selector inside a single platform:
"Access refers to users' exposure to and opportunity to evaluate potential romantic partners they are
otherwise unlikely to encounter… Matching refers to a site's use of a mathematical algorithm to select
potential partners for users," and at p. 39, "Sites that emphasize access position themselves as
relationship supermarkets, where they advertise the range of their wares. In contrast, sites that
emphasize matching services position themselves as real estate agents of love, where they advertise
their experience and expertise."

**Kneeland and Kleinbaum as a clean illustration of the chapter's first question (C12, 1-2).** The
headline survives and is in the Confirmed citations above; the illustration does not. Two lenses refused
it. The corporate offsite does not act on everyone: it acts directly on a subset the firm selects for
attendance, which is itself a selector-like property, and the authors attribute the everyone-effect to a
different and indirect mechanism — "indirect interventions that signal the value of collaboration to
everyone, even those who do not attend." That is an organizational signal about corporate values, not a
focus supplying reachability. If the study is used at all, report it as evidence that a setting effect
and a signaling effect are hard to separate empirically, which is a complication for the chapter rather
than an illustration of it.

WHAT SURVIVES FROM C12 AND MUST BE USED. The disciplined phrasing. The setting mechanism was named in
1981 and, on Hachen et al.'s own account, "with very few exceptions, empirical assessments of the theory
have only focused on a narrow range of its predictions premised on comparative statics." Their result:
"musical genres function primarily as taste-diffusers, club types exclusively as tie generators, and
course areas and activities as both tie generators and taste diffusers." Say the mechanism is pre-named
and under-tested. Do not say it is unstudied.

## How this lands in section 2

The opening move has to change, and the change costs the section nothing it should want to keep. Section
2 currently arrives at the setting/selector distinction as something the tradition failed to notice.
Simmel noticed. On the first page of "The Triad," in the same breath as introducing the mediator, he
names a power outside the parties to which all stand in the same relation, whose common relation
establishes a unification among them, calls that mediation *gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung* — Wolff's
"group-forming" — and says it belongs to a later discussion, because here the third stands at such a
distance that no properly sociological interaction encompasses all three. Credit him with seeing that
not every third is a pair-broker. Do not claim he drew the chapter's line, and do not say he dropped it:
he says he will return to it, and the *Streit* chapter contains related material. What survives as new
is holding both kinds of third to one test.

The three-question test itself takes a hit and needs a repair. Simmel's own *divide et impera* case is a
superior who forbids by general prohibition associations that "are merely possible and very often do not
exist even as wishes of the elements separated by the prohibition." That third acts on everyone at once
and is ignorant of particulars, so on questions (1) and (3) it scores as a setting — while destroying
reachability rather than supplying it, and while being a deliberate strategic agent. The section needs
either a fourth discriminator (does the third add or subtract possible encounters?) or an explicit
sentence saying the three questions sort by scope, counterfactual and knowledge, and are not valence
tests. Write that sentence rather than hope no reader tries the case.

The warrant for the section's central absence gets stronger and one of its two legs gets amputated. Do
not write that brokerage research excludes non-human intermediaries; Kwon et al.'s criterion (5)
excludes non-human *networks* and simulations, and a referee will check. Write instead that the field
scopes the case out three ways at once: it screens out studies that treat networks "as a collection of
dyads, thereby neglecting the role of third-party"; it defines brokerage as "one actor (the broker)…
connected to two other actors (alters)"; and it screens out networks outside a work or professional
context, which deletes *Slacker*'s Austin by construction. Then close with the measurement point, which
is the hardest thing in the section to rebut: every operationalisation is a node property, and a setting
has no node. Print the naming trap alongside it — Gould and Fernandez's "coordinator" is a within-group
pair-broker whose exemplar is a Federal Reserve Bank clearing for all the private banks in its area, not
anything like a setting.

Replace every sentence beginning "scholars have not" with the regex result. Three canonical statements,
three empty sets: no Feld, foci, focus theory, homophily or propinquity anywhere in the *Annals*
brokerage review; no broker, brokerage, matchmaker, intermediary or third party anywhere in Kalmijn and
Flap; no Feld, foci, focus theory or Simmel anywhere in Finkel et al. Keep the claim at the document
level. At the tradition level it is false, and the section's own sources refute it: Small and Gose join
routine organizations to brokerage, and Fang, Wu and Clough cite Feld while writing about platform
firms.

The platform-as-third paragraph carries a wrong fact and gains a better one. Ajunwa's *tertius bifrons*
faces the platform's own interest and the employer's, behind an appearance of serving employer and
applicant both; "regulator" does not appear in her post. Her stated asymmetry is configurability — the
system is customizable at the employer's behest and never at the applicant's — which is a selector power
in the chapter's exact sense, and closer to "reads the parties and prices the match" than the
workers/regulators gloss ever was. Do not repeat her genealogy: *iungens* is Obstfeld's, not Simmel's,
and the string occurs zero times in the whole Wolff translation. In the same paragraph, redirect the
Stark citation. "Principles of Algorithmic Management" contains no Simmel, no tertius, no triad, no
broker and no intermediary; the owner–provider–user geometry is Stark and Pais 2020, which the project
already holds. Stark's "user" is also a mild complication worth one clause, since he offers it as the
category that absorbs workers, specialists, customers, citizens and suppliers alike, which cuts against
dividing selectors by the identity of the selected party.

One rival must be answered rather than pre-empted, and the answer is short. Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen
call online dating *dis*intermediation and have the numbers: previously-strangers up from 81.0 to 89.5
percent, brought-together-by-a-third-person down from 11.2 to 3.7. Every figure measures a human third,
and the authors say so — "the removal or subordination of the *human* intermediary." Their series is
silent on whether a non-personal third arrived. Then use their opening paragraph as the section's
exhibit: one sentence calls friends and family an "intermediation," the next calls them "the essential
network foci" citing Feld, and Feld's foci are entities around which joint activities are organized, not
persons. That is the conflation section 2 exists to undo, performed in PNAS, by authors who are not
careless. Nothing else in this run makes the section's case as economically.

Two locator traps to fix before anything goes to print. The "unite and separate" line is not in "The
Triad": Wolff's sentence is "each one operates as an intermediary between the other two, exhibiting the
twofold function of such an organ, which is to unite and to separate," and it sits at 135–36 in "The
Isolated Individual and the Dyad." Halevy et al. quote it to p. 135, and their own reference list gives
the book as New York, Simon and Schuster, edited by "K. Wolf" — three errors in one entry, in the very
paper the section cites. Get the imprint right: Glencoe, Illinois; The Free Press; 1950. And say the
setting mechanism is pre-named and under-tested rather than unstudied, on Hachen et al.'s own account
that empirical assessment has covered only a narrow range of focus theory's predictions. Drop the
corporate-offsite study as an illustration; attendance is selected by the firm and the authors credit
the everyone-effect to indirect signaling, which makes it a complication rather than a showcase.
