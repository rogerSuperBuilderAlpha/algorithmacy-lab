# Literature gap-check: film-theory candidates for the Slacker chapter

Date: 2026-07-02. Method: targeted web searches + URL fetches per candidate, plus a
Scholar Gateway full-text semantic search (Wiley-weighted corpus) as a falsification
probe for candidate 8. Every substantive claim below is tied to a fetched URL listed
in the Verified References section; anything I could not confirm against a fetched
page is marked **UNVERIFIED**. No quotes or page numbers are invented.

Chapter sections referenced: **§1** = rival taxonomies of the centerless multi-character
film; **§4** = the camera as engineering third (agent that brokers handoffs);
**§6** = the trained viewer (a century of editing taught audiences to read
third-party-composed adjacency).

## 1. Allan Cameron, *Modular Narratives in Contemporary Cinema* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2008)

**VERDICT: USEFUL** — taxonomy footnote in §1; no evidence he treats *Slacker*, so not a rival that must be answered.

**What it argues (verified via Senses of Cinema review [R1]):** Cameron sorts modular
narratives into four types — anachronic (*Pulp Fiction*, *Memento*, *Eternal Sunshine*,
*Irreversible*), forking-path (*Groundhog Day*, *Run Lola Run*), episodic ("structures
that critically weaken or disable the causal connections of classical narrative,"
split into the abstract series, e.g. *Drowning by Numbers*, and the narrative
anthology, e.g. *Dekalog*, *Nashville*, *Short Cuts*), and split-screen (*Timecode*).
The review characterizes his thesis as modular narrative achieving a domestication
of modernist excess within popular cinema.

**Bearing on the chapter:** His "episodic / narrative anthology" category is the slot
where *Slacker* would live, and it overlaps Ramírez Berg's daisy-chain taxonomy (already
cited). The review mentions neither *Slacker*, Linklater, daisy-chains, nor Manovich.
**UNVERIFIED:** whether the book contains even a passing *Slacker* mention — the
archive.org copy exists ([R2]) but search-inside is disabled ("Item not available")
and the Google Books API quota was exhausted; I could not run a full-text check.
Safe handling: cite Cameron once in §1 as the other standard modular-narrative
taxonomy alongside Ramírez Berg, noting his categories are temporal/structural and
contain no brokering-agent account.

**Slot:** §1 (one footnote).

## 2. Thomas Elsaesser, "The Mind-Game Film" (2009) and *The Mind-Game Film* (Routledge, 2021)

**VERDICT: USEFUL** — boundary-drawing in §1, with a bonus assist to §6; not must-cite because *Slacker* is definitionally outside his category.

**What it argues (verified via fetched essay PDF [R3]):** Mind-game films (*Memento*,
*Mulholland Drive*, *The Usual Suspects*) deliberately puzzle viewers via unreliable
narration, temporal manipulation, and withheld information; they institute new
"spectator contracts" in which viewers become puzzle-solvers. Elsaesser ties the
category to DVD/home-video culture: pause-and-examine, rewatch-to-verify habits
trained audiences to treat films as intricate puzzles. The essay does not treat
*Slacker*, Linklater, or network narratives as primary examples; mind-game films are
distinguished from ensemble/multi-strand films by their emphasis on deception and
cognitive challenge. The posthumous book version — *The Mind-Game Film: Distributed
Agency, Time Travel, and Productive Pathology*, Routledge, 2021, edited by Warren
Buckland, Dana Polan, and Seung-hoon Jeong (per search-result metadata including a
*New Review of Film and Television Studies* review listing: 330 pp., ISBN
9780415968126 — **page itself not fetched**, Routledge 403'd; treat exact page count
as UNVERIFIED).

**Bearing on the chapter:** Two uses. (a) §1: *Slacker* deceives no one — its problem
is adjacency without connection, not epistemic games — so Elsaesser marks the border
of the chapter's territory. (b) §6: his DVD-culture argument is an independent,
citable instance of the chapter's core mechanism (industrial viewing technology
training a reading competence), one medium-generation after Benjamin/Kuleshov and one
before platforms. That strengthens the training-history arc.

**Slot:** §1 (boundary), optional §6 (training precedent).

## 3. Seymour Chatman, *Coming to Terms: The Rhetoric of Narrative in Fiction and Film* (Cornell UP, 1990)

**VERDICT: MUST-CITE** for §4.

**What it argues (verified via Alphaville article [R4] and secondary summary [R5]):**
Chatman insists every film has a narrating agency: the cinematic narrator is "the
transmitting agency, immanent to the film, which presents the images we see and the
sounds we hear" ([R4], citing Chatman). Against Bordwell's narration-without-a-narrator
("to give every film a narrator or implied author is to indulge in an anthropomorphic
fiction"; "the narration... creates the narrator" — Bordwell as quoted in [R4]),
Chatman holds that viewers *reconstruct* rather than construct the narrative "from
the set of cues encoded in the film" ([R5]), endorsing a communication/sender model.
The same Alphaville piece situates Branigan (narrator/character as "convenient labels
used by the spectator in marking epistemological boundaries") and George Wilson
(narration attributable to the real author, no fictional shower needed), and notes
Gunning's observation that Bordwell smuggles anthropomorphic agency back in.

**Bearing on the chapter:** The engineering-third claim — the camera as an agent that
*brokers* handoffs — is exactly the kind of film-internal presenting-agency claim this
debate adjudicates. Citing Bordwell alone leaves the chapter open to the objection
that its "third" is Chatman's cinematic narrator under a new name, or Bordwell's
agentless narration anthropomorphized. The chapter needs one paragraph placing the
engineering third relative to Chatman (yes, an immanent presenting agency), Bordwell
(yes, a process the viewer completes), and Branigan/Wilson — and arguing what's new:
the third is defined *relationally* (it brokers between parties who never meet),
not semiotically (it does not "tell" anything).

**Slot:** §4.

## 4. Miriam Hansen — "The Mass Production of the Senses" (1999) and *Cinema and Experience* (2011/2012)

**VERDICT: MUST-CITE** for §6 — confirmed; this is the strongest single upgrade available to the training claim.

**What it argues (verified against the full essay text [R6], quotes exact, bracketed
page markers from the Project MUSE pagination, *Modernism/Modernity* 6.2 (1999),
pp. 59–77):**
- Modernism should be widened to "cultural practices that both articulated and
  mediated the experience of modernity, such as the mass-produced and mass-consumed
  phenomena of fashion, design, advertising, architecture and urban environment, of
  photography, radio, and cinema" — a modernism she calls "vernacular" (p. 60).
- "American movies of the classical period offered something like the first global
  vernacular" (p. 68), succeeding "because it played a key role in mediating competing
  cultural discourses on modernity and modernization" (p. 68).
- Cinema was "not only part and symptom of modernity's experience and perception of
  crisis and upheaval; it was also, most importantly, the single most inclusive
  cultural horizon in which the traumatic effects of modernity were reflected,
  rejected or disavowed, transmuted or negotiated" (pp. 68–69).
- "the cinema not only traded in the mass production of the senses but also provided
  an aesthetic horizon for the experience of industrial mass society" (p. 70).
- She reads Benjamin's and Kracauer's 1920s–30s writings as theorizing this relation
  as "a new mode of reflexivity" operating "not just on the level of individual,
  philosophical cognition but on a mass scale" (p. 69), with Kracauer's cinema as an
  alternative public sphere and "social horizon of experience" (p. 69).

**Cinema and Experience (verified via UC Press page [R7]):** *Cinema and Experience:
Siegfried Kracauer, Walter Benjamin, and Theodor W. Adorno* (University of California
Press, Weimar and Now series; paper ed. dated Oct 2011 on the press page, commonly
cited 2012; SCMS Kovács Award 2012). The press description confirms it reconstructs
these thinkers' "theory of cinema and experience" and frames it as addressing
"questions being posed anew as moving image culture evolves in response to digital
technology" — i.e., Hansen herself extends the training/mediation argument toward
the digital, which is precisely the chapter's platform turn.

**Bearing on the chapter:** The chapter currently grounds the training claim in
Benjamin SW3 + Kuleshov (a theorist and a technician). Hansen supplies the
historiographic program in between: classical continuity cinema as the mass sensory
training institution of modernity, globally distributed. She converts the chapter's
"a century of editing trained audiences" from an assertion into a cited literature.
Use "Mass Production" for the thesis and *Cinema and Experience* for the
Benjamin/Kracauer scaffolding and the explicit bridge to digital moving-image culture.

**Slot:** §6 (load-bearing).

## 5. Jonathan Crary, *Techniques of the Observer* (1990) / *Suspensions of Perception* (MIT Press, 1999)

**VERDICT: USEFUL** — genealogical footnote for §6; needed only if §6 claims attention-management *began* with cinema.

**What it argues (verified via archive.org item description [R8]):** *Suspensions of
Perception* argues "the ways in which we intently look at or listen to anything result
from crucial changes in the nature of perception" traceable to the late 19th century;
focus is roughly 1880–1905, where the modernization of subjectivity coincided with
the industrialization of visual/auditory culture. Its central paradox: attention was
simultaneously the ground of individual autonomy/creativity and a central element in
the functioning of disciplinary institutions, consumer culture, and mass spectacle.
(MIT Press and caa.reviews pages 403'd; publication year 1999 corroborated by the
Monoskop file listing surfaced in search [R9] — the archive.org description itself
omits year/publisher.)

**Bearing on the chapter:** Crary shows third-party management of attention predates
editing — cinema industrialized an already-contested faculty. One footnote in §6
inoculates the chapter against "why start the training history at cinema?" It is not
must-cite because the chapter's claim concerns a specific *reading competence*
(third-party-composed adjacency), not attention per se.

**Slot:** §6 (footnote).

## 6. Deleuze's balade / Rob Stone, *The Cinema of Richard Linklater: Walk, Don't Run*

**VERDICT: MUST-CITE (Stone)** for §1/§4 — Linklater scholarship demonstrably routes *Slacker* through Deleuze, and the chapter must differentiate itself from that reading.

**What is verified:**
- Stone's monograph exists in two editions: Wallflower Press, London/New York, 2013
  (archive.org metadata [R10]); Google Books page [R11] confirms Columbia UP
  distribution, 2013, and the chapter list — ch. 3 is titled **"The Form and Content
  of Slack"** — and its topic word-cloud includes "Deleuze" and "time-image."
- Independent corroboration that the Deleuzian routing of *Slacker* is established:
  John Marlovits, "Give Me Slack: Depression, Alertness, and Laziness in Seattle,"
  *Anthropology of Consciousness* 24:2 (2013), 137–157, doi:10.1111/anoc.12011
  (full-text passages retrieved via Scholar Gateway [R12]) reads *Slacker* explicitly
  against Deleuze: the film "is not action driven, is not based on... the
  'movement-image'"; its characters answer to Deleuze's postwar "seers" ("they saw
  rather than acted"); "the frustration that viewers experience in the film's refusal
  to provide a coherent plot *is the point*." Marlovits even calls the slacker "a kind
  of industrial slowdown and disengagement for the digital age."
- **UNVERIFIED (marked):** the widely-circulated description sentence for Stone's
  book — the time-image "can be seen as a slacker that rebels by inaction," slacking
  "stalls the narrative and loosens the sensory-motor schema, thereby allowing 'a
  little time in the pure state' (Deleuze) to rise up to the surface of the screen" —
  appeared in two search-result summaries (dokumen.pub / publisher-description
  snippets) but the hosting pages returned 403, and Goodreads' description omits it.
  Do not quote it with a page number until checked against the physical book.
  Whether Stone uses the term "balade" specifically: UNVERIFIED.

**Bearing on the chapter:** The existing Deleuzian account explains *Slacker*'s drift
as *time* loosening the sensory-motor schema — coherence dissolves and that is the
achievement. The chapter's claim is the inverse: coherence *survives*, and something
does the work — the engineering third. Stone (the standard monograph, ch. 3) must be
cited as the reigning reading to be displaced: Deleuze names what is loosened; the
chapter names what still binds. This is a productive foil, not a threat.

**Slot:** §1 (rival reading) and §4 (foil for the camera argument).

## 7. Maria Poulaki / Warren Buckland (puzzle films, complex narratives)

**VERDICT: MUST-CITE (Poulaki)** for §1; **SKIP (Buckland as such)** — his puzzle-film category excludes *Slacker* by construction, and he enters the bibliography anyway as editor of the Elsaesser essay.

**What Poulaki argues (verified via colab.ws mirror of the Screen abstract [R13]):**
"Network films and complex causality," *Screen* 55:3 (September 2014), 379–395,
doi:10.1093/screen/hju020. In multi-character network films, classical causality
appears loose; using *Burn After Reading* and a framework from complex-systems and
network theory, she argues "an emergent type of causality is at play in network films,
which follow the organizational laws of complex systems, rather than those of
narrative organization."

**Bearing on the chapter:** This is the single most direct rival to the engineering
third: Poulaki explains coherence in centerless multi-character films *without any
broker* — bottom-up self-organization replaces both classical causality and any
presenting agency. The chapter must engage her account and say why *Slacker* is a
bad fit for it: in *Slacker* the characters do not form a system (they interact once,
pairwise, and never recur — no feedback, no emergence *within* the diegesis), so the
coherence must be supplied from outside the character network — by the composing
third. That contrast sharpens the chapter's central claim. Neither her account nor
Buckland's puzzle-film volumes ([R14] Routledge/Wiley listings) contains a
camera-brokered-coherence argument — verified as far as abstracts/tables of contents
allow; full texts not fetched.

**Slot:** §1 (the strongest rival, one substantive paragraph).

## 8. *Slacker* + algorithms/platforms/digital culture (post-2015): is the ground open?

**UPDATED 2026-08-02 — THE BROAD CLAIM DOES NOT SURVIVE.** Ann Althouse wrote on July 11, 2026 that
rewatching *Slacker* "might feel like scrolling in TikTok" and that she likes the fragmentation.
An undated, unattributed Movie Browser page says the film "predicted both mumblecore and the infinite
scroll of internet rabbit holes." Neither item develops the comparison, and neither is scholarship, but
both defeat any sentence claiming that nobody has connected *Slacker* to TikTok, scrolling, or feed
logic. Richard Linklater also supplied the older genealogy himself in 2011: the first remote-control
generation learned to make its own narratives from "five minutes of this and then one minute of that,"
and he saw *Slacker* as "either channel surfing or ... going in and out of different movies," a
"primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."

**NARROW VERDICT: THE SCHOLARLY CLAIM SURVIVES.** No peer-reviewed account located here develops the
resemblance through camera-selected handoffs, co-optation, or the difference between a fixed cinematic
order and a personalized platform order. The contribution is the explanation, not the observation.

**Searches run:**
1. Web search: Linklater *Slacker* + algorithm/platform/"digital culture"/attention
   economy → only generic platform-studies literature, no *Slacker* connection.
2. Web search: "Slacker (1991)" + algorithm/algorithmic/"platform capitalism" →
   nothing; closest is a 2008 The Other Journal essay on narrative structure without
   digital framing.
3. Web search: *Slacker* + "network narrative"/"database"/internet-precursor →
   nothing scholarly. Linklater's channel-surfing account was verified against Marc
   Savlov's 2011 *Austin Chronicle* interview.
4. Web search: 30th-anniversary/"predicted the internet" essays → Criterion's
   anniversary materials surface, but nothing arguing a structural film-to-feed link.
5. Scholar Gateway semantic search (15 passages, 12 articles, corpus window
   2009–2026) [R12]: zero hits connecting *Slacker* to algorithms/platforms; the only
   *Slacker*-substantive result is Marlovits 2013 (Deleuze/affect, see candidate 6).
6. Journalistic spot-check (fetched [R15]): a June 2026 Substack essay on rewatching
   *Slacker* treats it as a *pre-digital elegy* ("There was no staring at phones or
   doomscrolling"), while Althouse's July post makes the opposite comparison. The two
   reactions establish the live question; neither explains the form.
7. Exact-phrase searches for *Slacker* with TikTok, Instagram, infinite scroll,
   social-media feed, swipe, and scrolling found Althouse and the low-confidence Movie
   Browser line, but no peer-reviewed treatment.

**Limits of the falsification attempt (state in the chapter's own terms, not here):**
JSTOR/Project MUSE/Google Scholar could not be queried directly from this
environment, and the Scholar Gateway corpus is Wiley-weighted; a library-database
sweep before submission is cheap insurance. The searches support only the narrower
claim above. Sirůček 2025 covers the general apparatus-to-algorithm move but not
*Slacker*. Alexandra Anikina's "Automating Platform Spectators" and Tina Kendall's
"From Binge-Watching to Binge-Scrolling" theorize feed form without applying it to
Linklater.
