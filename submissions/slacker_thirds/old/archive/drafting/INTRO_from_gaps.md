# The question formed from the gaps, and the introduction it produces

2026-08-18. Part I reviews each published answer and names its gap. Part II states the question the gaps produce. Part III drafts the introduction, which performs Part I without announcing it. The introduction replaces the Open and drift-split sections of OUTLINE_v15 and absorbs their budgets (~600 words of the ~1,000 those two sections held).

## Part I — the answers and their gaps

**Małecka 2015 (with Miley 2019, Johnson 2012).** Answer: the film coheres thematically; the characters "unite by unanimously doing nothing," and the shared refusal of the work ethic is the glue. Gap: a shared property distributes across scenes and joins none of them. Ninety people who each refuse the same economy still make ninety scenes. Theme explains the film's tone. It cannot explain its sequence.

**Price 2003 (with Bartie).** Answer: ideas are the motor; "conversation, and ideas themselves, become the determining force of the narrative," and the camera "follows whatever person happens to walk into" it. Gap: the ideas in the film do not travel. Each monologue dies with its speaker, unanswered by the next scene. And "follows... whatever happens" attributes passivity to an apparatus that picks one passerby among several, every time, and never doubles back. The account presupposes the selection it needs to explain.

**Soldani 2017.** Answer: the scene performs itself; a drifting dérive camera, the 24-hour frame, and shared duration convert the Austin indie scene's collective body into a generational emblem. Gap: this explains who is in the film and why the day feels continuous. It cannot explain the cut. A dérive is aimless; the cut decides. Her own shift of emphasis from space to time leaves the space's work unexamined, and the space is working: it keeps supplying the next person.

**Stone 2013/2018 (with Boom 2022, Ramirez 2011).** Answer: drift. Walking is philosophy in action, the long take is the present tense, the swerves and lacunae are constitutive, the camera's dérive weaves "a seamless urban tissue." Gap: flânerie is first-person and aimless. In this film the walkers never choose whom we see, and the chooser never wanders. Each hand-off is a decision the drift readings describe as an accident.

**Erickson 2022 (with Macor 2010, Psaltidis 2024).** Answer: Austin. The film documents a scene and a city, was materially produced by the network it depicts, and hardened into the emblem a generation uses to mourn the place. Gap: these are answers about what the film shows and how it got made. The city inside the film also functions. Portrait answers explain the film's cultural coherence and leave its formal coherence untouched.

**Berg 2006.** Answer: classification. Slacker defines the daisy chain plot, which "jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit." Gap: "jumps" is the entire mechanism on offer. The taxonomy names the structure the way a field guide names a bird, and explains it as much.

**Bizarro 2025 (with Marlovits 2013).** Answer: nothing coordinates, and that is the point. The film refuses narrative; the viewer's frustration is the argument; non-narrativity protects its people from the stories told about them. Gap: the position proves too much. A pure refusal of narrative would play as shuffle. Slacker is legible at every hand-off; viewers who cannot say what it is about can always say where they are. The strongest position in the field cannot say why a plotless day is easy to follow.

**Oake 2004 (with Newman 2011).** Answer: the coherence lives in the viewer, a Gen X spectatorship competent at exactly this kind of watching. Gap: competence is competence at something. Naming the reader presupposes the structure being read.

**Bordwell 2008, Poulaki 2014, Ciafone 2014, Jeong 2021.** Answer: network machinery; plotlines that intertwine, causality that loops back as emergence, meetings that arrive as magic, networking as the condition of global subjectivity. Gap: every mechanism requires strands to re-cross, and Slacker's never do. The film sits on all their lists and breaks all their machines. Jeong alone notices the camera-agent, "the camera's hesitation about whom to follow," and then reads the hesitation as a soul rather than an office.

**The convergent gap.** Every answer locates the coordinating work inside the story (theme, idea, causality), inside the picture (portrait, myth, scene), or inside the viewer (competence, frustration). No answer asks who performs the hand-off, and on what authority the next stranger arrives.

## Part II — the question

**What coordinates the strangers of *Slacker*?**

Formed against the gaps: theme and idea fail because they distribute; drift fails because it cannot decide; portrait fails because it shows rather than works; taxonomy names without explaining; refusal predicts an illegibility the film does not have; spectatorship presupposes the structure; network theory requires the reunions the film withholds. What remains is coordination performed from outside the story, and the question of what performs it.

---

## Part III — draft introduction (~600 words, Chicago endnotes)

*Slacker* follows about a hundred people through one day in Austin. There is no protagonist. Almost no one meets twice, and nobody introduces anybody. The day still holds, and audiences follow it without strain. The question of this chapter is what coordinates the strangers of *Slacker*.

The published answers place the coordinating work inside the story. The characters unite in refusing work, so the film coheres as a shared ethic.[^1] The camera follows an idea from mouth to mouth, so the film coheres as one long conversation.[^2] Each answer names a property the scenes hold in common, and a common property joins nothing. Ninety people who each refuse the same economy still make ninety scenes. The monologues do not travel; each dies with its speaker. Something moves the film from one refusal to the next, and the refusals do not do it.

A second family of answers gives the work to drift. The camera performs a dérive through pool halls and coffeehouses and reveals a scene in the act of performing itself.[^3] The walking is philosophy, and the long take is the present tense.[^4] The drift readings describe the path and miss the decision. A dérive is aimless and first-person. The cut in *Slacker* is neither. It leaves a speaker mid-sentence, picks one passerby from several, and never returns. Whatever chooses is not wandering.

A third family points to Austin. The film documents a walkable bohemia at its last moment and became the emblem a generation now uses to mourn it.[^5] The portrait answers say what the film shows. The city in the film also does something. It keeps the next person within reach of the last one, all day long, and it introduces nobody. A portrait does not hold a film together. An arrangement might.

The formal literature names the structure and stops. In the standard taxonomy of alternative plots, *Slacker* defines the daisy chain: the story jumps from one protagonist to the next and never revisits.[^6] Jumps is the whole account. The strongest position in the field goes further and holds that the missing plot is the point: the film refuses narrative, and the refusal protects its people from the stories told about them.[^7] That is right about the plot. It proves too much about the film. A pure refusal of narrative would play as shuffle, and *Slacker* is legible at every hand-off; viewers who cannot say what it is about can always say where they are. Something makes a plotless day easy to follow, and no account of refusal supplies it.

Theories of the network film do not supply it either. Their glue is convergence: plotlines that intertwine, consequences that propagate, strangers whose paths finally cross.[^8] *Slacker* has none of these and holds anyway. The film sits on the network lists and breaks the network machinery, which also means the machinery was never what held such films together in the first place.[^9]

My claim is that the coordination is real and external. Two jobs, divided between two agents, do all of it. Austin holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody. The camera selects which stranger we see next. And at the doors where the day's access is priced, listed, and refused, a person still says so and can still be argued with. The platforms that later ran this arrangement kept the holding, the selecting, and the grant. They withdrew the person. *Slacker* shows the arrangement while someone still stands in it, answerable even while giving no reason. The film will ask after a man named Gary and decline to produce him, and by the end of the chapter that refusal will read as the arrangement's signature.
