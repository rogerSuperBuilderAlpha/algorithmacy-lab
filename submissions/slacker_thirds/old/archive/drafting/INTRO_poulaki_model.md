# Introduction rewritten on the Poulaki model

Model: Maria Poulaki, "Network Films and Complex Causality," Screen 55, no. 3 (2014): 379–395. Full text read from the uploaded PDF. Part A maps her moves with page references. Part B is the rewritten introduction, each paragraph keyed to the move it imitates. Part C lists the deliberate deviations.

---

## Part A — the model's move map

**Move 1, the opening (p. 379).** Film first, in the first four words, with year and director in parentheses. A concessive aside grants the film's familiar identity ("aside from being a parody of the spy movie genre") before reassigning it to the essay's category ("can also be considered a complex film"). A critic's phrase is borrowed immediately, cited, and made to carry the puzzle ("a chaotic world through a seemingly chaotic narrative"). Then the method sentence: "In this essay I use Burn After Reading as an illustration, as a metaphor, and as an exemplary case... particularly when it comes to the issue of causality." Then the claim sentence: "I describe... and argue that..." Three sentences, and the object, the use, and the thesis are all on the table.

**Move 2, patient synopsis with analytic asides (pp. 379–381).** Two full pages of plot description, plain and unhurried, salted with analytic remarks that train the reader: "This is the first in a series of many small ironic twists, which the viewer soon learns to anticipate." The synopsis is doing argument work disguised as description.

**Move 3, the literature by name, in the body (pp. 382–384).** Scholars appear as agents of their own claims: "Bordwell proposes the term 'network narratives'"; "His contention is that..."; "Bordwell points out that..." Block quotes are given room. Positions are stated at their strongest before any pressure is applied.

**Move 4, the concede-then-gap engine (p. 384).** Her signature: "Bordwell's observation of 'loose causality' in contemporary complex films is valid in the sense that many events in network narratives 'just happen'... However, Bordwell does not elaborate on the characteristics of this loose causality, which is mostly negatively defined." Grant validity in a specified sense; then name what the account fails to elaborate; then diagnose why (negative definition).

**Move 5, the question as pivot (p. 383).** The research question arrives mid-essay, after the literature has earned it, in two-part grammar: "does causality play any role in network narratives, and if so what forms does it take?" Also as paragraph hinges: "What, however, is the relationship between chance and causality?"

**Move 6, first-person commitment (pp. 379, 388, 394).** "I describe... and argue"; "I would argue that one of the ways..."; "Thus I would argue that..." The I commits and is rationed: it appears at the thesis, at the section-turning claims, and at the close.

**Move 7, the close (pp. 394–395).** The field's insufficiency stated flatly ("remains insufficiently addressed in film studies"), the required shift named, and a short final paragraph that redefines the key term and ends on the essay's own vocabulary.

## Part B — the introduction, rebuilt on those moves

*Keyed: [M1] etc. mark which model move each paragraph runs.*

[M1] The 1991 film *Slacker* (Richard Linklater), aside from being a founding document of American independent cinema, can also be considered a coordination puzzle: it depicts, as its first reviewer wrote, "no plot, no major characters, no suspense: just fleeting glimpses of bohemia in its twilight phase."[^1] In this chapter I use *Slacker* as an illustration and as a limit case of the multi-character film, particularly when it comes to the issue of coordination. I describe the arrangement through which the film holds roughly one hundred unacquainted characters in one legible day, and argue that this arrangement consists of two jobs performed by two agents: the city of Austin, which holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody, and the camera, which selects which stranger the viewer sees next.

[M2] The film opens with the arrangement already at work. A man played by Linklater himself, credited as "Should have stayed at bus station," rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a taxi driver who never once answers him. His monologue proposes that every road not taken continues somewhere as its own reality; he gets out on the line that gives him his credit. Moments later a woman lies struck in the street, a jogger shouts at bystanders not to touch her, and a passing motorist presses his business card on a stranger before driving off. The camera then abandons the victim and follows the driver responsible into his house, where officers arrive to take him away for running over his mother. In the taxi the passenger had remarked that there is always someone getting run over. The viewer soon learns to anticipate such hand-offs: a character walks away from one scene and into the next, and the film goes with whichever body the cut prefers.

[M3] The scholarship on *Slacker* has answered the coordination question mainly by locating a property that all the scenes share. The characters "unite by unanimously doing nothing," so the film coheres as a common ethic of refusal.[^2] Alternatively, "conversation, and ideas themselves, become the determining force of the narrative," so the film coheres as one continuous exchange of talk.[^3] [M4] These observations are valid in the sense that the film's tone and its talk are remarkably consistent from scene to scene. However, a property that every scene possesses cannot join any two of them, and the film's monologues do not in fact travel: each dies with its speaker, unanswered by the scene that follows.

[M3] A second line of interpretation attributes the continuity to drift. The camera performs a dérive through the spaces of the Austin scene, revealing a generation in the act of performing itself;[^4] the characters' walking is philosophy in action, and the long take is its present tense;[^5] the scenes "create the sense of a seamless urban tissue."[^6] [M4] The drift readings are valid as far as the camera's path is concerned. However, drift is mostly negatively defined, naming the absence of a destination, and it does not elaborate on the one act the film performs at every juncture: the selection of a single passerby from several available, a selection no character makes and the film never revokes.

[M3] The formal literature names the structure and stops there. In Charles Ramírez Berg's taxonomy of alternative plots, *Slacker* defines the "daisy chain," in which the story jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit any of them.[^7] The strongest recent position goes further and holds that the missing plot is itself the point: the film refuses narrative, and the refusal protects its characters from the stories told about their generation.[^8] [M4] The refusal reading is valid at the level of plot, where nothing accumulates and nothing resolves. However, it proves too much about the film as an experience. A genuine refusal of coordination would play as shuffle, and *Slacker* is legible at every hand-off; viewers who cannot say what the film is about can always say where they are. [M5] If neither a shared ethic, nor the circulation of talk, nor the camera's drift, nor the refusal of narrative accounts for the hand-offs, does coordination play any role in *Slacker* at all? And if so, what performs it, and what form does it take?

[M3/M4] Theories of the network narrative, where the question might be expected to find an answer, require materials this film withholds. Their accounts of multi-character coherence run through convergence: plotlines that intertwine, consequences that propagate across strands, strangers whose paths finally cross.[^9] *Slacker* appears on the network filmographies and supplies none of these; no line re-crosses, and nothing propagates. The film is what that literature would have to call a borderline case, the film that holds together after every binding agent the theory recognizes has been removed. [M6] I would argue that the film's coordination is real and external to its story. Austin holds the day's strangers within reach of one another and introduces none of them. The camera selects, at every juncture, which of them we see. And at the doors where the day's access is priced, listed, and refused, a person still pronounces the refusal and can still be argued with. The platforms that later operated this same arrangement retained the holding, the selecting, and the grant, and withdrew the person. The film will ask after a man named Gary and decline to produce him, and the argument of this chapter is that the two refusals are the same refusal.

## Part C — deliberate deviations from the model

Three of her habits stay out. Poulaki uses dashes freely; house rule excludes them, so colons and commas do that work. Her closing antithesis form ("not loose, but complex and nonlinear") is excluded by the same rule; the final paragraph commits positively instead. And her block quotes are deferred: an introduction of this length cannot afford them, though the chapter body should use one or two (Simmel's deferral sentence is the natural candidate), since giving an opposing or founding text room to speak at length is part of what makes her essay read as scholarship.

Two of her habits are adopted beyond the introduction and should govern the whole rewrite: positions stated at their strongest before pressure is applied, and the concede-then-gap engine ("valid in the sense that... However, [it] does not elaborate...") as the standard way of leaving every source. Poulaki now also enters note 9 on her own terms, which the chapter earns by using her method against her corpus.
