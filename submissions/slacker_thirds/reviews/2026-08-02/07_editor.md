# Editor's review — "The Setting and the Selector: *Slacker* and Two Ways to Coordinate Strangers"

**Verdict: minor revisions — accept in principle, conditional on two things, and the first of them is
not optional: the film has to come back into the chapter, and somebody has to describe a shot.**

---

## Step 0 — Register

First person, contractions, scenes before theses, concessions in stride: that is the chapter's chosen
voice and it is a good one, so I flag none of it. What follows is judged as an edited-collection
editor judges a submission — does it belong in a book about *Slacker*, will a reader who came for
Linklater stay, and is the extra four hundred words worth the argument I would have to have with the
press about them.

---

## Part 1 — Fit, structure, pacing

### Does it belong in the book?

Yes, and I want it. The idea is real: a third party can put people within reach of each other without
arranging any particular meeting, or it can choose, and *Slacker* runs both at once — the city doing
the first, the camera doing the second. That distinction is the chapter's own, it is stated in plain
sentences rather than in figures of speech, and §4 turns Bordwell's throwaway "wiggly, knotted string"
into evidence. §1, §4 and §7 are film-native and publication-ready in shape. §6 is film theory in a
film book and sits fine. Nothing in the argument needs re-arguing.

### Does it love the film, or use it?

It admires the film's design. It rarely shows any pleasure in watching it. There is exactly one
sentence in 5,400 words where the chapter's affection for *Slacker* is audible — "a camera that took
nothing from them except a day, and gave the day back watchable" — and that sentence is the best in
the chapter. Everything else about the film is method. A *Slacker* reader will notice.

### Is there enough film in it? No, and it got thinner.

Counting scenes the chapter actually engages: the taxi monologue (§1), the hit-and-run handoff (§4),
the moon-landing walker (one sentence, §3), the pap-smear pitch (one clause, §3), and one
oblique-strategies card, unquoted and unnamed (§7). Five moments out of a film your own
`research/scene_inventory.md` breaks into thirty-five segments, and whose summary line reads: "That
leaves roughly thirty segments untouched, including the film's most direct statements about media,
seriality, and cameras — the themes the chapter is about."

Worse, v3 has *less* film than v2. The Super-8 finale mention is gone. The card's line — the one film
quotation the ending had — is gone. Compression took words out of the film and left the sociology
whole.

Paragraph audit, since it is the venue's test:

| § | Words | Paragraphs touching the film |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 598 | most — the strongest section, and the model |
| 2 | 892 | **one clause**: "Austin in 1990 is a setting. Linklater's camera is a selector." |
| 3 | 656 | two sentences of scene, then census tables, agglomeration economics and *Papachristou* |
| 4 | 964 | most — the best section |

| 6 | 787 | one paragraph on *Slacker*, the rest cinema-as-apparatus, which is fine here |
| 7 | 764 | most — distribution history is film history |

§2 and §5 together are 1,630 words — thirty per cent of the chapter — in which *Slacker* appears in
about forty. The 2026-07-03 panel flagged exactly these two sections for exactly this reason. §5 has
improved at the end; §2 has not improved at all. A reader who bought a book about Linklater and hits
900 words of Simmel, Burt, Obstfeld, Gould–Fernandez, Kalmijn–Flap, Small–Gose, Finkel and *PNAS*
before the film returns is a reader who starts skimming, and skimmers do not come back for §4, which
is the section that earns the chapter its place.

### Nobody ever describes a shot.

This is the finding I care about most. The chapter's thesis is that a camera chooses. In 5,400 words
it never says what the camera looks like doing it. No framing, no camera height, no take length, no
account of whether the thing pans, walks, tracks or simply waits; nothing about sound across a seam;
no timecode anywhere. The one visual description of a handoff in the chapter belongs to Chris Walters
in 1990 — "The camera eye slowly pulls back until it sees another nameless guy … and follows him
upstairs into another story already in progress." A 1990 newspaper critic did the looking, and the
2026 chapter quotes him doing it.

Your own `outline_v3.md` budgets 200 words for §4 move 1, "The first handoff, read closely, with
Walters's 1990 description alongside it." The draft spends about 120, and Walters carries them. The
plan was right and the draft under-delivered on it.

### Pacing, opening, ending

**Opening: strong, and I would not touch the first paragraph.** "The first face in *Slacker* belongs
to the director" is the right first sentence, and the credit joke — "Should have stayed at bus
station" — is the right first laugh. The one pacing note: by word 300 the reader is in 16mm, $23,000,
eleven weeks, sixteen screens and a box-office record number. Production data this early reads as
throat-clearing by other means. Move the gross and the screen count to note 3 and let the film's
method run one more beat before the trade figures arrive; you save about forty words and buy the
opening another paragraph of air.

**Middle: the sag is §2, and it is structural, not stylistic.** The section's front half (Simmel, the
definitions, the three questions, the *divide et impera* limit) is apparatus the reader will use. Its
back half — "What I am claiming is narrow," the two qualifications, and the *PNAS* paragraph — is
disciplinary bookkeeping: which literature failed to cite which other literature. It is addressed to a
management sociologist who is not buying this book. That is where the chapter's surplus lives.

**Ending: the last paragraph withholds its own best line and then closes on a sentence about the
chapter rather than the film.** "the film headed the charge off itself on one of the oblique-strategies
cards a passerby hands out in it" — headed it off *how*? The reader who has not memorised *Slacker*
learns nothing. The card says "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy." That is the
sentence the whole paragraph is reaching for, and v2 had it. I know why it went: `outline_v3.md`'s §4
do-not-use list bars "The film's signature line as a quotation" because four books drop "thing." That
is a reason for a note recording the variant, not a reason to suppress the film's own sentence at the
chapter's last beat. Also, that clause is the only claim about the film's content in the chapter with
no footnote attached to it.

Then: "Both kinds of coordination do their work in the open here, in a film anyone can watch, which is
the rarest thing about it." A reader who loves this film will not agree that this is the rarest thing
about it, and will resent being told. End on the day.

### The word count — my answer to your query

Yes, the extra four hundred words earn their place. No, they do not earn it where they currently sit.

The two paragraphs you are defending both stay. The Sundance thirds keep §1's premise from being
refutable in one sentence, and a referee who found that paragraph missing would ask why the premiere
went unmentioned. Cameron is the strongest published objection to your §5 and meeting it head-on is
the difference between an argument and a brief. Cutting either to reach a ceiling would be cutting
the two places where the chapter is most honest. Your cut ledger ranks them 6 and 3; I would rank
them 1 and 2 for *retention*.

The four hundred words are in §2. "What I am claiming is narrow" through "leaves the arrival of a
non-human one untouched" is roughly 205 words of literature audit that goes almost whole into a note
— the three-empty-sets warrant is a note's natural shape, and the *PNAS* observation is a note's
natural pleasure. Add the forty from the §1 box-office move and you are at about 5,160 before you add
anything.

Then spend it back on film: about 120 words describing two handoffs in §4, and about 60 putting
*Slacker* into §5 and §6. That lands you near 5,340 — over the ceiling, but over it in film rather
than in sociology. As the editor I will take that argument to the press and win it. I would not have
won it for a literature audit.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and the slop audit

### Ban 1 — no metaphor doing argumentative work

Largely honoured; this draft argues in statements. Two catches.

**§4.** "His word is string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order." Bordwell
reached for *string* to be dismissive, not to assert anything about sequence, and the chapter takes an
entailment of his figure as evidence for its own claim. The claim is true and is stated literally two
sentences later ("In presentation it is an ordered walk: scene one, then scene two, in that sequence,
chosen"). Let the literal sentence do the work and stop mining the metaphor.

**§7's title, "Enclosure."** The word appears in the heading and nowhere in the section. A reader has
to supply the commons analogy unaided, and it is doing the section's framing work by itself. Either
cash it in one plain sentence or retitle it something a table of contents can use.

### Ban 2 — no throat-clearing

**§5.** "Surge pricing is the wrong example here, and saying why sharpens the distinction." The second
clause announces the value of the move instead of making it. Delete it; the paragraph then sharpens
the distinction in front of the reader, which is better than being told it will.

**§5, softer.** "What a decade of subsequent work adds is a precise account of what a selector can do
that a camera cannot, and it comes to three things," followed by "The first is that…", "The second
thing is that…", "The third thing is that…" The scaffolding is legitimate but it is also the moment
the film leaves the room for 500 words. See the rewrite in Part 3, which does both jobs at once.

### Ban 3 — no meta-commentary, no forward references

**§4.** "That decision is constrained, and the constraint matters later." That is a forward reference
in plain clothes, and the cut ledger records you killing its louder sibling ("…and §5 is where that
gets settled"). Cut the second clause. §5 will make the constraint matter without an usher.

**§7.** "…which is the scale to hold in mind before comparing any of this with a recommender" —
instructions to the reader plus a gesture forward. The sixteen screens make the point on their own.

### Ban 4 — no unexplained jargon

Mostly excellent: *tertius gaudens*, dérive, civil inattention, structural hole, disintermediation and
nonterminal all get their plain sentence. Three misses, all in the two sections a film reader is
already struggling through.

- **§3, "agglomeration."** "Economists have a name for what a place like that does. Gilles Duranton
  and Diego Puga set out how agglomeration works…" The sentence promises the name and then never says
  what it means. One clause fixes it.
- **§3, "assortative matching," "observationally equivalent," "measured elasticity."** Three unglossed
  economics terms in two sentences. "assortative matching runs *negatively* with local market size" is
  the least legible sentence in the chapter for this book's reader.
- **§2, "*divide et impera*."** Italic Latin, unglossed, in a sentence already carrying a long Simmel
  quotation. The gloss costs three words.

### Slop audit (register-aware)

**No agentless passive of note.** The prose runs on named agents: Linklater pays and walks, Turan
disliked and described, Feld named the mechanism, Amazon reads and removes and declines to explain,
the Amsterdam court held. That is authored prose and it is the clearest signal in the file that a
person wrote it.

**The antithesis machine is under control.** `, not` / `rather than` / `is not a` combined run about
4.6 per thousand words, under the house target. One watch item: §7 has three "rather than"
constructions inside 120 words ("applying a default rather than killed by the bankruptcy," "editorial
promotion rather than personalisation," "changed shape rather than shrinking"). Vary one.

**The real tic is the paragraph closer.** Twenty-one of fifty-four body paragraphs end on a sentence
of thirteen words or fewer, and nearly every one is the same *kind* of sentence — a verdict that
reframes what came before:

> "So I want to know what people were following." / "Nobody is owed a return." / "One instance is
> enough." / "The evidence sits on the pricing side." / "…is a skill meeting a craft." / "She said no,
> and Linklater knew whom to ask."

Individually they are good. Several are the best sentences in their sections. At better than one
paragraph in three, arriving in the same slot every time, the shape stops reading as emphasis and
starts reading as a metronome — the one uniformity in the draft that a weekly-submissions editor
clocks. Convert five or six to end on the evidence instead and the rest recover their force. The
easiest candidates are the ones whose verdict merely restates the paragraph: "One instance is enough,"
"The evidence sits on the pricing side," "…is a skill meeting a craft."

**One instance of self-narrating rigor.** §2: "The warrant is documentary and anyone with three PDFs
can check it." The first clause announces the quality of the evidence; the sentences after it supply
the evidence. Keep the second half, which has your voice in it.

**No verbatim idea-repetition of note.** Setting and selector recur because they are the chapter's
terms. The viewer-not-characters point is made once in §4 and used in §5 without restatement. Good
discipline.

## Part 3 — Ranked revisions, paste-ready where I can write them

**1. §4 — describe two handoffs. Only you can write this one.**

After "A conventional film lives there for ninety minutes; this one gives it about two, then leaves
the son mid-arrest for a bystander who happened to watch," insert roughly 100 words of your own
looking, with timecodes: where the camera stands when the son is walked out, whether it pans or
tracks or simply lets the bystander walk into the frame, how long the shot holds before it commits to
him, what the sound does across the seam. Then do the same in twenty words for one later handoff that
works differently. I cannot draft this and no reviewer can. You have the disc; the chapter's whole
thesis is about this camera, and at present the only person who describes it is a newspaper critic in
1990.

**2. §7 — restore the card. Replace:**

> *Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself on one of the
> oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it. Its people withdrew from plot, career and
> convergence and stayed coordinated anyway — put within reach by a cheap and legible city, and shown
> to us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from them except a day, and gave the day back
> watchable. Both kinds of coordination do their work in the open here, in a film anyone can watch,
> which is the rarest thing about it.

**with:**

> *Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself. A woman
> stands on the street handing cards to passersby — oblique strategies, she says — and the one a
> stranger draws and reads aloud says: "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy." Its
> people withdrew from plot, career and convergence and stayed coordinated anyway — put within reach by
> a cheap and legible city, and shown to us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from them
> except a day, and gave the day back watchable.

Add a note: the subtitle track and Rosenbaum have "not the same thing as apathy"; several books drop

**3. §5 — put the camera in the room at the top. Replace:**

**7. §4 — stop mining the metaphor. Replace:**

**8. §3 — let the moon-landing man do the work your Schegloff paragraph needs. Replace:**

> A man leaves a coffee shop and a stranger falls into step beside him, delivering an unbroken

**with (check the walk against the disc before pasting):**

> A man walks home and a stranger falls into step beside him — "Say, pal! Did I overhear you say you

> anti-gravity drive taken from the Nazis, colonists on Mars since 1962. The man answers in syllables,
> and answers every time.

Your own Schegloff paragraph argues that in co-presence a member cannot simply decline to answer.
The film hands you the demonstration — "Mm-mmm," "Yeah," "Yeah, that's my birthday" — and the current

**9. §3 — gloss the economics. Replace:**

> No number attaches to it, because sharing, matching and learning produce observationally equivalent

**with (confirm my rendering of Mion and Naticchioni against the paper before pasting):**

> No number attaches to it. A big city helps people share suppliers, find matches and learn from one

## Verdict

**Minor revisions — accept in principle.** The argument is sound, the sources have been through a hard
verification pass, the prose is authored, and §4 alone would justify the chapter's place. One revision
round, no re-review, conditional on two things:

1. **The film comes back.** Two handoffs described in your own eyes in §4; *Slacker* present in §5 and
   §6 rather than waiting at the end of them; the card quoted in §7. The scene inventory you already
   built names the three best candidates for the second of these — the Video Backpacker for §6, the
   failed guest list and the transferable hand-stamp for §5, the passed-around camera at the party for
   §4 — and I would take any two. (Your §4 do-not-use list bars quoting the party dialogue; paraphrase
   is fine and the disc-check debt is already logged.)
2. **§2's literature audit goes to a note**, which is also how the chapter comes in near enough to
   5,000 that I can defend the overage.

On the query itself: keep the Sundance paragraph, keep Cameron, and send me the chapter at roughly
5,300 with the extra words in the film. I will take that to the press. Terminology change from the
abstract: approved, and the plainer pair is better — flag it in the front matter as you drafted.
Citation style: Chicago notes is what the collection uses; convert nothing.

## Biggest genuine strength

§4, and specifically the move that separates the story-world chain from the presentation-order walk,
then notices that the whole network-narrative literature dropped presentation order on purpose. That
is a real contribution to how this film gets read, it is grounded in the film's actual first ten
minutes, and it converts a dismissive aside by the most cited living formalist into the chapter's best
evidence. The Poulaki paragraph that follows — granting her the drift, keeping the choosing, and then
using her own floor ("A catalogue is not a system…") against her — is the most skilful piece of
argument in the draft.

## The one thing only the author can supply

**The looking.** Every other gap in this chapter can be closed by an editor, a copyeditor or a
research assistant: the glosses, the note conversions, the cadence, the word count. Nobody but you can
sit down with the disc and a stopwatch and say what the handoffs actually look like — where the camera
is standing when it decides to leave, how long it waits, whether it commits to the new body before or
after the old one is out of frame, and whether the seam sounds continuous when the image is not. The
chapter argues that a camera chooses. Until it shows that choosing happening on screen, in your
sentences rather than Chris Walters's, it is a very good argument about a film rather than a reading
of one.
