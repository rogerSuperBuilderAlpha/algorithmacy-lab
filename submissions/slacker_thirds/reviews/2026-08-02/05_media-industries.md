# Review — media-industries lens (§7, "Enclosure")

**Verdict: major revisions.** The repairs are confined to §7 and cost under 150 words of rewriting, but
one of them is a claim that contradicts its own source's explicit caveat, and another is a count that
does not add up against §5. Both sit in the section's payoff sentences.

## Step 0 — register note

The first person, the contractions, the em-dashes and the concessions delivered in stride are the
chapter's method, and I have not treated any of them as faults. "The fourth power is missing from the
page and I am not going to supply it" is the register working — an epistemic commitment stated in the
first person, not throat-clearing, and I would keep it verbatim if the arithmetic around it were right.

Everything below either states a claim §7 makes that its source does not support, or offers a
replacement that is shorter, more concrete, or both. Where a fix costs words, I say where to find them.

## Part 1 — theoretical rigor and structure

### 1.1 The powers do not add up

§5 names three powers and numbers them: "it comes to three things. The first is that it prices each
match… The second thing is that it reads the parties… The third thing is that it can remove a party."

§7 then writes: "Amazon publishes its own account of how titles are licensed for Prime Video, and
**three of the powers described above are in it**." What follows is reading, removal, and *declining to
explain* — and declining to explain was never named as a power. Then: "**The fourth power** is missing
from the page… Amazon publishes nothing about pricing." Pricing is §5's *first* power, not a fourth.

So the section claims a three-for-three match where the record supports two-for-three, and invents a
fourth slot to hold the miss. The deep-research file operated on a four-power list ("Amazon publishes
three of the four powers the chapter attributes to platforms: it reads…, it removes…, and it refuses
explanation"); §5 was compressed to three and §7 kept the old bookkeeping. It is a compression artifact
and it is the first thing a referee will circle, because §5's three-power structure is what the whole
back half of the chapter runs on.

The honest version is better than the broken one. Two of three appear; the third is absent and the
chapter says so; and Amazon adds a power the chapter had not counted, which is precisely the one the
section's closing contrast needs. Rewrite at 3.1 below.

A second, smaller slippage in the same sentence. §5's "reads the parties" means the selector knows
*these particular counterparties* and varies what it does accordingly — Dubal's drivers. Amazon's
"performance metrics and viewing behavior (like buy/rent transactions, unique customer streams, or
total hours)" is reading *the audience* in aggregate and scoring *the title*. That still satisfies the
third of §2's three questions, but it is a different object, and one clause should say which.

### 1.2 Tzioumakis is handled well on the artifact and badly on the frame

Take the good half first, because it is genuinely good. "The third tier's fall from 10.22 to 2.11
percent… is, in his phrase, only 'seemingly suggesting' a collapse, because he reclassified the largest
earners upward himself." I retrieved the version of record and that is right. His sentence reads: "This
2.11 percent compares with 10.22 percent for an average of 105 distributors in the 1998–2008 period,
seemingly suggesting a massive shift in this category (many more distributors vie for much less market
share). **However**, as I mentioned earlier, the 10.22 percent in 1998–2008 includes former studios
such as MGM, companies such as Lionsgate and Artisan that were having major runaway hits, companies
that aspired to mini-major status such as Newmarket, and other entertainment conglomerate-owned
subsidiaries such as IFC Films." Very few people who cite that number carry the "However." This chapter
does. Credit where it is due.

Three problems around it.

**(a) The frame attributes to him the refusal of a claim he establishes.** "The easy version of this
story is that the studios closed their specialty divisions and the streamers took over, and Yannis
Tzioumakis's own numbers refuse it." The first conjunct is his own documented premise — his article is
built on the Great Studio Pullback, and his body text reads "The closure of four studio specialty film
divisions (Warner Independent, Picturehouse, Paramount Vantage, and Miramax) and the failed relaunch of
United Artists." He refuses the *second* conjunct and the inference, not the closures. As written, the
sentence reads as though the chapter has not noticed what its source argues.

**(b) "the market" is the domestic theatrical gross, and "the acquisition market" is not what he
measures.** His tables are headed "Domestic Market Share" and are sourced "All figures have been
obtained from The Numbers" — box office, tier by tier, 1998–2019. He hedges his own conclusion to it:
the late indies "became the dominant force in the industry's middle tier in the 2010s, **at least when
it comes to the theatrical film market**." The chapter's "The acquisition market changed shape rather
than shrinking" therefore rests on a source that measures release-side receipts. The section's own
research file records that no refereed account of the 2024–26 acquisition market exists at all. One
word fixes it: *distribution*.

**(c) "looks more like the present than like the boom in between" has nothing under it.** The evidence
that was supposed to carry it — Sundance 2026, A24 taking *The Invite* at eight figures over Netflix,
Searchlight, Focus, Apple and Sony, and only about a dozen of ninety-plus premieres arriving with
distribution — never made it into the chapter. What is left is an unsourced comparative about a market
the chapter elsewhere concedes nobody has studied, and it is contestable in both directions on the
project's own record ($25m for *CODA*, $20m for *Fair Play*, a seven-figure worldwide deal for
*Leviticus*). Cut it, or restore one clause of trade evidence to hold it up.

### 1.3 Bengesser, Hilborn and Steemers: the discipline is named and then broken

This is the most serious finding in the section, and I retrieved the CC-BY version of record from
Aarhus PURE to check it rather than take the research file's word.

The chapter writes: "Their landing-page figures measure editorial promotion rather than personalisation
— **they cleared cookies daily and used fresh accounts to strip personalisation out** — which makes the
point sharper. A page that chooses what you see next, **without knowing who you are**, is the nearest
thing streaming has to Linklater's camera."

The article, p. 1523, verbatim: "The chosen platforms were searched daily for UK screen content using
'pure', newly created accounts with limited personal information (age) and no expression of genre
preferences. Moreover, to limit personalisation as much as possible, browser data and cookies were
cleared every day. **However, it is by no means certain that this can be excluded totally.**"

And in their limitations, p. 1526: "For complex sites like Netflix and Prime Video, documentation is
time-consuming, subject to human error, and it is not entirely clear whether personalisation can be
[excluded]."

And in the conclusion, p. 1527: "**more evidence is needed about the algorithmic filtering and
promotional processes happening between VoD catalogues as database back-ends and VoD landing pages as
interface front-ends**."

Three things follow. The authors do not claim personalisation was stripped out; they claim it was
limited as far as they could and say twice that they cannot be sure. They explicitly decline to
characterise what produces the landing page as editorial rather than algorithmic — that is the open
question they end on. And the chapter's payoff, "a page that chooses what you see next, without knowing
who you are," converts a methodological control the authors themselves distrust into a property of the
artifact. The deep-research file's gloss ("so it measures editorial promotion") overstated the source,
and the chapter inherited the overstatement.

The good news: the argument survives the correction, and the corrected version is more interesting. A
research team can suppress most of what the page knows about a viewer and *still* finds fewer than one
British title in ten on the landing page. That is a selector operating below personalisation, on
everybody, which is exactly the camera-shaped thing the chapter is looking for — and it is a stronger
claim than "the page doesn't know who you are," which is false of the page any actual viewer opens.

### 1.4 The two-stage numbers chain as a funnel that is not one

"15,584 British titles in European video-on-demand circulation become 1,165 in Netflix's European
catalogues, and inside a national catalogue fewer than one title in ten reaches the landing page."

The two figures check out — p. 1517: "Considering the vast number of total British first-produced
titles in VoD circulation (15,584 unique films and TV titles), the British content across all European
Netflix catalogues consists of relatively few titles, namely 1165 unique British first-produced film
and TV titles." Two repairs to the second clause.

First, the denominator. Table 2's landing-page counts (Denmark 51, Germany 42, Netherlands 48, Italy
46) run against per-country counts of *British* titles in the national Netflix catalogue (567, 602,
935, 746) — 9.0, 7.0, 5.1 and 6.2 percent. The article's own sentence: "When only looking at the
landing pages, the share of **British content** featured is below 10% for all three markets." The
chapter's "inside a national catalogue fewer than one title in ten" drops the qualifier and reads as a
statement about the catalogue at large.

Second, the "and" makes the two stages look nested — 15,584 → 1,165 → a tenth of that. They are not.
The per-country catalogue counts are not subsets of the 1,165, and the chapter's own next sentences
insist the stages are separate. A single word ("of those still in a national catalogue" / "of the
British titles in any one national catalogue") settles it.

Third, and cheapest: the landing-page figure is a seven-day snapshot on a laptop in one country, and
the authors flag it — "Interface monitoring provides only a snapshot of what is being promoted at a
particular point in time." "Reaches the landing page" states a standing property. "Turned up on the
landing page in the week they watched it" states what was measured, at the same length.

### 1.5 The 1991 half rests on one informant, and does not say so

The chapter's disclosure habits are good elsewhere — "I have read the abstract only," "his survey is
non-exhaustive and outdoor-only, and he says so." §7 is where the habit lapses. Pierson supplies the
chain, the Sundance thirds, the Dobie complaint, the *Film Comment* route to the buyer, and the
video-release refusal. Macor supplies the rest — and Macor's account of the video refusal is itself
built on a Pierson interview (20 September 2004) and Pierson's own *Spike, Mike Reloaded*, 194–95. So
the two apparently independent sources are one informant plus his interviewer. The section's own
research recorded this and asked for it in the prose: "the producer's representative has no scholarly
literature at all, which means Pierson's memoir is not one account among several but *the* account — a
dependency the section should name." It is not named. Twenty-five words.

Related and smaller: "Linklater and Pierson took the video release to **a named vice-president** at
Orion and lost." Naming that the person had a name, while withholding it, undercuts the sentence the
whole section turns on — refusal was attributable. She is Susan Blodgett, who ran Orion's home-video
division. Attribute the refusal.

Also related: "The earlier gate had responded — a paragraph in *Film Comment* reached a buyer, who
telephoned relatives in Austin to ask whether the Dobie sellouts were real." Macor's sentence continues
past where the chapter stops: "but Pierson insists that Barker had done this before he traveled to
Maine in early August." Two participants disagreeing about whether the phone call was a check or a
courtesy is a sharper picture of a chain made of people than the tidy version, and it costs a clause.

### 1.6 The section's strongest available fact is missing, and it is the one the title promises

"Enclosure" is the section's title and never appears in its body, never gets a sentence of definition,
and is exposed on its own record — Tzioumakis says the tier refilled, Waldfogel documents "substantial
growth in the number of movies brought to market." What would earn the word is sitting in the research
file, dated, verbatim, and unused. I fetched the Amazon page myself to confirm it:

> "Access to self-service submissions is by invitation only. We are not currently accepting new content
> submissions for rent or buy from content providers that do not already have an account."

> "We aren't accepting unsolicited submissions for content for 'Included with Prime'/SVOD. Rights
> holders can't request consideration at this time."

Against Smits's 2017 fieldwork finding the same route "unfiltered, with no gatekeeping restrictions."
That is a door that was open nine years ago and is shut now — a dated enclosure inside the platform
era, and the exact structural successor to Pierson walking an unsolicited 16mm film to a buyer. Without
it the section documents a *narrowing* (Bengesser) and a *shape change* (Tzioumakis) and calls the
result enclosure. With it, the title is earned in one sentence.

### 1.7 Is the Amazon material fairly read? Mostly — with two omissions that cut against the chapter

Every quotation is verbatim; I checked all four against the live page (last updated 2025-08-05). Two
things the page says are left out, and both matter for fairness.

**The dropped hedge.** Amazon's sentence begins "**Unless otherwise indicated,** removed titles (or
titles not selected for licensing) may not be resubmitted or appealed." The chapter quotes it without
the qualifier. Quoting a policy's absolute-sounding clause while silently dropping the company's own
escape hatch is the move a media-industries referee is trained to catch.

**The dropped concession.** The sentence immediately before it reads: "We understand you invest a lot
in your content and **will provide you with visibility in your Slate portal if your title isn't
selected for licensing or removed from our catalog**." Amazon promises the verdict and withholds the
reasons. The chapter presents the page as pure refusal to explain. Including the concession makes the
contrast *better*: Blodgett gave reasons and could be argued with; Amazon gives an outcome and a
sentence explaining that there will be no explanation.

**Is the absence of a pricing claim handled honestly? Yes — and it is the best moment in the section.**
"The fourth power is missing from the page and I am not going to supply it" is exactly right in
temperament. One narrowing: "Amazon publishes nothing about pricing" is a universal claim about a
company, and the evidence supports only a claim about a page. I searched the retrieved page for
"price," "pricing" and "royalty" and found nothing, which establishes that *this* page carries no
pricing terms. Write that.

## Part 2 — the four bans, and the slop audit

**Ban 1, metaphor doing argumentative work.** Clean in §7. "Every link in the chain… was a person who
could be telephoned" states the literal claim inside the metaphor. "Enclosure" in the title is the one
exposure, and §1.6 is the fix.

**Ban 2, throat-clearing.** Clean.

**Ban 3, meta-commentary.** One instance, and it is an instruction to the reader rather than a claim:
"…sixteen placements argued for one at a time, **which is the scale to hold in mind before comparing
any of this with a recommender**." Say the thing instead of telling the reader to hold it in mind.

**Ban 4, jargon unexplained.** "Producer's representative" is glossed on first use, which is the right
call. "Enclosure" is not — see above. "Prominence" is doing technical work (it is a policy term of art
in European AV regulation) and the chapter's "Prominence decides what is found" does define it in
passing; that is enough.

**Slop audit.** §7 runs 764 words and carries six antithesis constructions: "not a national audience
reached but," four "rather than," and "more like the present than." Just under eight per thousand,
against the repo's own target of well under five. Four of them earn their contrast. Two do not, and
both are in sentences I am already asking you to change: "measure editorial promotion rather than
personalisation" (§1.3 replaces it) and "changed shape rather than shrinking" (keep this one — the
wrong alternative is exactly the one a reader reaches for). Kill "contestable rather than proprietary"
in the closing paragraph if a word is needed; the positive alone carries it.

**One verbatim repeat.** §1: "when Orion Classics released it nationally the following July it took in
something over a million dollars on a top of sixteen screens." §7: "Orion Classics bought it and
released it nationally on a top of sixteen screens." The §7 gloss is new; the setup is not. Ten words
available.

**One unit error, twice.** Box Office Mojo's field is "Widest Release **16 theaters**," not screens. A
sixteen-theatre platform release and a sixteen-screen one are different objects to anyone in this
subfield. Fix in §1 and §7.

## Part 3 — ranked, paste-ready

**1. The Amazon paragraph (fixes 1.1, 1.5-Blodgett, 1.7). Net +20 words.**

> What changed is what a party is told. Amazon publishes its own account of how titles are licensed for
> Prime Video, and two of the three powers are in it. It reads, though what it reads is the audience:
> "we continuously review performance metrics and viewing behavior." It removes: content "may be
> removed at any time at the sole discretion of Prime Video." The third is missing from the page and I
> am not going to supply it; the page says nothing about what a licence pays. It adds one I had not
> counted. Amazon will give a rights holder the verdict — "visibility in your Slate portal if your
> title isn't selected" — and, "unless otherwise indicated," nothing else: a rejected title "may not be
> resubmitted or appealed," and "we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was
> or was not selected for licensing." Susan Blodgett, the vice-president who ran Orion's home video,
> also said no. Linklater knew whom to ask why.

**2. The Bengesser paragraph (fixes 1.3, 1.4). Net +8 words.**

> Cathrin Bengesser, Matthew Hilborn and Jeanette Steemers measured both: 15,584 British titles in
> European video-on-demand circulation become 1,165 across Netflix's European catalogues, and of the
> British titles in any one national catalogue fewer than one in ten turned up on the landing page in
> the week they watched it. Licensing decides what can be found. Prominence decides what is found. They

> excluded totally." Even so, a page that chooses what you see next, and chooses the same thing for
> everybody, is the nearest thing streaming has to Linklater's camera.

**3. The Tzioumakis paragraph (fixes 1.2). Net −14 words.**

> The easy version of this story is that the studios closed their specialty divisions and the streamers
> took over. Yannis Tzioumakis establishes the first half and his numbers refuse the second. The third

> modest offer taken on a template.

*(If the trade evidence goes back in, the last sentence can carry its comparison: "…taken on a
template — which at Sundance 2026, where only about a dozen of ninety-plus premieres arrived with a
distributor, would not be remarkable." That needs the IndieWire wrap-up re-cited; the research file's

**4. The submission door (fixes 1.6). +48 words, and it earns the section title.** Place it directly
before the Amazon paragraph:

> In 2017 Roderik Smits found the route into Amazon "unfiltered, with no gatekeeping restrictions." The
> page now reads: "Access to self-service submissions is by invitation only." For streaming it is
> flatter — "We aren't accepting unsolicited submissions… Rights holders can't request consideration at
> this time." Pierson took an unsolicited 16mm film to a buyer. On this route nobody can.

**5. The single-informant disclosure (fixes 1.5). +26 words.** After "Pierson, a producer's
representative whose job was introducing films to buyers, took it to them":

**6. Two small ones. −12 words.**

- "…sixteen placements argued for one at a time, which is the scale to hold in mind before comparing

## Verdict

**Major revisions**, scoped to §7. Nothing here needs new research — every fix above is drawn from
sources the project has already verified, and I re-retrieved the three that matter. But the section
currently states as fact something its own source twice declines to state, and miscounts its own
argument's three powers in the paragraph where the argument lands. Those are not line edits.

## Biggest genuine strength

The section refuses two easy wins in a row, and both refusals are correct on the record. It carries
Tzioumakis's "However" — the reclassification that makes the third-tier collapse an artifact — where
almost every citation of that number drops it. And it declines to assert a pricing power it cannot
source, in a sentence that says so out loud: "The fourth power is missing from the page and I am not
going to supply it." A section about platforms that will not explain themselves earns its standing by
explaining itself, and this one does. Fix the count around that sentence and it becomes the best
paragraph in the chapter.

## The one thing only the author can supply

Whether §7 is about *enclosure* or about *replacement*. The evidence assembled here supports the second
cleanly and the first only with the Amazon submission door added — and even then, Tzioumakis's refilled
middle tier and Waldfogel's "substantial growth in the number of movies brought to market" are standing
counter-evidence that the section neither cites nor concedes. A media-industries referee will produce
both. The author has to decide whether the claim is that fewer things reach viewers (which needs the
counter-evidence met) or that the *route* changed from one made of addressable people to one that
narrows twice and explains neither (which the section proves, and which does not need the word
enclosure at all). Retitling is cheaper than winning the first argument.
