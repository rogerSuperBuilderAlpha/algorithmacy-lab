# Panel review — critical theory (lens: section 6)

**Verdict: major revisions**, and the scope is narrow. Section 6 opens on a quotation that says something
other than what the chapter has it saying, and three of its load-bearing moves are asserted where the
sources would have earned them in a clause. Everything else I was asked to look at holds. Sections 1–5
and 7 need nothing from me.

## Step 0 — register

The first-person steering, the scenes-before-theses order, the concessions taken in stride: all of that
is working, and §6 is the best-paced section in the chapter. Nothing below asks for a nominalisation, a
hedge, or a passive; every rewrite I propose is shorter or the same length as what it replaces, in the
chapter's own voice.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1.1 The Münsterberg sentence is about the theater, and it reverses his thesis

This is the one thing in the section that is wrong on the record rather than merely thin.

> Hugo Münsterberg answered in 1916, in the first book of film psychology: "our attention is constantly
> drawn in accordance with the intentions of the producers."

I read the passage in two independent texts (Project Gutenberg #15383, and the page-marked 1916 D.
Appleton scan at archive.org, `photoplaypsychol00mnrich`). The sentence sits on p. 76, inside
Münsterberg's account of the **stage**, and the paragraph it opens is the setup for the theater's
limits. In full, with what surrounds it:

> How does the theater performance differ in this respect from life? … If we really enter into the
> spirit of the play, our attention is constantly drawn in accordance with the intentions of the
> producers.
> Surely the theater has no lack of means to draw this involuntary attention to any important point. To
> begin with, the actor who speaks holds our attention more strongly than the actors who at that time
> are silent. (pp. 76–77)

Two pages of stagecraft follow. Then p. 77 ends: "The question arises: how does the photoplay secure the
needed shifting of attention?" — which is where the film argument starts. So the chapter's page cite
(76–77) is honest and the words are verbatim; the referent is wrong. A collection on Linklater will have
at least one reader who has taught *The Photoplay*, and this is the first sentence of the section.

The replacement is on p. 78, is about film, and is stronger for the chapter's purpose:

> If we follow the play in a genuine attitude of theatrical interest, we must accept those cues for our
> attention which the playwright and the producers have prepared for us.

There is a second problem underneath, and it is the interesting one. Münsterberg's whole theory runs the
opposite way from the chapter's. The photoplay, for him, does not subject the viewer to an arranger; it
externalises the viewer's own mind. His italicised claim, at pp. 87–88:

> The close-up has objectified in our world of perception our mental act of attention and by it has
> furnished art with a means which far transcends the power of any theater stage.

And at p. 89: "It is as if the outer world itself became molded in accordance with our fleeting turns of
attention." Münsterberg is a neo-Kantian, and the argument of *The Photoplay* is that film obeys the
laws of the mind — attention, memory, imagination, emotion — one chapter each. Recruiting him as the
first witness for "you learn to follow somebody else's choices" without saying that costs the chapter
more than it saves, because the concession is free and the register already runs on concessions taken in
stride. Say the direction of fit is his, not yours, and the 1916 date still does the work you want.

A third, smaller thing. The question §6 asks is genetic — *where does a 1991 audience get the skill* —
and Münsterberg answers a different one. He describes how attention gets steered; he says nothing about
acquisition. Ildirar and Ewing are the section's actual answer to the question as posed, four paragraphs
later. Münsterberg's job is to date the observation, not to answer it, and the sentence should say so.

### 1.2 Benjamin's valence is right, and it is asserted rather than shown

> His bet was hopeful; the training was to produce a public equal to the machinery.

Hopeful is defensible; I checked. But the chapter gives a reader no reason to believe it, and the reason
sits in the next sentence of the same paragraph on the same page. From the Belknap Second Version, p.
108, immediately after the training sentence the chapter quotes:

> Dealing with this apparatus also teaches them that technology will release them from their enslavement
> to the powers of the apparatus only when humanity's whole constitution has adapted itself to the new
> productive forces which the second technology has set free.

That is the hope, and it arrives with its condition attached — release, but only on a whole-constitution
adaptation nobody was steering. Quoting it costs one sentence and does three jobs: it earns "hopeful,"
it keeps Benjamin from reading as a cheerful ancestor of the cognitive-science paragraphs that follow,
and it hands §7 the vocabulary it is reaching for.

While I was in the essay I checked the surrounding frame, because "a public equal to the machinery" is
not quite what is there. The training sentence sits at the end of §VI, in the argument about the *second
technology*, whose aim Benjamin says is "an interplay between nature and humanity," and whose origin
"lies, in other words, in play." The goal is *Spiel*, not competence. "Equal to the machinery" imports a
skill relation — the chapter's own thesis — into a sentence that is about emancipation. Small, but the
chapter is precise everywhere else, and precision here is cheap.

And there is a condition in §XIII the chapter would want if it knew about it. Benjamin says every person
now has a legitimate claim to be reproduced, then, p. 115: "In western Europe today, the capitalist
exploitation of film obstructs the human being's legitimate claim to being reproduced." The hope is
conditional on the mode of production, and the obstruction is the film industry's publicity machine,
star careers, polls, beauty contests. That is §7's argument, in 1936, from the author the chapter is
already citing. Right now Benjamin appears in this chapter as a psychologist of apperception and not as
a critical theorist at all, which is the single largest missed opportunity in the section.

### 1.3 The removal power is already in Benjamin, and the concession makes §5 stronger

§5 says platforms hold three powers a camera lacks — pricing, reading, removal — and §6 never revisits
it. But removal is in the essay §6 is built on. Second Version, p. 111, in the passage that also gives
the chapter its examiner:

> Meanwhile the work process, especially since it has been standardized by the assembly line, daily
> generates countless mechanized tests. These tests are performed unawares, and those who fail are
> excluded from the work process. … These tests, unlike those in the world of sports, are incapable of
> being publicly exhibited to the degree one would desire. And this is precisely where film comes into
> play. Film makes test performances capable of being exhibited, by turning that ability itself into a
> test.

I want to be exact about how much this costs, because it is less than it looks. Benjamin's tested party
is the film *actor*, and his examiner is the director; nothing there has the apparatus testing a
spectator, and no price is attached to anything anywhere in the essay. §5's sentence — "A camera can
pass you over. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take you off the street" — survives intact.
What does not survive is the tacit periodisation, in which the apparatus trains you first and learns to
exclude you later. Conceding the exclusion and resting the novelty on pricing is the stronger claim, and
it is also the one the section's own research file arrived at.

The chapter's footnote 56 already carries the Zohn correction, and it is right on both halves. I
confirmed the wording and the placement: "The public is an examiner, but an absent-minded one" is the
last sentence of §XV in Zohn's *Illuminations* translation, immediately before the Epilogue; and in the
Second Version "examiner" occurs twice, once as "the examiner in an aptitude test" (the director, p.
111) and once as "closer examination" (p. 119). Nothing to fix. It is worth noticing what the correction
hands you, though: Benjamin's examiner is a *selector*, not a spectator. The chapter passes that by in a
footnote.

### 1.4 Hansen is bounded correctly and then spent on nothing

The bounding is right, and I verified both sentences against the full text (p. 69 for the quotation,
which falls before the [End Page 69] marker; p. 59 for the scope). Her sentence is verbatim. Her own
frame is "roughly from the 1920s through the 1950s … and the contemporaneity of a particular kind of
cinema, mainstream Hollywood." The chapter states both. Good.

The trouble is what the sentence is doing there. Hansen is introduced with "keeps that half in view," an
antecedent a reader cannot resolve — half of what? Benjamin's bet has just been called hopeful, and
nothing has been split in two. Then the sentence she supplies is undercut inside its own em-dash, twice,
before it has said anything. She arrives, is qualified, and leaves, and the paragraph would read
identically without her.

Her actual argument would do work here. "The Mass Production of the Senses" is about cinema as a
*reflexive* horizon — she reads Benjamin and Kracauer "as an effort to theorize this relation as a new
mode of reflexivity" (p. 69) — which is the difference between an audience trained and an audience
practising. That distinction is precisely what §6's closing question needs and does not have. If Hansen
is going to cost a sentence, make her carry the reflexivity, not the horizon.

One consequence to face directly: with Hansen bounded to three decades, the chapter's "a century of
cinema" (§7, twice) is now unsourced. I think it is fine — Münsterberg 1916 to Loschky 2015 is a century
of citations, and the claim is about filmmaking practice rather than about anyone's thesis — but say it
in your own voice rather than letting it drift back onto her.

### 1.5 The evidentiary keystone has a population, and the chapter does not name it

> Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing ran the classic Kuleshov montages … for viewers who had never watched
> a film. … Experienced viewers did connect them.

I read the accepted manuscript. The first-time viewers are twenty people aged 58–72 living in small
isolated houses in the mountains south of Isparta, Turkey, in a district only recently connected to the
electrical grid; seven were illiterate; mean schooling 1.95 years; many assumed television was "a visual
radio." The "experienced viewers" are not a filmgoing audience either — they are twenty people from the
same region, same dialect, same lifestyle, "with a little more access to luxuries," mean schooling 3.1
years, whose relevant experience is *some television*. The authors report that the control group was
significantly younger, F(57,2) = 3.7, p = .03.

None of that weakens the finding. It sharpens it, and in the chapter's favour: the experience that
produces cut-reading turns out to be a modest quantity of television, not a lifetime of cinema, which is
exactly why the skill could be general enough to carry an audience through *Slacker*. What the chapter
cannot do is leave it unsaid. A reader who pictures "experienced viewers" as a normal audience has been
allowed to picture the wrong study, and a reader who notices what the study actually is will ask why the
chapter's one piece of hard evidence for a learned Western perceptual competence is a comparison between
two groups of elderly Turkish villagers, presented without its population. That is the shape of a
critique the chapter should make itself rather than receive: the film-cognition literature's standard
design treats the non-viewer as the natural baseline and the conventional reading as the acquired
achievement, and it is worth one clause to say whose convention is being acquired. The age confound is
the authors' own, reported by them, and belongs in the note.

### 1.6 The tyranny of film may be motion, and the same team said so

The chapter's craft paragraph rests on Smith and Henderson's edit blindness and on Loschky and
colleagues. I verified the Loschky sentence in the published article — "our analyses of attentional
synchrony showed strong evidence that the filmmakers influenced when and where viewers attended,
irrespective of their viewing condition or whether they made the critical inference" — and its stimulus,
a twelve-second, six-shot sequence from *Moonraker*.

Loschky's own team then tested what drives the effect. Hutson, Chandran, Magliano, Smith and Loschky,
*Cognitive Science* 46, no. 5 (2022), abstract, which I read via PubMed (PMID 35579883): "The goal of
the present study was to test if the tyranny of film is driven by motion. … The current study found that
when the scene was shown as a slideshow, the context manipulation produced differences in attentional
selection (i.e., it attenuated attentional synchrony)."

If a large part of the tyranny of film is motion rather than arrangement, then the bridge from
cut-reading to selector-reading is weaker than the paragraph implies, because motion is a brute attractor
and a recommender has none. This is the mechanism-level objection to §6's whole thesis, it comes from the
authors the section already cites, and the section's own research file said to absorb it in two places.
Ducking it is the one place where I think the chapter's otherwise excellent habit of naming what cuts
against it has failed.

### 1.7 The political argument: earned three-quarters of the way, then asserted at the end

The discipline in this chapter is unusual and I want to say so before I object. §6 refuses the transfer
claim on the record rather than hedging it. §5 declines to assert Dubal's personalisation and says why.
§7 refuses to supply Amazon's missing fourth power. Cameron is quoted against the chapter's own
interest. That is a chapter that has decided not to get its politics for free, and most of it succeeds.

Two places where it does not.

**The closing.** "Reading them is the precondition, and reading is the part with a century of teaching
behind it." That sentence asserts a political priority — comprehension first, contestation after — and
the chapter's own best evidence points the other way. Heiland's riders who read the algorithm *wrongly*
were disciplined into riding faster. Heiland's riders who read it *rightly*, on proximity to
restaurants, "used this knowledge to influence the work intensity" — that is, they used an accurate
reading to optimise themselves inside the system. On the chapter's own two data points, accurate reading
produced compliance in one case and self-intensification in the other, and contestation in neither.
There is a real answer available: reading is necessary and demonstrably insufficient, and the century of
teaching produced spectators, not parties. But the chapter has to say it, because as printed the last
paragraph takes back the honesty of the paragraph three above it.

**The nostalgia.** §7's normative centre is address — a chain of people who could be telephoned, whose
refusals had names. The chapter never asks who got to place the call. §3 does this work beautifully for
the street: it establishes that a code licensing the white twenty-something with the pap smear "has
always been available against somebody else," with *Papachristou* and May behind it. §7 needs the same
two sentences and does not have them. Linklater reached Pierson because Pierson's job existed and
Linklater was legible to it; a buyer telephoned relatives in Austin to check the Dobie sellouts, which is
a network doing what networks do. Named refusal is a real good and it was unevenly distributed, and
saying so is what keeps enclosure from reading as a lament for a better gate.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and an AI-slop audit

**Ban 1, metaphor doing argumentative work.** §6 passes, once by a hair. "*Slacker* takes away the
alibi" puts the figure before the literal claim rather than after it, but the literal claim lands in the
next two sentences and the figure is a compression rather than a substitute. Leave it. "Hinson's
'miraculous ease' is a skill meeting a craft" is a restatement of two literal claims, so it is fine.
Chapter-wide I found nothing that violates this ban.

**Ban 2, throat-clearing.** One instance, §6: "What is actually measured is this." Its only job is to
announce that measurements are coming. It is defensible as an implied claim — *this and no more is
measured* — but that claim is not in the sentence. Fix in Part 3.

**Ban 3, meta-commentary.** One violation, and it is in §4, not §6: "That decision is constrained, and
the constraint matters later." That is a forward reference to §5. The sentence works without the second
clause; the constraint will announce itself when Uber arrives.

**Ban 4, unexplained jargon.** Two in one sentence of §6: "diegetic sound" and "cuts violating the
180-degree rule." Both are film-studies house vocabulary, and this collection's readers own them — but
the chapter is also addressed to sociology and platform readers, and it glosses "structural hole,"
"civil inattention," "dérive" and "daisy chain" for exactly that reason. Consistency says gloss these
too; four words apiece. "Apperceptions" rides inside the Benjamin quotation and is fine, since the
chapter's own gloss of the training claim carries the sense.

**Slop audit.** The prose is not machine-shaped and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Two measurable
tics, both mild:

- *The pseudo-cleft opener.* "What X is/does…" opens eleven sentences across 5,414 words: "What the film
  does next," "What it can do to you," "What I am claiming," "What makes these scenes play," "What
  licenses the monologue," "What connects one scene to the next," "What links *Gomorrah*," "What is
  actually measured," "What workers build instead," "What replaced that chain," "What changed is." Each
  is good; eleven is a metronome, and three of them cluster in §§6–7 where the chapter most wants to
  sound like it is thinking rather than reciting. Convert two or three to plain subject-verb.
- *"Rather than"* appears thirteen times (2.4 per thousand words). Six are load-bearing contrasts a
  reader would otherwise get wrong. The rest are decoration: "the driver they identify is market size
  rather than density as such," "a preliminary posture rather than a final judgment," "learned rather
  than given," "the authors propose this rather than show it," "measure editorial promotion rather than
  personalisation," "changed shape rather than shrinking," "contestable rather than proprietary."
  Cutting three would not cost a single claim.

No filler transitions, no empty closers, no agentless passives of consequence (four instances of
`has been/have been`, all of them either quoted or genuinely agentless). Paragraph shapes vary. The
§6 budget is the only structural problem: 787 words against the outline's 660. My Benjamin and
Münsterberg repairs add about fifty; pay for them by cutting the Brodsky sentence (Smith, Essex and
Bedford already carry the transfer limit) and by tightening the Kuleshov numbers into one sentence.

## Verdict

**Major revisions**, scoped: §6 needs items 1–6 and §7 needs 7–8. Everything else on this list is
half an hour's work. Nothing in the section's argument is wrong; the section has simply been letting
its sources be authorities instead of witnesses, and in one case has an authority saying the opposite
of what it is quoted for.

## Biggest genuine strength

The section refuses to buy its own conclusion. It states the transfer claim, then reports that nobody
has tested it and that the field which would has no film variable anywhere in it, then declines to
predict the result — and then, instead of closing on that vacancy, replaces the missing skill with what
workers demonstrably build: half an accurate model and half folklore the platform has no reason to
correct. "Convergence is uneven, and nobody has a reason to correct the flattering half" is the best
sentence in the chapter, and it is earned three ways over. Most chapters that reach this argument assert
the transfer and move on. This one turned an absence into the finding, which is the harder and better
paper.

## The one thing only the author can supply

Whether cinematic training produced *spectators* rather than *parties* — and whether the chapter is
willing to say so. Everything §6 measures is a competence at receiving an arrangement: following a cut,
attributing intent to a hidden arranger, not noticing the choosing. Nothing in it is a competence at
answering back. If that is right, the century of teaching is not a resource for contesting selectors but
part of what makes them uncontested, which turns the chapter's closing from hopeful into something
sharper and truer to its own evidence. Benjamin was already there — the training is toward the
apparatus, and the release is conditional on something outside the training — and Hansen's reflexivity
is the strongest available argument against it. That is an author's judgment about what the chapter is
for. I can tell you the evidence supports the darker reading; only you can decide whether to take it.
