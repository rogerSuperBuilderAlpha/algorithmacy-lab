# Review 02 — Urban Sociology (Lofland, Goffman, Milgram, Oldenburg, Anderson, agglomeration, Austin 1990)

Panel of 2026-07-22. Target: `chapter/chapter.md` (read in full, all 50 body paragraphs plus notes,
bibliography, filmography). Constraints honored: July 3 "Declined" items not re-flagged; "Accepted" items
treated as settled; abstract-verbatim lines off-limits; the six new scene insertions audited hardest.

## Verdict

**Minor revisions.** The single most important fix: the §5 club micro-thirds sentence (lines 251–253) calls
three devices "engineering thirds" when, under the chapter's own three-question test, only the guest list
comes close — the entry stamp reads a type-mark and the 2 a.m. eviction acts on the whole crowd blind. The
sociology everywhere else now holds; the fix is a reframe, not a retreat, and it makes the passage stronger.

## Step 0 — Register and bar

Register: first-person essay in the Bordwell/Hansen mold per `style_models.md` — contractions, scene-first
section openings, concessions in stride, questions asked and answered, aphorism budget ~3. The accepted
abstract's own lines ("Cities do this. Matchmakers do this. Apps do this."; "Austin hosts while the camera

Bar: the house style's invariants (named agents, claim-first, earned contrast only, no performed candor)
plus the 2026 checklist, weighted toward structural tells over word-lists. My job is to make the piece
better at being itself; any rewrite of mine that is more nominalized than the original is wrong by rule.

## Part 1 — Is the sociology sound?

### 1.1 The §5 club micro-thirds fail the chapter's own test (MAJOR — new insertion, lines 251–253)

The insertion reads: "So are the film's small engineering thirds: a doorman holding a guest list that's
missing Steve's name, a paid-entry stamp pressed onto a cousin's licked wrist, a barman emptying the club at
two. Each is visible and arguable, and two get gamed on screen."

Run the chapter's own three questions (§2, lines 76–83) on each:

- **The doorman with the guest list.** Acts on particular persons (named on a list), is the only route in,
  reads the parties themselves. Three engineer-ish answers — and yet he brokers no pair. The chapter defines
  the engineer as "the pair-brokering third" and says a pure engineer "picks the pair." The doorman picks no
  pair; he picks who enters the field at all. He is access control on the setting — the host's boundary
  worked by hand — not a matchmaker. Urban sociology has a whole literature on exactly this figure
  (nightlife gatekeeping, door policy as boundary work), and none of it treats the bouncer as a broker.
- **The paid-entry stamp.** A stamp reads nothing about the party except one type-mark: paid or not. It acts
  on anyone bearing it. By Q1 and Q3 that is host machinery — it is literally the "types, at most" case the
  chapter assigns to the pure host. That it can be gamed by a lick and a press is the tell: no engineer's
  read is defeated that cheaply.
- **The barman emptying the club at two.** Closing time acts on the whole crowd at once, reads nobody, and
  brokers nothing. It is a convention of the setting with the force of law behind it — structurally the same
  kind of thing as civil inattention or the street code, i.e., the host's rule-set. I suspect the drafted
  intent was a parallel with the camera deciding "when a scene ends," but the camera ends scenes one at a
  time, discretionarily; the barman ends everyone's scene by rule. The parallel fails at exactly the point
  the test probes.

So the sentence, as written, hands a hostile referee the chapter's only internal contradiction: the
three-question test is the chapter's proudest apparatus, and its first application to new material breaks
it. The fix is happily better than the original, because the three devices actually span the host–engineer
space the chapter set up at lines 80–83 ("two corners of a larger space, not a tidy binary"): a gate that
reads persons, a gate that reads types, a rule for the whole crowd. Framed that way the club becomes the
chapter's space-between-the-corners in miniature, it echoes the Anderson-derived "blindness to persons, not
to types" line from §3 (the guest list is engineer-ish precisely because it reads persons), and the
public-vs-proprietary punch of the paragraph survives intact — even sharpens: the city's selection devices
are so public they get gamed on camera, while nobody is allowed to film the matching engine. Exact rewrites
in Part 3, R1 (two options).

Optional footnote gesture, author to verify before adopting (I cannot check it from here): the nightlife
door-policy literature — I believe May and Chaplin have a *Qualitative Sociology* piece (~2008) on race,
dress codes, and nightclub exclusion — would anchor "a gate for persons" to the same racial-boundary point
the Drag sentence already makes. Verify author, year, and claim against the source first; do not adopt on my
memory.

### 1.2 Prior-art trap: "What the broker literature never did..." (line 72–74) vs. Mario Small

The claim: "What the broker literature never did was set the plain old setting beside the broker and hold
the two to the same test." The July 3 panel already narrowed this once (Feld accepted, item 16). The nearest
remaining rival my lens knows: **Mario Small, *Unanticipated Gains* (2009)** — childcare centers as
organizational brokers, built explicitly on Feld's foci. Small puts setting and brokerage in one frame,
which is closer than anything currently acknowledged. But note what Small shows: his settings *actively
broker* — field trips and deadlines compel ties — that is, hosts behaving as engineers. He fuses the two
seats; he does not hold them apart and test them against each other, and he has no equivalent of the
host/engineer contrast or the three questions. The novelty claim **survives, narrowed**: not "never put them
in one frame" but "never held them apart under one test." I'd spend one footnote on Small rather than hope
no referee knows him — he is widely read, and a network- or urban-trained reader will reach for him within a
page of meeting Feld. (Adjacent but not threatening: Klinenberg's "social infrastructure" names the host
*function*, not the seat; Jacobs's "public characters" are persons inside settings, not the setting-as-third.
Neither needs text.) Author must verify the Small characterization against the book before adopting.

### 1.3 The Anderson race sentence is right but unanchored, and half of it re-asserts an unverified claim (lines 107–111)

"The blindness that matters here is blindness to persons, not to types: the same code that lets a white
twenty-something pitch Madonna's pap smear on the Drag polices other bodies off it, and a mile east the film
hosts no one at all."

Two problems, neither with the sociology, which is now correct (this is accepted item 27 and it is the right
use of Anderson — the canopy as permitted exception, the code that sorts kinds):

1. The sentence carries **no note**. It is the one place the chapter makes a race claim, and it stands
   citation-naked while everything around it is anchored. Append the existing Anderson entry (already in the
   bibliography and in note 15) to note 17, or give the sentence its own note. Zero body words.
2. "a mile east the film hosts no one at all" asserts film content — no scenes east of I-35 — and the July 3
   triage explicitly parked "East-Austin film claims" as *unverified, author options rather than asserted*.
   This half-sentence asserts it. I am not re-flagging a declined style item; I am flagging that a
   declined-as-unverifiable factual claim has re-entered the text as an assertion. Two ways out: (a) check it
   against Gaughen's location retracing (note 25 — he mapped the locations; if his list confirms nothing east
   of the highway, cite him here and the claim is armored); (b) if Gaughen doesn't settle it, soften to what
   the code claim needs, which is only the Drag half of the sentence. This goes on the author disc/source
   checklist either way.

### 1.5 Minor: Oldenburg's "literal host who greets the regulars" (line 85–86)

Oldenburg's third place is defined by neutral ground, leveling, conversation, and above all the regulars;
the proprietor-as-host is present in *The Great Good Place* but marginal to the concept. "A room with a
literal host" leans on the weakest plank of his account for the sake of the wordplay. The contrast the
chapter needs is only "his third is a room; mine is a seat," which the sentence already delivers. If a word
is needed elsewhere, "who greets the regulars" is cuttable without loss; if kept, it will not draw blood —
a wince risk, not an error. Low priority.
