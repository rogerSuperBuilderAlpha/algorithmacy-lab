## 3. Part 1 — Findings, ranked by damage

**F1. The withdrawn answerer is already named — on the page the chapter cites.** *Section: "The same jobs without an answerer," note 33.* Rosenblat and Stark, p. 3771 — the exact page note 33 cites for template responses and "escalate to manager" — continues: "Although drivers primarily communicate with Uber by e-mail, there is no managerial correspondent empowered with the role of 'foreman' to mediate drivers' frustrations with the company," and, a sentence on, calls for "[a] contextually aware manager able to explain what is happening." That is the vacancy, named as a missing person, in 2016, in the chapter's own source. `VERIFIED` — I retrieved the full PDF (ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/4892/1739, curl tier 2) and confirmed the line sits on p. 3771. The damage is not that the argument is pre-empted — Rosenblat and Stark name a missing foreman, not a third, and they have no 1990, no Simmel, no Reich, no door. The damage is that a reader who checks note 33 will find the chapter's central image half-coined on the cited page and uncredited, and will read the novelty claim as carelessness rather than as the genuine narrowing it is. The fix costs nothing: one sentence in note 33 (P5) that quotes the foreman line and thereby converts the chapter's biggest exposure into corroboration. My wider sweep (below, Part 2e) found the vacancy described repeatedly under other names — never with the door, and never as a third.

**F2. The Review Center sentence commits an anachronism a platform reader will catch at once.** *Section: "The same jobs without an answerer."* The body reads: "Uber maintains a Review Center where a deactivated driver can lodge an appeal, and researchers who studied the channel found template responses, drivers writing 'escalate to manager'…" The definite reference "the channel" points at the Review Center. But Rosenblat and Stark's data are from a nine-month study ending 2015 — CSR e-mail support, some of it outsourced to staffing agencies — and Uber's in-app Review Center launched roughly 2023. The researchers studied the channel the Review Center replaced, eight years before it existed. `VERIFIED` both ends: the article (retrieval above; CSR e-mail apparatus at 3771) and Uber's own deactivation page, which presents the Review Center as a recent build ("That's why we built an in-app Review Center and are continuing to expand it around the world"; WebFetch tier 1, uber.com/us/en/drive/driver-app/deactivation-review/). The claim's substance survives — the appeal channel's texture is template — but the sentence as written attributes the evidence to the wrong artifact. P1 fixes it in the author's own syntax and saves two words. Note that the fix also *helps* the argument: the platform built the address twice, and the answerer arrived neither time.

**F3. Two of the three Uber quotations do not match the cited pages.** *Section: same; note 31.* Checked word for word on 2026-08-19:

- *Ratings:* "who don't meet the minimum average ratings for their city may lose access to all or part of the Uber Marketplace Platform" — **exact** on the General Community Guidelines (WebFetch tier 1; the source subject is "Drivers, riders, delivery people, or merchants," so the chapter's trim starting at "who" is legitimate). `VERIFIED`.
- *Withheld reason:* the chapter quotes the deactivation page as promising to communicate "the reasons for the decision, except when doing so poses a risk to other users." The page reads: "Uber will make every effort to be clear, empathetic, and consistent in our communications and specific and transparent about **the reasons behind our decision, except where** doing so poses a risk to other users." "For the decision" vs. "behind our decision"; "when" vs. "where." `VERIFIED` misquote (WebFetch tier 1; page accessed one day after note 31's access date, so a silent revision is conceivable but unlikely). P3 conforms the quote and, by keeping Uber's "specific and transparent" framing… no — the shorter splice (P3) drops the PR framing and saves two words. Either way the conformed words must go in before press; a misquoted platform policy in a chapter about withheld reasons is the one error this section cannot afford.
- *"Sole discretion":* neither cited page attaches "sole discretion" to deactivation. On the Community Guidelines the phrase governs a different act — "We may, at our sole discretion, put a hold on your account or turn your account inactive until our review is complete" — the interim hold, not the refusal. The phrase does exist for termination, but in Uber's General Terms of Use: "Uber, in its sole discretion, may immediately terminate these Terms or any Services with respect to you, or generally cease offering or deny access to the Services or any portion thereof, at any time for any reason" (`VERIFIED`, WebFetch tier 1, uber.com/us/en/legal/general-terms-of-use/). The driver-facing Platform Access Agreement §5.3 (2020 texts) says "we may terminate this Agreement or permanently deactivate your account without notice if we determine in our discretion that a Material Breach or Violation has occurred" (`VERIFIED`, two PAA PDFs via curl tier 2). Fix: keep the body as is and add the General Terms of Use to note 31 (P4). The PAA's "without notice… in our discretion" is the better instrument for a law reader, but "sole discretion" is quotable as it stands once the note points at the document that says it.

**F4. "The answerer" is stated one property short, and the gap admits a counterexample.** *Section: same.* The chapter's definition — "someone standing in the doorway who hears the objection as it is made" — specifies co-presence and timing but not authority. Uber ran physical Greenlight Hubs for years: a deactivated driver could stand in front of a human being and object as the objection occurred, and it moved nothing, because the person hearing was not the person deciding. Conversely, the Review Center's human reviewer (the EU documents assume one; Uber implies one) decides but never hears the objection as made. The doorman's property is the *conjunction*: the same person decides and absorbs the objection, in one exchange, before the queue moves. The chapter's own conclusion already has the formula — "turns him away, gives no further reason, and hears about it to his face" — but the platform section, where the platform-studies reader is waiting, gives only the hearing half. P2 closes it at the cost of one word. With that conjunction stated, the "a driver is dealing with *some* human somewhere" objection dissolves cleanly: the platform decomposed the doorman into an address (the form), a decider (the policy plus whoever executes it), and a hearer (support), and no two of the three coincide. That decomposition is a real structural claim, not a rhetorical one — but only if the text states the property whose decomposition it is.

**F5. The mapping's second act is looser than the other three, and the chapter's own survey says so.** *Section: same.* "The ratings ledger is the list" is the weakest limb of the act-for-act claim. Steve's list was invisible to Steve and consulted at the point of refusal. The ratings ledger is the one record a driver *can* see; the record that actually triggers most deactivations is the complaint file behind it — the chapter's own source reports 42% of deactivated drivers told only that customers had complained (Fired by an App, p. 19, `VERIFIED`, full PDF via curl tier 2). So the platform kept two records: a visible ledger that legitimates and an invisible file that decides — which is a *deeper* echo of the guest list than the ledger alone, since the guest list's whole force was that only the door's own record could confirm it. I do not propose body words for this; the mapping holds at the altitude the chapter claims (unilateral record-keeping, consulted in the keeper's own forum). But the author should know the limb is soft, and if a synthesis seat wants one strengthening clause, "the ratings ledger and the complaint file behind it are the list" (+5) buys the precision. A second, smaller looseness: at the door the grantee pays for access; at Uber the platform pays the grantee. The dependence runs deeper in the second case — an evening versus a livelihood — so the disanalogy favors the chapter, but a hostile reader could raise it, and the chapter never does.

**F6. The EU paragraph is verified and understated — the directive's best clause for this chapter goes unused.** *Section: same; note 34.* All three instruments check out exactly (retrieval detail in VERIFY): art. 10(5)'s "shall be taken by a human being" is verbatim; Reg 2019/1150 art. 4 requires statements of reasons and 30 days for termination; DSA arts. 17 and 20(6) say what note 34 says they say. Two refinements. First, the directive's art. 11(1) requires "access to a contact person designated by the digital labour platform **to discuss and to clarify the facts, circumstances and reasons having led to the decision**." That is not human review of a file; that is a legislated answerer — the discussing, clarifying person the chapter says the platform withdrew. The body's "legislate the doorman back into the doorway" currently rests on the human-being clause, which guarantees only a human decider somewhere; the contact-person clause guarantees the conversation. Quote it in note 34 (P6) — it is the strongest single piece of evidence the section owns and it is currently invisible. Second, scope: the body says "a platform worker's account," but art. 10(5) covers every "person performing platform work" — the directive's algorithmic-management chapter deliberately reaches the genuinely self-employed, which was its contested point. The compression understates the counter-movement; P8 fixes it for +3 if the budget allows. (A note-level genealogy point, free to ignore: recital 48 frames art. 10(5) as going beyond GDPR art. 22's "human intervention" right, so the counter-movement is a decade old, not new; "has begun trying" remains defensible for the doorway-specific remedies.)

**F7. "Enrolled without a yes" invites the contract lawyer's one-word rebuttal: consent.** *Section: the Viljoen paragraph.* Every driver clicked "I agree" on the Platform Access Agreement. The chapter's Roycean point survives the objection — the click is a yes to the company's terms, not an act of claiming a shared event with the drivers he is grouped against; Viljoen's horizontal relation operates regardless of the vertical contract — but the text never says so, and this is the objection a platform-work-law reader reaches for first. P7 pre-empts it in fifteen words in the author's diction. This is my only recommended addition, and I rank it below every subtraction.

**Minor.** "Linklater glossed the structure twenty years on": the interview quote (verified verbatim, see Part 2g) glosses the *viewing experience* — remote control, multiplex drift — not the coordination arrangement; "the structure" claims slightly more than the remark contains. The very next sentence ("The comparison extends beyond viewing habits, because…") already concedes this and takes the weight onto the chapter's own shoulders, so the gloss is not carrying more than it can bear — it is carrying exactly its own weight, visibly. No change needed; "glossed the film" would be marginally more exact if a synthesis pass is touching the sentence anyway.

Clean for my lens: the opening, the Simmel section (note 20's placement of Vallas & Schor's *tertius gaudens* and Ajunwa's *tertius bifrons* as thirds-between is correct and correctly distinguished from the deferred third), the Royce section, the two-jobs section, "Keeping the jobs apart" (Barns's "basic match-making capabilities" is used within its meaning), and the conclusion.

## 4. Part 2 — Seat-specific audit

**(a) Is "the address exists, the answerer does not" real or rhetorical?** Real, once P2 lands. The property is decomposable and the platform decomposed it: address (a channel that receives), decider (an authority that disposes), hearer (a person who takes the objection as made). At the door all three are one body; at Uber no two coincide. Template responses are what an address sounds like when nobody behind it can dispose; "escalate to manager" is drivers reinventing the doorman from below. As currently drafted the definition specifies hearing but not deciding, and the Greenlight Hub — a human who hears and cannot decide — walks through the gap. One clause closes it.

**(b) Does the contrast survive the addressability/accountability concession?** Yes, and the chapter should not be pushed to claim more than it does. Having conceded that the doorman can be as arbitrary as any system, three things still separate the two cases, and the text delivers all three: timing (Steve's refusal "failed in public, and the group could renegotiate on the sidewalk"; the deactivation notice "arrives after the fact, and the queue has already moved on"); reversibility (the Video Backpacker's "can't press rewind" — a genuinely earned image, since the film supplies it); and the counter-movement, which does the normative work the concession seemed to surrender. The chapter's argument is not that answerers are fairer — it says they aren't — but that their absence is the thing three EU instruments and two Washington-state regimes were written to repair: "That such remedies were thought necessary is itself evidence of the vacancy they address" is the section's best sentence and it is verified all the way down. The contrast does normative work by proxy: the legislators did the valuing, and the chapter only has to report it.

**(c) Formal or decorative?** Formal on three of four limbs, loose on one (F5). Price: unilateral setting holds (upfront pricing has decoupled fare from fee since the quote-level description, but "sets the fare and takes its fee" remains true at the altitude claimed). Forum: the Review Center is an in-house forum by Uber's own description; Reg 2019/1150 art. 4(3) even legislates the internal forum ("the opportunity to clarify the facts and circumstances in the framework of the internal complaint-handling process"). Withheld reason: verified as written policy, which is the mapping's cleverest limb — at the door the withholding was a silence, at the platform it is a clause. Record: see F5. On the brief's load-bearing question 5 ("the same gesture seen at forty years' distance"): the sentence survives if "gesture" means the fourth act, the withholding — which recurs act-for-act — and not the whole scene, which differs by exactly the variable the chapter names (the person). The chapter's own preceding sentence ("kept both jobs and the grant intact and withdrew the person") sets that reading up; I judge the sentence defensible as placed.

**(d) The survey.** The body hedge is adequate and the numbers hold: 66% of all 810 respondents reported temporary or permanent deactivation (report p. 15), 30% "of surveyed drivers who have been either temporarily or permanently deactivated" got no explanation (p. 19) — the chapter's "thirty percent of those" reads the conditional correctly. The report's own methods page concedes the sample "may be more likely to be inclined to participate in a survey as a way of documenting or sharing their experience with deactivation" (p. 11), which note 32 fairly compresses. The two-thirds figure can support no population inference and the body's "self-selected sample" flags exactly that; the 30% figure, conditional on deactivation, is the one doing argumentative work and is the more robust of the two. No change. `VERIFIED` throughout (full PDF, curl tier 2).

**(e) Prior art on the withdrawn answerer.** I searched beyond the chapter's sources. What exists: Rosenblat & Stark's missing "foreman" (F1 — the close call, and it is on the cited page); Tarafdar et al.'s "absence of mutually co-constructed dialogue" (in the chapter's own note 33 source — abstract, verified); Gray and Suri's *Ghost Work* on "algorithmic cruelty" with "no one at the helm"; Rosenblat's *Uberland* marketing itself as a book about "a faceless boss"; Almada's contestability-by-design line, which holds that a human in the loop is insufficient absent a "dialectical exchange" between decision subjects and human controllers — the nearest conceptual statement of the answerer's defining property; the HCI contestability literature (Vaccaro & Karahalios; Lyons et al.); Bovens's accountability-as-answerability in the governance literature; Van Loo on platforms as in-house courts ("The Corporation as Courthouse"), which pre-figures the third act of the mapping. What does not exist, as far as retrieval can show: any source running a door grant against deactivation act for act; any source pairing Simmel's postponed third with the platform's outside position (the chapter's notes 20–21 correctly clear the thirds-between); any source dating the withdrawal against a pre-platform baseline. **The vacancy is well documented under scattered names; the geometry, the baseline, and the door are the chapter's.** The novelty claim survives — provided F1's acknowledgment goes in, because the difference between "nobody has seen this" and "everybody has half-seen it and here is the whole" is precisely the difference the foreman citation marks. If an editor asks "isn't this just contestability?", the answer the chapter is entitled to: contestability names a design goal; this chapter names the person, and dates the withdrawal.

**(f) EU verification.** All retrieved this session (EUR-Lex blocks direct fetch with HTTP 202; Wayback snapshots of the CELEX HTML via curl, tier 3). Directive (EU) 2024/2831 art. 10(5), verbatim: "Any decision to restrict, suspend or terminate the contractual relationship or the account of a person performing platform work or any other decision of equivalent detriment shall be taken by a human being." Art. 11(1): explanation right plus the contact-person clause quoted in F6; art. 11(2): reviewed reply within two weeks. Reg 2019/1150 art. 4(1)–(2): statement of reasons on a durable medium; 30 days' notice for termination. DSA art. 17(1): "clear and specific statement of reasons" for the four restriction types including account termination; art. 20(6): complaint decisions "under the supervision of appropriately qualified staff, and not solely on the basis of automated means." Note 34's parentheticals are accurate summaries in every particular. The counter-movement claim holds.

**(g) Linklater.** Quote verified verbatim against the *Austin Chronicle* page (curl tier 2, 403 on WebFetch): "I saw Slacker as you're either channel surfing or you're going in and out of different movies. And that was a really primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex." Context is the TV-remote generation and the multiplex. Assessment at the end of Part 1: carrying its own weight, no more, and the chapter says so itself.

## 6. Verdict

The revisions are minor but not optional — two misquoted platform texts and an anachronistic attribution, in the section whose subject is withheld and mangled reasons, would hand a hostile reviewer the chapter's own weapon.

- **Biggest genuine strength:** the counter-movement paragraph. Few chapters get to end an analogy with the opposing side's statute conceding the point.
- **The one thing only the author can supply:** the current US rideshare Platform Access Agreement. My §5.3 verification runs on 2020 texts; the current agreement's deactivation clause should be conformed (and the *Uberland* deactivation page reference from the standing list remains open).
